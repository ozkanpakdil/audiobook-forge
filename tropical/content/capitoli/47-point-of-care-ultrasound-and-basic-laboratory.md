# Chapter 47 — Point-of-Care Ultrasound and Basic Laboratory Skills

Ultrasound has become the stethoscope of the modern remote clinician, and this chapter teaches you to carry it as one. A machine the size of a thick book now answers questions that once demanded a hospital: is there blood in the abdomen, is there fluid around the heart, is the lung wet, is the fetus alive. It uses no radiation, it costs nothing per scan, it can be repeated as often as the patient's condition changes, and it brings the answer to the bedside rather than the patient to the machine. Used well, it shortens the distance between suspicion and decision; used carelessly, it replaces a good clinical judgment with a bad picture. The same chapter then turns to the second craft the remote nurse carries alone, the basic laboratory, because a clinic that can confirm malaria, measure hemoglobin and glucose, read a urine dipstick, test for the human immunodeficiency virus and syphilis, and match blood safely is a different clinical place from one that cannot. The recommendations here follow the World Health Organization, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the clinical and laboratory guidance of Doctors Without Borders, and every figure and regimen in what follows is current guidance rather than settled truth, so verify the current guideline before you act on any of it.

Point-of-care ultrasound is not a smaller version of the radiologist's complete study, and the difference matters more than the machine. The radiologist surveys an organ system and describes everything seen; you ask a single question with a yes or no answer and stop once it is answered. Is there free fluid, is the right ventricle collapsing, does the lung slide. This focus is what makes the skill learnable in weeks rather than years, and it keeps the scan honest, because the moment an untrained hand goes looking beyond the question, mistakes multiply. The method never changes: decide the question first, choose the probe and view that answer it, hold still, interpret, record. The scan never replaces the history and the examination, because the question you bring to the probe was chosen by everything else you already know about the patient.

The three probes that matter in remote practice each trade depth for detail, and the trade is physics rather than preference. Sound at a higher frequency paints a finer picture but tires quickly and cannot reach far, while sound at a lower frequency travels deep but paints coarsely. The curvilinear probe, working around 2 to 5 megahertz, is the abdominal and obstetric workhorse, reaching the liver, the aorta, the kidneys and the pregnant uterus through a wide curved window. The phased array probe, with a small flat face, slips between the ribs and is the cardiac probe, though it also reaches the abdomen in a pinch. The linear probe, working around 6 to 13 megahertz, lives near the surface, with the neck vessels, the arm veins, the lung surface, abscesses and foreign bodies. Gel or clean water must stand between probe and skin, because sound does not cross air, and a dry probe sees nothing at all.

The machine's controls are few, and the three that matter are depth, gain and the freeze button. Depth sets how much tissue fills the screen, and the structure you are asking about belongs in the middle third, because anything pressed against the very top or bottom of the display is hard to interpret and easy to misjudge. Gain is brightness, and it is the control that misleads beginners most: turned too high, a dark fluid collection fills with gray noise until free fluid looks like tissue, and turned too low, faint real findings vanish. The freeze button stops the moving picture so you can study it, measure it and save it, and saving is a habit to form early, because a stored image can be reviewed by a distant mentor tomorrow while a live glance cannot. Orientation comes from the marker embossed on the probe and the dot on the edge of the screen: hold the probe marker toward the patient's head or the patient's right, and the screen dot tells you which side of the image is which. Every modern machine carries presets named for abdomen, heart or vessel, and starting from the right preset saves a minute of fumbling at the bedside.

Artifacts are the shadows and echoes that mislead a beginner, and learning them early prevents most early errors. Bone and stones stop sound cleanly and throw a dark shadow behind them, while gas scatters it into a dirty, noisy shadow that flickers when the patient moves or belches. A mirror artifact can duplicate the liver across the diaphragm and invent a structure that does not exist, and an edge shadow drops a dark stripe off the rounded side of a cyst or vessel. True fluid, by contrast, is black, homogeneous and quietly bordered, and its edges come to sharp points where it wedges between organs, which is how free blood announces itself. When an image makes no sense, do not turn the gain and hope; move the probe, ask the patient to breathe deeply or roll to one side, and compare with the other flank, because a real finding stays and an artifact dissolves.

The focused abdominal scan for free fluid in trauma is the classic remote application, and it is built on anatomy rather than dexterity. The examination, the focused assessment with sonography in trauma, looks at the places where blood pools in a patient lying on their back: the right upper quadrant where the liver meets the right kidney, in the space named Morrison's pouch, the left upper quadrant around the spleen, the pelvis behind the bladder or uterus, and the sac around the heart. Slide the curvilinear probe through each window, hold still through several breaths, and look for black, sharply pointed collections where none should be. The reasoning is simple, because the peritoneum is a basin with dependent pockets and free fluid obeys gravity, so a negative scan in every window makes significant intraperitoneal bleeding unlikely. The interpretation belongs to the whole patient, because this scan sees peritoneal blood and says nothing about the retroperitoneum, the bowel or the solid organs themselves, and a battered patient with a negative scan still needs observation and often transfer. In the unstable patient a positive scan is not a reason to keep scanning but a reason to call the surgeon, activate the transfusion plan and prepare the road, which is how the trauma guidance of the World Health Organization and Doctors Without Borders intends it to be used.

The cardiac scan answers three questions, and each can change a resuscitation, and all three can be learned from two or three views. The easiest window in a breathless supine patient is the subcostal view, with the probe held flat under the rib cage looking up at the heart, while the parasternal long axis view between the ribs of the left chest shows the left ventricle in profile. The first question is whether there is fluid in the pericardium: an anechoic stripe circling the heart means an effusion, and when that fluid presses hard enough the right ventricle squeezes inward as it relaxes, the sign of tamponade, because the inelastic sac stops the heart from filling. The second is whether the heart is beating at all, a question that matters in arrest, because a still heart despite an organized rhythm on the electrocardiogram changes the prognosis and the conversation with the family. The third is whether the left side is full or empty: a small, thick-walled ventricle slamming almost shut with each beat suggests a circulation that is empty and needs volume, while a dilated, sluggishly contracting one suggests failure or overload rather than dehydration. The inferior vena cava adds a slow measure of the tank, since a flat vein that collapses with each breath supports the empty side of the answer and a wide, motionless vein supports the full or obstructed side.

The lung scan is the most elegant example of turning a limitation into a tool, because ultrasound cannot pass through air, and the lung is air. What the probe watches instead is the pleural line, the bright shimmering ribbon where the lung surface meets the chest wall, and how it behaves with each breath. In the normal lung the line slides sideways with respiration, the sliding sign, and where the lung slides there is no pneumothorax at that spot, because a collapsed lung no longer rubs against the chest wall. Absent sliding after chest trauma, a procedure or unexplained hypoxia means pneumothorax until proved otherwise, and if the probe finds a point on the chest wall where the sliding returns, the lung point, the diagnosis is as close to certain as bedside medicine allows. Vertical comet-tail artifacts called B-lines, when more than three fan out from one patch of pleura, mean the lung substance is wet, the profile of pulmonary edema or pneumonitis, while a patch of lung that looks like liver with bright branching specks inside is a consolidated pneumonia. Fluid above the diaphragm, black and curving with the breath, is a pleural effusion, and unlike computed tomography, or CT, the scan can be repeated every hour to watch a fluid plan succeed or fail.

The obstetric scan earns its place in remote practice through four questions: is the pregnancy in the uterus, is the fetus alive, how does it lie, and how grown is it. A heartbeat is visible from about six weeks of pregnancy, a little later through the abdominal wall, and the flicker in the chest is the single most reassuring and most devastating finding the machine delivers; count it over time and expect 110 to 160 beats per minute in mid and late pregnancy. The lie and position answer themselves once you find the head: a head down over the pelvis is cephalic, a head under the ribs is breech, and a shoulder or back over the pelvis is transverse, which means this delivery needs planning and probably referral. Gestational age comes from measurement, since before about 13 weeks the crown-rump length on one careful view is the most accurate measure in all of obstetrics, and later the width of the head and the length of the femur are compared with tables the machine carries. The estimated weight follows, and the value of knowing it shows in the decisions it drives, because a baby much smaller than dates raises intrauterine growth restriction and a pregnancy beyond any date the mother knows raises questions the scan alone cannot settle. Be honest about the limits of your training: an intrauterine pregnancy makes an ectopic pregnancy far less likely but does not fully exclude one, and a claim about the placenta's position is not yours to make until you have been trained to make it, because false comfort is worse than no scan at all.

The aorta and the kidneys complete the abdominal panel, and both scans take minutes. With the curvilinear probe held transversely from the rib margin to the navel, the aorta lies in front of the spine as a pulsating circle, and a side-to-side width less than 3 centimeters is normal, while 3 centimeters or more is an aneurysm whose risk of rupture rises steeply with every centimeter of growth. The patient who makes this scan matter is the older man with sudden abdominal or back pain and a falling blood pressure, and here is the trap the scan must be taught with: a ruptured aneurysm bleeds into the retroperitoneum, where this scan cannot see it, so a normal-looking aorta in a collapsed patient does not exclude rupture and must not slow the transfer. Each kidney is found on its flank as a bean of gray tissue with a black collecting system at its core, and when that core splits into ballooned black cones the outflow is obstructed, most often by a stone, which throws its own clean shadow. Follow the tract to the bladder and measure the residual after the patient passes water, because a bladder that never empties explains a fever, a sepsis and a wet bed. Repeated over days, the kidney scan becomes a monitor that tells you whether an obstruction is settling or a drainage plan is working, which is the quiet way this machine earns its electricity.

Vascular access is the scan that most quickly turns the machine from a diagnostic instrument into a lifesaver, and it is where the linear probe earns its keep. When veins collapse in shock, edema, obesity or a squirming child, the landmark method fails, and the probe shows the vein before the needle ever touches the skin. Vein and artery identify themselves on the screen, because a vein flattens when you press with the probe while an artery resists and pulses, and the internal jugular vein in the neck sits just beside the carotid artery, a relationship the scan must respect every single time. Watch the needle enter in the plane of the probe, follow its tip as a bright dot sliding toward the dark lumen, and never advance a tip you cannot see, because the tip you have lost is the one that perforates the far wall or the artery. Once you believe you are in, confirm it: blood flashes back, saline flushes without swelling, and bubbles swirl in the vein on the screen, while a flush into an artery brightens and streams the wrong way. Practice on the easy patient with the good veins while nothing is at stake, because the scan you have done twenty times is the one that behaves when it matters.

Knowing what ultrasound cannot do protects patients more than any new view, so finish the training with the limits. Ultrasound does not pass through gas or bone, so bowel and skull keep their secrets, and the retroperitoneum hides behind gas-filled loops. It is operator dependent to a degree no other bedside skill is, and a normal scan from an untrained hand is worth little, because the machine documents nothing unless you save, and a saved image reviewed by a mentor is the only way your learning becomes visible to anyone but you. A negative scan never overrides the clinical picture, since the angry abdomen with a negative focused assessment with sonography in trauma still needs observation, the patient whose lung slides can still be desperately sick, and the fetus can hide anomalies beyond any untrained eye. It cannot measure organ function, it cannot diagnose bowel injury, and it cannot turn a nurse into a radiologist, and pretending otherwise is how a good tool causes bad care. The scan is a question asked of the body, and when the answer is beyond your training, the correct act is to ask a distant mentor by saved image, or to refer, or both.

The second half of this chapter moves from the screen to the bench, because the basic laboratory of a remote clinic is usually a nurse working alone with a box of reagents and an unreliable fridge. The tests you can run are few, and each earns its place by changing a decision today rather than by decorating a chart: the malaria film and the rapid diagnostic test, hemoglobin, blood glucose, the urine dipstick, the tests for the human immunodeficiency virus and syphilis, and the grouping and crossmatching that make transfusion survivable. Running them well has less to do with chemistry than with discipline, because the errors of a small laboratory are almost never subtle failures of science; they are expired strips, warm reagents, unlabeled tubes, mislabeled patients and results nobody wrote down. The nurse who runs these tests is also their quality officer, their stock clerk and their auditor, and the rest of this chapter walks through each test and then through the system that keeps them honest. Keep one habit above all: a test whose result cannot be trusted is worse than no test, because it manufactures false certainty, and false certainty in a remote clinic kills with a straight face.

The malaria film remains the reference standard, and it is the one test in this chapter where your technique changes the answer directly. The thick film smears a larger volume of blood in one spot, dries unstained, and concentrates parasites so that a light infection can still be found; its cost is that the red cells are destroyed in staining, so species and density are hard to judge from the thick film alone. The thin film spreads blood in a single layer of intact red cells, fixed with a quick dip in methanol before staining, and it shows the parasite inside the red cell, where its shape and its dots name the species, above all the difference between the parasite Plasmodium falciparum, the species that causes severe and cerebral malaria, and the milder species that relapse. Staining is typically Giemsa diluted in buffered water, roughly one part stain to nine parts water for around ten minutes, but follow the local laboratory protocol and verify it, because timing and dilution differ between programs. Read the thick film for the yes or no, counting parasites against a fixed number of white blood cells, commonly 200, and read the thin film for the species and the percentage of red cells infected, which together give the parasite density per microliter that treatment response is judged by. A clean slide, an even smear, a stain at the right acidity and a patient correctly labeled are the whole of the science, and the rest of the skill is patience at the microscope.

The malaria rapid diagnostic test exists because microscopy needs a microscope, a stain, electricity and a practiced eye, and none of these are guaranteed at two in the morning. The strip carries antibodies against parasite antigens, commonly histidine-rich protein 2 for Plasmodium falciparum or parasite lactate dehydrogenase, and a colored line forms when antigen is present; the workflow is a drop of blood, a fixed number of buffer drops, and a strict reading window, usually at 15 to 20 minutes, because a line read too early understates and a line read too late invents. Store the kits dry and cool, never freeze them, and treat the expiry date as law, because heat and humidity quietly destroy the antibodies months before the date passes. A positive test in a fever is a diagnosis, but a negative test deserves more respect than it usually gets, since light infections, and parasites whose histidine-rich protein gene has been deleted, which is increasingly reported in several regions, can produce a false negative. The World Health Organization therefore asks that treatment follow a confirmed test wherever testing exists, and that a negative result in a severely ill patient never be allowed to close the case, because severe malaria kills between one clinic visit and the next. The right sequence is the test, the treatment of whatever it shows, the film for species and density when the patient is sick, and the retest when the story and the strip disagree.

Hemoglobin measurement at a distance works through four methods, each a compromise you should understand rather than merely perform. The direct-reading hemoglobinometer takes one drop of blood into a small cuvette and converts its color to a number on the spot, and it is the most accurate portable option provided the cuvettes are dry, unscratched and within date. The microhematocrit gives the packed cell volume instead: spin a heparinized capillary tube of blood in a small centrifuge for about five minutes, read the column of packed red cells against the scale, and treat a packed cell volume of roughly 33 per cent as equivalent to about 11 grams per deciliter of hemoglobin, remembering that the relationship drifts in pregnancy and in iron deficiency. The copper sulfate method is pure physics, because a drop of blood either sinks or floats in a solution calibrated to a chosen threshold, so it does not measure hemoglobin but sorts donors and patients into above and below, and it keeps working with no electricity at all. The hemoglobin color scale of the World Health Organization compares a drop of blood on filter paper against printed standards and reads to within about one gram per deciliter, which is enough to find the severe anemia that matters. Whatever the method, agree the thresholds with your protocol before the emergency, because numbers around 7 grams per deciliter in pregnancy and 4 grams per deciliter in a stable child are where transfusion decisions turn in current guidance, and the current national guideline, not this paragraph, must be checked before blood is ordered.

Blood glucose is the cheapest piece of information per test in this chapter, and the glucometer strip is also the most commonly ruined test in remote clinics. The machine reads a drop of capillary blood on an enzyme strip, and the strips die fast in heat, humidity and open air, so keep the container closed with its desiccant, write the date each vial is opened, and discard within the window the manufacturer prints, usually a few months. Wash the finger or discard the first drop when hands are unwashed, never squeeze the fingertip hard, because tissue fluid dilutes the drop and falsely lowers the reading, and run the control solution with each new vial and whenever a reading makes no clinical sense. The thresholds to carry in your head are simple: below 70 milligrams per deciliter is hypoglycemia and needs feeding or sugar now, and below 40 milligrams per deciliter is severe and needs intravenous dextrose immediately, with the exact doses and concentrations taken from the current severe illness guideline before you need them. In severe malaria, severe dengue, severe malnutrition, sepsis and any convulsing or depressed patient, glucose belongs in the first minutes of assessment and then on a schedule, commonly every four to six hours, because hypoglycemia recurs and announces itself as a worsening of every other symptom. Write the numbers down with the time, because a glucose trend is a story while a single glucose is a snapshot, and the trend is what tells you whether the patient is drifting toward a crisis or away from one.

The urine dipstick is a battery of chemical tests on one plastic strip, and it earns a paragraph of warnings because its errors are quiet ones. Dip a fresh midstream sample, drain the excess against the container rim, and read each pad at its stated time against the chart on the bottle in good light, because a pad read too early or too late returns a confident wrong answer. The nitrite pad needs bacteria to have dwelt in the bladder for hours, so a negative nitrite does not exclude infection in a pregnant woman or small child who voids often, and the leukocyte esterase pad can be falsely quiet in a patient with very few white cells. The blood pad detects intact red cells, but it also detects free hemoglobin and myoglobin, so it turns positive in malaria with hemolysis and in snakebite with muscle injury, which is not bleeding from the kidney but is easily misread as such. Protein matters most in pregnancy, because two plus or more in a woman with high blood pressure or symptoms is pre-eclampsia until excluded, and the quantitative confirmation is 300 milligrams or more in a full day of urine, so the dipstick is the trigger rather than the verdict. Glucose on the dipstick is common in later pregnancy from the normal lowering of the kidney's threshold and does not by itself mean diabetes, while a very dilute sample makes everything less sensitive, so read the specific gravity pad first and judge the rest accordingly. Strips expire, heat ruins them months before the printed date, and a bottle stored loose in a dusty bag should be treated as suspect, so date every bottle when opened and keep it lidded.

The tests for the human immunodeficiency virus and syphilis arrive as rapid kits, and the counseling around them is as much the intervention as the chemistry. Test with informed consent, in privacy, and with a plan for whatever the result is, because a test whose positive result leads nowhere is a harm rather than a service. The algorithm for the human immunodeficiency virus in most national guidelines is a sequence of rapid tests: a sensitive screening test first and, if positive, a confirmatory rapid test based on a different antigen, read exactly at the stated minute and never from a line you half see. Respect the window period, which is why an exposed patient with a negative test is told when to return rather than told they are safe forever, and the same principle governs the exposed infant, whose early tests reflect the mother's antibodies rather than the infant's own. A positive result is delivered in the same unhurried voice as a negative one, with immediate linkage to antiretroviral therapy, which current World Health Organization guidance starts on the day of diagnosis, and with partner notification raised gently and only with the patient's consent. The register where results are recorded is confidential, and so is the conversation, because in many communities the wrong person learning a result causes more harm than the virus itself, and the nurse holds that boundary.

Syphilis pairs with it in pregnancy because congenital syphilis is entirely preventable with one injection, and the rapid treponemal test detects antibody that lasts for years or for life, so a positive line may mean active syphilis or an infection treated a decade ago. Where a confirmatory non-treponemal test such as the rapid plasma reagin exists, it is used to show active disease and to follow the response to treatment; where it does not, the national protocol decides whether to treat on the single positive, and in pregnancy the answer is usually to treat. Current World Health Organization guidance treats early syphilis with benzathine penicillin 2.4 million units intramuscularly as a single dose, and late or unknown-duration syphilis with the same dose once weekly for three weeks, with alternatives for the penicillin-allergic patient set by the national guideline, and these regimens are revised and must be verified before you inject. Expect the Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction within the first hours after treatment, a fever with chills, headache and transient worsening as the dying spirochetes provoke inflammation, which is common, usually brief and occasionally dangerous in pregnancy, so watch the fetal heart and the contractions and keep the patient near for those hours. The counseling follows the same discipline as for the human immunodeficiency virus: consent, privacy, the meaning of the result in plain language, treatment of the partner, and the return visit confirmed rather than assumed.

Crossmatching where no blood bank exists is the most serious test in this chapter, and it should be done rarely, deliberately and inside a written protocol, because it is the one place where a laboratory error kills within minutes. Select donors with the standard criteria: healthy, commonly between 18 and 60 years of age, weighing at least 50 kilograms, with a hemoglobin above the national threshold, usually 12.5 grams per deciliter, and defer anyone with anemia, current infection, a risk behavior for a transmissible virus, recent illness or surgery, or a pregnancy. Screen the unit with rapid tests for the human immunodeficiency virus, hepatitis B, hepatitis C and syphilis, and in a malaria-endemic area for malaria as well, because transfusing parasites into a sick recipient is a real and avoidable harm. Group the blood on a tile with antisera, where cells that clump in anti-A serum or anti-B serum name the group and the rhesus antiserum names the rhesus, and repeat the grouping on the recipient with the same care. The major crossmatch mixes donor cells with recipient serum and looks for clumping, and the minor crossmatch mixes donor plasma with recipient cells, and clumping in either means the unit is refused, while in an emergency uncrossmatched group O rhesus negative blood is the fallback chosen knowingly, with consent, never the shortcut of assumed compatibility. The whole act, from donor interview to bedside identity check, belongs to the nurse, and the check of names, groups and expiry between two people at the bedside is the last and most important test of all.

Transfusion itself is a monitored procedure, and the nurse is the monitor. Take the temperature, pulse, blood pressure and respiratory rate before the unit starts, fifteen minutes into it and hourly after, because most severe reactions announce themselves in the first minutes, and the first signs are fever, restlessness, a racing pulse, a rising respiratory rate or a falling blood pressure. A hemolytic reaction from mismatched blood shows fever with back or flank pain, dark urine, falling blood pressure and bleeding from puncture sites, and the response is the same for any serious reaction: stop the unit, keep the line open with saline, reassess the patient, send the unit and a fresh sample back to the bench, and document everything with times. Milder fever and rash reactions are managed by slowing or stopping and reviewing according to the national guideline, and the difference between a minor reaction and a fatal one is decided at the bedside, so a reaction is never treated as background noise. The blood itself lives between 2 and 6 degrees Celsius in a monitored fridge, leaves the fridge for one patient only, and once out should start within the usual 30 minutes and finish within about 4 hours, because warm blood breeds bacteria and a unit hanging too long becomes a sepsis. These rules are blood service standards, and the current national standard and the local blood service guideline, not memory, are what govern your fridge log, your discard times and your paperwork.

Quality control without a quality department is a set of small habits performed daily rather than a department, and the habits are what make results trustworthy. The refrigerator temperature is read morning and evening with a maximum and minimum thermometer and written in a log, with an agreed alarm threshold so that a failing fridge at night is a discovered problem rather than a morning surprise. Reagents are dated the day they are opened, stored at the temperature their label demands, and used first expiry first out, and a monthly count of stock, matched against the register of tests performed, tells you what to order before the shortage instead of after it. Cold chain is verified rather than assumed, because a cool box with conditioned ice packs and a thermometer travels with every reagent, and the receiving nurse records what arrived and at what temperature. Controls are run whenever a new lot of anything is opened: the positive and negative control in the kit box, the control solution for the glucometer, the known-positive serum kept for the rapid tests, and a known sample through the hemoglobin method, and a failed control takes that test out of service until the failure is explained. Competence is audited the same way, through a logbook of who performed which test, periodic slides sent to a reference laboratory to be read blind against your own reading, and honest removal of yourself from a test you have not performed recently enough to trust your hands.

The discipline of writing results down is the quiet spine of the whole chapter, because in a clinic with rotating staff and no server, the register is the chart, the audit and the memory of the facility. Every result is written with the date, the time, the patient's name and identifier, the method, the result with its units, and the initials of the person who performed it, in a book that never leaves the facility and is reviewed at handover. Trends matter more than snapshots, and they exist only if the numbers sit in one place: the parasite density at diagnosis and again at 24 and 48 hours to show the treatment working, the glucose series, the hemoglobin before and after transfusion, the dipstick protein across the antenatal visits. An image saved or a simple sketch of a positive finding, with the date, extends the register to the ultrasound, and an image that can be sent to a mentor doubles as teaching and documentation at once. A result recorded and not acted upon is paperwork, and a result acted upon and not recorded is a rumor, so the entry in the book and the entry in the patient's chart close the loop together. The register is also the record that survives you, and in a referral, a mass campaign or an outbreak investigation it becomes the evidence on which others decide, so write it for the stranger who will read it next year.

Training and logistics keep both crafts alive, and both are nursing responsibilities rather than footnotes. Ultrasound skill is built by supervised repetition, not by watching, and a common pathway is dozens of supervised normal scans per application, a logbook of images reviewed by a mentor who can be reached by phone or saved image, and a standing rule against interpreting alone what you have not been signed off to interpret. The machine needs care the way a microscope does: probes are never dropped, cables are never wound tightly, the screen is shielded from dust and direct heat, and the unit is charged and stored in its case between uses. Gel is a consumable that runs out far from resupply, so ration it, keep the bottle capped against drying, and know that clean water works for a quick look though it demands a firmer hand, while improvised covers of clean plastic sheeting let the probe travel between patients without carrying their skin flora. Power is a clinical issue here as everywhere, so know how many hours the battery holds, which solar or generator arrangement recharges it, and which examinations to ration when the sky is overcast for a week. The same logic governs the bench, with a microscope under its dust cover, reagents off the floor and away from the fridge's exhaust, and a named person for every inventory, because equipment without an owner decays into scenery.

Safety around ultrasound deserves its own words, because the question is asked often and deserves an honest answer. At the low outputs used for diagnostic imaging, no harm has been demonstrated across decades of obstetric use, but prudence stands as a principle: use the lowest power that answers the question, spend the least time that answers it, and never scan out of curiosity what you can learn by hand. The real harms of this tool are indirect, and they are the false reassurance of a scan read wrongly, the delay while an untrained operator searches an abdomen while the patient needed the road, and the privacy taken from a patient examined without explanation, cover or a chaperone when culture asks for one. Explain the scan in plain language before you start, say what the picture can and cannot tell, and share the finding honestly, because the machine in your hand changes what the patient believes is happening to them. The same consent, privacy and honest explanation govern the rapid tests, and a patient who understands what is being tested and why cooperates better, returns sooner and tells the neighbors the truth rather than the rumor.

To summarize, point-of-care ultrasound extends the physical examination with questions a handheld machine answers in minutes, and the method is always the same: a focused question, the right probe, a steady hand and an honest record. Learn the probes and the three controls, respect the artifacts, and let the focused assessment with sonography in trauma, the cardiac and lung scans, the obstetric scan, the aorta and kidney scans and the vascular access scan each answer only what they are able to answer, remembering always what ultrasound cannot see. The laboratory the nurse runs alone earns trust through the malaria film and rapid diagnostic test, the hemoglobin methods that work without mains power, the glucose meter with its fragile strips, the urine dipstick read at its stated times, the rapid tests for the human immunodeficiency virus and syphilis delivered with consent and linkage to care, and the rarely performed, carefully protocolized crossmatch followed by a monitored transfusion. Quality is a set of daily habits rather than a department, with the temperature log, the expiry discipline, the cold chain, the controls run on every new lot and the register in which every result is written with time, name and initials. The nurse in these settings is the operator, the analyst, the quality officer and the person who writes it all down, and every figure and regimen in this chapter is current guidance rather than settled truth, so verify the current guideline before you act.
