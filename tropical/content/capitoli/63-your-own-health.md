# Chapter 63 — Your Own Health

The clinician is the most valuable piece of equipment in any remote clinic, and the only one that cannot be replaced by a donation. A funder can ship a refrigerator, an ultrasound machine, or a crate of antibiotics, and all of those things will arrive eventually and be installed by someone. Nobody can ship a replacement for you. When you are the person who runs the antenatal clinic, sets the fracture, reads the malaria film, and knows which child is failing to thrive, your absence is not a staffing inconvenience. It is a hole in the middle of the service, and the patients who fall into that hole are the ones you were there to protect. This chapter is about keeping the equipment in working order, which means treating your own body and mind as a clinical responsibility rather than as a private matter.

There is a particular trap in this work, which is that caring for other people is a legitimate reason to postpone caring for yourself, and a very persuasive one. The clinic is always open, the queue is always long, the motorbike is always broken, and there is always a reason why your own dental abscess, your own cough, or your own missed vaccination can wait until the next rotation. The principle to hold instead is that your health is one of the systems the clinic depends on, exactly like the cold chain or the generator. When it fails, everything downstream fails with it, and the failure is sudden. So the same seriousness you would apply to a patient's fever must be applied to your own, and the same documentation must exist, because a health problem that is not written down is a health problem that will be forgotten.

Preparation begins before departure, and the vaccines are the first item because they are cheap, durable, and almost completely protective. The World Health Organization publishes the vaccines recommended for health workers and for travel to specific regions, and the national schedule of your destination country may add requirements that your home country does not. The practical rule is to start at least 6 weeks before departure, because several of these vaccines need more than one dose and because the immune response takes time to develop. That timeline is frequently ignored, and the nurse who receives a posting with 10 days of notice ends up taking whatever can still be given and accepting the gaps. Write the plan down, with the dates for each dose, and treat those dates like a drug regimen rather than like a suggestion. Records must be kept in two places, a paper card you carry and a photograph or a scan stored where you can reach it.

Several vaccines deserve specific attention for the nurse going to tropical practice. Hepatitis A and hepatitis B can be given together as a combined vaccine, and the hepatitis B series of three doses is the one that matters most, because it protects against a blood-borne virus you will meet through needle-stick injuries and through contact with blood in a clinic where gloves run short. Typhoid vaccine is available as an injectable dose or as a course of oral capsules, and neither is fully protective, so it joins hand hygiene rather than replacing it. Yellow fever vaccine is a single dose that is required for entry to some countries and whose certificate becomes valid 10 days after administration. Rabies pre-exposure vaccination simplifies the response to a bite or a scratch, but it does not remove the need for post-exposure treatment, and a tetanus booster every 10 years keeps a mundane wound from becoming a fatal one. These schedules are revised, they differ between countries, and the current World Health Organization or Centers for Disease Control and Prevention guidance must be checked before your appointment, because the edition that was current when your colleague trained may already have changed.

The record of your vaccination matters more than most people expect, and losing it is a real operational problem. Keep the international certificate with your passport, keep a copy in your personal file, and keep a photograph of both on a device that travels with you. If you work for an organization, ensure that your occupational health record names every vaccine, its date, and its batch number where known, because a needle-stick injury will send you looking for that information in a hurry. When you arrive at a new post, give the medical officer a written summary of your immunizations, your chronic conditions, your allergies, and the drugs you take, and keep a copy for yourself. Update it after every vaccination and after every change of medication, because a stale summary can mislead the person treating you.

Malaria prophylaxis is where good intentions most often collapse, and the collapse is understandable. The tablets are taken for months, they can cause unpleasant effects, the risk feels abstract on a quiet week, and nobody is checking. Then the nurse who has been advising patients about adherence for a year quietly stops taking her own tablets, and 6 weeks later she is the patient with fever and a positive blood film. The principle is to choose the drug deliberately, in advance, with a clinician who knows your history, and then to take it on a fixed schedule that does not depend on how you feel. The main options include atovaquone with proguanil, doxycycline, mefloquine, and tafenoquine, and the choice depends on the resistance pattern where you are going, on your pregnancy status, on your psychiatric history, and on whether you have glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency. Guidance on which drug suits which destination is published by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and by the World Health Organization, and it changes as resistance spreads, so verify the current recommendation for your exact area before you commit to a drug for a long deployment.

Each option carries its own adverse effects, and knowing them in advance is what turns a nuisance into a manageable plan. Doxycycline commonly causes stomach upset, sun sensitivity that can be severe in equatorial light, and vaginal candidiasis in women, and it must be taken every day without gaps because it has a short duration of action. Mefloquine can produce vivid dreams, anxiety, insomnia, and in a small proportion of people serious neuropsychiatric effects, which is why a personal or family history of depression, anxiety, or seizures makes it a poor choice. Atovaquone with proguanil is generally well tolerated but is costly and must be taken with food at the same time each day. Tafenoquine is taken less often but requires testing for glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency first, because it can destroy red cells in people who lack the enzyme.

The discipline of continuing is mostly arithmetic, and the arithmetic is easy to get wrong. You must begin before arrival, continue through the whole stay without gaps, and continue for the period of time the chosen drug requires after you leave the malarious area, which is 4 weeks for doxycycline and 1 week for atovaquone with proguanil. Count the tablets you will need, add a margin for loss and for delays, and carry the prescription with the drug so that a customs officer or a pharmacy can see what it is. If you travel to a different region, the correct drug may change, and a visit to a travel medicine clinic between postings is worth the time. Some clinicians also carry a course of standby emergency treatment for malaria, to be used only when fever appears and no reliable testing or care is within reach, and the current World Health Organization guidance describes when that approach is appropriate. Both the prophylaxis and the standby treatment must be verified against the current edition of the guidance before you rely on them, because regimens and patterns of resistance are revised continuously.

Even with perfect prevention, exposures happen, and the needle-stick is the one that will find you. The risk depends on the size of the needle, the depth of the injury, the amount of blood involved, and the prevalence of blood-borne viruses in the population you serve, which in many tropical settings is high. The immediate response is not complicated and it must be automatic: wash the wound with soap and water, let it bleed briefly under running water, do not scrub it or squeeze it hard, and if blood or body fluid has reached an eye or the mouth, irrigate that area with clean water or saline for several minutes. Do not apply bleach, do not cauterize, and do not delay to finish the procedure you were doing. Then report it the same day, because everything that follows depends on the clock. This is the moment when the habit of writing things down protects you, because the time of injury, the source, and the action taken are the facts that determine your treatment.

The most time-critical element of a needle-stick is the post-exposure drugs for human immunodeficiency virus, and the reason is biological. The virus establishes itself in the body over hours to days, and antiretroviral drugs given early can interrupt that process before it becomes permanent infection. Current guidance from the World Health Organization and from national programs recommends starting a combination of antiretroviral drugs as soon as possible, ideally within 2 hours and certainly within 72 hours of the exposure, and continuing for 28 days. A commonly used regimen is tenofovir disoproxil fumarate 300 milligrams once daily, plus lamivudine 300 milligrams once daily, plus dolutegravir 50 milligrams once daily, though the exact combination depends on local resistance patterns and on whether the source person is already on treatment. The drugs have their own adverse effects, including nausea, headache, kidney effects, and liver effects, so monitoring is part of the course. This is a situation where the protocol must be known before the injury happens, because there is no time to research it afterward, and where the current guideline must be checked rather than remembered, because these recommendations are revised.

The needle is not the only exposure, and the same protocol must cover the others. Blood splashed onto broken skin or a mucous membrane, a bite that breaks the skin, a splash of cerebrospinal fluid or amniotic fluid, and unprotected sexual contact in the field all require assessment. Testing the source person, with consent and confidentiality, is what determines whether the full drug course is needed, and the process must be organized in advance so that a frightened patient is not approached clumsily at the moment of crisis. Hepatitis B exposure in a vaccinated clinician with documented protective antibody is handled differently from exposure in someone who was never vaccinated, and unvaccinated staff may need immunoglobulin and a vaccine course, while tetanus cover should be confirmed at the same time. The exposure must be written in an incident register, not because anyone wants to blame you, but because patterns of injury reveal faults in equipment and procedure that can be fixed.

Fever in yourself must be treated with exactly the seriousness you would apply to a patient, and this is harder than it sounds. The clinician who has malaria is a patient, not a colleague with a mild illness, and the temptation to take a couple of tablets and stay on duty is the precise mechanism by which severe malaria develops in health workers. Fever in a tropical setting demands a malaria test, preferably both a rapid diagnostic test and a thick and thin blood film, and a single negative test does not end the assessment if the clinical picture is convincing. It also demands the same differential thinking you would apply to anyone else, including dengue, typhoid, a rickettsial infection, leptospirosis, a urinary tract infection, or an ordinary respiratory illness. Do not prescribe for yourself from memory, and do not use your own professional knowledge to talk yourself out of a diagnosis. Ask a colleague to examine you, tell them the whole history, and accept the plan they make.

There is a specific danger in being both the prescriber and the patient, and it is that you will choose the diagnosis that lets you keep working. The nurse who treats her own fever with an antimalarial and continues the clinic may be treating the right disease with the wrong dose, or the wrong disease entirely, and the second possibility is the one that kills. If you test positive for malaria, take the full course of artemisinin-based combination therapy at the correct dose, record it, and arrange cover for the clinic before you need it. If you develop any danger sign, such as confusion, difficulty breathing, dark urine, inability to keep fluids down, or a fever that does not settle, you need treatment given intravenously and referral, and the referral decision must be made by someone who is not you. A named colleague should know where you keep your medical information and should be authorized to act if you become unable to decide. The clinic plan for your own illness is a document worth writing on a calm day and worth rehearsing.

Water and food are the exposures you can control most easily, which makes the failures less forgivable. Drinking water should be boiled, filtered, or treated with chlorine, and the treatment must be done correctly rather than symbolically, because a single untreated container can undo a month of care. Ice is water you did not treat, raw vegetables may have been washed in water you did not treat, and salads, unpasteurized milk, and undercooked meat carry the same lesson. The nurse who teaches a village about boiling water and then accepts a glass of untreated water to avoid giving offense has traded a small social discomfort for a bout of hepatitis A, typhoid, or a parasitic infection. You can accept hospitality without drinking the water by taking the glass, holding it, and drinking from your own bottle, and the maneuver is worth practicing before you need it. Food that is freshly cooked and still hot is safer than food that has been sitting, and street food is safer when you can see the heat and the clean hands that produced it. Insecticide-treated bed nets, repellent, and long sleeves belong to the same list, because vector-borne infection is also a personal exposure you can reduce.

Sleep is a clinical variable rather than a luxury, and its loss degrades the exact skills a remote nurse depends on. Decision-making, the recognition of a deteriorating patient, arithmetic for drug doses, and the ability to stay calm in a crisis all fall away with accumulated sleep debt, and the person who is most affected is often the last to notice. The clinic schedule is usually built around the patients rather than around the staff, and the on-call rota often means that one person is available for everything at every hour. That structure must be interrupted deliberately, with protected sleep after a night call, a rota that rotates the night burden, and a rule that nobody drives a long road after a night without sleep. Naps of 20 to 30 minutes can restore alertness for a few hours, and they are far better than a third cup of coffee. Caffeine taken late in the day buys an hour and costs the following night, so its timing matters as much as its quantity, and so does the alcohol that is offered at the end of a hard day.

The body's limits also include heat, food, and movement, and remote practice punishes all three. Working in equatorial heat without shade or without enough salt and water leads to heat exhaustion and occasionally to heat stroke, and the warning signs are headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion, and the paradoxical stopping of sweat. Drink before you are thirsty, take the salt your body needs if you are sweating heavily for hours, and schedule heavy physical work for the early morning. Eating irregularly in a place where meals depend on the market and the season produces the same hypoglycemia and poor judgment you would look for in a patient, and it is easy to skip lunch and call it dedication. Movement is also part of maintenance, whether it is a daily walk, the stairs instead of the lift, or a routine that keeps strength and balance. The clinician who is physically depleted makes worse decisions and recovers more slowly from every illness, which is a clinical risk to the patients as much as a personal cost.

A chronic condition does not pause because you have taken a posting, and managing one at a distance is the same problem your patients face, only with more knowledge and less excuse. Diabetes, hypertension, asthma, epilepsy, thyroid disease, inflammatory bowel disease, and a history of mental illness all require a supply of medicine, a way to monitor, and a plan for what happens when the supply is interrupted. Before departure, calculate the quantity you need for the whole assignment, add a margin of at least one quarter, and find out whether the drug is on the national formulary of the country you are entering. If it is not, you must carry your own, with the prescription, the generic name written clearly, and enough to cover a delay in your return. Insulin needs reliable cold storage, which in a place with unreliable power means planning a backup source and monitoring the temperature of whatever container you use. Ask yourself what you would do if the supply ran out, write the answer down, and identify the nearest facility that could help.

Monitoring a chronic condition without a laboratory requires equipment you can trust and reagents you can keep valid. A blood pressure cuff can be checked against a colleague's and recalibrated, a glucose meter needs strips that have not expired and that have been stored away from heat and moisture, and a peak flow meter or a simple thermometer needs no calibration at all. Record your own readings in the same way you record a patient's, with the date and the value, because a pattern over 3 months is far more informative than a single number. If you take a drug with a narrow margin, such as an anticoagulant or an antiepileptic, arrange for the blood tests it requires to be done at a referral laboratory, and plan the journey rather than hoping the test will come to you. Tell at least one colleague at the post what you have and what you take, because a medical emergency is the wrong time to reveal a history that could have been known. The same principle that makes you ask a patient about adherence applies to you, and self-deception about your own adherence is common and dangerous.

A small personal medical kit is the practical expression of everything in this chapter, and it should be assembled before departure and checked at every rotation. It should contain your own prescription medicines with a written list, a course of treatment for malaria suited to the area, a broad spectrum antibiotic for common infections, oral rehydration salts, a thermometer, a blood pressure cuff if you have a chronic condition, wound dressings, and the supplies for a needle-stick or a minor injury. Include a copy of your vaccination record, your prescriptions, your allergies, and the telephone numbers of a clinician at home who knows your history. Include a small supply of the drugs you would not want to be without if the clinic pharmacy were empty, such as an asthma inhaler or an epinephrine auto-injector. Check the expiry dates every 6 months and replace what has gone, because a kit full of expired medicine is a false reassurance. Keep the kit where you can reach it in the dark, and tell one colleague where it is.

The mental load of this work belongs in the same paragraph as the physical, because it produces physical consequences and it is treated as an afterthought. You will see deaths that were preventable, suffering you cannot relieve, and choices that no training prepared you for, and each of those leaves a residue. The residue appears in poor sleep, irritability, a shortening fuse with colleagues and patients, a sense that nothing you do matters, and a growing distance from the people at home who cannot understand what you have seen. None of that is weakness, and naming it early is a clinical act rather than a confession. The chapter on burnout and moral injury takes this subject further, and it is worth listening to next, but the essential point here is that protection belongs to you and to your team, not only to the organization. The colleague who notices and asks the direct question is often the most effective intervention available in the field, and you are that colleague for someone else.

Several practical measures reduce the load, and they are worth building into the routine rather than the crisis. Regular contact with home, at a fixed time and in a form you can keep, sustains the relationships that hold you together when the work is hard. A boundary around the clinic, whether it is a walk at the end of the day or a rule that the radio is switched off during one meal, keeps the work from filling every hour. Supervision, whether by telephone, radio, or a visit from a senior colleague, gives the difficult cases somewhere to go other than your own head, and the habit of writing them down helps in the same way. Watch your use of alcohol, because a drink that helps you sleep on a hard day becomes the thing that is doing the sleeping for you. Recognize your own warning signs and tell someone when they appear, because insight fades exactly when it is most needed.

The check-up during home leave is easy to postpone and expensive to skip, and it should be scheduled before the leave begins. Book the appointments early, because a 4 week leave disappears quickly, and put the ones that require fasting or a second visit at the start rather than at the end. The list includes a general medical review, blood pressure and blood glucose, a dental examination, an eye examination, cervical or prostate screening as appropriate, and the laboratory tests that your age and history require. It also includes the vaccines that have expired or that your posting has made relevant, and a review of every medicine you take, including the ones you buy yourself. Mental health deserves a real appointment, not a five minute conversation at the end of a consultation about something else. Bring the written summary of your field records, because the clinician at home will otherwise have to reconstruct 2 years of your life from memory.

The clinician seeing you at home needs a specific kind of information, and the tropical exposure history is what most of them will not think to take. Tell them where you have served, for how long, whether you lived in a rural or an urban area, what water you drank, whether you walked barefoot, and whether you had any episode of fever, diarrhea, or skin problem that resolved without a diagnosis. That history determines which screening tests are worth doing, which may include a blood count for eosinophilia, a stool examination, a urine examination for schistosomiasis, and a test for tuberculosis infection, because exposure in a clinic or a community can be significant. Some infections are silent for years, and the point of the screen is to find them while they are still treatable rather than when they present as disease. No screening list is universal, and the correct tests depend on your exact exposure and on national guidance, so ask what the current recommendation is for a returning health worker and verify it before the appointment. Take the answer in writing and file it with the rest of your record.

The team's duty to each of its members is the part that no individual can carry alone, and it works in both directions. You owe your colleagues an honest answer when they ask how you are, and you owe them the same question asked without an agenda. A small team in a remote place should know each other's chronic conditions, allergies, and the person to call in an emergency, and that information should be kept where the whole team can reach it without awkwardness. When a colleague is unwell, the clinical response is the same as for a patient: assess, treat, and remove that person from duty if the illness makes their work unsafe. If a colleague withdraws, drinks more, or stops laughing at things that used to be funny, that is a finding to act on rather than a mood to tolerate.

The organization employing you has obligations that a nurse should know before signing, and a refusal to ask about them is a common mistake. Ask about medical evacuation cover for staff, about health insurance that works in the country of posting, about the supply of personal protective equipment and its fit, about rest days and leave, and about what happens to your post if you become ill. Ask whether there is a staff medical kit with the drugs and supplies that the team itself might need, and whether anyone is responsible for checking its expiry dates. Ask who covers the clinic when a staff member is sick, because the answer reveals whether the duty of care is real or rhetorical. Ask about the policy for reporting an exposure, and about confidentiality when a staff member is diagnosed with a blood-borne infection.

To summarize, your health is the equipment the clinic cannot replace, and it deserves the same assessment, documentation, and follow-up you give a patient. Prepare before departure with the vaccines recommended by the World Health Organization and with the malaria prophylaxis chosen for your exact destination, take it without gaps, and continue it for the full period after you leave. Know the exposure protocol before the needle-stick happens, start post-exposure drugs for human immunodeficiency virus within hours, and report every injury the same day. Treat your own fever as seriously as anyone else's, test for malaria, and arrange cover and referral in advance rather than improvising while ill. Control the water and the food, protect your sleep, and manage any chronic condition with a surplus of medicine and a written plan for interruption. Carry the mental load openly, keep the check-up on home leave, and accept that your colleagues are part of your safety system and you are part of theirs. Keep every regimen in this chapter framed as what current guidance says, and verify the current guideline before you act on it, because recommendations change and your situation is specific.
