# Chapter 32 — The Pre-Travel Consultation

The pre-travel consultation is the moment when tropical medicine stops being a subject and becomes a plan for one person, and for a healthy traveler it is often the only contact with the health system before departure. Its purpose is narrow and practical, which is to estimate what this particular journey will expose this particular body to, and then to reduce that exposure with vaccines, medicines, behavior, and equipment. The visit is not a lecture about exotic diseases and not a catalogue of everything that could go wrong. It is a matched intervention, and the match comes from the questions the nurse asks and from a careful reading of the itinerary rather than from the reputation of the country on the ticket. Nurses carry most of this work, and in many clinics the advanced practice nurse assesses, prescribes under protocol, counsels, and documents within one appointment. The quality of the consultation depends less on memorized facts than on a reliable sequence that never omits the unglamorous parts.

Structure the visit in three movements and keep the order steady, because a fixed sequence is what protects the consultation when the appointment runs short. The first movement is the risk assessment, a structured conversation about the trip and the traveler. The second is the plan, which converts that assessment into vaccines, medicines, behavior, and equipment. The third is the record and the follow-up, which allows the plan to survive contact with the journey. Begin with a pre-visit questionnaire sent by message or handed over at check-in, because it buys the one resource that travel medicine never has enough of, which is time. The form asks for destinations in the order of travel, dates, purpose, accommodation, rural travel, fresh water contact, animal contact, sexual activity, and previous travel, together with the medical questions that change everything, which are pregnancy, immune status, chronic disease, current medicines, and allergies. A traveler who completes it in the waiting room has already done half the assessment, and the nurse then spends the appointment confirming, correcting, and explaining.

Begin the risk assessment with geography and time, because those two variables decide most of what follows. Ask for the itinerary city by city and region by region rather than for the country alone, since malaria may be absent from one province and intense in the next. Ask when the traveler arrives and when they leave, and ask about the season at the destination rather than the season at home, because the rains change mosquito abundance, road access, and the availability of care. Ask how long the trip lasts, since a two week business visit and a six month stay with family are entirely different problems. Ask where the traveler will sleep, because a screened hotel room with air conditioning removes much of the night-biting risk that a rural guesthouse or a tent preserves. Ask the purpose of travel, because a conference in a capital city, a pilgrimage, a safari, and a family visit expose the same body in different ways.

Medical history is the second half of the assessment, and it is where the nurse works as a clinician rather than as an adviser. Ask about every prescription medicine, every nonprescription medicine, and every herbal or traditional product, because interactions with antimalarial drugs are common and occasionally serious. Ask directly about heart rhythm problems and psychiatric history, because those two answers remove some antimalarial options immediately. Ask about epilepsy, kidney disease, liver disease, and glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency, because each changes drug selection and because many travelers do not know their status. Ask about pregnancy, the possibility of pregnancy, and breastfeeding, and ask about immune status, including HIV infection, cancer treatment, transplant, biologic therapy, and long term corticosteroid use. Ask about allergies, including to the sulfa drugs present in some antimalarials and antibiotics, and ask about previous vaccinations and previous travel illness.

Destination risk must then be delivered as prose, because the listener has no map and no table to consult. Describe risk as a pattern of exposure rather than a label attached to a country, and name the hazards the itinerary actually creates. A dry season trip to a West African city with hotel accommodation is not the same risk as a rainy season trip to the same country with two weeks in a village. Explain the vector and its habits first when vector-borne disease dominates, because the habit is what the traveler can act on. The Anopheles mosquito, which transmits malaria, bites between dusk and dawn and therefore yields to nets, screens, and repellent used at night. The Aedes mosquito, which transmits dengue, chikungunya, and Zika virus, bites in daylight, breeds in domestic containers of clean water, and is untouched by a bed net. Fresh water contact leads to schistosomiasis, and altitude is a hazard that has nothing to do with latitude.

Draw on published country guidance, and say plainly that the sources do not always agree, because they weigh local data differently. The World Health Organization publishes international travel and health guidance, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention publishes destination pages organized by country, and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control publishes its own assessments, and a nurse who reads all three will sometimes find different recommendations for the same itinerary. That disagreement reflects genuine uncertainty, since transmission is focal, seasonal, and incompletely measured. Never promise that a destination is safe and never imply that a vaccine or a tablet abolishes risk. The correct sentence is that this is what current guidance says for this itinerary, and that these measures reduce risk substantially without removing it.

Malaria chemoprophylaxis is the decision most travelers come for, and the mechanism explains why it is a choice rather than a single answer. The parasite enters the blood through the bite of an infected mosquito, travels to the liver, multiplies there, and returns to the blood to cause illness, and the available drugs interrupt that process at different points. A drug acting on the liver stage can stop the infection before it reaches the blood, while a drug acting on the blood stage suppresses illness for as long as it is taken. That is why the timing of the first and last doses differs between products, and why the final dose matters as much as the first. The choice depends on the destination and its resistance pattern, the length of the trip, the traveler's medical history, cost, and what the traveler can actually take reliably. Treat adherence as a clinical variable rather than a moral one, because the best regimen is the one the traveler will finish.

Describe the options one at a time, with the dose spoken plainly and then verified. Atovaquone with proguanil is a fixed combination tablet containing 250 milligrams of atovaquone and 100 milligrams of proguanil, taken by mouth once daily with food, beginning 1 to 2 days before arrival and continuing for 7 days after leaving the risk area. It is generally well tolerated, but it is expensive and not recommended in severe kidney impairment. Doxycycline is 100 milligrams by mouth once daily, beginning 1 to 2 days before arrival and continuing for 4 weeks after departure. It is inexpensive and effective, but it causes photosensitivity, it can irritate the esophagus when a capsule is swallowed lying down, and it can provoke vaginal candidiasis. It is avoided in pregnancy and in children under 8 years of age.

Mefloquine is 250 milligrams by mouth once weekly, beginning 2 to 3 weeks before travel so that tolerance can be judged, continuing weekly during the trip and for 4 weeks afterward. It is contraindicated in people with a history of seizures, a history of psychiatric illness, or certain disorders of heart rhythm, and the nurse must ask those questions directly. Chloroquine remains useful only where the parasite is still fully susceptible, given as 300 milligrams of base once weekly, which equals 500 milligrams of the chloroquine salt. Tafenoquine is a longer-acting option available in some countries, given as 200 milligrams by mouth once daily for 3 days before travel, then 200 milligrams once weekly during travel, then a single 200 milligram dose after return, and it must not be used in pregnancy or in glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency. Primaquine may be added after leaving an area where Plasmodium vivax is common, at 30 milligrams of base by mouth once daily for 14 days, but only after glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase status is checked, because it can cause severe hemolysis in deficiency.

Resistance patterns narrow the choice, and they change over time, which is why no remembered regimen should be trusted indefinitely. Chloroquine resistance in Plasmodium falciparum is widespread across most of the tropics, so chloroquine is reserved for the few remaining susceptible areas, mostly in parts of Central America and the Caribbean. Mefloquine resistance is concentrated in parts of Southeast Asia, particularly along the borders of Thailand, Myanmar, and Cambodia, so mefloquine is a poor choice there. Atovaquone with proguanil and doxycycline have the broadest activity and are the usual first choices where resistant Plasmodium falciparum circulates. Consider what the traveler can buy and take locally, because a regimen interrupted by an unavailable refill is worse than a slightly less elegant regimen that lasts. Every recommendation here is a summary of what current guidance says, the regimens are revised as resistance and evidence change, guidance differs between countries, and the current national guideline must be checked before a prescription is written.

Adherence deserves its own conversation, and an honest discussion is more useful than a warning. Travelers stop their tablets for predictable reasons, which include side effects, forgetfulness, the belief that the risk has passed, and the belief that a few missed doses do not matter. Some save the drug for the days they are bitten, which misunderstands the mechanism, because the drug must already be in the blood before the parasite arrives. Others stop doxycycline the day they leave the risk area and abandon the 4 week tail that covers the incubation period after return. Ask the traveler to describe how the regimen will fit the daily routine, and solve the specific obstacles, such as a work schedule that makes a weekly dose easy to forget. Suggest pairing the dose with a fixed habit such as breakfast, explain the post-travel continuation plainly, and give a written and spoken plan for a missed dose.

The travel health kit is a portable pharmacy matched to the itinerary, and the nurse builds it rather than merely recommending it. The kit exists because the first hours of an illness often happen far from a pharmacy, where the available medicines are unfamiliar, substandard, or falsified. A basic kit includes oral rehydration salts, an antidiarrheal medicine, an antibiotic for traveler's diarrhea, a fever reducer, an antihistamine, an antiseptic, bandages and blister care, sunscreen, an insect repellent, and enough of every personal prescription for the whole trip plus a few extra days. For remote travel, add a water treatment method, a mosquito net, a thermometer, and sterile needles and syringes where injections may be needed. Any controlled substance needs a signed letter from the prescriber stating the diagnosis, medicine, dose, and travel dates, and it should travel in its original labeled container. Write the medicine list using generic names, pack it in hand luggage, and check the destination's rules about restricted medicines before departure.

Food and water advice follows from a simple mechanism, which is that most traveler's diarrhea is acquired by swallowing organisms that came from human or animal feces. The safest water is water that has been boiled, and bringing it to a rolling boil for 1 minute is sufficient at ordinary altitudes, with 3 minutes advised above 2,000 meters. When boiling is impossible, filtration through a device with a pore size of 0.2 micrometers removes bacteria and protozoa, and treatment with chlorine or iodine tablets adds a second layer, though it works less reliably against the parasite Cryptosporidium. Bottled water with an intact seal is generally safe, and the seal is the point, because refilled bottles are common. Avoid ice unless it was made from safe water, avoid raw vegetables and unpeeled fruit, and avoid unpasteurized dairy where tuberculosis and brucellosis circulate in cattle.

Traveler's diarrhea is the most common illness of the journey, and most of it is self-limited, which is why the plan is rehydration first and antibiotics second. The illness usually begins within the first week, produces loose stools, cramps, and sometimes fever, and settles within 3 to 5 days without treatment in most cases. Oral rehydration salts are the mainstay, started at the first loose stool rather than after dehydration appears. Loperamide reduces cramps and frequency, given as 4 milligrams by mouth at the start and then 2 milligrams after each loose stool, with no more than 16 milligrams in a day, but it should not be used with fever or blood in the stool, because slowing the bowel in invasive infection can worsen it. When treatment is needed, azithromycin is given as 1,000 milligrams by mouth as a single dose or 500 milligrams once daily for 3 days, and it is preferred in Southeast Asia and South Asia, where resistance to the fluoroquinolones is common. Ciprofloxacin remains an option where susceptibility is likely, and rifaximin is used for noninvasive disease. These regimens are revised periodically, guidance differs between countries, and the current guideline must be checked before a drug is prescribed or a self-treatment kit is assembled.

Insect avoidance is the intervention that works when no vaccine and no tablet exists, and it depends on knowing when and where the local vectors bite. The Anopheles mosquito transmits malaria and bites mainly between dusk and dawn, so the goal is to keep it away from sleeping people, which means an insecticide-treated bed net, screens on windows, air conditioning where available, and repellent applied before sleep. The Aedes mosquito transmits dengue, chikungunya, and Zika virus, bites during the day, and breeds in small containers of clean water around houses, so nets offer nothing against it and the defenses are repellent on exposed skin, long sleeves and trousers, and the elimination of standing water. Sandflies transmit leishmaniasis, are tiny, quiet, and active from dusk into the night, and pass through ordinary mesh, so a finer net and repellent on ankles and wrists are needed. Ticks transmit rickettsial disease and tick-borne encephalitis, wait in vegetation, and are avoided with long trousers tucked into socks and a body check at the end of the day.

Repellent must be used correctly or it accomplishes nothing. The repellent diethyltoluamide is effective at concentrations of 20 to 50 per cent, and higher concentrations do not work better, they simply last longer. Apply it to exposed skin and to clothing rather than under clothing, and reapply it after swimming, after heavy sweating, and every few hours while the vector is active. Do not apply it to the hands of young children, to broken skin, or around the eyes and mouth, and wash it off when the exposure ends. Permethrin is applied to clothing and to bed nets rather than to skin, and it kills or disables insects that land on the fabric, which is why treated clothing and treated nets add protection that repellent alone cannot provide. The honest limit is that no repellent prevents every bite, so insect avoidance is a set of layers rather than a single product.

Altitude illness is a hazard of the itinerary rather than of the country, and it belongs in every consultation that includes mountains. As a traveler ascends, the barometric pressure falls, so each breath delivers fewer oxygen molecules even though the proportion of oxygen in the air is unchanged. The body compensates by breathing faster and producing more red blood cells, and symptoms appear when that compensation is outpaced by the rate of ascent. Acute mountain sickness usually begins within 6 to 12 hours of arrival at altitude and produces headache plus at least one of nausea, dizziness, fatigue, or difficulty sleeping. The most effective prevention is a graded ascent, which above 3,000 meters means sleeping no more than 500 meters higher than the previous night and taking a rest day every 1,000 meters of gain. Acetazolamide at 125 milligrams by mouth twice a day, begun the day before the ascent, reduces symptoms for many travelers, and dexamethasone at 4 milligrams by mouth every six hours is reserved for treatment or forced rapid ascent. The definitive treatment for severe altitude illness is descent, and no medicine substitutes for it.

The timeline problem is the structural difficulty of pre-travel care, and every experienced nurse has met it. Some vaccines need weeks to produce protection, with hepatitis A requiring about 2 to 4 weeks after the first dose, the inactivated Japanese encephalitis vaccine requiring 2 doses 28 days apart, rabies pre-exposure requiring 3 doses across 21 to 28 days, and the oral typhoid series requiring 4 capsules on alternate days across a week. At the same time, many travelers book within 2 weeks of departure, and some arrive 3 days before the flight with a guidebook and no immunity. The rule is not to turn them away, because late advice still prevents illness, and a single dose of hepatitis A vaccine given days before departure provides substantial short term protection that can be completed after return. Prioritize what cannot be compressed, which includes yellow fever vaccine when a certificate is required, since the certificate only becomes valid 10 days after vaccination and cannot be issued at the airport. Then decide what can be accelerated, what can be replaced by behavior, and what can wait until after return.

Special travelers require the assessment to be repeated with different weights, and the first group is the traveler visiting friends and relatives. These travelers often return to the family's region of origin, stay in a private home rather than a hotel, remain for weeks or months, spend time in rural areas, and have close contact with children and animals. Their risk of malaria, typhoid fever, hepatitis A, tuberculosis, and dengue is frequently higher than that of the leisure traveler, yet they are the least likely to attend a travel clinic and the most likely to believe that childhood residence left them immune. That belief is partly true for some infections and dangerously false for malaria, because immunity to malaria fades within months of leaving an endemic area. Discuss cost, because cost is why many present late or not at all, and point out that prophylaxis is inexpensive compared with treating severe malaria abroad. Address family pressure, needle fear, and the notion that vaccines are for children, and consider serological testing before vaccinating adults who may have been infected in childhood.

Pregnant travelers need a plan that protects two patients at once, and pregnancy changes both the susceptibility to severe disease and the safety of the tools. Malaria in pregnancy is more likely to be severe, more likely to cause anemia, and more likely to end in miscarriage, premature delivery, or low birth weight, so prevention becomes more important rather than less. At the same time, the live attenuated vaccines, including yellow fever, measles, mumps, rubella, and chickenpox, are generally avoided in pregnancy, and doxycycline is avoided because it affects the teeth and bones of the fetus. Mefloquine may be used in some trimesters under current guidance, while atovaquone with proguanil and tafenoquine are generally avoided, so the choice of prophylaxis is narrow. The safest conversation is often whether the trip can be postponed or moved to a lower risk itinerary. Where travel is unavoidable, confirm that the destination has obstetric care and that the traveler knows the warning signs of malaria and of preterm labor.

Children bring a different arithmetic, because doses are given by weight and some products have minimum ages. Ask for the child's weight rather than the age alone, since weight determines the dose of every medicine and the number of doses in some schedules. Children tolerate dehydration from diarrhea poorly, so oral rehydration salts belong in the kit and the caregiver must be taught to give them by small, frequent sips. Malaria in a young child can deteriorate within hours, so fever in a child in an endemic area is an emergency rather than a wait and see problem, and the family should identify the nearest reliable facility before arrival. An adult should apply repellent to the child's exposed skin, sparing the hands and face, and the child should sleep under an insecticide-treated bed net every night.

Older adults bring chronic disease and polypharmacy into the consultation, and both change the plan. Ask about heart disease, kidney disease, diabetes, and lung disease, and review every medicine for interactions with antimalarials and antibiotics, since the list is often long and the traveler may not know why each drug was prescribed. Discuss venous thrombosis on long flights in plain language, with hydration, movement, and compression stockings where appropriate. Discuss travel insurance and medical evacuation explicitly, because older travelers with chronic disease are the group for whom evacuation cover is not a luxury, and ask to see that the policy covers the intended activities and the pre-existing conditions. Warn against attributing fever, breathlessness, or confusion to age or to the flight, because that assumption delays the diagnosis of malaria, pneumonia, and pulmonary embolism.

Immunocompromised travelers require the most careful consultation and often the shortest list of options. Live attenuated vaccines are generally contraindicated when immunity is impaired, which removes yellow fever, measles, mumps, rubella, chickenpox, oral typhoid, and BCG from the plan, and inactivated alternatives are used where they exist. The response to any vaccine may be weaker, so protection is partial and behavioral measures carry more of the burden. Malaria is more likely to be severe in the immunocompromised and in people living with HIV who are not taking effective treatment, so chemoprophylaxis is usually more important rather than less, and the choice must account for interactions with antiretroviral therapy. Every immunosuppressed traveler should carry a written summary of the diagnosis, the medicines, and the treating clinician's contact details, and should know which hospital at the destination can manage the condition. Coordinate with the treating specialist before departure rather than deciding alone.

Documentation is the part of the consultation that outlives the conversation, and it protects the patient, the next clinician, and the nurse. Record the itinerary and the dates of travel, the risk assessment in the traveler's own terms, the vaccines given with their dates and sites, the medicines prescribed with dose and duration, the advice given, and the advice declined. A note that says only that counseling occurred is worthless later, while a note that names the specific hazards discussed and the specific plan agreed is a clinical document. Complete the international certificate of vaccination where one is required, and make sure the traveler knows when it becomes valid and that it must be carried with the passport rather than packed in a suitcase. Give the traveler a written copy of the medication list in generic names, together with allergies, chronic conditions, and emergency contacts. Where a controlled substance or an injectable medicine is involved, hand over the signed letter at the visit, because it cannot be produced from another continent.

The nurse's role when advice must be compressed into a short appointment is a matter of triage rather than of speed. Identify the few items that carry the largest reduction in risk for this particular itinerary, deliver those completely, and summarize the rest in writing for later reading. The highest yield items are usually the antimalarial plan and its adherence, the food, water, and diarrhea plan, the insect avoidance plan, and the vaccines that cannot be given late. Use the pre-visit questionnaire to move the assessment out of the appointment, use standing orders and protocols to move prescribing out of the queue, and use a printed or digital itinerary summary so that the traveler leaves with something to reread. Teach back is the technique that makes a compressed visit honest, because asking the traveler to explain the plan in their own words uncovers the misunderstanding that a nod conceals. A short appointment that ends with a correct and repeatable plan is a success, and a long appointment that ends with a satisfied but confused traveler is not.

Know the limits of the compressed visit and refer when the case exceeds it. Refer when the traveler is pregnant and the prophylaxis choice is genuinely uncertain, when immune status is complex or changing, when a psychiatric or cardiac contraindication rules out the available antimalarials, and when the itinerary includes extreme altitude, remote wilderness, or an activity such as diving that has its own medical standards. Monitoring and adverse effects are part of the plan, because a medicine stopped on the second day protects no one. Tell the traveler which effects are expected and harmless, such as mild nausea with atovaquone with proguanil or vivid dreams with mefloquine, and which should stop the drug and prompt contact, such as palpitations, confusion, severe anxiety, or a spreading rash. Explain that doxycycline can cause a burn-like reaction on exposed skin and that mefloquine's neuropsychiatric effects can appear at any point in the course, and give a telephone or message route for questions, because a traveler who can reach the clinic is more likely to report a problem than to abandon the tablets.

The post-travel period is the second half of prevention, and it is where the tropical medicine nurse most often saves a life. Fever in a returning traveler is a medical emergency until malaria has been excluded, and the interval between return and illness can be as short as a few days or as long as several months, depending on the infection. Tell the traveler to seek care for fever rather than to self-treat with leftover antibiotics, and to name the destinations and the dates without being asked, because that sentence changes the tests that are ordered. Fever with diarrhea, jaundice, rash, or bleeding requires urgent assessment, and fever in a pregnant traveler requires immediate attention. Where a returning traveler has a nationally notifiable illness, the nurse should know the reporting route and the timelines, because notification is a clinical duty. Keep the pre-travel record accessible, because it turns a vague history into a precise one when the next visit happens at a different clinic.

Prevention delivered in a consultation is the most cost-effective work in tropical medicine, because it trades a conversation for a hospital bed. The traveler who never develops malaria frees a bed, a blood film, and a clinician's attention for the patient who does, and where those resources are scarce the trade is not theoretical. The consultation also protects the destination, because a traveler who carries an infection home can seed an outbreak in a place that has the vector but not the immunity. Equity matters here, since the traveler with the least money and the least access to advice often carries the greatest risk. A pre-travel consultation is therefore never only about the individual in the chair. Every regimen, dose, and interval described above is a summary of current guidance rather than a settled fact, guidance is revised and differs between countries, and the current national guideline must be checked before anything is prescribed.

To summarize, the pre-travel consultation is a structured exercise in matching a specific journey to a specific body, and it succeeds when the nurse moves through a fixed sequence without skipping the tedious parts. Assess the itinerary, the season, the accommodation, the purpose of travel, the medical history, the medicines, the pregnancy status, and the immune status before offering anything. Describe destination risk as a pattern of exposure, name the vector and its habits, admit what the guidance does not know, and choose malaria chemoprophylaxis according to resistance, tolerance, cost, and the traveler's ability to finish the course. Build the kit, teach the food and water rules, teach insect avoidance and altitude prevention, and deal honestly with the timeline by prioritizing what cannot be compressed. Give special attention to travelers visiting friends and relatives, to pregnant travelers, to children, to older adults, and to the immunocompromised, whose risks and options differ from the average. Document the plan and the advice declined, monitor tolerance and adverse effects, and arrange the post-travel follow-up that catches the illness prevention could not stop. Guidance is revised, countries differ, and the current national guideline must be checked before prescribing, but the sequence itself remains the most reliable protection a traveler can carry.
