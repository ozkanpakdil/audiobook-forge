# Chapter 14 — Rickettsioses and Other Vector-Borne Bacteria

The bacteria carried inside ticks, mites, fleas and lice cause diseases that are common, dangerous and hard to confirm at the bedside. This chapter covers the rickettsioses, caused by the genera Rickettsia and Orientia, and the other vector-borne bacteria that behave similarly, namely Coxiella burnetii, the Bartonella species, the Borrelia species, and the organisms causing anaplasmosis and ehrlichiosis. The guidance followed here is that of the World Health Organization, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control, and every dose is current guidance rather than settled truth. Regimens are revised, guidance differs between countries and editions, and the current guideline must be checked before prescribing, because a few days of delay in starting the right antibiotic can turn a manageable fever into a fatal illness.

A rickettsia is a bacterium so dependent on the interior of a living cell that it cannot be grown on ordinary laboratory media. These organisms are transmitted by the bite of an infected arthropod, or by scratching infected feces into broken skin, and they invade the endothelial cells that line the smallest blood vessels. Once inside they multiply, rupture the cell and spread to neighboring endothelium, provoking an inflammation of the vessel wall best understood as a vasculitis rather than a toxemia. The skin expresses that vasculitis as a rash, the lung as leak and hemorrhage, the brain as confusion, and the circulation as falling pressure, which is why a patient with no rash can still be desperately ill.

The shared picture begins after an incubation of usually 5 to 14 days with fever, severe headache, muscle aching and deep malaise, and a rash follows in many but not all of these diseases. Fever with headache and rash is the classic triad, but it is complete in only a minority of patients at the first visit, so waiting for it is a mistake. The eschar at the bite site is the most useful physical finding in this chapter and the one most often missed, because it is painless and hides in folds of skin. Examine the scalp, behind the ears, the armpits, the groin, the perineum, the belt line and the breasts in good light, and document any crusted black lesion with a red rim.

Scrub typhus is caused by Orientia tsutsugamushi and transmitted by the bite of a chigger, the larval stage of a trombiculid mite of the genus Leptotrombidium, and not a tick. The larva feeds once, taking tissue fluid rather than blood, and it acquires the organism from infected rodents before passing it on. These mites live in the soil of scrubland, rice paddies, rubber plantations, riverbanks and secondary forest, so their distribution follows moisture and vegetation rather than political boundaries. The disease is not transmitted from person to person, but agricultural workers, soldiers and ecotourists who walk the same ground are all at risk.

The geography of scrub typhus was once drawn as a triangle covering Japan, Korea, China, Taiwan, Southeast Asia, India and northern Australia, and that region remains the core of the endemic area. In the last two decades it has been recognized far outside the triangle, in the Middle East, Africa, South America and islands of the Pacific and Indian Oceans. In much of Asia it is a leading cause of undifferentiated fever and a common reason for admission with fever of unknown origin. Outbreaks have followed floods, displaced populations, military operations and the clearing of land, so scrub typhus belongs in the differential for any febrile patient with a rural exposure almost anywhere in the tropics.

After an incubation of 6 to 21 days, scrub typhus begins with fever, headache, muscle pain and cough, and an eschar is present in roughly half of patients. Lymph nodes enlarge, the conjunctivae redden, and a flat spotted rash may appear on the trunk and spread outward. If treatment is delayed, the organism damages the pulmonary capillaries, the patient develops inflammation of the lung tissue and then acute respiratory distress syndrome, and respiratory failure becomes the most common cause of death. Inflammation of the brain, inflammation of the heart muscle, acute kidney injury, shock and bleeding from the gut also occur, and mortality without treatment ranges from about 6 per cent to 30 per cent or more when diagnosis is delayed.

Scrub typhus responds quickly to doxycycline, and current guidance describes 100 milligrams by mouth twice a day for 7 days for adults, with a single 200 milligram dose used in mild illness in some settings. Azithromycin at 500 milligrams once daily for 3 days is an accepted alternative and the preferred choice in pregnancy, while intravenous doxycycline at 100 milligrams every 12 hours is used when the patient cannot swallow. The fever typically falls within 24 to 48 hours of the first dose, and that rapid defervescence is itself a diagnostic clue. Read those numbers as an illustration rather than a prescription, because guidance is revised and the current World Health Organization and national guideline must be checked before treatment is ordered.

The question of doxycycline in children and in pregnancy comes up with every one of these diseases, and the concern is staining of developing teeth and effects on growing bone. That concern arose from repeated long courses given for acne, and short courses of the kind used for rickettsial disease have not been shown to cause clinically important staining. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the American Academy of Pediatrics both recommend doxycycline for suspected rickettsial illness in children of any age, because the risk of the disease outweighs the risk of the drug. In pregnancy the balance is harder, since the drug crosses the placenta, but a rickettsial infection can kill the mother, so azithromycin is often preferred for scrub typhus and specialist advice should be sought.

Rocky Mountain spotted fever is caused by Rickettsia rickettsii and transmitted by hard ticks, chiefly the American dog tick and the Rocky Mountain wood tick, with the brown dog tick responsible for outbreaks in the southwestern United States and northern Mexico. Despite its name, most cases in the United States occur in the south-Atlantic and south-central states, and the disease also occurs across Central and South America. The tick must remain attached for several hours to transmit the organism, and children, older adults and people with glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency are at greater risk of severe disease. The illness begins abruptly after an incubation of 2 to 14 days with fever, severe headache, muscle pain and vomiting, and the patient often looks toxic from the first day.

The rash usually appears 2 to 5 days after the fever, starting as small pink spots on the wrists and ankles and spreading inward over the arms, legs and trunk. It often involves the palms and soles, and it later becomes spotted with bleeding as the vasculitis deepens. It may be faint, hard to see on deeply pigmented skin, or entirely absent in a minority of patients, so its absence never excludes the diagnosis. Severe disease brings gangrene of the limbs, inflammation of the brain, respiratory failure, kidney injury and shock, and without treatment reported case fatality reaches 20 to 30 per cent, most deaths following a delay in starting doxycycline.

Doxycycline is the treatment of choice at every age, and current guidance gives 100 milligrams by mouth twice a day for adults and 2.2 milligrams per kilogram of body weight twice a day for children, with a maximum of 100 milligrams per dose. Treatment continues for at least 3 days after the fever settles, usually 5 to 7 days in total and longer when the disease is severe. Here dose verification is not a formality, because the pediatric dose is calculated by weight, the maximum for each administration must be respected, and recommended durations differ between countries. Confirm the weight, confirm the concentration on the label, and confirm the current guideline before the first dose, because a delay while the team debates the diagnosis is more dangerous than the antibiotic itself.

Mediterranean spotted fever, also called boutonneuse fever, is caused by Rickettsia conorii and carried by the brown dog tick, Rhipicephalus sanguineus, which thrives in kennels and around human dwellings. It occurs around the Mediterranean basin, along the Black Sea, and in parts of Africa, the Middle East and India. A black eschar, sometimes called the tache noire, marks the bite site in most patients, and fever, headache and muscle pain follow, with a rash that often includes the palms and soles. Most cases are mild, but severe forms with involvement of several organs occur, particularly in people with diabetes, alcohol use disorder, glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency or advanced age, and doxycycline is the mainstay of treatment.

African tick bite fever is caused by Rickettsia africae and transmitted by Amblyomma ticks, which are aggressive, active by day and abundant in the game reserves and savanna of sub-Saharan Africa and the Caribbean. It is now one of the most common causes of fever in travelers returning from safari, and clusters occur in groups who travel together. Several eschars are common, often with enlarged regional lymph nodes, while the fever, headache and muscle pain are usually mild, and a rash is absent or sparse in many patients. Other organisms in the spotted fever group cause Indian tick typhus, Queensland tick typhus, Siberian tick typhus, Japanese spotted fever, and the infections due to Rickettsia parkeri and Rickettsia felis, and doxycycline treats all of them.

Murine typhus is caused by Rickettsia typhi and maintained in rats and their fleas, with the cat flea also playing a part, and it spreads when infected flea feces are scratched into a bite wound or inhaled as dust. It is a disease of ports, warehouses, grain stores, sewers and crowded urban neighborhoods, found worldwide in warm and coastal regions. Unlike scrub typhus and the spotted fevers it does not produce an eschar, and a rash appears in only about half of patients, usually on the trunk. Fever, headache and muscle pain dominate, severe cases with pneumonia, kidney injury and neurological involvement do occur, and doxycycline at 100 milligrams twice a day for 7 to 10 days is the standard treatment.

Epidemic typhus is caused by Rickettsia prowazekii and transmitted by the body louse, which lives in clothing and feeds on people who cannot wash or change their clothes. It appears where cold, crowding, poverty and displacement meet, in refugee camps, prisons and war zones, and it has shaped the outcome of armies and famines for centuries. Fever, headache, a rash beginning on the trunk and spreading outward, confusion and shock characterize the severe form, and mortality without treatment can reach 20 per cent or more. The organism can persist in lymph nodes for years and cause a returning illness known as Brill-Zinsser disease, which can seed a new outbreak wherever lice are present; doxycycline at 100 milligrams twice a day for 7 to 10 days is effective, and delousing interrupts transmission.

Q fever is caused by Coxiella burnetii, which is not a rickettsia in the strict sense, and it is not transmitted by a bite at all. The organism forms a resistant spore-like structure that survives drying and is shed in the birth products, urine, feces and milk of infected sheep, goats and cattle. Infection follows inhalation of contaminated dust or aerosol, so the people at risk are farmers, shepherds, abattoir workers, veterinarians, laboratory staff, and anyone living downwind of a lambing or kidding shed. The incubation period is usually 1 to 3 weeks, and because a single infected placenta can contaminate a large area, the nurse should ask directly about animal contact and occupational exposure.

Acute Q fever is often silent or mild and settles on its own, but when it produces illness it usually takes the form of fever with severe headache, an atypical pneumonia, or inflammation of the liver. The chest radiograph may show patchy shadowing, and the patient may have a cough and breathlessness out of proportion to the physical findings. The dangerous consequence is chronic Q fever, which appears months to years later and most often infects the heart valves, particularly in people with pre-existing valve disease, vascular grafts, immunosuppression or pregnancy. It is difficult to diagnose because blood cultures are usually negative, so it requires PCR and serology together with careful clinical assessment, and a post-infectious fatigue syndrome can also leave the patient disabled.

Acute Q fever is treated with doxycycline at 100 milligrams by mouth twice a day for 14 days, one of the few regimens in this chapter that runs beyond a single week. Chronic Q fever, especially infection of the heart valves, requires prolonged combination treatment with doxycycline and hydroxychloroquine, often for 18 months to 3 years, under the care of an infectious disease specialist. Q fever in pregnancy carries a risk of miscarriage, stillbirth and preterm delivery and requires specialist care, while avoiding birth products, controlling dust and pasteurizing milk are the practical preventive measures. Confirm every regimen against the current national guideline, because the durations are long, the alternatives are few and the consequences of an error are serious.

Bartonellosis, also called Carrion disease, is caused by Bartonella bacilliformis and transmitted by sandflies of the genus Lutzomyia in the high Andean valleys of Peru, Ecuador and Colombia. It occurs at altitudes of roughly 500 to 3000 meters and has two distinct stages, which can appear in the same patient weeks apart. The first is Oroya fever, the acute and dangerous phase, and the second is verruga peruana, the eruptive phase with its crop of red nodules. The sandfly is small, bites at night and breeds in the sandy soil of these valleys, so the exposure history is unusually specific and unusually helpful.

Oroya fever begins after an incubation of about 2 to 3 weeks with fever, severe pallor, yellowing of the eyes, enlargement of the liver and spleen, and swollen lymph nodes. The organism invades the red blood cells and destroys them, producing a rapidly progressive anemia that the bone marrow cannot replace quickly enough. The greatest danger is secondary infection, particularly with Salmonella species and other organisms from the gut, which is a leading cause of death in the acute phase. Untreated mortality in the acute stage has historically been very high, and even with treatment the patient may need a blood transfusion, so watch for worsening anemia, falling blood pressure and any new infection.

Treatment of the acute stage uses doxycycline, ciprofloxacin or chloramphenicol, sometimes in combination, while the eruptive stage responds to azithromycin or rifampin. Chloramphenicol has been favored because it also covers the Salmonella co-infection, but its rare and serious effect on the bone marrow makes the choice a careful one. Fluoroquinolones are avoided in children and in pregnancy in most circumstances, and every dose and duration must be checked against the current World Health Organization and national guidance. Cat-scratch disease, caused by Bartonella henselae and transmitted by a kitten scratch or bite, is far more common and usually far milder, producing enlarged regional lymph nodes and fever that settle without treatment, though azithromycin shortens the course.

The Borrelia species cause two groups of disease, the Lyme group and the relapsing fevers, and both belong in the differential diagnosis for a febrile patient with the right exposure. Borrelia burgdorferi and its relatives are transmitted by Ixodes ticks, which are temperate-zone vectors maintained by rodents and deer rather than tropical ones, but travelers and migrants move the disease across borders. The early illness is marked by erythema migrans, a slowly expanding red ring at the bite site, with fever, headache and muscle aches. Untreated, it can spread to cause weakness of the facial nerve, inflammation of the coverings of the brain, inflammation of the heart and later arthritis, and a late skin change called acrodermatitis chronica atrophicans also occurs.

Early Lyme disease is treated with doxycycline at 100 milligrams by mouth twice a day for 10 to 14 days, or with amoxicillin at 500 milligrams three times a day, or with cefuroxime axetil at 500 milligrams twice a day. Pediatric doses are calculated by weight, and when the nervous system or the heart is significantly involved, intravenous therapy such as ceftriaxone is used for 14 to 28 days. In pregnancy amoxicillin is generally preferred, and some patients report persistent symptoms after adequate treatment, which is a real problem but does not respond to prolonged antibiotic courses. Verify the regimen and its duration against the current national guideline, and remember that prevention rests on repellent, permethrin-treated clothing and a full-body tick check.

The relapsing fevers are caused by several Borrelia species and transmitted either by body lice, in the case of Borrelia recurrentis, or by soft ticks of the genus Ornithodoros that live in rodent burrows and animal shelters. The hallmark is a fever that lasts several days, breaks, then returns after an interval, often for several cycles, as the organism changes its surface proteins and escapes the immune response. Louse-borne relapsing fever spreads when an infected louse is crushed on the skin, while the tick-borne form follows the bite of a soft tick that feeds quickly at night and is rarely noticed. During the febrile episodes the organisms are numerous in the blood and can sometimes be seen on an ordinary stained blood film, while complications include inflammation of the liver, inflammation of the brain, bleeding and loss of pregnancy.

Louse-borne relapsing fever responds to a single dose of doxycycline at 200 milligrams or a single dose of benzylpenicillin, while the tick-borne form is treated with doxycycline at 100 milligrams twice a day for 7 days. The first dose can trigger a Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction, in which fever, shivering, a rise in heart rate, falling blood pressure and worsening of symptoms appear within a few hours as the organisms are killed and their contents released. This reaction is common and can be fatal if it is not anticipated, so the first dose should be given where the patient can be observed, with fluids and support for the circulation close at hand. Do not give the first dose at night and send the patient home, and tell the family that the reaction is expected; corticosteroids are sometimes used on specialist advice.

Anaplasmosis is caused by Anaplasma phagocytophilum, which infects a type of white blood cell called the neutrophil and is transmitted by Ixodes ticks, while ehrlichiosis is caused by Ehrlichia chaffeensis, which infects the monocyte and is transmitted chiefly by the lone star tick. Both produce a nonspecific febrile illness with headache, muscle pain and malaise, and both bring low white cell counts, low platelet counts and raised liver enzymes. A rash is uncommon in anaplasmosis and appears in only a minority of patients with ehrlichiosis, so these diseases usually present without any skin finding, which is the trap. Severe disease can bring respiratory failure, shock and neurological damage, and doxycycline at 100 milligrams twice a day, or the equivalent weight-based dose in children, is given for 7 to 14 days.

The diagnostic difficulty in this group is not that the tests are inaccurate but that they arrive too late to guide treatment. Serology by indirect immunofluorescence requires an acute sample taken in the first days of illness and a convalescent one taken 2 to 4 weeks later, and the diagnosis is supported by a fourfold rise between them. A single early sample is usually negative, so a negative result says nothing useful at the moment of decision, and the old Weil-Felix test has been abandoned because it is neither sensitive nor specific. PCR on blood, and on a swab or biopsy of an eschar, can give an answer early, but its sensitivity falls once antibiotics have been given, so take the sample with the first dose.

For all of these reasons the guiding principle is to treat on suspicion and not to wait for confirmation. The World Health Organization and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention both state that when the clinical picture and the exposure history suggest a rickettsial illness, effective antibiotic treatment should begin immediately, without awaiting laboratory results. In Rocky Mountain spotted fever the risk of death rises markedly when doxycycline is started after the fifth day of illness, and the same pattern is described in severe scrub typhus. Antibiotics given early can blunt the antibody response and complicate a later serological test, but that is a reason to record the treatment and interpret the result with care, not to withhold the drug from a deteriorating patient.

The differential diagnosis of these illnesses is the differential diagnosis of fever itself, and a few alternatives must be excluded alongside any rickettsial diagnosis. Malaria is the first, because it is common in the same places, it can kill within a day, and it is confirmed or excluded by a test available almost everywhere. Dengue and the other viral diseases carried by mosquitoes and ticks, leptospirosis, typhoid fever, meningococcal disease, viral hepatitis, measles and ordinary bacterial sepsis all overlap with the picture described here. Co-infection is not rare, so send the malaria test, the blood cultures and the rickettsial sample together and start empirical treatment for the most dangerous possibility.

The logistics of this chapter matter as much as the medicine, because the tests are slow to arrive while the drugs are simple. Doxycycline is inexpensive, stable and widely available as tablets and capsules, and it should be part of the essential stock of any clinic that sees febrile patients in an endemic area. The intravenous form may be unavailable, serology requires a reference laboratory and a cold chain, results may take weeks, and PCR requires the correct tube, prompt transport and a laboratory that offers the test. The nurse who manages the stock register, checks expiry dates, rotates supplies and arranges transport is doing work that directly determines whether a patient is treated on time, so never let a pending result or an empty shelf delay the first dose.

Monitoring in these illnesses is mostly the careful repetition of simple observations, and it is where the advanced practice nurse earns the title. Record temperature, heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate and oxygen saturation at fixed intervals, increase the frequency when the patient is unstable, and assess mental state at every set of observations. Measure urine output and fluid balance, watch for the swelling and crackles that signal fluid overload or injury to the lung, and follow the hemoglobin, the platelet count, the liver enzymes and the kidney function. Compare each result with the last rather than with the normal range alone, because deterioration in scrub typhus and in Rocky Mountain spotted fever often occurs in the second week, and a patient who improves and then worsens must be reassessed from the beginning.

Doxycycline is well tolerated, but it has practical effects the nurse must anticipate and teach. Nausea, vomiting and abdominal discomfort are the most common, and they can be reduced by taking the medicine with food, although milk may reduce absorption in a patient whose treatment depends on adequate blood levels. Irritation and ulceration of the esophagus occur when the capsule is swallowed with too little water or when the patient lies down immediately afterward, so each dose should be taken with a full glass of water and the patient should remain upright for at least 30 minutes. Sensitivity to sunlight is a real risk in the tropics, yeast infections can follow a course of treatment, and antacids, calcium, iron and magnesium should be separated from the dose by about 2 hours.

Supportive care follows the same principles as in any severe febrile illness, and it sometimes saves the patient while the antibiotic is working. Give medicine to reduce fever for comfort, maintain hydration by mouth or intravenously, provide oxygen and breathing support for respiratory failure, and support the circulation in shock. Corticosteroids are used in some severe scrub typhus and in the Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction only on specialist advice, because the evidence is limited and the risk of harm is real. Population prevention means controlling the vector that matters for each disease, with rodent and flea control for murine typhus, delousing for epidemic typhus, and repellents, permethrin-treated clothing and a tick check for the rest. An insecticide-treated bed net protects against mosquitoes at night and against lice not at all, so it should never be presented as protection against ticks, mites and chiggers.

The nursing role in this chapter can be stated in a few concrete habits. Take the exposure history with the same care you take in malaria, asking about farming, camping, safari, military service, displacement, animal contact and the kind of housing the patient lives in. Perform a complete skin survey for the eschar in good light, document it precisely, and prompt the team to start empirical doxycycline when the picture fits rather than accept a plan that waits for serology. Verify every dose against the current guideline, monitor for the complications and adverse effects described here, and educate the patient and the family about fever control, hydration and when to return. Recognize that pregnancy, childhood, older age, immunosuppression and absence of the spleen all raise the risk of severe disease, and let each lower your threshold for admission.

Consider a composite patient such as a 34 year old farmer from a rural district in northern Thailand who presents in the rainy season with 8 days of fever, severe headache, a dry cough and breathlessness. He has no rash, and the examination finds a black crusted lesion in the left groin that he had not noticed and which he attributes to a scratch. His chest radiograph shows patchy shadowing on both sides, his platelet count is low and his liver enzymes are raised, while malaria testing is negative. The correct response is not to wait for rickettsial serology, which will not return in time to help, but to start doxycycline on the basis of the exposure, the eschar and the lung involvement, and to support his breathing while the antibiotic takes effect.

To summarize, the rickettsioses and the other vector-borne bacteria gather many diseases under a single clinical habit, which is to think of them before the laboratory does. Scrub typhus, the spotted fever group, murine and epidemic typhus, Q fever, bartonellosis, the Borrelia infections, anaplasmosis and ehrlichiosis all present as fever with headache and often a rash or an eschar, and all can become severe when treatment is delayed. The tests that confirm them arrive after the decision point, so the decision rests on the exposure history, the physical examination and the deliberate search for the eschar. Doxycycline treats most of them, it is safe in short courses in children, and it is used in pregnancy when the illness is serious enough to threaten the mother. The nurse's contribution is to ask about the bite, to undress the patient and look, to insist on empirical treatment rather than watchful waiting, and to verify each dose against the current guideline.
