# Chapter 44 — Pediatrics and Severe Acute Malnutrition in Remote Practice

Every chapter in this book meets a clinician somewhere, but this one meets you in the hardest place, a health post where you are the only person able to assess a sick child. More than five million children die each year before their fifth birthday, most from pneumonia, diarrhea, malaria, measles, and the malnutrition underneath them all. Severe acute malnutrition contributes to roughly one in every ten deaths in children under five and multiplies the danger of every infection beside it. This chapter follows the systematic assessment of the sick child, the treatment of dehydration, the staged care of severe acute malnutrition, and the outpatient program for the child who can still eat. Every dose and volume here reflects current World Health Organization guidance, but guidance is revised and differs between countries, so verify the current national guideline before you prescribe or act.

The integrated management of childhood illness is a systematic way of examining a sick child, built for a setting where a laboratory may not exist and one visit may be the only chance. It asks you to follow the same order every time rather than chase the complaint the mother offers first, because the complaint that brings a child in is often not the condition that will kill that child tonight. For each child you check the danger signs, then cough or difficult breathing, diarrhea, fever, and ear problem, then nutrition, immunization, and any other concern. You classify rather than merely diagnose, placing the child in a category that carries an action, such as severe pneumonia, some dehydration, or severe acute malnutrition with complications, and then you treat, counsel, and write the follow-up date where the next nurse can find it.

The general danger signs mean immediate action, before any other question is answered. A child who cannot drink or breastfeed, who vomits everything, who has had convulsions, or who is lethargic or unconscious is in danger now. For the young infant the list is equally urgent: not feeding well, convulsions, fast breathing of 60 breaths or more per minute, severe chest indrawing, fever of 37.5 degrees Celsius or more, a temperature below 35.5 degrees Celsius, and movement only when stimulated. Any one of these signs means urgent treatment and usually referral, so in a remote post you begin treatment while you arrange the journey. Stridor in a calm child, and a child too weak to cry, are equally alarming, and in pediatrics the sickest child is often the stillest.

The examination is quick, ordered, and almost entirely done with your hands and eyes. Count the respiratory rate for a full minute while the child is calm, because crying doubles it, and know your thresholds: 60 or more per minute under 2 months, 50 or more from 2 months to 12 months, and 40 or more from 12 months to 5 years. Look for lower chest wall indrawing, listen for stridor, and note whether the child can feed. Weigh the child, measure length or height, measure the mid-upper arm circumference on the left arm midway between the shoulder and the elbow, and check the immunization card. Press both feet for a few seconds and look for pitting edema, because bilateral pitting edema is itself a form of severe acute malnutrition, and write every number down as you go.

Nutritional status is judged in three ways, and each catches children the others miss. The weight-for-height measure compares the child with a reference child of the same height, and a value below minus 3 standard deviations from the median defines severe wasting. The mid-upper arm circumference is the fastest field measure, and a value below 115 millimeters in a child from 6 to 59 months defines severe acute malnutrition, while 115 to 125 millimeters defines moderate acute malnutrition. Bilateral pitting edema of both feet defines severe acute malnutrition regardless of weight, and it is the sign most often missed. A child can be severely malnourished with a normal-looking weight if the child is stunted, so trust the numbers over your impression and record each measurement on the growth card.

Dehydration kills through shock, and the signs that predict it are physical and behavioural rather than historical. Ask the mother about diarrhea and vomiting, but base your classification on what you see: the child's general state, whether the eyes are sunken, whether the child drinks eagerly or poorly, and how fast a skin pinch returns. A child who is restless and irritable, with sunken eyes, who drinks eagerly, and whose skin pinch returns slowly has some dehydration. A child who is lethargic or unconscious, with sunken eyes, who cannot drink or drinks poorly, and whose skin pinch returns very slowly has severe dehydration. The number of stools matters less than these signs, so let the mother's report raise suspicion without settling it, and weigh the child at admission.

Rehydration follows the degree you have just classified, and the plan is chosen for the child rather than the disease. A child with no dehydration goes home on the first plan: extra fluids after every loose stool, continued feeding, and clear instructions on when to return. A child with some dehydration stays for the second plan, which gives oral rehydration salts at 75 milliliters per kilogram over 4 hours, offered by cup or spoon while breastfeeding continues. Reassess after 4 hours: if the signs have gone, switch to the first plan, and if some dehydration persists, repeat the second, while a child who vomits waits about ten minutes and then continues slowly. Give zinc, 20 milligrams by mouth once a day for 10 to 14 days, or 10 milligrams for an infant under 6 months, to shorten the diarrhea.

Severe dehydration is treated as shock and demands the third plan, and the pace is measured in minutes. For a child under 12 months give 30 milliliters per kilogram of Ringer's lactate solution intravenously in the first hour, then 70 milliliters per kilogram over the next 5 hours. For a child from 12 months to 5 years give the same 30 milliliters per kilogram in the first 30 minutes, then 70 milliliters per kilogram over the next 2 and a half hours. If you cannot place an intravenous line, or if the child is not in shock, a nasogastric tube is a legitimate route and far safer than delay. Start oral rehydration salts as soon as the child can drink, usually after 3 or 4 hours, reassess the pulse, breathing, and skin pinch at least every hour, and never leave a child on intravenous fluids without a nurse watching.

A severely malnourished child with diarrhea is a different patient, and the ordinary rehydration plan can kill that child. The malnourished body holds sodium and leaks potassium, its heart is fragile, and a rapid infusion of fluid can flood the lungs and stop the heart. For this reason the World Health Organization uses a special rehydration solution for malnutrition, known as ReSoMal, which contains less sodium and more potassium and magnesium. Give it slowly, 5 milliliters per kilogram every 30 minutes for the first 2 hours, then 5 to 10 milliliters per kilogram per hour for up to 10 hours, alternating with the starter formula. The goal is to replace the deficit gradually, and you stop when the child is rehydrated, when weight begins to rise, or when the edema begins to fall. Intravenous fluid is reserved for the child in shock, given slowly and in small volumes.

Severe acute malnutrition is not simply hunger, and treating it as hunger is one of the ways a child dies in your care. The malnourished child has adapted to scarcity: the metabolism slows, the sodium and potassium pumps in every cell begin to fail, fluid shifts out of the vessels, and the body burns its own tissue to stay alive. The heart muscle is thin and weak, the liver is fatty, the kidneys conserve water poorly, the immune system is badly impaired, and the gut lets bacteria cross into the blood. A severely malnourished child can die from an infection a well-nourished child would shrug off, and from food given too quickly, which is why the first feed is deliberately weak.

The child in front of you may show none of the noisy signs you were taught to fear. A severely malnourished child often has no fever with a serious infection, because the body cannot mount the response, and the child may be hypothermic instead. The child may be quiet, still, and seemingly content, with cold hands and feet and a weak cry. Bilateral pitting edema can hide the wasting, so a child who looks plump may be severely malnourished, and puffiness of the eyes and feet is not good nutrition. Assume that every severely malnourished child has an infection even when nothing points to one, and look for the hidden sites in the mouth, ears, skin folds, lungs, and perineum.

Hypoglycemia is the first killer of the first hours, and it is silent. A severely malnourished child has small glycogen stores and a high demand, so blood glucose can fall below 3 millimoles per liter without convulsions or sweating. Check the blood glucose on admission in every child, and treat immediately if it is low. A conscious child takes 50 milliliters of dextrose at 10 per cent by mouth or by nasogastric tube, and an unconscious child takes 5 milliliters per kilogram of dextrose at 10 per cent intravenously, followed by the same oral dose. Then start the starter formula at once and feed every 2 to 3 hours, day and night, because the treatment of hypoglycemia is a rhythm of feeding rather than one dose of sugar. Hypothermia travels with it, and an axillary temperature below 35.5 degrees Celsius means the child must be warmed actively and gently, skin to skin against the mother, with a hat, dry blankets, and a warm room.

The World Health Organization organizes the care of severe acute malnutrition into ten steps, and the order of the steps is the treatment. During stabilization you treat and prevent hypoglycemia and hypothermia, treat and prevent dehydration, correct electrolyte imbalance, treat and prevent infection, and correct micronutrient deficiencies, and only then do you begin cautious feeding. The course then runs through three phases: stabilization in the first week, transition in the second week, and rehabilitation from about the third week until discharge. In stabilization the child receives the starter milk known as F-75, which is low in energy and protein, given often and in small volumes, and in transition the child moves to a higher-energy milk known as F-100 or to ready-to-use therapeutic food, so that each phase keeps its own food, its own volume, and its own dangers.

The starter formula is deliberately weak, and understanding why makes its slowness easier to accept. F-75 provides about 75 kilocalories in every 100 milliliters, with modest protein and added potassium, magnesium, and micronutrients. It is given at about 130 milliliters per kilogram per day divided into feeds every 2 to 3 hours, which is 8 feeds in 24 hours. The sodium is kept low and the potassium high because the malnourished cell holds too much sodium and too little potassium, so the formula treats that imbalance as much as it feeds. Give the feed by cup, spoon, or syringe, use a nasogastric tube only when the child cannot take enough by mouth, and stop to reassess if the child vomits repeatedly, becomes more lethargic, or shows a rising pulse and breathing rate.

Feeding too fast kills through a mechanism called refeeding syndrome, and the mechanism is worth hearing in plain words. When a starved cell suddenly receives glucose, insulin rises, and the cell pulls phosphate, potassium, and magnesium out of the blood and into the cell. The blood is left dangerously low in all three, the thin heart muscle cannot contract properly, the rhythm can become unstable, and the child can die suddenly in the night. Fluid can also overload the lungs and circulation, especially if too much sodium and water are given while the child is being fed. This is why the first formula is weak, why the volumes are calculated by weight, and why feeds are frequent and small, and refusing to feed a child faster is not neglect but the protection that saves the first week.

Transition begins when the child is stable, has a good appetite, and is losing edema, usually in the second week, and the formula then becomes stronger. F-100 provides about 100 kilocalories in every 100 milliliters, given at 150 to 220 milliliters per kilogram per day, or the child moves to ready-to-use therapeutic food, a peanut-based paste needing no cooking and no clean water. In rehabilitation the target is catch-up growth of at least 5 grams per kilogram per day, and good programs aim for 10 grams per kilogram per day or more. Weigh the child every morning before the first feed and watch the curve, and remember that emotional and sensory stimulation belongs here too, because a child who is played with, talked to, and held gains weight faster than a child left alone in a cot.

Every child with severe acute malnutrition receives antibiotics, whether or not there is an obvious infection, because the immune system cannot be trusted to announce one. For the child admitted with complications, the World Health Organization recommends ampicillin, 50 milligrams per kilogram intravenously every 6 hours, with gentamicin, 7.5 milligrams per kilogram intramuscularly or intravenously once a day, for at least 7 days. For the child treated at home without complications, the recommendation is amoxicillin, 15 milligrams per kilogram by mouth every 8 hours for 5 days, with metronidazole, 7.5 milligrams per kilogram every 8 hours for 7 days, added when there is severe diarrhea or infected skin. Check the doses against the current World Health Organization guideline and your national formulary, because national regimens differ.

Vitamin A deficiency is both a cause and a consequence of severe acute malnutrition, and it can blind a child within days, so every malnourished child receives it. The dose depends on age: 50,000 international units for an infant under 6 months, 100,000 international units from 6 to 11 months, and 200,000 international units for a child 12 months and older, given by mouth on the first day and repeated on the second. Measles is a catastrophe for a malnourished child, so vaccinate at admission if the child is 6 months or older and not already vaccinated, and give a second dose at 9 months as the routine schedule requires, while in an outbreak the World Health Organization recommends vaccination from 6 months of age. Vitamin A is safe at these doses but toxic in excess, so record each dose and do not repeat it within a month without a clear reason.

Beyond vitamin A, several micronutrients are given from the first day, and iron is deliberately not one of them. Give folic acid, 5 milligrams on the first day and then 1 milligram a day, potassium at 3 to 4 millimoles per kilogram per day, magnesium at 0.4 to 0.6 millimoles per kilogram per day, zinc at 2 milligrams per kilogram per day, and a multivitamin supplement. Iron is withheld until the child is in rehabilitation, has a good appetite, and is gaining weight, and then it is given at about 3 milligrams of elemental iron per kilogram per day. The reason is that free iron feeds bacteria and can worsen infection during the acute phase, and early iron has been associated with higher mortality.

The first day has a rhythm, and a nurse who runs it the same way every time will not forget a step. On admission, weigh and measure the child, check the temperature and blood glucose, classify dehydration, screen for danger signs, and take the history for feeding, diarrhea, fever, cough, and the household. Put the child in a warm place, start antibiotics, and give vitamin A and the other micronutrients as the guideline directs, but if the child is hypoglycemic or hypothermic, treat that first, before any feed. Feed every 2 to 3 hours, day and night, record the volume taken at every feed rather than the volume offered, and write the baseline weight on the chart so that tomorrow's weight can be compared with it.

Most children with severe acute malnutrition can be treated at home, and that is the great advance of recent decades. The World Health Organization recommends outpatient care for a child with severe wasting or nutritional edema who passes the appetite test, has no complication, and is clinically well and alert. The complications that force admission include any general danger sign, persistent vomiting, convulsions, severe dehydration, severe pneumonia, high fever, hypothermia, severe anemia, and any condition needing intravenous treatment or a transfusion. A child who is not eating, or whose mother cannot bring the child back weekly, also belongs in inpatient care, and the decision is never permanent, because a child who fails the appetite test at the next visit moves to the ward and a child in the ward moves home as soon as the criteria are met.

The appetite test is the single most useful bedside decision in the outpatient program, and it takes half an hour. Wash your hands, sit the child on the mother's lap, and offer a known amount of ready-to-use therapeutic food, about one quarter to one third of a sachet for a small child. Let the child eat without force or distraction for up to 30 minutes, and watch how eagerly the paste is taken. The child passes if the offered amount is eaten, and fails if it is not, or if the child is too weak, too breathless, or too ill to eat, and a failed test is a clinical finding rather than a judgment about the mother. Repeat the test at every weekly visit, because appetite can return or disappear, and the result is what moves the child between care settings.

The weekly outpatient visit is short but it must be complete, because it is the only contact the program has with the child. Weigh the child, measure the mid-upper arm circumference, check both feet for edema, and ask about feeding, appetite, diarrhea, vomiting, fever, cough, and any danger sign. Do the appetite test, give the ready-to-use therapeutic food ration for the coming week, give the daily amoxicillin if the child is in the first days of treatment, provide vitamin A when it is due, and check the immunization card. Praise what the mother is doing well, because the program depends on her, and make the next appointment explicit in day, hour, and place. A child who is not gaining weight after two weeks, or who is losing weight, needs a careful examination and probably admission.

Recognize the child who must move from home to hospital before the child is in extremis. Any danger sign, a failed appetite test, new or worsening edema, refusal to eat, persistent vomiting, or diarrhea that does not settle means admission. A child who has not gained weight after 2 weeks of outpatient treatment, or who has lost weight at any visit, needs a full reassessment for infection, tuberculosis, human immunodeficiency virus, and inadequate feeding at home. A child who deteriorates during inpatient care, with a rising pulse or respiratory rate, a falling temperature, or increasing lethargy, needs escalation and probably referral to a facility with oxygen, transfusion, and a physician. In a remote post, arrange referral while the child is still stable enough to travel, in daylight, with a referral note, a supply of feed, and an escort who knows the destination.

Record the weight every morning before the first feed, the temperature and pulse every 4 hours in the ward, the respiratory rate at the same interval, the volume of every feed, and every stool, vomit, and dose. Watch for the early signs of fluid overload and heart failure: a pulse that rises by 25 beats per minute and a respiratory rate that rises by 5 breaths per minute above the previous readings, with increasing breathlessness, a new cough, or puffiness of the eyelids. If those signs appear, reduce the feed, return to the starter formula, and reassess, because catching overload early prevents a death from too much food or fluid. Weigh daily in the ward and weekly at home, plot the weights so the curve speaks before the child does.

Some things must never be done to a severely malnourished child, and naming them protects the child when you are tired. Never give a diuretic for nutritional edema, because the edema is a shift of fluid rather than excess fluid, and a diuretic will only dehydrate the child and disturb the electrolytes. Never give iron during stabilization, and never give a high-protein or high-energy diet in the first week, because both increase a metabolic load the child cannot carry. Never rehydrate a severely malnourished child with standard oral rehydration salts or rapid intravenous fluid, except for shock, and then only with the slow regimen. Never force-feed a child or leave a feed unfinished without writing it down, and never send a child home without a follow-up appointment and a clear explanation of the danger signs.

The things that save children in a remote clinic are ordinary supplies, and their absence is a clinical emergency. Keep ready-to-use therapeutic food in a cool, dry, dark place, off the floor, protected from rodents, and rotate stock so the oldest sachet is used first, because the paste is heat sensitive. The powdered therapeutic milks must be mixed with clean water in a clean container, used within 24 hours, and never boiled after mixing, because heat destroys the vitamins. Keep a stock of vitamin A capsules, amoxicillin suspension, the rehydration solutions, zinc, folic acid, potassium, and magnesium, and count them weekly so a stock-out is seen before it becomes a death. Protect the cold chain for measles vaccine, with a cold box and ice packs ready for the day the refrigerator fails. The equipment list is short: a scale accurate to 100 grams, a length board and a height board, color-banded mid-upper arm circumference tapes, thermometers, a blood glucose meter with unexpired strips, feeding cups, nasogastric tubes, soap, and warm blankets. Check the scale against a known weight every week, because a drifting scale makes the program blind.

Documentation is not administration; it is the memory of the clinic and the safety net for the child. Record every admission in the nutrition register with the name, age, sex, weight, height, mid-upper arm circumference, edema, appetite test result, immunization status, and date. Keep an individual card at the bedside or with the mother, on which every feed, dose, temperature, and weight is written, and use the same card at every weekly visit so the curve is continuous. Record discharges, transfers, deaths, and defaulters, and keep a defaulter list with a name and a way to reach the family, because a child who stops coming is at high risk and invisible unless someone writes the child down. Report the tallies to the district each week or month as your system requires, since the numbers drive the supply of therapeutic food and the staffing of the ward.

The nurse is the clinician in this chapter, and the role carries more autonomy than in any hospital. You assess, classify, prescribe according to a protocol, feed, monitor, teach, and decide when to refer, often without a physician within a day's travel. Work with community health workers, who can screen with a mid-upper arm circumference tape, refer, and follow children in their villages, and supervise them with regular visits and simple feedback. Task-sharing saves lives only when the tasks are taught, supervised, and supported with supplies and referral, so treat the community health worker as a colleague and give that worker the tools the work requires. Teach the mother from the first day, because she will feed the child at 2 in the morning, and teach the grandmother and the father too, because they decide what happens in the house.

Counseling is the difference between a child who finishes treatment and a child who relapses, so it belongs in the clinical encounter and not in a leaflet. Explain the diagnosis in plain words: the child is severely malnourished, the body has adapted to too little food, and recovery takes weeks. Show the mother how much ready-to-use therapeutic food to give, how to store it, and how to feed the child little and often, and explain that breastfeeding must continue and energy-dense family food must be added. Teach the danger signs that mean return immediately: inability to drink or breastfeed, vomiting everything, convulsions, unusual sleepiness, fast or difficult breathing, fever, diarrhea, swelling of the feet, or refusal to eat. Agree on the follow-up date, ask the mother to repeat the plan back to you, and write it on the card, because the mother is the most important member of the team.

Discharge is a milestone, not an ending, and the criteria should be clear before the child reaches them. A child may go home when the weight-for-height is at or above minus 2 standard deviations, or the mid-upper arm circumference is 125 millimeters or more, with no edema, sustained over at least 2 consecutive measurements, and when immunization is up to date and the mother can describe the feeding plan and the danger signs. After discharge, keep the child in growth monitoring and supplementary feeding if the program offers it, and see the child again at 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months, because relapse is common in the first months. Prevention is the long game: exclusive breastfeeding for the first 6 months, timely and adequate complementary feeding, vaccination, deworming, vitamin A supplementation, insecticide-treated bed nets, and water, sanitation and hygiene in the household. Treat a child who recovers and then returns with edema as a signal that something at home has not changed.

To summarize, the child who arrives at your remote clinic is assessed in the same systematic order every time, with the general danger signs sought first and the whole child examined rather than the loudest complaint. Dehydration is classified by the signs that predict it and rehydrated by degree, with the severely malnourished child treated differently, more slowly, and with a solution made for that child. Severe acute malnutrition is a physiological emergency, not hunger, and its first hours belong to warmth, glucose, antibiotics, micronutrients, and small frequent feeds of starter formula, because feeding too fast can kill through refeeding syndrome. The child who passes the appetite test and has no complication can be treated at home with ready-to-use therapeutic food and weekly follow-up, while the child who fails the test or deteriorates belongs in a bed with close monitoring. The unglamorous work carries the outcome, the accurate scale, the full stock, the honest chart, the taught mother, and the nurse who checks the current guidance. Most of these children live when the ordinary steps are done in the right order.
