# Chapter 27 — HIV, Tuberculosis, and Malaria Co-Infection

Three infections dominate the tropical medicine clinic more than any others, and they dominate it together rather than separately. The human immunodeficiency virus, the cause of AIDS, weakens the immune system slowly over years. Tuberculosis and malaria are the two infections that most often kill the person whose immune defenses have been worn down. Wherever one of the three is common, the other two are usually common as well, because all three grow from the same soil of poverty, crowding, displacement, malnutrition, and fragile health services. A nurse who understands this convergence stops treating three separate problems and begins treating one patient who carries a tangled risk. This chapter therefore moves through the biology of the overlap, the decisions about testing and timing, the drug interactions that change what can safely be given, and the follow-up work that determines whether any of it lasts.

The reasons the three diseases cluster are social before they are biological. Tuberculosis spreads through shared air in crowded rooms, so it flourishes in prisons, mines, barracks, informal settlements, and refugee camps. Malaria follows the mosquito, so it flourishes where standing water, warm temperatures, and houses that do not keep insects out allow the vector to bite at night. The human immunodeficiency virus spreads through sexual contact, through blood, and from mother to child, and its burden is heaviest where poverty limits education, condoms, testing, and treatment. Malnutrition weakens resistance to all three, and each infection worsens nutrition in turn. Migration and displacement carry all three into new populations while interrupting the treatment that was controlling them. When these forces act on the same community, the same person, and the same household, they produce a burden that neither the malaria program nor the tuberculosis program nor the HIV clinic can manage alone.

The biology of the overlap runs mainly through one cell. The human immunodeficiency virus infects and destroys the helper T lymphocyte, the cell that coordinates the immune response to organisms that live inside cells. As that population falls, the macrophage can no longer be activated properly, and the granuloma that had walled off dormant tuberculosis bacilli for years begins to fail, so latent infection reactivates into active disease. Malaria behaves differently, because it does not depend on that same pathway, yet the person with advanced HIV still has higher parasite densities, more fever, more severe anemia, and a greater risk of dying during an episode of severe malaria. The virus also raises the risk of new tuberculosis infection after exposure and speeds the progress from infection to disease. Tuberculosis in turn raises the viral load and accelerates the decline of the immune system, so the two infections push each other forward. This is why tuberculosis is the leading cause of death among people living with HIV, and why malaria must be treated as a serious illness rather than a routine fever in the same population.

Tuberculosis in a person with HIV often looks unlike the textbook description. Early in the immune decline, the disease still favors the upper lobes of the lung with cavities and a productive cough. As immunity falls further, the chest radiograph becomes atypical, with lower zone infiltration, enlarged lymph nodes, or a normal appearance, and the sputum smear is frequently negative because the bacilli are few. Extrapulmonary disease becomes common, involving lymph nodes, the pleura, the pericardium, the abdomen, the bone marrow, and the meninges, and disseminated disease can present as a prolonged fever with enlarged liver and spleen and no localizing sign. The clinician must therefore lower the threshold for investigation, because a negative smear and an unremarkable radiograph do not exclude tuberculosis in a person with advanced HIV. Weight loss, night sweats, and a cough that will not settle should trigger a full workup rather than a course of antibiotics and a wait.

Malaria in a person living with HIV is more severe at every step. Parasite densities are higher, fever lasts longer, severe anemia is more common, and the risk of cerebral malaria and death rises, particularly when the immune decline is advanced and when the person is not taking cotrimoxazole prophylaxis. The effect is not symmetrical, because malaria transiently raises the viral load and may accelerate the loss of helper T lymphocytes, so an episode of malaria can move the HIV disease forward. In pregnancy the combination is especially dangerous, because both infections contribute to maternal anemia, miscarriage, stillbirth, low birth weight, and infant death, and each one makes the other harder to treat. The practical consequence is that a positive malaria test in a person with HIV never means only malaria. You must ask whether tuberculosis has been excluded, whether prophylaxis is being taken, whether the antiretroviral regimen is working, and whether the anemia is severe enough to need transfusion or a change of plan.

A single fever may have more than one cause at the same time, and this is the rule rather than the exception in co-infection. A patient with HIV who presents with fever, cough, and a positive malaria test may also have tuberculosis, and treating only the malaria produces a brief improvement followed by deterioration. The nurse should treat the confirmed malaria while continuing to investigate, because the disappearance of parasites does not explain a cough that has lasted 3 weeks or a weight loss that has lasted 3 months. Conversely, a patient started on tuberculosis treatment whose fever persists may have malaria, a bacterial infection, or the immune reconstitution reaction described later in this chapter. Holding several possibilities open at once, and documenting the reasoning, is what prevents the missed second diagnosis.

Every person diagnosed with tuberculosis should be offered an HIV test, and this should happen at the first encounter rather than after treatment has begun. The World Health Organization recommends provider-initiated testing with an opt-out approach, meaning that the test is presented as a routine part of tuberculosis care, the patient is told why it matters, and the test proceeds unless the patient declines. The reason is practical, because the result changes the treatment regimen, the timing of antiretroviral therapy, the choice of prophylaxis, and the intensity of follow-up, so a delayed result delays all four. The same offer belongs in the reverse direction, because every person entering HIV care should be screened for tuberculosis symptoms, and every person with a new HIV diagnosis in a high-burden setting needs a systematic assessment for active tuberculosis and consideration of preventive treatment. The nurse who normalizes the offer, explains that the result is confidential, and answers the question the patient is afraid to ask does more to find cases than any poster on the wall.

The offer of an HIV test belongs throughout tropical medicine, not only in dedicated tuberculosis and HIV clinics. Any adult with a fever of unknown origin, severe or recurrent malaria, a sexually transmitted infection, chronic diarrhea, tuberculosis at any site, shingles, unexplained weight loss, or a persistent skin problem should be offered a test. Pregnant women should be tested at the first antenatal visit and retested in the third trimester where the incidence is high, because treatment protects both mother and child. Children who are malnourished, repeatedly ill, or failing to thrive should be tested where the mother's status is unknown. The test itself is rapid and simple, usually a finger-prick blood sample read within minutes, but the result must be confirmed by a second, different test before it is communicated as a diagnosis. A positive screening test is a preliminary result, and the patient deserves an explanation of that distinction in plain language.

Confidentiality is not a courtesy in HIV care, it is a clinical instrument. If a patient believes that a result will be shared with family, employers, or neighbors, that patient will avoid testing, postpone treatment, and conceal missed doses, and the whole cascade weakens. Disclosure should be supported rather than demanded, because the patient carries the risk, and the nurse's task is to help the patient weigh who needs to know, who will be supportive, and how to be safe. Partner notification can be offered as a service the patient controls, and in many programs the patient is encouraged to bring partners in for testing voluntarily. Where a partner is at risk and the patient refuses disclosure, the local legal framework and the guidance of the World Health Organization and the national program set the limits of what the clinician may do, and those limits must be known before the situation arises. The same conversation should include the fact that treatment which suppresses the virus protects the partner, which is one of the strongest reasons to start therapy and stay on it.

Antiretroviral therapy is now recommended for every person living with HIV, regardless of the helper T lymphocyte count and regardless of symptoms, and it should begin as soon as the patient is ready and the diagnosis is confirmed. The World Health Organization has moved to rapid initiation, ideally on the same day as the diagnosis, because every week of delay carries a measurable risk of illness and death and because a patient who leaves the clinic untreated may not return. The exceptions are narrow and clinical. In cryptococcal meningitis, therapy is usually delayed by 4 to 6 weeks, because starting too early in that infection increases mortality. In tuberculous meningitis, therapy is generally delayed for 4 to 8 weeks for the same reason. For everyone else, the default is to start promptly, and the nurse should treat the start date as a clinical target with the same weight as a prescription.

When tuberculosis and HIV are diagnosed together, the order and timing of treatment matter as much as the drugs. Current guidance from the World Health Organization is to begin tuberculosis treatment first and to add antiretroviral therapy within the first 8 weeks, with the interval chosen according to the helper T lymphocyte count. When that count is very low, therapy begins within 2 weeks, because the risk of death from HIV outruns the risk of the immune reaction. When the count is higher and the tuberculosis is not meningitis, therapy can begin after 2 to 4 weeks, which allows the patient to settle on the larger regimen before the second one is added. In tuberculous meningitis, therapy is deferred for 4 to 8 weeks. These intervals are revised as trials accumulate and differ between national programs, so the current guideline must be checked before the start date is set.

First-line antiretroviral therapy in most programs today is a single tablet taken once daily that combines tenofovir, lamivudine, and dolutegravir. This combination is potent, well tolerated, and forgiving of a missed dose, and it suppresses the virus quickly in most patients. Dolutegravir is given at 50 milligrams by mouth once daily in the ordinary case. Tenofovir is given at 300 milligrams and lamivudine at 300 milligrams in the fixed-dose combination, though weight bands for children and lower doses for kidney impairment change the arithmetic. Where dolutegravir is not available, efavirenz at 600 milligrams by mouth once daily with tenofovir and lamivudine remains a common alternative, and nevirapine is still used in some settings. The nurse should verify the dose against the national guideline, the patient's weight, kidney function, pregnancy status, and the other medicines in the regimen, because the tablet that suits one patient may be wrong for the next.

Rifampicin is the backbone of tuberculosis treatment and the most disruptive drug in the room, because it strongly induces the liver enzymes that clear other medicines. When it meets the protease inhibitors, the blood levels of those drugs fall so far that the virus is no longer suppressed, and the standard guidance is therefore not to use protease inhibitors with rifampicin at all. Where a protease inhibitor is essential, the tuberculosis regimen may be changed to rifabutin, which induces the same enzymes far less, but even then the protease inhibitor dose usually needs adjustment with a boosting agent, and that decision belongs to an experienced clinician. The same enzyme induction lowers the levels of many other drugs, including hormonal contraceptives, some anticonvulsants, azole antifungals, and methadone, so a medication review is part of every tuberculosis start. The nurse should ask about every medicine the patient takes, including those bought without a prescription and those given by traditional healers, because the interaction that causes failure is often the one nobody mentioned.

With dolutegravir, the interaction with rifampicin is manageable but requires an explicit dose change. When the two are given together, dolutegravir is increased from 50 milligrams by mouth once daily to 50 milligrams by mouth twice daily, taken 12 hours apart, so that the enzyme induction does not leave the patient with too little drug. When rifampicin is stopped at the end of tuberculosis treatment, the dolutegravir dose returns to once daily, and that step must be recorded and communicated, because a patient left on the higher dose or dropped to the lower dose at the wrong moment loses either safety or potency. Some guidance, including recommendations from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, has supported 50 milligrams twice daily with rifampicin, while newer formulations of dolutegravir at higher strength allow once-daily dosing with rifampicin in certain regimens. Because these recommendations have changed more than once in recent years and differ between countries, the current national guideline and the current product information must be checked before every change. The nurse's role is to verify, to write the change plainly on the patient's card, and to confirm at each visit which dose the patient is actually swallowing.

Malaria treatment brings its own interactions. Artemether with lumefantrine is the most widely used artemisinin-based combination therapy, and lumefantrine is metabolized by the same liver enzymes that rifampicin induces, so cure rates can fall when the two are used together, and some programs prefer a different combination in that situation. Artesunate with amodiaquine is another common option, and amodiaquine can add to liver and bone marrow toxicity when combined with other drugs. Efavirenz lowers the level of some artemisinin compounds, while dolutegravir and artemether with lumefantrine can be given together with attention to timing, and the practical rule is to check the interaction table in the current guideline rather than to reason from memory. Cotrimoxazole interacts with several drugs as well, adding to the risk of high potassium and kidney injury with some blood pressure medicines and increasing the blood-thinning effect of warfarin. A single pharmacist review, or a nurse working from the program's interaction chart, catches most of these problems before they reach the patient.

Tuberculosis treatment itself follows the same principles in a person with HIV as in anyone else, with two additions. The standard regimen for drug-susceptible pulmonary disease is 2 months of isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol, followed by 4 months of isoniazid and rifampicin, given daily and supervised where the program can provide it. Isoniazid is given at 5 milligrams per kilogram by mouth daily, usually 300 milligrams for an adult, rifampicin at 10 milligrams per kilogram, pyrazinamide at 25 milligrams per kilogram, and ethambutol at 15 milligrams per kilogram, all as fixed-dose combinations where possible. Pyridoxine at 25 to 50 milligrams by mouth daily is added to prevent the peripheral neuropathy that isoniazid causes, and that prevention matters more in people with HIV, in pregnancy, in diabetes, and in malnutrition. Treatment is prolonged in some situations, including tuberculosis of the bone and of the central nervous system, and the duration is decided by the national program rather than by habit. The first addition is antiretroviral therapy, timed as described above, and the second is vigilance for the immune reconstitution reaction, which is the next subject.

When antiretroviral therapy restores the immune system, that recovery can itself cause illness, and the phenomenon is called immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome. The mechanism is an exaggerated inflammatory response to an infection that was already present, or to dead organisms still in the tissues, as the newly competent immune cells arrive and react vigorously where previously they could not. It typically appears in the first 4 to 8 weeks after therapy begins, though it can occur up to several months later, and it is more likely when therapy starts while the helper T lymphocyte count is very low and the infection burden is high. The two forms are distinguished by whether the infection was known before. In paradoxical immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome, a patient already being treated for a known infection suddenly worsens. In unmasking immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome, a previously unnoticed infection reveals itself as the immune system begins to fight it. Neither form means that the antiretroviral therapy has failed, and neither means that the antimicrobial treatment should be stopped.

The appearances are varied and can be alarming. In tuberculosis, the classic pattern is a patient who was improving on treatment and then develops new fever, enlarging and painful lymph nodes, worsening breathlessness, or new infiltrates on the chest radiograph, while the tuberculosis itself is under control. In cryptococcal meningitis, the reaction can produce worsening headache, raised pressure in the head, and new neurological signs. In other patients, the syndrome presents as painful skin lesions, an enlarging liver, inflammation of the eye, or a collection of fluid around the heart or the lung. The timing, the clinical picture, and the exclusion of drug resistance, treatment failure, and a new infection are what support the diagnosis, because there is no laboratory test that confirms it. The nurse who knows the pattern will notice the change on the day it appears rather than at the next scheduled visit.

Management rests on treating the underlying infection properly, because the reaction is a sign that the organism is being attacked, not a reason to abandon treatment. Antiretroviral therapy is continued in almost every case, because stopping it reverses the immune recovery and does not reliably end the reaction, and the important exception is severe cryptococcal disease with raised pressure in the head, where specialist advice and sometimes a brief delay are needed. Symptoms are relieved with anti-inflammatory treatment, and corticosteroids are used when the reaction is severe or threatens an organ, as in tuberculosis involving the central nervous system or the pericardium, where prednisolone or dexamethasone is added under specialist guidance. The complications that make the reaction dangerous must be watched for, including airway obstruction from enlarged lymph nodes, compression of the spinal cord, and raised pressure in the head, and these require urgent referral. In many patients the reaction is mild and settles over weeks with explanation and simple pain relief, and reassurance is a real treatment.

Tuberculosis in pregnancy threatens two patients, and it is treated rather than deferred. Untreated disease causes miscarriage, premature birth, low birth weight, and death of the mother, and the first-line drugs are considered safe enough in pregnancy that the risk of not treating is far greater than the risk of treating. Isoniazid, rifampicin, ethambutol, and pyrazinamide are used in the standard regimen, with pyridoxine added to prevent neuropathy and seizures in the newborn. Streptomycin is avoided because it damages the hearing of the fetus. Active tuberculosis is not a reason to avoid breastfeeding, and the mother should be supported to continue, though she should wear a mask while she is infectious and the infant should be assessed. Pregnant women with HIV should also be offered preventive treatment for latent tuberculosis where the program provides it, and the choice of preventive regimen depends on current national guidance. The nurse should arrange the birth plan, the infant's assessment, and the screening of the household together, because a mother with tuberculosis usually lives with people who are also at risk.

Tuberculosis in children is harder to diagnose and easier to prevent, and both facts shape nursing practice. Children often cannot produce sputum, their chest radiographs are difficult to interpret, and the tuberculin skin test is unreliable in the very young and in those with HIV, so the diagnosis usually rests on a history of contact with an infectious adult, on symptoms, on examination, and on a decision by an experienced clinician. Every child in a household with an adult who has pulmonary tuberculosis should be screened, and those who are well but infected should receive preventive treatment according to the national guideline. Dosing is by weight, and children are given child-friendly dispersible fixed-dose combinations, with isoniazid at 10 milligrams per kilogram, rifampicin at 15 milligrams per kilogram, pyrazinamide at 35 milligrams per kilogram, and ethambutol at 20 milligrams per kilogram by mouth daily, adjusted as the child grows. BCG vaccination at birth protects against the most severe forms of tuberculosis in infancy but does not prevent infection or transmission, so it never replaces the other measures. Growth monitoring and nutritional support belong in the same visit, because a child who does not gain weight is a child who is not responding.

Malaria in a person with HIV is treated with the same artemisinin-based combination therapies used in anyone else, and the choice depends on the local resistance pattern and on the medicines the patient already takes. Artemether with lumefantrine is the most common, given as a weight-based course of 6 doses over 3 days, with the first dose repeated if the patient vomits within 30 minutes. Artemisinin-based combination therapy is given by mouth for uncomplicated disease, while severe malaria is treated with artesunate intravenously at a weight-based dose and then followed by a full oral course once the patient can swallow. The nurse must verify the dose against the patient's weight, confirm that the patient has eaten enough fat to absorb lumefantrine properly, and check for interactions with the antiretroviral regimen as described earlier. Follow-up should include a repeat test to confirm clearance in areas where resistance is a concern, and a reassessment of the anemia, because the fall in hemoglobin can be delayed. The World Health Organization warns that resistance to artemisinin is spreading in parts of Southeast Asia and has emerged in East Africa, so a patient who does not clear parasites on time needs prompt review rather than a second identical course.

Cotrimoxazole prophylaxis is one of the most valuable and least glamorous interventions in co-infection care. It is a combination of trimethoprim at 160 milligrams and sulfamethoxazole at 800 milligrams, given as one tablet by mouth daily, and in children the dose is weight-based. It protects against Pneumocystis pneumonia, toxoplasmosis, and a range of bacterial infections, and it also reduces episodes of malaria, which makes it doubly useful in a person living with HIV in a malarious region. It is started for adults with advanced immune suppression, defined by the helper T lymphocyte count, or with a World Health Organization clinical stage that warrants it, and it is continued until the immune recovery is secure and sustained on antiretroviral therapy. It is stopped when the patient has been stable on therapy with a good count for a defined period, and the current guideline defines that period. The nurse should watch for rash, nausea, low white cell counts, high potassium, and kidney injury, and should never restart the drug after a severe reaction, because sulfonamide hypersensitivity can be fatal on re-exposure.

Prevention of malaria in a person living with HIV combines the usual vector measures with the specific protection that cotrimoxazole already provides. Insecticide-treated bed nets should be provided and their use explained and checked, because a net that is stored in a cupboard protects nobody. Indoor residual spraying reduces the mosquito population and the human biting rate, and it works best when a high proportion of households in the community participate. Where cotrimoxazole is being taken, it replaces the intermittent preventive treatment with sulfadoxine and pyrimethamine that is otherwise given to pregnant women, because the two sulfonamide drugs should not be combined. Pregnant women who are not taking cotrimoxazole should receive that preventive treatment according to the national schedule, usually at each antenatal visit after the first trimester. Seasonal preventive treatment for young children in areas of high transmission is a separate program and should be integrated with these efforts rather than run in parallel.

Adherence decides the outcome of all three infections, and it is a behavior shaped by circumstance rather than a character trait. A patient who misses doses is usually responding to side effects, a pill burden that is too large, food requirements that cannot be met, a long walk to the clinic, a stockout at the pharmacy, depression, alcohol use, or a fear of being seen with the medicines. Adherence counseling therefore begins with a simple question about how the medicines are actually being taken, asked without blame, and continues with practical problem solving. Once-daily fixed-dose combinations, taking medicines at the same time as a daily habit such as a meal or a radio program, and involving a treatment supporter chosen by the patient all improve the odds. The nurse should explain what each medicine does and what happens if it is stopped, because understanding is the foundation of persistence. When a patient reports stopping treatment, the right response is to find out why and to restart, not to lecture.

Retention in care is measured, and the measurements should be acted on. Every clinic needs a simple system that records the next appointment, identifies who missed it, and produces a list of people to contact within a few days, because a patient who is lost to follow-up is at high risk of death and of developing resistant disease. Contact should begin with a telephone call or a text message, using the number and the method the patient consented to, and should escalate to a home visit by a community health worker where that is acceptable to the patient. The person who makes the call needs training in confidentiality, because a message that reveals a diagnosis to a relative can destroy the relationship that the tracing was meant to save. Tracing should aim to solve the problem that caused the absence, whether it was transport, cost, side effects, work, or fear, and to offer a route back that does not shame the patient. A clinic that counts its losses and goes looking for them will keep far more people in care than one that waits.

Stigma is a clinical variable, not a background sentiment, and it changes what patients do. Enacted stigma is the discrimination a person actually experiences, in the family, in the workplace, or in the health service. Anticipated stigma is the discrimination a person expects, and it is often the more powerful of the two, because it prevents people from being tested, from collecting medicines, and from disclosing to a partner. Internalized stigma is the shame a person turns against themselves, and it is strongly associated with depression and with stopping treatment. The nurse shapes all three every day, through the words used at the desk, the placement of the queue, the privacy of the counseling room, and the tone of the question about how the medicines are going. A diagnosis spoken aloud in a waiting area can cost a patient a marriage or a job, and the harm is entirely preventable. Where the law criminalizes a person's behavior or identity, the nurse should know the local rules and should never become an instrument of them.

Depression, anxiety, and alcohol use disorder are common in this population, and they undermine every other intervention. Depression reduces adherence, delays clinic attendance, and increases mortality, and it is treatable. Screening should be routine, brief, and repeated, using the tools the program provides, and a positive screen should lead to assessment and management rather than to a note in the file. Alcohol use increases the risk of missing doses, of liver injury from tuberculosis drugs, and of unsafe sex, and it should be discussed without moralizing. Pain, poor sleep, and the fear of dying are frequent and should be named and treated, because untreated distress is read by patients as evidence that nothing can be done. The nurse is often the first person to hear about these problems, and the response should be practical, offering a referral, a follow-up appointment, and an explicit statement that the patient is not being abandoned.

Monitoring is how you find out whether the plan is working, and it has to be feasible in the setting where you work. Viral load measurement is the preferred way to follow HIV treatment, taken at 6 months after starting and then at least once a year, with a rising result meaning adherence problems or resistance and needing investigation rather than an immediate switch. The helper T lymphocyte count remains useful for decisions about prophylaxis and for tracking immune recovery where viral load is unavailable. Tuberculosis treatment is monitored by symptoms, by weight, and by sputum testing at defined points, and in a person with HIV the smear may remain negative throughout, so clinical response and culture where available carry more weight. Malaria is monitored by repeat testing, by the resolution of fever, and by the recovery of the blood count. Kidney function, liver enzymes, blood counts, and blood glucose should be checked at the intervals the national guideline specifies, and the results should be explained to the patient rather than filed.

Adverse effects are predictable, and anticipating them prevents most of the harm they cause. Dolutegravir is generally well tolerated but can cause weight gain and, rarely, allergic reactions and liver injury. Tenofovir can reduce kidney function and lower bone mineral density, and the older form of tenofovir is more likely to do so than the newer form, so kidney function should be checked before and during treatment. Efavirenz can cause vivid dreams, dizziness, and mood change, which is why it is taken at night and why depression must be screened for. Isoniazid injures the liver and causes peripheral neuropathy, which pyridoxine prevents, and rifampicin injures the liver, turns urine and secretions orange, and interacts with many other drugs. Cotrimoxazole can cause rash, suppression of the blood count, and high potassium. Artemisinin-based combination therapies are well tolerated, though lumefantrine can disturb sleep and appetite, and any drug that prolongs the electrical recovery of the heart must be used with care when other such drugs are given.

Drug-resistant tuberculosis changes the whole plan and must be identified rather than assumed. Molecular testing at the time of diagnosis detects resistance to rifampicin, which usually signals resistance to isoniazid as well, and that result should trigger a full susceptibility test and referral to a program that manages resistant disease. Treating a resistant infection with the standard first-line regimen produces failure, continued transmission, and further resistance, so the correct response to a rifampicin-resistant result is to stop and rebuild the regimen under expert guidance rather than to add a single drug. In a person with HIV, the second-line tuberculosis drugs bring additional interactions, especially with certain antiretroviral agents and with drugs that affect the rhythm of the heart, and the timing of antiretroviral therapy may need to change. These patients are best managed by a team that includes an infectious disease or tuberculosis specialist, and the nurse's job is to ensure that the referral happens, that the specimens reach the laboratory, and that the patient is supported through a longer and harder treatment. Injection-free regimens have widened access to treatment in recent years, but availability and eligibility vary, and the national program is the authority on what is offered.

The nurse's role in co-infection is broad, because in many settings the nurse practitioner is the person who holds the whole picture together. That means offering the HIV test, screening for tuberculosis symptoms at every visit, recognizing severe malaria, checking each prescription against the current guideline and the patient's other medicines, and following up the patient who does not return. It includes teaching the patient what each medicine is for, how to take it, what side effects to report, and when to come back urgently, and it includes protecting confidentiality at every step. Task sharing allows nurses to initiate and manage antiretroviral therapy and tuberculosis treatment where national policy permits, and that authority carries an obligation to work within the protocol and to refer when the situation exceeds it. Supply is part of clinical care, and the nurse should report stockouts and expiry dates as patient safety events, because a regimen interrupted by an empty shelf produces resistance as surely as a regimen taken badly. The integration of services matters too, because a patient who must attend three clinics on three different days will attend none of them reliably.

Nothing in this chapter should be read as settled. Antiretroviral regimens, tuberculosis regimens, malaria treatments, and preventive schedules are revised as evidence accumulates, and the guidance of the World Health Organization, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control differs in emphasis and sometimes in substance from one another and from national programs. Doses depend on weight, on age, on pregnancy, on kidney and liver function, and on the other medicines being taken, so the same drug can require three different doses in three patients sitting in the same room. Before prescribing or administering, the nurse must verify the dose against the current national guideline and the product information, confirm the patient's weight and allergies, check the interaction list, and confirm what the patient is already taking at home. This verification is not a formality, it is the intervention that prevents most avoidable harm in this field, and it should be done out loud with the patient whenever possible.

To summarize, HIV, tuberculosis, and malaria cluster in the same populations because they are driven by the same poverty, crowding, displacement, and weak services, and they worsen one another through the immune system and through the daily realities of treatment. Every person with tuberculosis should be offered an HIV test, every person with HIV should be screened for tuberculosis, and malaria in a person with HIV should be treated as a potentially severe illness rather than a simple fever. Antiretroviral therapy is now recommended for everyone and should begin promptly, with tuberculosis treatment started first and therapy added within 8 weeks, sooner when the helper T lymphocyte count is very low and later in tuberculous meningitis. Rifampicin dominates the interaction list, forcing the choice of a different regimen or a doubled dolutegravir dose, and the immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome must be recognized as a treatable consequence of recovery rather than as failure. Cotrimoxazole prevents pneumonia, bacterial infection, and malaria, and isoniazid prevents tuberculosis in those who are infected but not ill. Adherence, retention, and tracing are clinical interventions with the same standing as any prescription, and stigma is a variable that determines whether any of them happens. Above all, every regimen described here is subject to revision and to national variation, so the current guideline must be checked before every dose, and the nurse who verifies, explains, follows up, and treats the patient with dignity is the strongest link in the chain.
