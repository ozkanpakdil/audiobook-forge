# Chapter 26 — Pregnancy, Neonates, and Children

Pregnancy, the newborn period, and early childhood are the stages of life when a tropical infection causes the most harm and when a nurse has the greatest power to change the outcome. A pregnant woman in a village where malaria is transmitted carries two patients inside one body, and the placenta that should nourish her fetus can become a reservoir for parasites. A newborn delivered without clean cord care can develop tetanus before the first week of life is over, and a small child with diarrhea can lose a dangerous volume of fluid within hours. This chapter follows those three populations through the infections that threaten them and closes with the antenatal and newborn care that prevents most of the damage. It draws on guidance from the World Health Organization, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control, and it offers every regimen as current guidance rather than settled truth.

Pregnancy changes the way a woman handles infection before any pathogen arrives. The immune system shifts away from the aggressive cellular responses that could reject a fetus and toward antibody-mediated tolerance, which leaves some intracellular organisms less well controlled. Plasma volume expands by roughly one half, so hemoglobin concentration falls even when iron stores are adequate, and the heart works harder with a larger stroke volume. The kidneys filter more blood each minute, so drugs cleared by the kidney may leave the body faster and a dose that was adequate before pregnancy may fall short. These are the reasons a pregnant woman can look well while carrying a heavy parasite burden, and the reasons the nurse must measure rather than assume.

Malaria in pregnancy is the central problem of this chapter, and its mechanism explains everything that follows. A woman in her first pregnancy has not yet built antibodies against the adhesion protein that the parasite places on the surface of an infected red cell. That protein binds to a molecule called chondroitin sulfate A on the lining of the placental intervillous space, so the infected cells stick there instead of circulating back to the spleen for destruction. The placenta becomes a dense collection of parasites, and a blood film drawn from the mother's finger can be entirely negative while her placenta is heavily infected. The first pregnancy carries the highest risk, because the antibody that blocks this binding develops only after repeated exposure.

The consequences of placental malaria fall on both patients. The mother becomes anemic because the parasites destroy red cells, because the inflamed placenta consumes her iron, and because she is often also iron deficient from poor diet and hookworm. Severe anemia in pregnancy leads to heart failure, poor tolerance of blood loss at delivery, and maternal death. The fetus suffers because an infected placenta transports oxygen and nutrients poorly, so the infant is born early or small. Low birth weight is the single strongest predictor of infant death in the first year of life, and placental malaria is one of its leading causes in tropical regions. Stillbirth and preterm labor also rise when malaria goes untreated, and the surviving infant may be born anemic.

Treatment of malaria in pregnancy depends on the trimester, on the species, and on how sick the woman is, and the guidance has changed in recent years. For uncomplicated malaria from Plasmodium falciparum, current guidance from the World Health Organization supports an artemisinin-based combination therapy in all trimesters, including the first, having moved away from the older practice of giving quinine with clindamycin in early pregnancy. The artemisinin drugs clear the parasite quickly and are better tolerated than quinine, which causes low blood glucose and a cluster of unpleasant symptoms that make adherence difficult. Because these regimens are revised as safety data and resistance patterns change, guidance differs between countries, and the current national guideline must be checked before any antimalarial is prescribed or given to a pregnant woman.

Severe malaria in pregnancy is an obstetric emergency as much as an infectious one. Current guidance recommends intravenous artesunate at a dose of 2.4 milligrams per kilogram, given at the start of treatment and again at 12 hours and at 24 hours, then every 24 hours until the woman can swallow oral therapy. The nurse's task during those first hours is to check the blood glucose repeatedly, because quinine and severe malaria both cause hypoglycemia and the symptoms of low glucose can be mistaken for worsening cerebral malaria. Fluids must be given carefully, since the pregnant woman is vulnerable to pulmonary edema. Fetal heart tones are monitored, and the obstetric team is alerted whenever there is bleeding, contractions, or a reduction in fetal movement.

Prevention in pregnancy is built from three simple tools, and the nurse usually delivers all three. The first is the insecticide-treated bed net, used every night by the whole household, which reduces exposure to the bites of the female Anopheles mosquito between dusk and dawn. The second is intermittent preventive treatment, which means giving a full treatment dose of an antimalarial medicine at scheduled antenatal visits whether or not the woman has parasites in her blood. Current guidance uses sulfadoxine with pyrimethamine, given from the second trimester onward, at least 1 month apart, for a total of at least 3 doses, and each dose is 1,500 milligrams of sulfadoxine with 75 milligrams of pyrimethamine. The third is prompt diagnosis and treatment of any fever. Women with HIV who take daily cotrimoxazole should not receive sulfadoxine with pyrimethamine, because the two overlap and increase the risk of serious skin reactions. Programs are revised, they differ between countries, and the current guideline must be consulted before any of these medicines is given.

Neonatal malaria is a different problem from malaria in the mother, and the nurse must recognize both its forms. Congenital malaria occurs when parasites cross the placenta or enter the infant's blood during delivery, and it is uncommon because maternal antibodies and fetal hemoglobin offer some protection. The infected newborn typically becomes unwell in the first weeks of life with fever, jaundice, pallor, an enlarged liver and spleen, and poor feeding, and these findings overlap with sepsis, so a blood film and a rapid diagnostic test are essential. Treatment follows weight-based pediatric dosing, not the adult regimen, and the choice of medicine depends on the species and on whether the infant is well enough to take it by mouth. Neonatal malaria can also be acquired after birth from local mosquitoes, which is why a treated bed net over the infant's sleeping place matters from the first night.

Dengue is caused by a virus carried by Aedes mosquitoes, and it behaves differently in pregnancy. Vertical transmission from mother to fetus is uncommon but well documented, and the risk is greatest when the mother's illness occurs close to delivery. Infection during pregnancy is associated with miscarriage, preterm birth, low birth weight, and, rarely, death of the fetus. The pregnant woman also faces a higher risk of severe dengue, with plasma leakage, bleeding, and organ impairment, because the cardiovascular changes of pregnancy make her less able to compensate for fluid shifts. Warning signs such as abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, bleeding gums, restlessness, and a rapid fall in the platelet count demand immediate referral.

There is no antiviral medicine for dengue, so care is supportive and precise. Fluids are given by mouth when the woman can drink and intravenously when she cannot, and the volume is adjusted to her weight, her urine output, and her clinical response rather than to a fixed formula. Pain and fever are treated with paracetamol at 15 milligrams per kilogram every six hours by mouth, and aspirin and the other anti-inflammatory medicines are avoided because they increase the risk of bleeding and are dangerous in pregnancy. The hematocrit and the platelet count are followed together, because a rising hematocrit with a falling platelet count signals plasma leakage and the beginning of the critical phase. If the woman is near term, the obstetric team plans delivery around the phase of illness, since bleeding risk peaks as the fever settles.

Zika virus belongs to the same family as dengue and is carried by the same Aedes mosquitoes, and its greatest danger is to the developing brain. When a woman is infected in the first trimester, the virus can cross the placenta and infect the neural progenitor cells that build the cortex, and the result is Zika congenital syndrome. The syndrome includes severe microcephaly with a partly collapsed skull, thin cortical gray matter, calcifications, and enlarged ventricles, together with eye abnormalities, joint contractures, and hearing loss. Not every infected pregnancy produces the syndrome, but the risk is highest with first-trimester infection. Diagnosis in the infant rests on a PCR test of blood, urine, or cerebrospinal fluid, supported by antibody testing and by imaging of the brain. There is no treatment that reverses the damage, so care is developmental, nutritional, and supportive, with early hearing and vision assessment.

Prevention of Zika is prevention of mosquito bites and prevention of sexual transmission, and the nurse is the person who explains both. A pregnant woman should use repellent that is approved in pregnancy, cover her arms and legs, stay in screened or cooled rooms, and sleep under a bed net, remembering that the Aedes mosquito bites during the day and not only at night. Because the virus can persist in semen for months, guidance recommends that a pregnant woman's partner use condoms for the duration of the pregnancy if he may have been exposed. There is no vaccine, and no medicine prevents the infection, so counseling is the intervention, and the nurse should give that advice plainly and without alarm.

Chagas disease is caused by the protozoan parasite Trypanosoma cruzi, which is transmitted by triatomine bugs in Latin America and can also pass from mother to fetus across the placenta at any time in pregnancy. Congenital transmission can occur in every pregnancy of an infected woman, not only the first, so screening is repeated rather than assumed. Most infected newborns show no signs at all, so the diagnosis must be sought deliberately. When signs are present they include low birth weight, prematurity, an enlarged liver and spleen, jaundice, anemia, and, rarely, meningitis or heart inflammation. Screening in pregnancy begins with a blood test for antibodies, and a positive result requires confirmation by a second, different test. The newborn is then assessed with a PCR test and with microscopy of the blood in the first months of life, because antibody tests in early infancy reflect the mother's antibodies rather than the infant's infection.

Treatment of congenital Chagas disease is highly effective when it is given early, and this is one of the clearest examples of why newborn screening matters. Current guidance recommends benznidazole at a dose of 5 to 7 milligrams per kilogram per day, divided into two doses and given by mouth for 60 days, or nifurtimox at 8 to 10 milligrams per kilogram per day, divided into three or four doses, also for 60 days. Infants tolerate these medicines far better than adults do, and the cure rate approaches 100 per cent when treatment starts in the first year of life. The medicines are not given casually, because benznidazole can cause a rash, a low white cell count, and nerve tingling, and nifurtimox can cause nausea, weight loss, and irritability. These regimens are revised, guidance differs between countries, and the current national guideline must be checked before treatment begins.

Lymphatic filariasis is caused by worm parasites transmitted by mosquitoes, and the medicines that treat it carry specific cautions in pregnancy. Diethylcarbamazine and ivermectin are not given during pregnancy, and albendazole is avoided in the first trimester, so a pregnant woman who is known to carry the parasite is usually deferred until after delivery. Mass drug administration programs ask about pregnancy before they distribute tablets for exactly this reason, and the nurse who screens at the distribution point is the safeguard. The infection itself does not need urgent treatment during pregnancy, and the swelling and skin changes of chronic disease are managed meanwhile with washing, elevation, skin care, and treatment of bacterial entry points. After delivery, treatment can be given, and breastfeeding is not a barrier to albendazole.

Neonatal tetanus is a disease of unclean birth, and it remains a killer in places where deliveries happen at home without trained hands. The bacterium Clostridium tetani lives in soil and in animal and human waste, and its spores survive for years. When the umbilical cord is cut with an unsterile blade, or dressed with ash, mud, or dung, the spores enter the stump and germinate in the dead tissue. The toxin they release travels along the nerves to the spinal cord, where it blocks the signals that normally relax a muscle, so the infant develops stiffness and then spasms. The disease does not spread from person to person, and it is entirely preventable by vaccinating the mother, delivering with clean hands and sterile equipment, and caring for the cord with chlorhexidine.

Caring for an infant with neonatal tetanus is one of the most demanding tasks in a small hospital, and the nurse carries most of it. The infant is placed in a quiet, darkened room, handled as little as possible, and shielded from noise, light, and drafts, because every stimulus can trigger a spasm that stops breathing. Human tetanus immunoglobulin is given to neutralize toxin that has not yet entered the nerves, metronidazole is given to reduce the bacteria in the wound, and the cord stump is cleaned. Spasms are controlled with diazepam or with magnesium sulfate, and the dose of either must be checked against current guidance because both can cause respiratory depression. Feeding is maintained through a nasogastric tube, and the airway, the oxygen saturation, and the blood glucose are monitored continuously.

A child is not a small adult, and the phrase is not a slogan but a description of how the body handles medicines. The stomach of an infant produces less acid, so acid-labile drugs are absorbed differently, and gastric emptying is irregular in the first months of life. The liver enzymes that break down many medicines mature over the first years, so a drug that is cleared slowly in a newborn may be cleared unusually fast in a toddler. Total body water is a larger fraction of body weight in a child, so water-soluble drugs have a larger volume of distribution and need a different dose per kilogram than they would in an adult. These differences mean that a dose must be calculated for the individual child and checked, never scaled down by eye.

Everything in pediatric tropical medicine rests on the arithmetic, and the arithmetic rests on an accurate weight. The child is weighed on a scale with light clothing, and the weight is recorded in kilograms and written on the chart where every clinician can see it. When no scale is available, weight is estimated from length using a color-banded length tape, or from mid-upper arm circumference in an emergency, and the estimate is replaced with a measured weight as soon as possible. The dose is then calculated as milligrams per kilogram from the current guideline, and the maximum adult dose is applied as a ceiling, because children reach adult doses as they grow. Paracetamol is 15 milligrams per kilogram every six hours, ibuprofen is 10 milligrams per kilogram every eight hours, and amoxicillin for pneumonia is 40 to 45 milligrams per kilogram per day in two divided doses, but each of these must be confirmed against the current guideline. A second nurse repeats the calculation independently, and the two results are compared before the medicine is drawn up.

The adult tablet is one of the most dangerous objects in a tropical clinic. A tablet designed for a 70 kilogram adult contains a dose that can overwhelm a 10 kilogram child, and the practice of breaking it by hand produces fragments of unpredictable size. Unscored tablets crumble, and enteric coatings are destroyed when a tablet is crushed, so the drug is released too fast or destroyed by the stomach. The nurse's countermeasures are simple and powerful, using pediatric formulations whenever they exist, dispensing oral syringes and dosing cups instead of spoons, marking the exact volume to be given, and writing the dose in words the caregiver can repeat back. Counseling should include what to do if a dose is missed or vomited, and the caregiver should demonstrate the measurement before leaving.

The integrated management of childhood illness approach organizes the care of a sick child under 5 years of age into a single structured assessment, and it is designed for nurses working without a laboratory. The clinician checks the general danger signs first, which are the inability to drink or breastfeed, repeated vomiting of everything, convulsions, lethargy or unconsciousness, and, in a young infant, movement only when stimulated. Then the child is assessed for cough and difficult breathing, diarrhea, fever, ear problems, anemia, and nutritional status, and each problem is classified as severe, moderate, or absent rather than given a specific diagnosis. The classification drives the action, so the nurse treats what can be treated on the spot, gives the first dose of a needed medicine, and refers the child who needs a hospital. Counseling the caregiver and arranging a follow-up visit are part of the same encounter, not an afterthought.

Fever in a child is a symptom that demands a cause, not just a number. In a place where malaria is transmitted, every child with fever should have a rapid diagnostic test or a blood film, because malaria and pneumonia and meningitis can look alike at the beginning. Paracetamol at 15 milligrams per kilogram every six hours by mouth makes the child more comfortable, and it does not treat the infection underneath. Aspirin is never given to a child with a fever, because it is linked to a rare but devastating liver and brain injury. The nurse records the temperature, the respiratory rate, the hydration, and the level of consciousness, and looks specifically for the danger signs that mean referral. A fever that persists after malaria has been treated and parasites have cleared requires a second look for another cause.

Nutritional assessment belongs in every pediatric encounter, not only in a feeding clinic, because infection and malnutrition travel together. The child is weighed and measured, and the weight is plotted against a growth standard so that the trend across visits is visible rather than a single point. Mid-upper arm circumference is measured on the left arm at the midpoint between the shoulder and the elbow, and a value below 11.5 centimeters in a child of 6 to 59 months identifies severe acute malnutrition, while a value below 12.5 centimeters identifies moderate acute malnutrition. The nurse presses the tops of both feet for several seconds to check for pitting edema, which in a wasted child signals kwashiorkor rather than simple hunger. A child with severe acute malnutrition is referred for therapeutic feeding, and vitamin A is given according to the current schedule, because it reduces death from measles and diarrhea.

Diarrhea kills children by removing water and salt, and the nurse's first task is to grade the dehydration before treating it. A child with no dehydration is alert, drinks normally, has moist eyes and a normal skin pinch, and can be managed at home with extra fluids. A child with some dehydration is restless and irritable, has sunken eyes, drinks eagerly, and has a skin pinch that returns slowly. A child with severe dehydration is lethargic or unconscious, cannot drink, and has a skin pinch that returns very slowly, and this child needs intravenous fluid immediately. The nurse also asks how many stools have been passed, whether there is blood, whether the child has been able to keep anything down, and whether the child is still breastfeeding.

Treatment follows the grade of dehydration, and it is one of the most cost-effective interventions in medicine. A child with some dehydration receives oral rehydration salts at 75 milliliters per kilogram over four hours, given by spoon, cup, or syringe in small frequent sips, and breastfeeding continues throughout. Every stool passed during treatment is replaced with an additional 10 milliliters per kilogram, and the mother is taught to give the same replacement at home. Zinc is given alongside the fluid, at 20 milligrams daily for 10 to 14 days for a child older than 6 months and 10 milligrams daily for an infant younger than 6 months, because zinc shortens the illness and reduces the next episode. A child with severe dehydration needs an intravenous fluid bolus and reassessment, and oral rehydration is started as soon as the child can drink. Regimens and fluid volumes are revised, they differ between countries, and the current guideline must be checked before treatment is prescribed.

Pneumonia is the other great killer of young children, and the nurse can often recognize it with a clock and a stethoscope. The respiratory rate is counted for a full minute while the child is calm, and the threshold for fast breathing is 60 breaths per minute in an infant younger than 2 months, 50 breaths per minute from 2 to 11 months, and 40 breaths per minute from 1 to 5 years. Chest indrawing, when the lower chest wall pulls inward on inspiration, is a sign of severe pneumonia, as are grunting, head nodding, and inability to drink. A child with fast breathing and no danger signs is treated with amoxicillin by mouth, while a child with any danger sign is given oxygen and the first dose of an antibiotic and is referred urgently. The nurse also checks that the child's vaccinations are current, because pneumococcal and measles vaccines prevent a share of these illnesses.

Vaccination is the most reliable protection a child can receive against several of the diseases in this book. Current guidance from the World Health Organization recommends BCG against tuberculosis and the first dose of oral polio vaccine at birth, followed by a series of visits that deliver polio, a pentavalent vaccine against diphtheria, tetanus, pertussis, hepatitis B, and Haemophilus influenzae type b, and vaccines against pneumococcus and rotavirus. Measles vaccine is given at 9 months in most tropical settings, with a second dose later, because maternal antibody can blunt the response before that age and measles is most lethal in infancy. A child who has missed doses is caught up rather than started again, and every opportunity to vaccinate is used, including a visit for another illness. Live vaccines are generally avoided in a child with severe immunosuppression, and the current national schedule must be consulted before any dose is given.

The vaccine only protects a child if it is still potent when it is given, which makes logistics a clinical skill. The cold chain must be maintained from the refrigerator to the outreach site, with a temperature log checked at every session and a vaccine vial monitor read before each dose. Vials that have been opened are handled according to the national open-vial policy, and expired or heat-exposed stock is discarded rather than used. Safe injection practice means a sterile needle and syringe for every child, no reuse, and immediate disposal in a sharps container. Adverse events following immunization are recorded, reported, and investigated, and the nurse should be able to distinguish the common local reaction from the rare serious event. The nurse who answers a hesitant parent with patience changes the vaccination status of the whole family.

Every medicine in this chapter has adverse effects that the nurse is positioned to catch first. Quinine causes low blood glucose, ringing in the ears, and impaired vision, and artesunate can cause a delayed destruction of red cells that appears 1 to 3 weeks after treatment, so a falling hemoglobin after apparent recovery is not a new disease. Antimalarial medicines of the primaquine family and some others cause hemolysis in a child with glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency, a genetic trait common in tropical populations, so the deficiency should be known before such a drug is used. Ivermectin should be avoided in a child who is very small or very ill, and diethylcarbamazine can provoke a brisk reaction as dying parasites release their contents. The nurse documents the drug, the dose, the time, and the reaction, and reports it through the national system, because that report protects the next child. Monitoring is not an extra task, and it is the other half of prescribing.

The antenatal visit is where most of this chapter's prevention actually happens, and its content is more than a blood pressure and a urine test. Current guidance from the World Health Organization recommends at least 8 antenatal contacts, with the first in the first trimester, so that problems are found early and the woman has time to act on advice. Each contact includes blood pressure, weight, and urine testing, and screening for HIV and syphilis, because treating those infections protects the infant as well as the mother. Iron and folic acid are given daily, usually 60 milligrams of elemental iron with 400 micrograms of folic acid, and tetanus vaccination is completed according to the woman's prior doses. An insecticide-treated bed net is provided, and intermittent preventive treatment for malaria is given from the second trimester. Deworming with a single dose of albendazole at 400 milligrams or mebendazole at 500 milligrams is offered after the first trimester in areas where soil-transmitted helminths are common.

The first hours of life are a second window of prevention, and the nurse's hands do most of the work. The newborn is dried immediately and thoroughly, placed skin to skin on the mother's chest, and covered, because keeping the baby warm prevents a cascade of problems in the first day. The cord is clamped and cut with clean equipment, and clamping is delayed for 1 to 3 minutes to allow the transfer of extra blood and iron. Breastfeeding is started within the first hour, and the baby is fed only breast milk for the first 6 months, which protects against diarrhea and pneumonia. The cord stump is kept clean and dry, and in settings with high neonatal mortality, chlorhexidine is applied to it to reduce infection. Before discharge, the nurse teaches the mother the danger signs of the newborn, poor feeding, fast breathing, fever, low temperature, jaundice, and convulsions, and arranges the first follow-up visit.

To summarize, pregnancy, the newborn period, and childhood are the stages when tropical infection does its greatest harm and when nursing care does its greatest good. The placenta concentrates malaria parasites, so a pregnant woman can be anemic and her infant small or stillborn even when her blood film is negative, and prevention rests on the insecticide-treated bed net, intermittent preventive treatment, and prompt diagnosis and treatment. Dengue threatens the pregnant woman with severe plasma leakage, Zika virus attacks the developing brain, Trypanosoma cruzi passes to the fetus in every pregnancy of an infected woman, the medicines for lymphatic filariasis are deferred until after delivery, and neonatal tetanus is prevented by maternal vaccination and clean cord care. A child is not a small adult, so every dose is calculated from a measured weight, checked independently, and never guessed from an adult tablet. The integrated management of childhood illness approach, fever care, nutritional assessment, oral rehydration salts with zinc, and vaccination carry most children through the dangerous years. Antenatal care and the care of the newborn are the interventions that prevent the rest. The nurse who measures, calculates, monitors, and teaches is the reason these children survive.
