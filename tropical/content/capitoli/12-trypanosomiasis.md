# Chapter 12 — Trypanosomiasis: Chagas Disease and Human African Trypanosomiasis

Trypanosomiasis is a single word for two diseases that share a genus of parasite and almost nothing else, and the nurse who keeps them apart from the first sentence will find the rest of this chapter far easier to hold in mind. Chagas disease is caused by the protozoan parasite Trypanosoma cruzi, it is transmitted by blood-sucking bugs of the subfamily Triatominae, and it belongs to the Americas, where it has infected an estimated 6 to 7 million people and causes thousands of deaths every year. Human African trypanosomiasis, long known to the public as sleeping sickness, is caused by Trypanosoma brucei gambiense in West and Central Africa and by Trypanosoma brucei rhodesiense in East and Southern Africa, it is transmitted by the tsetse fly, and it kills within weeks to months when it is not treated. Both diseases appear on the list of neglected tropical diseases maintained by the World Health Organization, and both are now the subject of serious elimination efforts that have changed their epidemiology within a single generation. This chapter takes each disease in turn. It follows the parasite and its vector first, then the stages of illness, then the laboratory work that tells the clinician which stage is present, then the medicines with their genuine dangers, and finally the nursing work that carries a patient through years of surveillance or months of treatment.

Trypanosoma cruzi is a protozoan with a life cycle that requires two hosts, an insect and a mammal, and the shape the parasite takes depends on where it happens to be. Inside the gut of the triatomine bug it multiplies as an epimastigote, a slender form with a flagellum that runs along the body and extends beyond it. When the bug takes a blood meal, it deposits feces on the skin, and the infective form waiting in those feces is the trypomastigote. The bite itself does not transmit the parasite. The person scratches the itch, and the feces are rubbed into the small wound, or into the conjunctiva, or into another mucous membrane, and the trypomastigotes enter the cells beneath. Inside the host cell they transform once more into amastigotes, the round forms that divide until the cell bursts and releases parasites that invade neighboring cells and, in time, reach the bloodstream. That last detail governs everything that follows clinically, because a parasite that hides inside cells and appears in the blood only intermittently is difficult to find by microscopy and is never fully cleared by the immune response. The chronic infection is therefore not a memory of an old illness. It is a living infection that persists for decades.

The vector is a triatomine bug, known in English as the kissing bug and in Latin America by many local names, and its habits explain why the disease is a disease of the rural poor. Triatomines are large, brown or black insects, 2 to 3 centimeters long, with a cone-shaped head and a distinctive pattern at the edge of the abdomen. They feed at night, they take a large blood meal that leaves them sluggish, and they commonly defecate while they are still on the skin, which is precisely how the parasite is delivered. Their bite is usually painless, so most patients have no memory of being bitten. They live in the cracks of mud walls, in thatched roofs, in the space behind a hanging picture, in dark corners, in animal pens, and in the crowns of palm trees. They come from a sylvatic cycle maintained by opossums and armadillos and rodents, and they have adapted to the domestic cycle maintained by dogs, cats, guinea pigs, and people. Control rests on the house rather than on the patient. Indoor residual spraying, plastering of walls to close the cracks, replacement of thatched roofs, and removal of animal shelters from the living space are the measures that have interrupted transmission across much of South America.

The burden of Chagas disease has a shape that has changed in the past thirty years, and migration is the reason. The disease is endemic in 21 countries of Latin America, from Mexico to Argentina and Chile, and the World Health Organization estimates that about 70 million people remain at risk of infection on that continent. Transmission by the vector has been interrupted or sharply reduced in Uruguay, Chile, Brazil, and several other countries, so new infections within those borders are now uncommon. Millions of people who were infected decades ago, however, remain infected for life, and many of them have moved north or across the ocean. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention estimates that more than 300,000 people with Chagas disease now live in the United States, and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control has documented tens of thousands of cases across Europe, concentrated where migrants from Latin America have settled. This is why the diagnosis must be considered in a patient who has never seen a triatomine bug. A woman who arrived from Bolivia or El Salvador as a child may carry the parasite for the rest of her life, transmit it to a pregnancy, and be told for years that her palpitations are anxiety.

The acute phase begins 5 to 14 days after vector transmission, or 20 to 40 days after transmission by contaminated food, and it lasts 4 to 8 weeks. In the majority of patients, especially adults, it passes almost unnoticed, with fever, malaise, headache, muscle aches, and a vague rash. When the parasite enters through the conjunctiva, the eyelid on that side becomes swollen and the eye weeps, a finding called the Romaña sign, and it is one of the few features that make the diagnosis obvious at the bedside. When it enters through the skin, a red indurated nodule called a chagoma may appear at the site. Children are more often symptomatic than adults, and they may develop enlargement of the liver, the spleen, and the lymph nodes. A small proportion develop severe acute disease, with myocarditis, pericardial effusion, or meningoencephalitis, and these patients can die within days. Infection in the newborn is especially dangerous, and it may present as prematurity, low birth weight, fever, jaundice, enlargement of the liver and spleen, and swelling of the whole body. Even when the acute illness is silent, it ends in the same place, which is a lifelong infection with the parasite held in balance by an immune response that cannot eliminate it.

The indeterminate phase is the long middle of Chagas disease, and it is where most patients spend most of their lives. It is defined by a positive serological test together with a normal physical examination, a normal electrocardiogram, a normal chest radiograph, and normal contrast studies of the esophagus and the colon. The patient feels well, works, eats, and sleeps without difficulty, and no medicine that can be offered in this period removes the parasite with certainty. The parasite persists in the tissues of the heart, the skeletal muscle, the smooth muscle of the digestive tract, and the autonomic nerves. The immune response keeps the parasite load low and the inflammation in check, and this uneasy equilibrium can hold for 10 to 30 years. Between 60 and 70 per cent of people in the indeterminate phase never develop clinically apparent disease at all. The remaining 30 to 40 per cent progress, at a rate that varies with age, sex, geography, the strain of the parasite, and the strength of the host response. The nurse who explains this phase honestly, as a long watch rather than a sentence of death, gives the patient something valuable and true.

Cardiac Chagas disease is the form that kills, and it begins with the electrical wiring of the heart. Inflammatory destruction of the conduction system and of the working muscle produces fibrosis that replaces conducting tissue with scar. The most characteristic finding on the electrocardiogram is a block of the right bundle branch together with a block of the left anterior fascicle, and the combination in a young man from an endemic area is almost diagnostic. Complete heart block, sick sinus syndrome, and ventricular arrhythmias follow, and they are the substrate for sudden death, which may be the first event of the disease. The left ventricle dilates, the apex thins into an aneurysm, and a mural thrombus may form and embolize to the brain, which is a common cause of stroke in young patients from Latin America. Heart failure develops with a prognosis worse than that of heart failure from other common causes. An implantable defibrillator is used in selected patients with dangerous ventricular rhythms, and anticoagulation is considered when a thrombus or an arrhythmia is present. Chagas disease also reactivates in patients who become immunosuppressed, especially with HIV infection or after organ transplantation, and it then produces fever, skin lesions, myocarditis, and meningoencephalitis rather than a quiet chronic state.

Digestive Chagas disease comes from the same destructive process in a different tissue, and it is less common than the cardiac form but deeply disabling. The parasite and the immune response destroy the neurons of the intramuscular nerve networks that control peristalsis, so the esophagus and the colon lose their ability to propel their contents forward. The esophagus dilates into a megaesophagus, and the patient reports difficulty swallowing solid food, a sensation of food sticking, regurgitation at night, foul breath, and weight loss. Aspiration of retained food during sleep can cause repeated pneumonia. The colon dilates into a megacolon, and the patient reports severe constipation, abdominal distension, and pain, and fecal impaction and volvulus can develop. Treatment of the achalasia may begin with balloon dilation and may end in surgery, and the megacolon often requires resection when it becomes intolerable. The nursing work is practical and unglamorous. It includes teaching patients to eat slowly, to chew thoroughly, to take smaller meals, to elevate the head of the bed, to record bowel movements, and to recognize the warning signs of obstruction before they become emergencies.

Congenital transmission is the route that keeps Chagas disease alive in countries where the insect has been controlled, and it deserves a place at the front of every nurse's mind. An infected mother can transmit the parasite across the placenta in roughly 1 to 5 per cent of pregnancies, and the risk is repeated with each pregnancy she has, so a woman with four children may have transmitted the infection to one of them. The World Health Organization recommends that pregnant women from endemic areas be tested, and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control urges the same for pregnant women who have lived in Latin America. The newborn should be examined for parasites in the blood by microscopy or by PCR early in life, and if those tests are negative the child should be tested by serology at 9 to 12 months, because antibody from the mother persists for months and a positive result before that time proves only that the mother is infected. Treatment of an infected infant is highly effective and can cure the infection outright, which is why the screening loop must actually be closed. A missed diagnosis in a newborn becomes a case of heart failure at the age of 40, and the nurse who arranges the test, records the result, and confirms that the family returns for the second test at 9 months is preventing a death half a century away.

Three other routes of transmission have to be remembered, because each of them can carry the infection into a person who has never been near the vector. Transfusion of infected blood was once the second most common route in Latin America, and it has been largely controlled there by universal screening of donated blood, though the risk persists wherever donors from endemic areas are not screened. Organ transplantation from an infected donor can transmit the parasite to a recipient whose immune system is then suppressed, producing severe disease, so donors with epidemiological risk should be tested. Oral transmission has gained importance in the Amazon basin, where contaminated food and drink, particularly the pulp of the açaí palm, sugarcane juice, and other freshly prepared fruit beverages, have caused outbreaks and family clusters. The parasite load in food-borne infection is higher than in vector-borne infection, and the acute illness is correspondingly more severe, with fever, vomiting, diarrhea, jaundice, and cardiac involvement. Laboratory accident and needlestick injury are rare additional routes, and they are managed like any other exposure, with prompt testing and specialist advice.

Diagnosis depends on which phase of the infection is present, because the parasite is abundant in the blood in one phase and nearly invisible in the next. During the acute phase, and in congenital infection and in reactivation, the trypomastigotes can be found by direct microscopy of a stained blood film or of the buffy coat, and PCR is more sensitive and is the method of choice in the newborn. In the chronic phase the parasite is rarely detectable in blood, and the diagnosis rests on serology. Current guidance requires two different serological tests, for example an enzyme immunoassay and an indirect immunofluorescence assay or an immunoblot, and the diagnosis is confirmed only when both are positive, because a single test produces false positive results in people with other infections and in people with autoimmune disease. PCR can detect intermittent parasitemia in chronically infected patients, but a negative PCR does not exclude the diagnosis. Blood banks in endemic countries screen every donation, and this screening has become a powerful surveillance tool in countries of migration. The nurse's part is to draw the correct tubes, to label them with the date and the clinical question, and to make sure the two serological tests are actually sent rather than one.

Treatment with benznidazole is the standard first choice in most settings, and it is a medicine that requires patient education as much as it requires a prescription. Benznidazole is a nitroimidazole that is activated inside the parasite into reactive compounds that damage its genetic material, and it is given by mouth at 5 to 7 milligrams per kilogram per day in two divided doses for 60 days. Food improves absorption and reduces nausea, so the nurse advises the patient to take each dose with a meal. The most common adverse effect is a skin rash, which may be mild and manageable with an antihistamine or may become a widespread eruption with fever, swollen lymph nodes, and involvement of the mucous membranes, in which case the drug must be stopped at once and urgent medical review arranged. Peripheral neuropathy begins as tingling in the hands and feet and can become permanent if the drug is continued. Bone marrow suppression, nausea, and headache also occur, so blood counts and clinical review are part of any course. Adherence to 60 days of a drug that makes the patient feel worse is the central challenge, and the nurse who warns about the rash in advance, who treats the nausea seriously, and who stays in contact by telephone is the person who keeps the course on track.

Nifurtimox is the alternative, and the two drugs are used according to national availability and national protocol rather than according to habit. Nifurtimox is a nitrofuran, and it is given by mouth at 8 to 10 milligrams per kilogram per day in three or four divided doses for 60 to 90 days, with higher weight-based doses used in children and lower doses used in adults. Its adverse effects are more often gastrointestinal and neurological than dermatological. Patients report nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, loss of appetite, and weight loss, and they report insomnia, irritability, difficulty concentrating, forgetfulness, and occasionally agitation or a frank psychotic reaction, especially in older patients and in those receiving high doses. Tingling and numbness of the extremities occur as they do with benznidazole. The decision about who should be treated has become more nuanced in the past decade. Treatment is clearly indicated for acute infection, congenital infection, infection in children, and chronic infection in adults under about 50 years of age who have the indeterminate form or only mild cardiac disease, and it is offered to women of childbearing age before pregnancy because the drug can prevent later transmission. Treatment is not given during pregnancy, is avoided in severe renal or hepatic impairment, and is of uncertain benefit in patients with advanced cardiomyopathy, in whom the parasite has already caused damage that antiparasitic treatment cannot reverse. Guidance on the late chronic form is being revised as new trial results appear, so the current recommendation must be checked for each patient.

No regimen in this chapter should ever be given from memory. Doses, durations, and eligibility criteria change as trials are published and as national programs revise their protocols, and the same disease is managed differently in Bolivia, in Brazil, in Spain, and in the United States. Guidance from the World Health Organization and from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention is revised on its own schedule and differs between the countries in which a nurse may practice, so the current national guideline and the current product label must be checked before any antiparasitic medicine is prescribed, dispensed, or administered. The dose is calculated from the weight actually measured that day and adjusted for the renal and hepatic function and the blood counts actually measured that week. A patient who has lost several kilograms during the acute illness may need a smaller dose than the one written at admission, and a patient who has gained weight during a long course may need a larger one. That is why the nurse reweighs the patient at every visit, reviews the latest laboratory results before the next dose is given, and treats every rash, every tingling finger, and every falling white cell count as information that may change the prescription.

The nursing care of a person with Chagas disease extends far beyond dispensing tablets. The nurse explains the difference between the acute, indeterminate, and chronic phases in plain language, because a patient who understands that he may remain well for decades is far more likely to accept follow-up than one who believes the diagnosis guarantees heart failure. The nurse arranges annual cardiac review with an electrocardiogram and, when the findings suggest structural disease, an echocardiogram, and teaches the patient the symptoms that require immediate care, which are fainting, palpitations that do not stop, chest pain, and sudden breathlessness. The nurse counsels the household about the vector, checks that spraying has been carried out, and urges that family members be tested, because an entire household may be infected. The nurse supports adherence through the nausea and the rash, and screens for depression and anxiety, which are common in a chronic diagnosis that carries stigma. When a patient with Chagas disease becomes pregnant, the nurse arranges maternal testing, infant testing at birth and at 9 to 12 months, and coordination with the pediatric team. Migrant patients also need an explanation of how the diagnosis will be handled in a country whose health system may not know the disease, and a written summary of the serology results to carry.

Human African trypanosomiasis begins on a different continent with a different insect, and the contrast with Chagas disease is instructive. Two subspecies of Trypanosoma brucei cause it, and they produce two different illnesses in two different parts of Africa. Trypanosoma brucei gambiense is found in West and Central Africa, it accounts for about 95 per cent of all cases, it is maintained largely in people, and it produces a chronic illness that can take months to years to reach the brain. Trypanosoma brucei rhodesiense is found in East and Southern Africa, it is maintained in cattle and wild game as well as in people, and it produces an acute illness that can reach the brain within weeks. Both are transmitted by tsetse flies of the genus Glossina. The disease has caused catastrophic epidemics in the twentieth century, particularly in the Congo basin, in Uganda, and in Angola, and as recently as the late 1990s tens of thousands of cases were being reported each year. The World Health Organization has recorded fewer than 1,000 reported cases per year since 2018, which is a historic achievement built on active case finding, vector control, and better medicines.

The tsetse fly is a large, brown, biting fly with a crossed-wing resting posture, and its biology limits where the disease can occur. Both male and female flies take blood meals from people and animals in the daytime, and their bite is painful enough that patients often remember it. The parasite does not simply ride on the fly's mouthparts. It multiplies inside the fly and migrates to the salivary glands, so a fly must remain infected for weeks before it can transmit the parasite, and this biological transmission means that the disease cannot spread explosively the way a mosquito-borne virus can. Different species of tsetse occupy riverine and savanna habitats, and people who fish, farm, collect water, or hunt near those habitats are the ones at risk. Control has moved away from the indiscriminate spraying of the past and now relies on insecticide-treated traps and screens, on the treatment of cattle with insecticide, and on the clearing of the vegetation in which the flies rest. There is no vaccine and no chemoprophylaxis, so prevention is a matter of clothing, repellent, and the avoidance of tsetse habitat during the hours of the day when the fly feeds.

The first stage of the infection begins at the site of the bite and then spreads through the blood and the lymph. A painful, indurated, red swelling called a chancre appears at the bite site after an incubation period of about 5 to 21 days in rhodesiense infection, and it is more often noticed in travelers and in patients with the East African form than in patients with the West African form, in whom the incubation period is longer and the onset is more insidious. The chancre lasts 2 to 3 weeks and then resolves, and the parasite moves to the hemolymphatic stage. The patient develops fever, severe headache, joint and muscle pain, malaise, and weight loss. The lymph nodes enlarge, and the enlargement of the nodes along the posterior edge of the neck, called the Winterbottom sign, is a classic finding in the West African form. A transient rash, itching, and painless swelling of the face and hands may appear. In rhodesiense infection the heart may be involved early, with arrhythmia, pericardial effusion, and heart failure, and jaundice and bleeding may occur. In gambiense infection the same stage is far quieter, and the patient may carry intermittent fever for months while continuing to work, which is why the diagnosis is often delayed until the parasite reaches the brain.

Sleep is disturbed in this disease in a way that is unlike ordinary insomnia, and the disturbance is so characteristic that it gave the illness its common name. The parasite crosses from the blood into the central nervous system, and the normal daily rhythm of sleep and wakefulness is dismantled. The patient is drowsy during the day and cannot stay awake at work, then lies awake at night, sometimes with an urgent desire to move about. The pattern becomes fragmented, with brief episodes of sleep appearing at any hour and vivid dreams, confusion on waking, and a reversal of the usual cycle. Family members describe personality change before the patient does, with irritability, apathy, loss of concentration, and behavior that seems out of character. The disturbance is not caused by fever alone. It reflects inflammation in the regions of the brain that govern the sleep cycle and the hormonal signals that regulate it, and it progresses as the neurological stage advances. A patient from an endemic area with daytime somnolence, night-time wakefulness, and a change in personality has a diagnosis that must be considered urgently, not a psychiatric problem to be managed with reassurance.

The neurological stage is the stage that kills, and its features come from widespread inflammation of the brain and its membranes. The patient develops tremor of the hands, tongue, and eyelids, unsteadiness of gait, slurred speech, and difficulty with fine movements. Seizures may occur, and abnormal involuntary movements appear. The patient may complain of intense pain on a light squeeze of the muscle, a finding called the Kerandel sign, and of tingling and numbness in the limbs. Psychiatric features are common and include apathy, agitation, hallucinations, and frank psychosis, and they may lead the clinician to the wrong diagnosis. As the disease advances, the patient becomes increasingly withdrawn, then stuporous, and finally comatose, and death follows from aspiration, seizures, malnutrition, sepsis, or heart failure. The speed of the progression is the main difference between the two forms. In rhodesiense infection the neurological stage may arrive within weeks of the first fever and the patient may die within months, whereas in gambiense infection it may take a year or more. Age, nutrition, and the delay before treatment all influence the outcome, and the neurological damage that is present at the start of treatment may not be fully reversible.

Before any treatment is chosen, the stage of the disease must be established by examination of the cerebrospinal fluid, because the medicines that treat the blood stage do not enter the brain in adequate amounts and the medicines that treat the brain stage are dangerous. A lumbar puncture is therefore mandatory in every patient with confirmed human African trypanosomiasis, and the result determines the drug. The cerebrospinal fluid is examined for the number of white cells, for the protein concentration, and for the parasite itself. Current World Health Organization criteria define the second stage for the West African form as a white cell count greater than 5 per microliter, or the presence of trypanosomes in the fluid, and the protein concentration is also considered. The thresholds used in different countries vary, and the count must be interpreted with the clinical picture. A follow-up lumbar puncture is repeated at intervals after treatment, commonly at 6 months and at 12 months or later, to define cure or relapse, and treatment failure is diagnosed by a rising cell count or the reappearance of parasites. The nurse's role in this procedure is substantial. The nurse explains why the test is necessary, checks that coagulation is safe, positions the patient correctly, keeps the patient flat afterward to reduce the headache that follows the procedure, monitors the puncture site and the level of consciousness, and ensures that the fluid reaches the laboratory promptly with the correct labels.

Pentamidine is the treatment for the first stage of the West African form, and it is given at 4 milligrams per kilogram per day intramuscularly or intravenously for 7 to 10 days. The drug concentrates in the tissues where the parasite lives and interferes with its energy metabolism and its genetic material. Its dangers are mainly metabolic and cardiovascular. Pentamidine can cause a sharp fall in blood pressure during or shortly after the injection, so the patient should lie down while the dose is given and the blood pressure should be checked afterward. It can cause hypoglycemia followed later by hyperglycemia and even diabetes, which means that blood glucose must be measured before and during treatment. It can prolong the electrical recovery of the heart and provoke dangerous rhythms, so an electrocardiogram is taken before treatment and repeated during it. Pain at the injection site and sterile abscess formation are common with the intramuscular route, so the nurse rotates the sites and inspects them daily. Renal function is checked, because the drug is cleared by the kidney, and the dose must be reviewed when renal function changes.

Suramin is the treatment for the first stage of the East African form, and it is given intravenously after a small test dose to detect a hypersensitivity reaction. The usual adult schedule is 1 gram intravenously on the first day and then on the third, fifth, and seventh days, followed by weekly doses to complete 5 to 6 doses in total, with children dosed by weight. Suramin binds to proteins in the parasite and interferes with its metabolism, and it does not cross into the brain, which is why it is useless once the second stage has begun. It can cause fever, chills, urticaria, and a general feeling of illness, especially after the first dose. It is toxic to the kidney, and the urine must be examined for protein and casts before every dose, with treatment withheld if new protein appears. It can damage the bone marrow and the nerves, and it can cause optic atrophy with loss of vision, which is rare but irreversible. Suramin also kills the adult worms of Onchocerca volvulus and can provoke a severe reaction in a patient who is co-infected, so the risk of onchocerciasis must be assessed before it is given. The nurse who checks the urine before each dose and who asks specifically about changes in vision is performing a task that protects the patient from permanent harm.

Melarsoprol is an organic arsenic compound that has treated the second stage of the disease for more than 70 years, and it remains in use for the East African form because no safe alternative has fully replaced it. It is given intravenously at 2.2 milligrams per kilogram per day for 10 days, and some protocols use a longer or repeated schedule, so the current national guideline must be consulted for the exact regimen. It is the most toxic medicine in this chapter. The most feared complication is a reactive encephalopathy, which occurs in about 5 to 10 per cent of treated patients and which can be fatal, and it is caused by the inflammatory response to parasites dying in the brain rather than by the arsenic alone. Corticosteroids are used in some protocols to reduce that reaction, but practice varies and the evidence is debated. The injection itself is painful and can damage the vein, so it is given through a well-placed cannula, and the arm is watched for thrombophlebitis. Other adverse effects include peripheral neuropathy, skin reactions, and myocardial damage. The nurse who administers melarsoprol must be prepared for a sudden deterioration in consciousness, seizures, or a focal neurological deficit, and must have the response plan, the corticosteroids, and the clinician at hand before the dose is given rather than after the deterioration begins.

For the second stage of the West African form, the modern treatment is a combination of eflornithine and nifurtimox, which has replaced prolonged eflornithine alone and shortened treatment from 14 days to 7 days of infusions. Eflornithine is given intravenously at 400 milligrams per kilogram per day in two divided doses for 7 days, and nifurtimox is given by mouth at 15 milligrams per kilogram per day in three divided doses for 10 days. Eflornithine blocks an enzyme that the parasite needs to make essential fatty molecules, a target that is absent in the human host, which is one reason the drug is relatively well tolerated. The adverse effects that matter are suppression of the bone marrow, with a fall in white cells and platelets, seizures, hypertension, hearing loss, nausea, and vomiting, and the nifurtimox adds its own gastrointestinal and neuropsychiatric effects. Blood counts and clinical review are therefore part of every course, and a patient who develops a fever during treatment needs immediate assessment because the white cell count may be dangerously low. The nursing workload is heavy, because two intravenous infusions a day must be given on schedule through a reliable line, the patient must be well hydrated, and the oral doses must be coordinated with the infusions. Even so, the combination is far safer than melarsoprol, and its introduction changed the prognosis of second-stage disease in Central Africa.

Fexinidazole is the most important recent advance, because it is the first oral medicine that treats both stages of the West African form in many patients, and it has turned a disease of infusions and injections into a disease that can be treated with tablets. The standard adult regimen is 1,800 milligrams by mouth once a day for 4 days, followed by 1,200 milligrams once a day for 6 days, with lower weight-based doses for children and for adults below a defined weight, and the drug is taken with food to improve absorption. It is effective in first-stage disease and in second-stage disease when the cerebrospinal fluid white cell count is below 100 per microliter and the patient is not severely ill, so the lumbar puncture remains essential to decide whether it can be used. It is not recommended for the East African form, for patients with severe second-stage disease, or for pregnant women and young children outside the studied age ranges, and these limits are revised as evidence accumulates. The adverse effects include vomiting, headache, insomnia, tremor, anxiety, and prolongation of the electrical recovery of the heart, so patients are observed during treatment. Fexinidazole can be given in a peripheral health center rather than a hospital, which is exactly why it has accelerated the elimination effort, and the nurse who supervises the 10-day course, confirms that each dose is swallowed, and follows the patient afterward is doing work that was previously impossible outside a referral center.

Once again, no dose in this chapter should be given from memory. The World Health Organization publishes treatment guidance for human African trypanosomiasis and revises it as fexinidazole and newer compounds are studied, national programs adapt those recommendations to their own supply and surveillance systems, and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention advises clinicians in the United States, where the disease is rare and always imported. The choice among pentamidine, suramin, melarsoprol, the eflornithine and nifurtimox combination, and fexinidazole depends on the subspecies, the stage, the cerebrospinal fluid result, the weight, the pregnancy status, the renal and hepatic function, and the medicine that is actually in the cupboard that week. The nurse must therefore confirm the current national guideline and the current product label before the first dose, verify the weight and the laboratory results on the day, and check every dose against the written order with a second person when the protocol requires it. Adverse effect surveillance is continuous rather than occasional. Blood glucose, blood pressure, cardiac rhythm, urine protein, blood counts, hearing, vision, and the level of consciousness are all monitored at intervals set by the drug being used, and the nurse records them so that a trend is visible before a crisis arrives.

The progress toward elimination is one of the genuine successes of tropical medicine, and it deserves to be told with precision. Reported cases of human African trypanosomiasis fell from more than 25,000 in 2000 to fewer than 1,000 per year since 2018, and several countries have reported no new cases for years, which has moved the World Health Organization target of eliminating the West African form as a public health problem by 2030 within reach. The tools that produced this result are unspectacular. Mobile teams travel to remote villages and screen entire populations with a card agglutination test for trypanosomiasis, followed by confirmation and staging, and they treat everyone who is infected, which reduces the human reservoir from which tsetse flies become infected. Insecticide-treated traps and screens are placed around villages and along riverbanks, cattle are treated with insecticide, and patients are followed for years to detect relapse. The remaining cases cluster in areas affected by conflict, in remote forest and riverine communities, and among people who move across borders, so the last stretch is the hardest and demands sustained surveillance rather than a final campaign. Chagas disease has its own version of this story, with vector transmission interrupted in several countries, blood supplies screened, and a growing program of congenital screening in both endemic and non-endemic countries.

Prevention and public health close the chapter, and they look different for the two diseases. There is no vaccine against either parasite and no chemoprophylaxis for travelers, so prevention of human African trypanosomiasis rests on avoiding tsetse habitat, wearing long sleeves and long trousers in the colors that attract the fly least, using repellent, and sleeping under a net in areas where the flies rest indoors, together with the community-level traps, screens, and insecticide treatment that reduce the fly population. Prevention of Chagas disease rests on the house and the blood supply. Indoor residual spraying, plastering of cracked walls, replacement of thatched roofs, separation of animal quarters from living quarters, and the use of bed nets in endemic areas reduce domestic transmission, while universal screening of blood donors and of organ donors prevents transmission through medical care. Screening of pregnant women and of infants, and treatment of those found infected, prevents the next generation of cases. The nurse who works in a migrant clinic performs the whole of this prevention in miniature by taking an exposure history, ordering the right serology, arranging infant testing, counseling the household, and making sure the result reaches the patient and the family rather than remaining in a file. Travel advice for the visitor to Latin America or to the African savanna is simple and honest, but the lasting work belongs to the residents of endemic communities and to the health workers who stay with them.

To summarize, trypanosomiasis is two distinct diseases caused by two species of the same genus, and treating them as one would be a clinical error. Chagas disease from Trypanosoma cruzi is transmitted by triatomine bugs, by blood, by organs, by contaminated food, and from mother to child, and it passes through an acute phase, an indeterminate phase that can last for decades, and a chronic phase that damages the conduction system of the heart and the motor nerves of the esophagus and the colon. Diagnosis in the chronic phase rests on two different serological tests, treatment with benznidazole or nifurtimox is most effective in the young and in the recently infected, and no medicine reverses established heart failure. Human African trypanosomiasis from Trypanosoma brucei gambiense or rhodesiense is transmitted by the tsetse fly, produces a chancre and then a hemolymphatic stage with fever and lymphadenopathy, and progresses to disturbed sleep and then to a meningoencephalitis that kills if it is untreated. The stage must be established by lumbar puncture, because pentamidine and suramin treat only the blood stage while eflornithine with nifurtimox, melarsoprol, and now oral fexinidazole treat the brain, and each carries its own serious adverse effects. For both diseases, the nurse's contribution is the same in spirit, which is to recognize the exposure, secure the correct test, support an uncomfortable and lengthy treatment, monitor the toxicity of dangerous medicines with real vigilance, and close the loops of screening, follow-up, and prevention that determine whether a curable infection becomes a chronic or fatal one.
