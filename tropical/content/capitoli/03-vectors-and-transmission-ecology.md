# Chapter 3 — Vectors and the Ecology of Transmission

A vector is a living organism that carries an infectious agent from one host to another, and in tropical medicine the word nearly always means an arthropod. Not every biting insect is a vector, and not every vector is competent for the pathogen at hand. Competence is a property of a specific pairing between an arthropod species, a pathogen, and a set of environmental conditions. A mosquito that feeds readily on human blood may still be incapable of supporting the development of a parasite it takes up in a meal. The pathogen must survive the arthropod's gut, multiply inside its body, and reach the salivary glands before the next bite can transmit anything. Every one of those requirements is temperature-sensitive, which is the biological reason that transmission has seasons and altitude limits. A species that is a competent vector at 28 degrees Celsius may be an incompetent one at 18 degrees Celsius, because the pathogen inside it develops too slowly to finish before the insect dies.

Epidemiologists compress these requirements into a measure called vectorial capacity, which depends less on how many vectors exist than on how often they bite people and how long they live. Long-lived, human-loving, frequently biting mosquitoes transmit efficiently, and a species with those traits maintains transmission even when its population is small. This is why counting mosquitoes in a trap is a poor direct measure of human risk. A village with many mosquitoes of a species that prefers cattle may be safer than a village with few mosquitoes of a species that prefers people. Survivorship matters more than abundance, because an infected mosquito must outlive the incubation period and then survive long enough to take a second blood meal. When a program reports success, the underlying claim is usually that daily vector survival has fallen, not that trap counts are lower.

The mosquitoes matter more than any other group of vectors, and they are usefully organized by genus because the genus predicts both the disease and the biting habit. Anopheles transmits malaria. Aedes transmits dengue, chikungunya, Zika and yellow fever. Culex occupies the middle ground and carries lymphatic filariasis, Japanese encephalitis and West Nile virus in various settings. These distinctions are clinical as well as entomological, because knowing which genus is active near a patient's home tells you when and where that patient was most likely exposed. A fever that began after a week of daytime gardening points toward Aedes, while a fever that began two weeks after sleeping unprotected near a river points toward Anopheles.

Anopheles mosquitoes bite chiefly between dusk and dawn, with peak activity in the hours around midnight and again near dawn in many settings. They rest indoors after feeding, on interior walls and under roofs, and they prefer human blood where that choice is available. Their larvae develop in clean or only lightly polluted standing water, so a river's edge, a rice paddy, a rain-filled tire rut, or a footprint in damp ground can all serve as habitat. The female must take a blood meal before she can lay eggs, and she returns repeatedly across her life, which is how a single mosquito infects several people. Because so much feeding and resting happens inside a house at night, the house itself becomes the point of attack. This is the biology behind the insecticide-treated bed net and behind indoor residual spraying.

Aedes mosquitoes behave almost as the opposite of Anopheles in the ways that matter for prevention. Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus bite during daylight, with sharp peaks in the early morning and late afternoon, and they feed indoors or outdoors without hesitation. They breed in small containers close to human dwellings rather than in marshes or ponds. Discarded tires, water storage drums, flower vases, roof gutters, bottle caps, and the water-holding axils of plants all support their larvae. Aedes aegypti is highly adapted to people and can complete its entire life cycle inside a dense city, which is why dengue surges in urban neighborhoods with no surface water at all. Their habit of taking several partial blood meals from several different people in one day makes them extraordinarily efficient amplifiers of a virus, and because they bite by day, a bed net that protects against malaria does very little against them.

Culex mosquitoes occupy an intermediate position and are the group most people overlook. Many species bite in the evening and through the night, outdoors as well as indoors, and several breed in water that is heavily polluted with organic matter. That preference ties them to the places where drainage fails, latrines overflow, and refuse collects standing water. Culex quinquefasciatus, the southern house mosquito, is the principal vector of lymphatic filariasis in urban and peri-urban settings across much of the tropics. Other Culex species transmit Japanese encephalitis in Asia, where the virus cycles between birds and pigs and spills into people near flooded rice fields. West Nile virus also moves through Culex mosquitoes, with birds as the amplifying host. Because Culex rests and breeds where Aedes and Anopheles do not, a program aimed at one genus leaves the others undisturbed.

Sandflies are small, hairy, moth-like flies of the genus Phlebotomus in the Old World and the genus Lutzomyia in the New World, and they transmit the leishmaniases. They are far smaller than mosquitoes, they fly weakly and only short distances, and they feed at dusk and through the night. Their silence and their size mean that many patients never recall being bitten, which is a common reason that a leishmaniasis diagnosis feels inexplicable to the person who has it. Sandfly larvae develop in cracks in walls, in rodent burrows, in animal sheds, and in moist organic debris, so the habitat is often the immediate domestic environment rather than a distant breeding ground. The clinical form depends on the species of Leishmania, ranging from a chronic skin ulcer to destructive mucosal disease to fatal visceral infection. Cutaneous disease is often mistaken for an infected wound and treated with antibiotics for weeks before the correct diagnosis is considered.

Tsetse flies, genus Glossina, transmit the trypanosomes that cause human African trypanosomiasis, also called sleeping sickness. They are large, dark, biting flies that feed during the day in shaded woodland, gallery forest, and along rivers, and both sexes take blood meals. Their bite is painful enough that patients often remember it, and a characteristic chancre may appear at the site. Tsetse flies are unusual among vectors in that they give birth to live larvae one at a time rather than laying eggs, which keeps their reproductive rate low. That low rate is a genuine vulnerability and helps explain why intensive trapping and insecticide-treated targets can suppress transmission. The East African form caused by Trypanosoma brucei rhodesiense and the West African form caused by Trypanosoma brucei gambiense progress at very different speeds. Both are fatal without treatment, which makes suspicion and early referral the nurse's most important contribution to their control.

Triatomine bugs, also called kissing bugs or cone-nosed bugs, transmit Trypanosoma cruzi, the agent of Chagas disease. They live in the cracks of poorly constructed houses, in thatched roofs, in mud walls, and behind pictures and furniture, emerging at night to feed on sleeping people. The bug usually defecates on the skin as it feeds, and the parasite enters when the sleeping person scratches the bite or carries the feces to a mucous membrane. Infection therefore is not caused by the bite itself but by contaminated feces, a detail that explains why bed nets are less effective against this vector than against mosquitoes. Transmission also occurs through blood transfusion, organ transplantation, and from mother to newborn, which is why screening blood donors and pregnant women matters even where the vector has been eliminated. Chronic infection can remain silent for decades before presenting as cardiac disease or disease of the digestive tract.

Ticks are not insects but arachnids, and they transmit a wider variety of pathogens than any other arthropod group. Hard ticks of the genera Ixodes, Amblyomma, Dermacentor, and Rhipicephalus carry the rickettsioses, including African tick-bite fever and Mediterranean spotted fever, and they carry the borrelioses as well. Ixodes ticks transmit the agents of Lyme disease in temperate regions and of relapsing fever in others. Amblyomma ticks transmit Rickettsia africae and also Rickettsia rickettsii, the cause of Rocky Mountain spotted fever, which despite its name is a disease of the Americas with substantial mortality when treatment is delayed. Dermacentor and Hyalomma ticks carry Crimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever virus, a disease whose case fatality can exceed 30 per cent and which spreads in hospitals through blood and body fluids. Because ticks attach for hours to days, a careful skin examination can sometimes find the vector still in place, and removing it properly is both diagnostic and therapeutic. Tick activity is tied closely to temperature and humidity, so an exposure history usually names a specific month and a specific landscape.

Snails are intermediate hosts rather than vectors in the strict sense, but they occupy the same position in the transmission cycle, and schistosomiasis cannot be understood without them. The parasite Schistosoma mansoni uses Biomphalaria snails, Schistosoma haematobium uses Bulinus snails, and Schistosoma japonicum uses Oncomelania snails. An infected person passes eggs in urine or stool, the eggs hatch in fresh water, and the resulting larvae penetrate a suitable snail. Inside the snail the parasite multiplies enormously, and after some weeks the snail releases free-swimming larvae that can penetrate human skin within minutes of contact. This is why a single brief wade through a lake, a canal, or an irrigation ditch can produce infection, and why recreational water contact in endemic areas is so often the exposure that patients forget to mention. The snail's own ecology, including its preferred water chemistry, flow rate, vegetation, and temperature, determines where transmission can occur at all.

Blackflies of the genus Simulium transmit Onchocerca volvulus, the parasite that causes onchocerciasis, or river blindness. They breed in fast-flowing, well-oxygenated rivers and streams, often below rapids or near the spillways of dams, and the adult females bite during the day. Their bites are painful and may leave blood stains on clothing, and a person who works or washes near such a river may be bitten hundreds of times in a season. Repeated infection produces the subcutaneous nodules and the skin disease, and after years the microscopic larvae damage the eye, which is how the disease earned its name. The dependence of the vector on swift, clean water is the reason that river blindness clusters along particular rivers rather than spreading evenly across a map. It also explains why the disease can be locally eliminated by vector control while the same parasite persists in a neighboring valley.

Fleas transmit plague, and the flea most responsible is Xenopsylla cheopis, the Oriental rat flea. Plague is a zoonosis maintained in rodents, and the bacterium Yersinia pestis circulates among them through flea bites. When a flea takes blood from an infected rodent, the bacteria multiply in its gut and can block its digestive tract, so that the next bite it attempts regurgitates bacteria into the wound. Fleas then leave a dying rodent and seek the next warm-blooded host, which may be a person, and this is the mechanism behind the explosive human epidemics of history. Bubonic plague follows the bite, while pneumonic plague follows inhalation and can then spread from person to person through respiratory droplets, which changes the entire public health response. Fleas also transmit murine typhus and serve as intermediate hosts in some helminth life cycles, so their significance extends well beyond plague alone.

Not every arthropod that carries a pathogen is a biological vector, and the distinction determines what kind of control will work. In biological transmission the pathogen multiplies, develops, or does both inside the arthropod, and the arthropod is an essential part of the life cycle. In mechanical transmission the arthropod is merely a contaminated surface, carrying the organism on its mouthparts, legs, or gut contents without any internal development. Houseflies and other filth flies move Shigella, Salmonella, and other enteric organisms mechanically from feces to food. Cockroaches can do the same, and their role in domestic diarrhea transmission is often dismissed too quickly. Mechanical transmission requires no incubation period in the vector, so a fly can be infectious immediately after leaving a contaminated source, and control rests on sanitation and food protection rather than on killing the fly.

The extrinsic incubation period is the interval between the moment a vector acquires a pathogen and the moment that vector becomes able to transmit it. It has no counterpart in the human host, and it is one of the most useful concepts in tropical prevention. During that interval the pathogen is developing inside an animal whose body temperature fluctuates with the weather, so the period is short in hot conditions and long in cool ones. If the vector's life expectancy is shorter than the extrinsic incubation period, that vector will almost never transmit anything, no matter how many people it bites. This single relationship explains why malaria transmission fades at high altitude, why dengue epidemics intensify in the hottest part of the year, and why warming can extend transmission into places that were historically safe. For a nurse explaining risk to a patient or a community, it is the clearest way to say that climate is not background scenery but part of the mechanism.

The practical consequence is that control measures buy time as well as prevent bites. Anything that shortens vector survival, even modestly, can push the average lifespan below the extrinsic incubation period and collapse transmission. This is why interventions that target the oldest mosquitoes, or that reduce the number of feeding cycles a mosquito completes, are disproportionately effective. It also explains why resistance to an insecticide can matter long before anyone measures a rise in mosquito numbers. If the insecticide stops killing vectors and merely irritates them, the vectors that survive are precisely the older ones that are already infectious. Programs therefore monitor not only how many vectors are present but how old they are and whether they survive to become infectious, and that measurement is difficult, which is one of the honest limits of field entomology.

Endophilic vectors rest indoors and take their blood meals inside houses, while exophilic vectors rest and bite outdoors. These terms describe behavior, and behavior is often a matter of degree and local adaptation rather than a fixed species trait. Anopheles gambiae is strongly endophilic in many settings, which is why spraying interior walls reduces transmission so effectively. If the same species shifts toward resting outdoors, the value of indoor spraying falls while its cost and its burden on the community stay the same. Exophilic vectors such as Aedes aegypti spend much of their time outdoors and in the area around the house, where indoor spraying reaches them poorly and where daytime biting defeats a bed net. Distinguishing these patterns decides whether a household intervention or an environmental one is the rational first choice, and it warns against copying a successful program from one district to a neighboring district without checking behavior there.

Breeding site ecology determines where a vector population can exist at all, and it is often the most actionable feature because habitat can sometimes be removed rather than treated. Anopheles gambiae favors temporary, sunlit, fresh water, while Anopheles funestus favors permanent, shaded water with vegetation. Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus favor small artificial containers that hold water for days at a time, and Culex quinquefasciatus breeds in organically rich, stagnant water. Drainage, filling, clearing vegetation, applying larvicides, and covering or emptying water containers are all direct attacks on breeding habitat, and each is specific to a species and a setting. Environmental management has a long history in tropical public health and remains the intervention least dependent on recurrent commodity supply. Its weakness is that it demands sustained labor, local knowledge, and community cooperation rather than a shipment of goods.

Helminths reach people through ways that have nothing to do with biting arthropods, and it simplifies the picture to remember that transmission is usually fecal or urinary contamination of the environment. Soil-transmitted helminths, including Ascaris lumbricoides, Trichuris trichiura, and the hookworms Necator americanus and Ancylostoma duodenale, pass eggs or larvae in human feces and infect the next person through contaminated soil on unwashed food or through skin penetration by larvae. Hookworm larvae in particular penetrate the skin of bare feet, which is why footwear and sanitation matter so much. Schistosomiasis depends on urine or stool reaching fresh water where the snail host lives, which makes latrine access and the avoidance of urination or defecation near water the central preventive messages. Strongyloides stercoralis is unusual because it can complete a cycle of autoinfection within the same person, which allows it to persist for decades after a person leaves an endemic area. Dracunculus medinensis, the guinea worm, reaches people when they drink water containing infected tiny crustaceans, and its control depends entirely on safe water supply and on finding and containing every case.

Any transmission cycle can be drawn as a chain of events, and each link is a possible intervention. The pathogen has to leave the reservoir host, be taken up by a vector, develop inside that vector, be delivered to a susceptible human, and establish infection. The human then becomes a source if the pathogen can leave the body again, whether to a vector, to another person, or into the environment. Control programs attack whichever links are most accessible, and the choice is always a judgment about cost, sustainability, and local acceptance. Bed nets and spraying attack the vector's contact with people, larval control attacks the vector's reproduction, water and sanitation attack the environmental links, and screening blood and treating patients attack the human reservoir links. A program that attacks only one link is vulnerable to the weakness of that link, which is why elimination strategies usually combine several.

The insecticide-treated bed net is the clearest example of an intervention that works through biology rather than through obedience. A net treated with insecticide does not depend on a person remembering to close a door or to behave correctly at every moment. When a net is used over a sleeping person during the hours when Anopheles bites, it does two separate things. It provides a physical barrier against bites, and it kills mosquitoes that land on the fabric. The killing effect matters beyond the individual sleeper, because it reduces the overall vector population and the average age of that population, which lowers transmission for the whole neighborhood. This is what public health workers mean when they describe the community effect of high net coverage. Framing net use as a question of obedience is both scientifically weak and practically counterproductive, because a person who sleeps under a net may still be bitten during the evening before bed, or may have holes that were never repaired. The more useful framing is that the net is a technology whose effect is conditional on coverage, condition, and timing, so monitoring asks about the proportion of households with at least one net, the ratio of nets to people, the condition of the fabric, and the season of use. It also asks whether nets are available at all, which is a supply question rather than a motivational one.

Indoor residual spraying is the other major household attack on endophilic vectors. The insecticide is applied to interior surfaces of walls, eaves, and roofs where the vector rests after feeding, and it kills on contact for months. Its effectiveness depends on the resting behavior of the local vector, on the quality of the application, and on the number of structures actually sprayed. Spray coverage is often the weak point, because homes may be locked, abandoned, or refused, and a sprayed district is not the same as a sprayed house. Community acceptance is essential, which means that spray teams must explain what they are doing, what will be removed from rooms, and what the smell and the residue mean. Spraying also requires trained applicators, functioning pumps, protective equipment, and insecticide that arrives before the transmission season, but its advantage over a net is that it protects everyone in the household at all hours, including people who do not sleep under a net.

Insecticide resistance is the central threat to every chemical control method, and it is monitored through standardized susceptibility testing of trapped vectors. Resistance arises when the same class of chemical is used repeatedly across a large area, selecting for insects that survive exposure. A vector population can develop resistance to one class while remaining susceptible to another, so programs rotate classes and combine them with non-chemical measures. Rotation is only rational if someone is measuring which chemicals still work, and that requires sentinel sites, live mosquito colonies, and laboratories that can perform the tests. Where monitoring is absent, a program may continue spraying a product that has ceased to kill, spending money and building community resentment without reducing transmission. The World Health Organization publishes guidance on resistance monitoring and on the choice of insecticides, and the current version must be consulted because recommendations change as resistance data accumulate. This is a place where a nurse can advocate, because the absence of entomological monitoring is usually a resource decision rather than a scientific one.

The unglamorous work of vector control is mostly logistics, and it decides the outcome more often than any biological insight. Bed nets must be procured in the correct size and insecticide class, shipped before the rainy season, stored dry, and distributed through a channel that actually reaches the households that need them. Indoor residual spraying depends on pumps, nozzles, spare parts, fuel, protective clothing, and a workforce that is trained and paid on time. Larvicides must be applied at the correct stage of the vector's development, which requires knowing when breeding begins, and that requires surveillance. Cold chain is not usually a problem for vector control itself, but it is a problem for the diagnostic and vaccination activities that often run alongside it. Every one of these tasks has a season, and a delay of a few weeks can mean that an entire transmission cycle passes unprotected. Programs that succeed treat supply and timing as clinical variables rather than administrative ones.

Monitoring and evaluation make the difference between a program that works and a program that believes it works. Coverage is measured by household surveys that count nets, their condition, and their use, not by the number of nets distributed. Entomological monitoring measures vector density, species composition, infection rates, insecticide susceptibility, and the proportion of vectors resting indoors. Human outcome monitoring tracks cases, test positivity, and location, which can reveal that transmission persists in a pocket the program never reached. Because any of these measures can improve for reasons unrelated to the intervention, programs compare areas and time periods rather than crediting themselves for a seasonal decline. Programs also fail for structural reasons that have little to do with biology, because a campaign that cannot reach the last village when the road floods each year leaves a pocket that reseeds the surrounding area, and a spraying program that depends on one donor's annual budget cannot sustain the pressure that elimination requires.

Every vector control measure and every antiparasitic drug has adverse effects, and a nurse who names them honestly protects both the patient and the program. Insecticides applied indoors can cause eye and skin irritation, headache, and respiratory symptoms, particularly when rooms are not ventilated or when residents return too soon after spraying. Nets treated with insecticide can cause skin irritation and, rarely, a burning sensation in the eyes. Mass treatment with preventive chemotherapy can produce nausea, abdominal pain, dizziness, and rash, and people who feel unwell after a tablet are less likely to accept the next round. Ivermectin used against onchocerciasis can provoke a reaction in people who harbor microfilariae, with fever, itching, rash, and sometimes more severe inflammation, and this reaction is a predictable consequence of killing the parasite rather than an allergy to the drug. Praziquantel can cause abdominal discomfort, headache, and dizziness that are usually mild and brief, and explaining that these reactions are expected and not a sign that the medicine is harming the person is part of the treatment itself.

The nurse's role in vector-borne disease begins before any program exists, in the recognition of an exposure that no one has named. The questions that matter are specific: where the patient sleeps, whether the house has screens or nets, what time of day the patient is outdoors, whether water stands near the home, whether animals are kept inside, and what the patient was doing in the weeks before the fever began. A patient with an eschar, a swollen lymph node, a painless skin ulcer that has not healed in months, or an unexplained eosinophilia may be carrying a vector-borne diagnosis that has been overlooked. Nurses see these findings first because they see the patient's skin, review the medication list, and take the history in a room where the patient is willing to talk. Recording the answers in the chart makes the next clinician's work possible, and repeating the exposure question when a fever persists is one of the highest-yield habits in tropical practice.

At the community level the nurse turns vector biology into instructions that people can follow. Nets should be tucked under the mattress, used every night of the year rather than only in the wettest months, and repaired when torn. Water containers should be covered and emptied on a weekly schedule so that larvae cannot complete their development. Standing water around the compound should be removed, which is often a task for a neighborhood rather than for a single household. Anyone with fever should be tested rather than treated blindly, and anyone with bleeding, confusion, or difficulty breathing should be referred immediately. Advice must acknowledge what families can actually do, because telling a household to eliminate standing water when it has no reliable water supply is not advice. The nurse who pairs a message with a realistic solution is far more likely to be heard.

Clinicians working in endemic areas also need personal protection that matches the vector, and this is a planning task rather than a formality. Repellents applied to exposed skin and permethrin-treated clothing reduce bites from mosquitoes and ticks. Long sleeves and trousers during the hours of peak biting are simple and effective where the heat allows them. Aerosol insecticides and window screens reduce indoor exposure, and a bed net is essential where Anopheles is active at night. For Crimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever and other infections that spread in hospitals, gloves, gowns, eye protection, and safe handling of sharps and body fluids are the measures that prevent transmission to staff, and ticks should be removed with fine forceps, pulled steadily close to the skin without crushing the body. Post-exposure planning, including whom to call and where to obtain evaluation, should be settled before deployment rather than during an illness.

Where treatment or prevention is prescribed for a vector-borne disease, the regimen must be verified against current guidance rather than recalled from memory. The World Health Organization publishes treatment guidelines for malaria, leishmaniasis, trypanosomiasis, Chagas disease, and the helminth infections, and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention publishes recommendations for clinicians in the United States. Doses and durations are revised as resistance emerges and as new evidence accumulates, and recommendations differ between countries even for the same disease. Current guidance for onchocerciasis, for example, gives ivermectin at 150 micrograms per kilogram by mouth, repeated according to the national program protocol rather than by individual preference. Treatment for Chagas disease with benznidazole or nifurtimox, treatment for leishmaniasis with liposomal amphotericin B or pentavalent antimonials, and treatment for plague with gentamicin or doxycycline all carry age-specific and weight-specific considerations that vary among guidelines. The current national guideline and the current World Health Organization recommendation must be checked before prescribing, and a pediatric dose calculated from a table that has since been revised is a preventable error.

Vector ecology is being reshaped by human activity at a pace that outstrips most programs' ability to adapt. Irrigation schemes, dams, and rice cultivation create year-round larval habitat where transmission was previously seasonal, and deforestation moves vectors and their hosts closer to settlements. Unplanned urban growth creates the containers, the standing water, and the dense human population that Aedes aegypti requires. Warming extends the range where the extrinsic incubation period is short enough for transmission, and shifting rainfall makes the timing of seasons less predictable. Extreme weather compresses all of this into sudden events, when flooding displaces people, disrupts sanitation, and concentrates vectors and hosts together. Because so many vector-borne diseases are zoonoses first and human diseases second, the human, veterinary, and environmental sectors have to work from the same map, since a human case may be the first sign of an animal outbreak.

When you arrive in a new setting, the fastest way to understand the vector-borne risk is to read the place in a fixed order. Identify the water, because water determines which larvae can develop and where. Identify the housing, because wall construction, roofing, screens, and eaves determine which vectors rest and feed indoors. Identify the animals, because they determine which zoonotic cycles are running, and identify the working and sleeping hours of the people, because those determine when exposure occurs. Identify the season, because the same village is a different risk in the dry months and in the wet months. Then ask which of those features can be changed and which cannot, and build the plan around the ones that can, because that sequence is what converts vector biology into a nursing assessment.

To summarize, a vector is a living link whose competence depends on the pairing of a pathogen, an arthropod, and an environment, and whose efficiency depends far more on how long it lives and how often it bites people than on how many of them exist. Mosquitoes of the genus Anopheles transmit malaria at night, Aedes mosquitoes transmit dengue, chikungunya, Zika, and yellow fever by day, and Culex mosquitoes carry lymphatic filariasis and several encephalitis viruses in between. Sandflies carry leishmaniasis, tsetse flies carry African trypanosomiasis, triatomine bugs carry Chagas disease, ticks carry the rickettsioses, the borrelioses, and Crimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever, snails support schistosomiasis, blackflies carry onchocerciasis, and fleas carry plague. Biological transmission requires the pathogen to develop inside the vector, which is why the extrinsic incubation period and vector survival govern so much of what control can achieve, while mechanical transmission requires only a contaminated surface and is answered by sanitation. Endophilic and exophilic behavior, breeding site ecology, and the water-borne and soil-borne routes of the helminths each point to a specific intervention, and every link in a transmission cycle is a place where a program can act. The insecticide-treated bed net works through biology rather than obedience, because it kills as well as blocks, and its community benefit depends on coverage, condition, and timing rather than on the virtue of the sleeper. Logistics, monitoring, resistance surveillance, and honest reporting of adverse effects decide whether any of this reaches the people who need it, and the nurse stands at the point where the biology becomes care. Verify every regimen against the current national and World Health Organization guidance before you prescribe, and let the ecology of the place tell you what to look for in the patient in front of you.
