# Chapter 16 — Schistosomiasis and Food-Borne Trematodes

Schistosomiasis, also called bilharzia and snail fever, is caused by blood flukes of the genus Schistosoma. The worms do not multiply inside the human body, so disease severity depends on how many larvae entered the skin and how many eggs the surviving adults release each day. Roughly 200 million people are infected worldwide, and most live in sub-Saharan Africa, where the disease is a leading cause of chronic bladder and liver illness. It is a neglected tropical disease in the fullest sense, common, disabling, and treatable with one inexpensive drug, yet ignored because it kills slowly rather than dramatically.

Several species infect people, and they differ in where the adult worms live and therefore in what they do. Schistosoma haematobium settles in the veins around the bladder and causes urinary disease. Schistosoma mansoni and Schistosoma japonicum settle in the veins draining the intestines and cause intestinal and liver disease. Schistosoma mekongi, Schistosoma intercalatum, and Schistosoma guineensis are less common and follow the intestinal pattern. Schistosoma japonicum is the most zoonotic, because it also infects cattle, water buffalo, pigs, and dogs, which makes environmental control far harder.

Each species has a narrow, predictable geography, which is the most useful fact in the exposure history. Schistosoma haematobium is found throughout Africa, in the Nile valley, and in parts of the Middle East. Schistosoma mansoni occurs in much of Africa, the Arabian Peninsula, Brazil, Suriname, Venezuela, and a few Caribbean islands. Schistosoma japonicum occurs in China, the Philippines, and parts of Indonesia, while Schistosoma mekongi is limited to the Mekong basin in Laos and Cambodia. Transmission needs three things at once: fresh water, the snail that can host the parasite, and human waste reaching that water.

Eggs leave the body in urine or stool, and in fresh water they hatch into ciliated larvae called miracidia, which swim until they find the correct snail. Inside the snail the parasite multiplies asexually, and after 4 to 6 weeks the snail releases thousands of fork-tailed larvae called cercariae. Cercariae survive only 1 to 2 days, swimming toward shadows and turbulence, which is how they find a human body. They penetrate intact skin within minutes, lose their tails, and become schistosomulae, which travel through the lymphatics and bloodstream to the lungs and then the liver. There they mature, pair into male and female worms, and migrate to their final veins, where the female begins to lay eggs about 4 to 6 weeks after the skin was penetrated, and sometimes as late as 8 to 12 weeks for Schistosoma japonicum.

Schistosoma haematobium uses snails of the genus Bulinus, Schistosoma mansoni uses Biomphalaria, Schistosoma japonicum uses Oncomelania, and Schistosoma mekongi uses Neotricula. These snails thrive in warm, slow-moving fresh water enriched by human activity. The great water projects of the twentieth century created enormous new habitat, and the Aswan High Dam, Lake Volta, and the Senegal River valley all increased transmission. Lakes and rivers used for bathing, swimming, laundry, fishing, and rice farming are the places where infection is acquired, and children who play in shallow water are infected most heavily.

Not every freshwater contact produces infection, and not every rash after swimming is schistosomiasis, because repeated exposure is what builds the worm burden that causes chronic disease. Cercariae of bird and rodent schistosomes also penetrate human skin but die there, producing an itchy raised rash within hours called cercarial dermatitis, or swimmer's itch. That rash is an allergic reaction, not an established infection, and it resolves without treatment. A rash on the same day is not schistosomiasis, and fever several weeks later should not be dismissed as a rash.

Acute schistosomiasis, often called Katayama syndrome, appears 2 to 8 weeks after exposure, exactly when the maturing worms begin to release eggs. It causes fever, dry cough, urticaria, muscle aches, headache, abdominal pain, diarrhea, and an enlarged tender liver, with marked eosinophilia and sometimes transient infiltrates on chest imaging. The mechanism is an immune reaction to migrating larvae and newly deposited eggs rather than a direct effect of worm numbers, so the illness can be severe even in a light infection. Katayama syndrome is seen mostly in travelers and migrants without prior exposure, because people who grow up in endemic areas acquire their first infection in childhood and later infections smolder rather than erupt.

Chronic urinary schistosomiasis begins insidiously, and its earliest reliable symptom is terminal hematuria, blood at the end of the stream, often with burning, frequency, and suprapubic discomfort. Eggs lodged in the bladder wall provoke granulomas that become polyps or flat sandy patches, and the resulting fibrosis stiffens the bladder and narrows the ureters. Untreated, this leads to hydronephrosis, recurrent urinary infection, stones, and eventually kidney failure. Genital infection is common and often overlooked: in women it causes vulval and cervical lesions, vaginal bleeding, pain during intercourse, and infertility, and it roughly doubles the risk of acquiring HIV. Long-standing bladder infection is a recognized cause of squamous cell carcinoma of the bladder, appearing at a younger age than bladder cancer elsewhere.

Intestinal and hepatic schistosomiasis follows infection with Schistosoma mansoni and Schistosoma japonicum. The gut disease causes colicky abdominal pain, bloody diarrhea, tenesmus, and sometimes colonic polyps, and the chronic blood loss contributes to iron deficiency anemia. The liver disease is the more dangerous half. Eggs that cannot escape the intestinal wall are swept back into the portal venules, where each provokes a granuloma, and years of inflammation around the small portal veins lay down collagen that thickens the portal tracts without destroying the liver cells themselves. This periportal fibrosis, also called Symmers pipestem fibrosis, causes portal hypertension with splenomegaly and esophageal varices, and the patient may present with sudden vomiting of blood. Synthetic liver function is often preserved, so normal liver enzymes do not exclude advanced disease, and coexisting hepatitis B or hepatitis C worsens the outcome.

Eggs can also reach the central nervous system, producing neuroschistosomiasis, which is uncommon but devastating. They travel through the valveless venous plexus connecting the pelvic veins to the veins around the spinal cord and lodge there, causing an inflammatory mass rather than a simple obstruction. Spinal involvement is more frequent than brain involvement, especially with Schistosoma haematobium and Schistosoma mansoni, and it presents as acute transverse myelitis or a conus medullaris syndrome, with lower back pain, ascending numbness, weakness, and loss of bladder control. Cerebral disease presents with seizures, headache, focal weakness, or signs of raised intracranial pressure, and it can be mistaken for a brain tumor.

Diagnosis begins with the exposure history and the blood count, but it cannot stop there. Eosinophilia is a useful clue and not a specific one. Detecting eggs confirms active infection and identifies the species, while detecting antibodies only proves that exposure happened at some point in the patient's life. Antigen tests sit in between, because they reflect living adult worms and therefore active infection. Egg output varies from day to day and hour to hour, so a single negative sample never excludes the diagnosis when suspicion is real.

Urine filtration is the standard test for Schistosoma haematobium. Collect 10 milliliters of urine, ideally between noon and 2 in the afternoon, when egg excretion peaks, filter it through a fine nylon membrane, and examine the sediment under the microscope. Report the result as eggs per 10 milliliters of urine so that it can be compared over time, and because excretion fluctuates, collect samples on 2 or 3 different days. In field programs a reagent strip that detects blood in the urine is a cheap and reasonably sensitive screen, because almost everyone with heavy Schistosoma haematobium infection has microscopic blood in the urine.

For intestinal species, stool examination is the mainstay. The Kato-Katz technique uses a template holding about 41.7 milligrams of sieved stool, which is pressed onto a slide, cleared with glycerol, and examined under the microscope, and the count is multiplied to give eggs per gram of stool. The method is inexpensive and quantitative, which is what programs need to measure intensity and to monitor treatment. Its weakness is that light infections are easy to miss, so 2 to 3 samples on separate days are recommended before a patient is declared uninfected. Schistosoma mansoni eggs have a prominent lateral spine, Schistosoma japonicum eggs a small lateral knob, and Schistosoma haematobium eggs a terminal spine.

Several other tests extend what you can do when microscopy is unhelpful. The circulating cathodic antigen test is a urine cassette test for Schistosoma mansoni that gives a result in about 20 minutes and is widely used in surveys, although it is less reliable for Schistosoma haematobium. Rectal snip biopsy, in which a small piece of rectal mucosa is examined for eggs, remains useful for intestinal species when stool studies are negative, and bladder biopsy at cystoscopy can confirm urinary disease and exclude cancer. Serology is most useful in travelers and in the acute phase, when eggs are not yet being passed, but a positive antibody test alone can never prove active infection.

Ultrasound has become essential because it shows the damage eggs have already done, not merely the presence of worms. In urinary disease the bladder wall thickens, polyps appear, and the ureters and kidneys may dilate, and the standardized Niamey protocol allows comparable measurements between clinics. In intestinal and hepatic disease the Cairo protocol grades periportal fibrosis and can show an enlarged spleen and collateral veins. Portable machines now allow these measurements in district hospitals, and fibrosis may partially reverse after treatment while advanced changes persist. A normal ultrasound does not exclude infection, so imaging must be read alongside the epidemiology and the laboratory result.

Treatment rests on praziquantel, and understanding how it works explains why the timing of the dose matters. The drug increases the permeability of the worm's outer layer to calcium, causing violent contraction and damaging the tegument, which exposes worm antigens to the immune system, and the damaged worms move from the mesenteric veins into the liver, where they are trapped and killed. Praziquantel acts on adult worms and has little effect on the immature schistosomulae present in the first weeks after exposure. This is why a patient treated too early often remains infected, and why the standard advice is to wait until 4 to 6 weeks after the last freshwater exposure. If a traveler is diagnosed in the first weeks, many clinicians treat the acute illness supportively and give praziquantel once the worms have matured.

The dose depends on the species. For Schistosoma haematobium and Schistosoma mansoni, current guidance from the World Health Organization and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention gives praziquantel at 40 milligrams per kilogram by mouth, usually as a single dose or divided into two doses taken 4 to 6 hours apart. For Schistosoma japonicum and Schistosoma mekongi the dose is 60 milligrams per kilogram, divided into two or three doses over one day. The tablets are 600 milligrams, so a dose is often three or four tablets, and in community campaigns the dose comes from a height pole rather than a measured weight. Praziquantel is taken with food and water, after a meal, because absorption improves and nausea is reduced.

Before you prescribe, verify. Dosing regimens are revised as new evidence appears, national programs adapt them to local species and supply, and guidance for pregnancy and young children has changed more than once in recent years. The World Health Organization now advises treating pregnant and lactating women in endemic areas rather than deferring treatment, and it supports treating children under 4 years of age after risk assessment, whereas older guidance restricted both groups. The current national guideline, the current product label, and the current program protocol must be checked before any dose is given, and a dose spoken from memory or copied from an old chart is not acceptable. Name the source of your regimen in your documentation so the next clinician can see what you followed.

Adverse effects of praziquantel are common, usually mild, and short-lived. Patients may report abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, headache, dizziness, drowsiness, fever, or itching, generally within a few hours of the dose and settling within a day. The drowsiness matters practically, because a patient should not drive or operate machinery for the rest of that day, and sedatives and antihistamines should be used cautiously at the same time. Rifampicin, carbamazepine, phenytoin, and dexamethasone lower the drug level and may reduce efficacy, while cimetidine raises it. Serious reactions are rare, but campaigns still need a plan for recording and referring them, and a nurse running a treatment day should have a clear route to a clinician and a vehicle.

After treatment, monitoring is where nursing follow-up earns its place. A test of cure is usually done about 4 to 6 weeks after treatment for antigen tests and about 3 months after treatment for egg counts, and the goal is a large fall in egg output rather than an absolute zero, because residual eggs and incomplete cure are both common. Eosinophilia should decline over several months. Reinfection is frequent wherever exposure continues, so a treated patient who returns to the same lake or canal may need repeated treatment, and the follow-up visit is the natural moment to teach prevention again. Patients with urinary disease need follow-up of blood in the urine, blood pressure, and kidney function, while those with hepatic disease may need endoscopy for varices. Children deserve growth and anemia monitoring, because chronic schistosomiasis blunts growth and worsens iron deficiency.

Praziquantel is also the backbone of public health control, delivered through mass drug administration to whole at-risk communities. Programs usually target school-age children and adults in high-prevalence areas, dose by height, and repeat treatment every year or every 2 years depending on measured prevalence. The tablet is 600 milligrams, and the height pole lets a team dose hundreds of people in a day without scales, though a nurse must supervise the pole, the quartering of tablets, and the recording. Coverage of at least 75 per cent of the target group is the benchmark that reduces transmission, and the World Health Organization coordinates a donation program that supplies the drug to eligible countries. Monitoring includes watching for vomiting soon after the dose, which may mean the drug was not absorbed and should be repeated.

Prevention is unglamorous, specific, and more durable than any single treatment. The first principle is to keep human waste out of fresh water, which means latrines people actually use, safe disposal of urine as well as stool, and sanitation that reaches schools and markets, not only households. The second is to reduce contact with contaminated water, which means a safe supply for drinking, bathing, and laundry so people are not forced into the canal or the lake. Snail control with molluscicides, drainage, and removal of aquatic vegetation can interrupt transmission at its source, and it works best combined with treatment rather than alone.

The food-borne trematodes are a different problem, and the nurse who understands the pattern can prevent them with a sentence. These flatworms have complex life cycles requiring a first intermediate host, usually a snail, and a second intermediate host, which may be a fish, a crab, a crayfish, or an aquatic plant. People become infected by eating that second host raw or undercooked, or by eating water plants carrying the encysted larvae, and the infection is never passed from person to person. The adults live for years in the bile ducts, the lungs, or the intestine, and the resulting disease is chronic, often silent, and occasionally malignant. Treatment is simple, but prevention is a matter of food preparation, which makes it a natural subject for nursing education.

The liver flukes are Clonorchis sinensis, the Chinese liver fluke, and the Opisthorchis species, chiefly Opisthorchis viverrini and Opisthorchis felineus. They occur in East and Southeast Asia, particularly China, Korea, Vietnam, Thailand, Laos, Cambodia, and parts of Russia, and the Mekong region carries the heaviest burden. The adult worms live in the bile ducts and release eggs that pass in the stool, hatch in water, enter a snail, and develop into cercariae that encyst in freshwater fish, especially the carp family. A person is infected by eating those fish raw, and the dishes that transmit infection include koi pla, a raw minced fish dish from northeast Thailand, pla ra and pla som, and the salted or fermented fish preparations sold in many markets. The larvae mature in the bile duct in about 3 to 4 weeks, and the adults can live 20 to 30 years.

Most people with liver flukes have no symptoms, which is why the infection is found by screening rather than by complaint. When symptoms appear they are vague: pain in the right upper abdomen, indigestion, fatigue, and occasionally jaundice. Heavy and long-standing infection causes recurrent cholangitis, gallstones, biliary obstruction, and pancreatitis, and it produces a characteristic thickening and fibrosis around the bile ducts. The most serious consequence is cholangiocarcinoma, cancer of the bile ducts, and the link is strongest for Opisthorchis viverrini and Clonorchis sinensis, both classified as carcinogenic to humans. Any patient from an endemic area with unexplained eosinophilia, biliary symptoms, or a bile duct abnormality deserves stool examination and serology.

Diagnosis depends first on thinking of it, then on finding the eggs. Stool microscopy with the Kato-Katz method or a concentration technique reveals small oval eggs with an operculum and a small shoulder, though light infections may require several samples. Ultrasound can show dilated intrahepatic bile ducts and periductal fibrosis, and computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging may be needed when cancer is suspected. When cholangiocarcinoma is possible, imaging of the biliary tree and referral to a center with surgical and cancer services takes priority over the parasitological diagnosis, because delay changes the outcome.

Treatment of liver flukes is praziquantel at 25 milligrams per kilogram by mouth three times a day for 2 days, a higher total dose and longer course than the single dose used for schistosomiasis. An alternative is tribendimidine, shown to be effective against Opisthorchis viverrini in trials in the region. Cure rates are good, but the drug does not undo the damage already done to the bile ducts, and treated patients from high-risk areas remain at elevated risk of cholangiocarcinoma, so the nurse's role in long-term follow-up is real. After treatment, stool examination should be repeated at about 1 month, and persistent eggs mean the course either was not taken or did not work.

Paragonimiasis is caused by lung flukes, chiefly Paragonimus westermani, and it is acquired by eating raw or undercooked freshwater crab or crayfish. The larvae excyst in the small intestine, penetrate the gut wall, cross the abdominal cavity and the diaphragm, and mature in the lungs, where they form fibrous cysts. The classic illness is a chronic cough with blood-stained, rusty sputum, chest pain, and low-grade fever, and the chest radiograph shows nodules, cysts, or ring shadows easily mistaken for tuberculosis. Eggs are found in the sputum and stool, and serology is useful when the parasite burden is low. In some patients the worms migrate to the brain and cause cerebral paragonimiasis with seizures, headache, and focal deficits, one of the treatable causes of epilepsy in endemic areas. Treatment is praziquantel at 25 milligrams per kilogram three times a day for 2 days, and triclabendazole is an alternative.

Fascioliasis, caused by Fasciola hepatica and Fasciola gigantica, is the liver fluke people acquire from plants rather than fish. It is found worldwide, with important foci in the Andean highlands, in Egypt, and in Iran, and it is classically linked to eating watercress, water spinach, or water mint from a stream where infected sheep or cattle graze. The larvae encyst on the plants, and people swallow them with the plant or with untreated water, and occasionally immature worms come from eating the raw liver of an infected animal. There are two phases. In the acute hepatic phase, 2 to 4 months after ingestion, the migrating worms cause fever, pain in the right upper abdomen, an enlarged tender liver, and striking eosinophilia. In the biliary phase, the adult worms obstruct the bile ducts and cause cholangitis, gallstones, and anemia.

Diagnosis of fascioliasis differs from that of the other flukes, and the difference matters. In the acute phase, stool examination is usually negative, because the worms have not reached the bile ducts and are not laying eggs, so serology is the test of choice and it is highly sensitive. Once the biliary phase begins, eggs can be found in stool, and imaging may show tracks left by the migrating worms in the liver. The treatment is specific: triclabendazole at 10 milligrams per kilogram by mouth, given as a single dose in some guidelines and as two doses 12 hours apart in others, and it is the drug of choice because it is active against both immature and adult worms. Praziquantel does not work against Fasciola, and giving it wastes time while the patient remains ill. Where triclabendazole is unavailable, nitazoxanide is a less effective alternative, and the World Health Organization provides the drug through a donation program.

The intestinal flukes are the least dramatic members of the group and easy to overlook. Fasciolopsis buski, the giant intestinal fluke, is acquired from water caltrop, water chestnut, bamboo shoots, and lotus grown in contaminated water in South and Southeast Asia. Echinostoma species come from raw snails, clams, fish, and amphibians, and Heterophyes and Metagonimus species from raw or salted freshwater and brackish fish. The adult worms attach to the wall of the small intestine, and light infections cause only mild diarrhea and abdominal discomfort. Heavy infection with Fasciolopsis can cause ulceration, protein loss, and edema, particularly in children, and chronic infection contributes to poor growth. Praziquantel at 40 milligrams per kilogram by mouth as a single dose treats most of these infections, although some echinostomes need a higher dose, and repeat stool examination confirms cure.

This is where nursing education does its most valuable work, because a general instruction such as cook your food well changes nothing. The teaching has to name the specific food and the specific practice. Tell a family in northeast Thailand not to eat koi pla, and explain that the fish must be cooked until the flesh flakes; tell a family in the Andes not to eat watercress picked from the stream where the animals drink, and to cook the plants bought at market; tell a family on the Mekong not to drink the juice from crab shells and not to handle raw crab and then touch the mouth. Explain that lime juice, salt, and alcohol do not kill the larvae, and that only thorough cooking, or freezing at minus 20 degrees Celsius for at least 7 days, makes freshwater fish safe. Involve market vendors, food handlers, teachers, and community leaders, because the food is prepared in the household and sold in the market, not in the clinic.

Some practical logistics decide whether a program works, and the nurse usually manages them. Praziquantel tablets are 600 milligrams and are scored, but breaking them accurately still requires technique and a tablet cutter where one is available, and the height pole must be used consistently by everyone on the team. The drug does not need a cold chain, but it should be stored dry and below 30 degrees Celsius, and stock-outs are the commonest reason a treatment round fails. Record the name, age, height band, dose, and any adverse event for every person treated, because coverage and safety data justify the next round. Praziquantel is on the World Health Organization list of essential medicines, and mass distribution is donated, so supply arrives on a program timetable.

The nurse's assessment role is mostly a matter of asking the right questions. Ask where the patient has lived and traveled, whether they swam or waded in fresh water, whether they worked in irrigated fields, whether they eat raw freshwater fish, raw crab, or uncooked water plants, and whether anyone in the household has similar symptoms. Ask about blood in the urine, blood in the stool, and blood when coughing, because patients often do not volunteer these symptoms unless asked directly. Consider female genital schistosomiasis in any woman from an endemic area with pelvic pain, bleeding, or infertility, and consider neuroschistosomiasis in any patient with unexplained spinal cord disease or new seizures. Then connect the finding to the specimen: urine at midday, stool on more than one day, sputum when there is blood in the cough, and serology when the exposure was recent.

Special populations require deliberate thought rather than blanket rules. Pregnant and lactating women in endemic areas should be treated according to current World Health Organization guidance, which favors treatment rather than delay, and children under 4 years are also treated after risk assessment in most programs. People living with HIV need their antiretroviral therapy reviewed for interactions, and women with female genital schistosomiasis need treatment and counselling about their increased risk of acquiring HIV. Migrants and refugees from endemic countries should be screened for eosinophilia and for evidence of schistosomiasis and other helminths, because the late complications are preventable and the treatment is simple. Patients with advanced liver disease need variceal surveillance, and patients with bladder cancer need referral rather than repeated courses of antiparasitic drugs.

Surveillance and program monitoring close the loop between individual care and public health. Prevalence in school-age children is the standard indicator, measured by urine filtration or a rapid antigen test at sentinel schools, and it tells a program whether transmission is falling or returning. The egg reduction rate after treatment measures drug efficacy and can detect reduced susceptibility, which has been reported in a few settings and is watched closely. Schistosomiasis is not usually notifiable in the way cholera or viral hemorrhagic fever is, but laboratory confirmation belongs in the patient record, and unusual clusters or treatment failures should be reported to the local health authority.

A word about what not to do, because over-treatment has its own costs. Praziquantel is safe and cheap, but it is not a substitute for diagnosis where diagnosis is possible, and it does not treat Fasciola or the tapeworms, so a patient with eosinophilia who receives it blindly may remain ill and lose confidence in the clinic. Giving repeated doses to a patient whose blood in the urine comes from bladder cancer or a urinary stone delays the real diagnosis. Let the exposure history, the species, the specimen, and the current guideline decide the plan.

To summarize, schistosomiasis is caused by blood flukes whose adult worms live in the veins around the bladder or the intestine, and whose eggs produce the granulomatous damage that causes blood in the urine, intestinal bleeding, periportal fibrosis with portal hypertension, and, with Schistosoma haematobium, squamous cell carcinoma of the bladder. Diagnosis rests on the exposure history and on finding eggs in urine or stool, supported by antigen tests, serology, and ultrasound. Praziquantel is the treatment of choice, at 40 milligrams per kilogram for Schistosoma haematobium and Schistosoma mansoni and 60 milligrams per kilogram for Schistosoma japonicum and Schistosoma mekongi, and every regimen must be checked against current World Health Organization or national guidance before it is prescribed. The food-borne trematodes, including the liver flukes Clonorchis and Opisthorchis, the lung fluke Paragonimus, and Fasciola with its specific treatment triclabendazole, are acquired from raw fish, raw crab, and uncooked water plants, and they cause chronic biliary, pulmonary, and intestinal disease. Control depends on latrines, safe water, snail reduction, repeated community treatment, and food education that names the specific food and the specific practice. Your part is to ask the exposure questions, collect the right specimen at the right hour, verify every dose, monitor the response, and teach the household what to change.
