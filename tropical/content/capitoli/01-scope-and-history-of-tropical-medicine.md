# Chapter 1 — Scope and History of Tropical Medicine: Neglected Tropical Diseases, the Global Burden, and One Health

Tropical medicine is the study and the practice of the infections and other health problems that cluster among the poorest people on earth, most of whom live between the tropic of Cancer and the tropic of Capricorn, but by no means all of them. The name is an accident of history rather than a description of cause. The diseases gathered under the label are not produced by warmth alone; they are produced by poverty, crowding, contaminated water, absent sanitation, bare feet, unventilated housing, and the animals and insects that share those conditions with people. A person can live on the equator in a sealed apartment with piped, treated water and meet almost none of these diseases. A person can live far north of the tropics, in a migrant labor camp or a neglected urban neighborhood, and meet several of them in a single season. What unites the field, then, is not latitude but the conditions that let a pathogen reach a human body and the systems that fail to stop it. This chapter is about the shape of that field, where it came from, what it measures, what it promises, and where the nurse stands inside it.

Climate and ecology are not irrelevant, and it would be dishonest to pretend otherwise. A mosquito needs warmth to survive and to complete the development of the parasite inside it, which is why malaria transmission fades at high altitude and in cold seasons. A snail that carries schistosomiasis needs fresh water of the right temperature, and a sandfly needs the warm, humid crevices of a mud wall. But warmth is a permissive condition, not a sufficient one, and the places where these diseases do their heaviest work are usually places where poverty has removed the protections that wealth supplies automatically. A child in a wealthy tropical city with window screens, running water, and a refrigerator rarely dies of diarrhea. A child 20 kilometers away in a settlement without a latrine, electricity, or reliable water may die of the same organism in a single afternoon. That contrast is the honest center of the discipline.

The modern discipline was born in the nineteenth century out of the needs of colonial administrations and of armies. European powers had learned that they could conquer territory in the tropics far more easily than they could keep their soldiers, sailors, and officials alive there. Malaria, yellow fever, dysentery, and typhoid killed more troops than combat did in several colonial campaigns, and the medical services that grew up around those losses were built first to protect Europeans. The great discoveries came from that work and from the curiosity that accompanied it. Patrick Manson, a Scottish physician working in China, showed in 1877 that a mosquito carried the filarial worm Wuchereria bancrofti, the parasite responsible for lymphatic filariasis. Ronald Ross, working in India in 1897, showed that mosquitoes of the genus Anopheles transmitted the malaria parasite. Walter Reed and his colleagues in Cuba demonstrated in 1900 that yellow fever was transmitted by the mosquito Aedes aegypti, a finding that later made the Panama Canal possible. The London School of Hygiene and Tropical Medicine and the Liverpool School of Tropical Medicine were founded at the end of the nineteenth century specifically to train the medical officers of empire.

Sleeping sickness, or human African trypanosomiasis, is the clearest example of how military and colonial medicine became tropical medicine. Epidemics of the form caused by the parasite Trypanosoma brucei gambiense swept through Uganda and the Congo basin in the first years of the twentieth century, killing hundreds of thousands of people and depopulating whole districts. David Bruce identified the tsetse fly as the vector in 1903. Colonial authorities responded with mobile teams that surveyed villages, examined lymph node fluid and blood, and treated cases with drugs that were themselves dangerous, including arsenical compounds that carried substantial mortality. Those campaigns were coercive, they were driven by the fear that the disease would cripple the labor force and the administration, and they also produced the first large-scale evidence that systematic case finding and treatment could break transmission. That contradiction, a program that did real good and at the same time treated people as a means to an economic end, runs through the whole history of the field and still shapes how communities regard outside health teams today.

The Rockefeller Foundation gave the emerging discipline its most influential institution building and its most consequential shift in focus. Its International Health Commission, created in 1913, began with hookworm and then moved into yellow fever, malaria, and other infections. The foundation's hookworm campaigns in the southern United States and in many low-income countries combined treatment with latrine construction and health education, which is recognizably the model of a modern neglected disease program. Its yellow fever work mapped the mosquito and the virus across three continents and helped develop a vaccine. Alongside the missionary hospitals and the colonial medical services, the foundation trained a generation of local health workers, built laboratories, and gathered the epidemiological data on which later programs would be designed. It also concentrated power and agenda setting in donors rather than in the communities being served, a problem that has not disappeared.

After the Second World War and the wave of independence that followed it, the discipline had to reinvent itself. Colonial medical services lost their reason for existing, and the question became how to deliver care to whole populations rather than to an occupying elite. The Declaration of Alma-Ata in 1978 gave that question its most durable answer by insisting that primary health care, delivered close to where people live, was the route to health for all. In the same years, critics inside and outside the field began to argue that the geographical name was hiding the real variable. Studies of tuberculosis, diarrhea, and malnutrition in wealthy cities showed patterns that matched those in tropical villages, while malaria and dengue could be found far from the equator wherever the ecology and the poverty allowed. The intellectual shift was from climate to class, from latitude to living conditions, and it changed both the research agenda and the language. It also carried a warning that the field has had to learn repeatedly, that a disease is not tropical because of where it is found but because of who is left exposed to it.

Poverty is now understood as the fundamental determinant rather than one factor among many. It governs the quality of housing, the presence of screens and ventilation, the distance to clean water, the availability of a latrine, the ability to buy shoes, the ability to reach a clinic, and the ability to take a full course of a medicine without running out of money. It also governs education, which shapes whether a mother recognizes danger signs and whether a community accepts a vaccine. Where these protections are absent, pathogens exploit the gap with great efficiency, and where they are present, the same pathogens become rare or manageable. That is why a program that only supplies a drug, a bed net, or a test will often underperform a program that also addresses water, sanitation, housing, nutrition, and the economics of the household. The nurse sees this arithmetic every day, because the nurse is usually the person who discovers that the prescription cannot be filled, that the walk to the clinic is 3 hours, or that the water source is the same stream that the children play in.

A disease becomes neglected not because it is unknown but because it is unprofitable to the people with the resources to solve it. The World Health Organization assembles its list of roughly 20 conditions from a set of criteria that are partly epidemiological and partly political. A condition is more likely to be included if it affects very large numbers of poor people, if it causes long-term disability, disfigurement, blindness, or stigma that outlasts the acute illness, if there is no market incentive for industry to develop new tools, and if a package of existing interventions could plausibly be delivered at low cost. Conditions have been added in recent years as the criteria were applied more openly. Snakebite envenoming and scabies were both added in 2017, and the deep fungal infections, including mycetoma and chromoblastomycosis, have been progressively recognized and grouped. The list is therefore a political document as well as a technical one, and its contents change with advocacy as much as with epidemiology.

The 20 conditions are not a random collection, and it helps to hear them grouped by what they do to a body. The helminth infections include the soil-transmitted worms such as Ascaris lumbricoides, the large intestinal roundworm, hookworm, and the whipworm Trichuris trichiura, along with schistosomiasis, lymphatic filariasis, onchocerciasis, and the food-borne trematodes. The protozoal infections include leishmaniasis, Chagas disease, and human African trypanosomiasis. The bacterial infections include leprosy, trachoma, yaws, the endemic treponematoses, and Buruli ulcer. The viral group is dominated by dengue, chikungunya, rabies, and the viral hemorrhagic fevers such as Lassa fever. Several conditions sit apart from these groups, including snakebite envenoming, scabies, tungiasis, mycetoma and the deep mycoses, podoconiosis, and echinococcosis, and each of them is neglected for the same underlying reason. Many of these conditions share a geography, a vector, and a season, which is why the programs that treat them are increasingly integrated rather than run one disease at a time. The patient in front of the nurse rarely has exactly one of them.

The scale of the burden is best heard as a set of figures that the World Health Organization and its partners publish and revise. Neglected tropical diseases as a group are estimated to affect about 1.7 billion people, which is close to one fifth of the world's population. Soil-transmitted helminths alone infect roughly 1.5 billion people, and about 240 million people are infected with schistosomiasis. Lymphatic filariasis infects about 120 million people and leaves tens of millions with lymphedema or hydrocele. Onchocerciasis infects about 15 million people and puts more than 200 million at risk of infection. Trachoma has left about 1.9 million people blind or visually impaired, and leprosy is still diagnosed in about 200,000 people each year. Dengue causes an estimated 100 million to 400 million infections annually, rabies kills roughly 59,000 people a year, and snakebite envenoming is estimated to kill between 81,000 and 138,000 people a year. Chagas disease affects about 6 million to 7 million people, and human African trypanosomiasis, once a disease of entire districts, has fallen to fewer than 1,000 reported cases a year, though these numbers are estimates with wide uncertainty and they are presented in this course as current guidance rather than as fixed truth.

One figure deserves its own explanation, because it changes how the whole burden is judged, and a disability-adjusted life year is a single unit of health loss that combines two different kinds of harm. The first is the years of life lost when a person dies before the expected age. The second is the years lived with a disability, weighted by how severe that disability is judged to be. One year of healthy life lost is one disability-adjusted life year. A disease that kills many people quickly produces a large total through the first component. A disease that blinds, disfigures, or paralyses a large number of people but rarely kills them produces a large total through the second. The measure was designed to allow governments and donors to compare very different problems, a fatal epidemic against a chronic disfiguring infection, on one scale. It is a blunt instrument, and it has been criticized for putting a numerical value on a life and for systematically undervaluing pain, stigma, and mental illness, but it is the instrument that decisions are actually made with.

The disability-adjusted life year matters to the nurse because it determines which diseases are visible to the people who write budgets. A condition that produces little mortality and much disability can look small in a hospital's death statistics and still consume an enormous amount of human life. Blindness from trachoma, lymphedema from lymphatic filariasis, disfigurement from leishmaniasis, epilepsy from neurocysticercosis, and the chronic heart failure and esophageal disease of Chagas disease all behave this way. They rarely appear on a death certificate as the cause of death, they cost families income and children's schooling, and they carry stigma that can be harder to live with than the physical impairment. Because they are measured poorly, they are funded poorly, and because they are funded poorly, they are measured poorly. Breaking that circle is one of the standing tasks of the field, and it is often nurses and community health workers who supply the data that finally make a local burden impossible to ignore.

Malaria, tuberculosis, and HIV attract most of the money in global health, and the reasons are worth naming plainly. These three kill large numbers of people, they are feared, and their epidemics are legible to wealthy publics in a way that a chronic skin disease is not. Each of them has a global fund, a donor alliance, and a set of well-known targets that can be reported to a parliament. Antiretroviral therapy for HIV turned a fatal infection into a manageable chronic condition and demonstrated that money spent well could change an epidemic, which made donors willing to spend more. Tuberculosis has a long history in wealthy countries, a familiar advocacy community, and the rising threat of drug resistance to hold attention. Malaria has the most effective single intervention in the entire field, the insecticide-treated bed net, and a clear mortality target that can be counted. The World Health Organization estimates roughly 249 million malaria cases and about 608,000 malaria deaths in a recent year, about 10.6 million tuberculosis cases and 1.3 million tuberculosis deaths, and about 39 million people living with HIV, and those numbers command attention.

The neglected diseases attract far less money for reasons that are structural rather than moral. Most of the people affected are poor and politically quiet, so they do not form the advocacy groups that shape budgets in donor countries. There is no large profitable market for a drug that must be given free to people who cannot pay for it, so industry has little commercial reason to invest in research and development. The diseases are spread across many conditions, each with a smaller number of deaths than malaria or tuberculosis, so each looks small on its own even when the group is enormous. The tools that do exist are often old, unglamorous, and cheap, which paradoxically reduces their appeal to donors who want to fund innovation. The counterargument that has changed practice is the rapid impact package, a bundled set of medicines delivered together to whole communities, which treats several diseases at once for a few cents per person per year. That model, and the London Declaration on Neglected Tropical Diseases of 2012, showed that elimination of several of these conditions was achievable within a generation rather than merely aspirational. Progress since then has been real and uneven, and it depends heavily on the nurses and community distributors who actually hand out the tablets.

One Health is the recognition that human health, animal health, and the health of the shared environment are one system, and that interventions aimed at only one part of that system fail in predictable ways. Many of the infections in this course are zoonoses, meaning they pass from animals to people, and their control depends on what happens in the herd, the flock, the rodent population, or the soil before a person ever becomes ill. Rabies persists because of unvaccinated dogs. Brucellosis persists because of unpasteurized milk and infected livestock. Cysticercosis, the cause of much epilepsy in many tropical settings, persists because of the cycle between pigs and human sanitation. Rift Valley fever moves with rainfall, mosquitoes, and livestock, and its outbreaks are announced by animal abortions before human cases appear. Leptospirosis follows rodents and floodwater, and anthrax survives in soil for years. Antimicrobial resistance, which is driven as much by antibiotic use in animals as by use in people, is a One Health problem before it is ever a hospital problem.

The practical consequence of One Health is that surveillance must cross professional boundaries. A livestock officer who reports a sudden cluster of abortions in goats is producing human health intelligence, and a nurse who asks a febrile patient about animals in the household is doing veterinary epidemiology without calling it that. Joint investigations, shared laboratories, and common case definitions between human and animal services are the mechanisms. The World Health Organization and the international animal health agencies have formal agreements to work this way, and the World Health Organization's guidance increasingly assumes it. The nurse's contribution is usually at the household level, where the history of animal contact, the source of milk and water, the presence of rodents, the state of the latrine, and the vaccination status of the family dog are all within reach of a well-asked question. Rinderpest, a disease of cattle, was declared eradicated in 2011, which is a reminder that a well-run animal health program can remove a human problem entirely.

Control, elimination, and eradication are three different goals, and confusing them leads to wasted money and broken promises. Control means reducing a disease to a level that is no longer a public health problem while accepting that transmission continues and that interventions must continue indefinitely. Elimination means reducing to zero the cases arising in a defined geographical area, or reducing the burden below a defined threshold, while the disease still exists elsewhere and the area must remain vigilant. Eradication means the permanent global reduction to zero of the cases of an infection, so that no further intervention is needed. Smallpox is the only human infection that has been eradicated, declared so in 1980, and Guinea worm disease is the closest to following it. Malaria eradication was attempted globally between 1955 and 1969 and failed, and that failure taught the field that eradication requires a tool that works everywhere, a system that reaches everyone, and a parasite with no non-human reservoir. The certification of elimination and the verification of eradication are formal processes run by the World Health Organization, with independent committees that review years of surveillance data rather than accepting a declaration. The distinction has a practical edge, because a program that claims elimination before it has the surveillance to prove it can lose the gains it made.

The coronavirus disease pandemic that began in 2020 struck these programs in a way that few emergencies had before. Mass drug administration campaigns were suspended because they brought crowds together, and bed net distribution was interrupted at the moment the nets were most needed. Measles, yellow fever, and polio vaccination campaigns were paused in dozens of countries, and the children who missed them are now the susceptible cohort of the next outbreak. Tuberculosis notifications fell sharply, which meant that people were not being diagnosed rather than that they were not being infected, and modeling studies predicted large excess deaths from malaria, tuberculosis, and HIV as a result. Health workers were redeployed to pandemic response, supply chains were diverted, and laboratory capacity that had served malaria, tuberculosis, and dengue was reassigned to coronavirus testing. At the same time, the pandemic demonstrated that community health workers, most of them nurses or trained from nursing practice, could deliver surveillance, vaccination, and education at scale, and it strengthened the argument for investing in them. The World Health Organization's road map for neglected tropical diseases from 2021 to 2030 had to be rewritten around these setbacks, and the honest assessment is that several elimination targets slipped by years.

The recovery has been uneven, and the lessons are now part of how the field plans. Programs that had strong community platforms and national ownership restarted quickly, while those that depended on external teams and imported supplies restarted slowly. The pandemic exposed how thin the margin is between a functioning program and a collapsed one, especially where a single supply chain, a single donor, or a single laboratory carries the whole system. It also showed that the same nurse who gives the vaccine, records the case, and answers the community's questions is the most resilient element of the system, because that role does not depend on electricity, reagents, or aircraft. The argument for integrating services, so that one visit can deliver a bed net, a deworming tablet, a vaccine, and a blood pressure check, became much stronger. So did the argument for routine surveillance that can detect an outbreak early and for honest communication that keeps trust when guidance changes. The field emerged from the pandemic with a clearer sense of what it can and cannot protect against.

The nurse sits at the point where all of this is delivered, and that is not a rhetorical flourish but a description of the system. In most low-resource settings, the nurse is the first clinician a patient sees, the person who takes the history, examines the patient, decides whether a test is needed, gives the first dose, and decides whether to refer. In many districts, the nurse is also the person who manages the cold chain, orders the supplies, supervises community distributors, fills the surveillance forms, and trains the village health worker. When a mass campaign runs, the nurse often trains the distributors, checks the dosing, monitors for adverse effects, and manages the rare severe reaction. When a patient with tuberculosis misses an appointment, it is usually the nurse who traces that patient, and when a child returns with the same fever a second time, it is usually the nurse who recognizes that the first diagnosis was wrong. The nurse is also the person who hears the reasons patients do not return, which is the information that no national program can collect from a distance. That information is what turns a campaign into a program and a program into a durable system.

Where these programs touch treatment, the numbers matter and they are spoken plainly. For lymphatic filariasis, current guidance from the World Health Organization sets out a single dose of albendazole 400 milligrams by mouth given with either ivermectin 150 to 200 micrograms per kilogram or diethylcarbamazine 6 milligrams per kilogram, repeated once a year for several years. For trachoma, azithromycin is given as a single dose of 20 milligrams per kilogram by mouth, up to a maximum of 1 gram. For schistosomiasis, praziquantel is given at 40 milligrams per kilogram by mouth, usually as a single treatment in mass campaigns. These regimens are revised as safety data, resistance patterns, and elimination targets change, guidance differs between countries and between programs, and the current national guideline must always be checked before any medicine is prescribed or dispensed. This is not a formality, because a mass campaign acts on whole populations at once, so an error in a dose or an overlooked contraindication is multiplied by thousands. The nurse who verifies the dose against the current protocol, who checks whether a person is pregnant or has a condition that forbids the drug, and who records what was given is the last real safeguard in that chain.

This chapter's themes converge on the nurse for reasons that are both clinical and structural. Neglect is not only a matter of money; it is also a matter of attention, and attention is created when someone records accurately, names a problem clearly, and refuses to accept that a preventable disability is ordinary. The nurse who documents the number of snakebites in a district, the nurse who reports an adverse reaction to a mass treatment, and the nurse who notices that the same village keeps sending children with the same condition is producing the evidence that changes policy. Task-sharing increasingly expands what nurses do, including independent prescribing and management of defined conditions under standing orders and clinical protocols, which is why the chapters that follow return repeatedly to algorithms, documentation, and supervision. In a field where physicians are scarce and distances are long, the quality of nursing practice is not a secondary consideration; it is the main determinant of whether a patient survives, recovers, or is disabled for life. The nurse who understands the history of this discipline, why its patients were neglected, and how the goals of control, elimination, and eradication differ is better equipped to explain to a community why a program exists, why a campaign will be repeated, and why the last case matters.

To summarize, tropical medicine is the practice of the infections that poverty assembles, and its geographical name conceals the fact that housing, water, sanitation, crowding, nutrition, and access to care determine who is exposed and who is protected. The field grew out of colonial and military medicine, was reshaped by the sleeping sickness campaigns and the Rockefeller Foundation, and then reoriented in the twentieth century toward primary health care and the recognition that poverty rather than latitude is the fundamental cause. The neglected tropical diseases are the conditions that are unprofitable to treat, roughly 20 of them on the World Health Organization's list, and they account for disability and disfigurement on a scale that a mortality count alone will always hide. The disability-adjusted life year is the measure that makes this harm visible and therefore fundable, and it explains part of the reason malaria, tuberculosis, and HIV attract resources while the chronic infections do not. One Health reminds the listener that human, animal, and environmental health are a single system, while control, elimination, and eradication are three distinct promises that require different tools, different surveillance, and different timelines. The pandemic that began in 2020 interrupted all of it, exposed how fragile the delivery systems were, and proved again that the community health worker and the nurse are the part of the system that keeps working. Because every one of these programs meets its patient at a clinic, a school, or a household through a nurse, the scope, history, and burden described here are not background to practice; they are the reason the practice exists.
