# Appendix A — Drug Formulary and Dosing: How to Read It, and What to Verify

This appendix gathers the medicines used throughout this book and speaks their doses aloud, class by class. It is a formulary in narrative form rather than a table, because a table invites copying while a sentence invites thought. For each drug it names what the drug treats, the adult and pediatric doses, the route, the frequency, the adverse effects that matter and the monitoring that keeps the patient safe. Every dose is offered as what current guidance says and not as settled truth, because regimens are revised, guidance differs between countries, and the guideline in force where you practice must be checked before anything is prescribed. The World Health Organization, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control publish the guidance this appendix follows.

Dose is never a single number that travels unchanged from one patient to the next. Weight, age, pregnancy, breastfeeding, kidney and liver function, co-infection and the indication all move the number, and the same drug may treat at one dose and prevent at another. Because a child is not a small adult and many drugs are dosed by weight bands, an accurate scale and a current weight chart are clinical instruments rather than clerical ones. Formulation matters too, because a tablet that cannot be crushed, a capsule taken with fat and a syrup measured in milliliters each demand a different instruction. Resistance redraws the map continuously, so the local guideline always outranks a remembered dose.

The antimalarials come first, and the artemisinin-based combination therapies are the backbone of treatment for uncomplicated falciparum malaria nearly everywhere. Each pairs a fast-acting artemisinin derivative with a slower partner drug that removes the residue and guards against resistance. Artemether with lumefantrine is the most widely used, and the adult dose is 4 tablets by mouth at the start, again 8 hours later, then twice a day for 2 more days, taken with food that contains fat. The pediatric dose rises from 1 tablet at 5 to less than 15 kilograms to 4 tablets above 35 kilograms. The common effects are headache, dizziness, nausea, vomiting and sleep disturbance, and any dose vomited within 30 minutes is repeated.

Severe malaria is treated with artesunate given intravenously, and here a drug changes the risk of death by a measurable margin. The adult dose is 2.4 milligrams per kilogram intravenously at the start, again at 12 hours, again at 24 hours, and then once a day until the patient can swallow tablets. Children under 20 kilograms receive 3 milligrams per kilogram per dose because they clear the drug faster. The powder is dissolved in its supplied solvent and given as a slow injection over 1 to 2 minutes, because a rapid push can drop the blood pressure. Destruction of red cells after recovery can cause anemia 1 to 3 weeks later, so a blood count is repeated in follow-up and a full course of oral combination therapy always follows.

Chloroquine still has a narrow place, defined by geography rather than habit. It remains effective against Plasmodium vivax in most of the world outside Oceania and parts of Southeast Asia, and it clears the blood but never touches the dormant liver forms. The adult treatment dose is 25 milligrams of chloroquine base per kilogram over 3 days, usually 10 milligrams per kilogram on the first day, 10 on the second and 5 on the third, all by mouth. Its effects include itching, nausea, headache and blurred vision, and prolonged high doses can damage the retina permanently. Overdose causes profound low blood pressure, rhythm disturbance and cardiac arrest, so the nurse confirms the species and the local resistance pattern before the first tablet.

The dormant liver forms of Plasmodium vivax and Plasmodium ovale need a drug that reaches where the other antimalarials cannot. Primaquine is given by mouth at 0.25 to 0.5 milligrams per kilogram once a day for 14 days for radical cure, and a single low dose of 0.25 milligrams per kilogram kills the gametocytes of falciparum malaria. Tafenoquine is given as a single 300 milligram dose by mouth in adults, and one dose removes the adherence problem of 14 days of tablets. Both can destroy red cells dangerously in people with glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency, so testing is required first, and both are avoided in pregnancy and in infants under 6 months. Both also cause nausea, abdominal pain and headache, so the nurse confirms the enzyme result and warns that the drug must not be restarted without a new test.

Three further drugs serve both treatment and prevention. Atovaquone with proguanil is taken by mouth with food as 4 adult tablets once a day for 3 days to treat uncomplicated malaria, with weight bands from 1 tablet at 11 kilograms to 4 tablets above 40 kilograms, while prevention uses 1 tablet daily from 1 to 2 days before exposure until 7 days after leaving. Mefloquine is taken at 250 milligrams once a week for prevention, starting 1 to 2 weeks before travel, and it is not used to treat an established infection. It can cause vivid dreams, insomnia, anxiety, depression and rarely psychosis, and it is avoided in people with epilepsy or psychiatric illness. Doxycycline is taken at 100 milligrams once a day by mouth for prevention, continuing for 4 weeks after leaving, and it causes sensitivity to sunlight and irritation of the esophagus, so it is taken with a full glass of water.

The antileishmanial drugs treat a parasite that hides inside the cells of the immune system, and the choice depends on the form of the disease, the country and what is in stock. Liposomal amphotericin B is preferred for visceral leishmaniasis in many programs, given at 3 milligrams per kilogram intravenously on days 1, 3, 5 and 7, while some national guidelines use a single 10 milligram per kilogram dose. The conventional deoxycholate form is given at 0.5 to 1 milligram per kilogram once a day intravenously over several hours, and it is much more likely to damage the kidneys and to cause fever, chills and muscle pain. The nurse follows the creatinine, the potassium and the magnesium and watches the fluid balance closely. Amphotericin B also treats severe histoplasmosis and cryptococcal disease, so the same monitoring discipline serves several chapters.

Miltefosine is the only oral antileishmanial in common use, given at 2.5 milligrams per kilogram per day by mouth in 2 or 3 divided doses for 28 days, with a maximum of 150 milligrams per day. It is teratogenic, so it is never given in pregnancy, and women of childbearing age must use effective contraception. Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea and a rise in creatinine are common, and the drug is taken with food to reduce the stomach upset. Sodium stibogluconate is given at 20 milligrams of antimony per kilogram per day intravenously or intramuscularly for 20 to 28 days, and it can disturb the heart rhythm, inflame the pancreas and injure the liver and kidneys. Paromomycin is given at 15 milligrams per kilogram per day intramuscularly for 21 days in some regions, and the electrocardiogram, the pancreatic enzymes, the liver enzymes, the renal function and the hearing are checked before and during treatment.

The trypanocides are few, old and difficult. Benznidazole is the first choice for Chagas disease in most settings and is given by mouth at 5 to 7 milligrams per kilogram per day in 2 divided doses for 60 days, with food. The effects that stop treatment most often are a skin rash, which may involve the mucous membranes, and a peripheral neuropathy that begins as tingling in the hands and feet and can become permanent if the drug is continued. Nifurtimox is the alternative, given by mouth at 8 to 10 milligrams per kilogram per day in 3 or 4 divided doses for 60 to 90 days, and it more often causes loss of appetite, weight loss, nausea, insomnia and neuropathy. Blood counts fall with both drugs, so a count is checked during the course, and the nurse warns about the rash in advance because a patient who expects it reports it early.

Human African trypanosomiasis is treated according to the species and the stage, and that distinction matters more than any other decision. Pentamidine is used for the early stage of the West African form at 4 milligrams per kilogram once a day intramuscularly or intravenously for 7 to 10 days, and it can make the blood sugar fall and then rise, drop the blood pressure and injure the kidney. Suramin treats the early stage of the East African form, beginning with a test dose of 100 to 200 milligrams intravenously, followed by 1 gram on days 1, 3, 5 and 7 and then once a week for 5 weeks. Eflornithine crosses into the brain and is given at 100 milligrams per kilogram intravenously every 6 hours for 14 days alone, or at 200 milligrams per kilogram every 12 hours for 7 days with nifurtimox, and it can suppress the bone marrow and cause seizures. Fexinidazole is an oral drug for the late stage of the West African form, given at 1.8 grams once a day for 10 days in adults above 35 kilograms and at lower weight-based doses in children. Confirming the stage, the species and the weight before the first dose prevents the most serious error in this family, which is treating a brain-stage infection with a drug that never reaches the brain.

The antifilarial drugs are given to whole communities as well as to individuals. Ivermectin is given by mouth as a single dose of 150 to 200 micrograms per kilogram, repeated once a year or every 6 to 12 months for lymphatic filariasis and onchocerciasis. It must not be given to a person heavily infected with Loa loa, because the rapid death of those parasites can inflame the brain and sometimes kill, and it is avoided in pregnancy and in children under 5 years or under 15 kilograms in most programs. Albendazole is given at 400 milligrams by mouth as a single dose alongside ivermectin or diethylcarbamazine in elimination programs, and diethylcarbamazine is given at 6 milligrams per kilogram per day by mouth in divided doses for 12 days. All three can produce the reaction that follows the death of the worms, with fever, itching, rash, headache, dizziness and joint pain, and this is expected, so the patient is warned and supported with fluids, rest and a simple analgesic.

The anthelmintics are among the safest medicines in this appendix, and the main risk is not the drug but the missed diagnosis. Albendazole is given as a single 400 milligram dose by mouth for roundworm, hookworm and whipworm infection, with 200 milligrams for children between 12 and 24 months, and mebendazole is given as 100 milligrams twice a day for 3 days, or as a single 500 milligram dose for hookworm and roundworm. Praziquantel is given at 40 milligrams per kilogram by mouth in 1 or 2 doses for schistosomiasis, at 60 milligrams per kilogram per day in 3 divided doses for 3 days for neurocysticercosis, and at 25 milligrams per kilogram 3 times a day for 2 days for food-borne trematode infections. Praziquantel can cause abdominal pain, nausea, headache, dizziness and drowsiness, so patients are advised not to drive on the day of treatment. Triclabendazole is the drug of choice for fascioliasis at 10 milligrams per kilogram by mouth once or twice, and niclosamide treats the adult tapeworms at 2 grams by mouth for adults and 50 milligrams per kilogram for children. Prolonged high-dose albendazole requires blood count and liver monitoring, and the nurse weighs the patient, checks for pregnancy and treats the whole household when the infection is a household problem.

The antiprotozoal drugs treat the intestinal and genital protozoa, and metronidazole carries the widest range of uses. Metronidazole is given by mouth at 500 milligrams 3 times a day for 7 to 10 days for giardiasis, at 750 milligrams 3 times a day for 7 to 10 days for intestinal amebiasis, at 500 milligrams every 8 hours intravenously for severe amebic colitis or liver abscess, and as a single 2 gram dose for trichomoniasis. The pediatric dose is usually 15 to 30 milligrams per kilogram per day in divided doses, depending on the indication. It causes a metallic taste, nausea and headache, and it produces a severe reaction with alcohol that includes flushing, vomiting, palpitations and low blood pressure, so alcohol must be avoided during treatment and for 3 days afterward. Tinidazole is given as a single 2 gram dose for giardiasis and trichomoniasis, or 2 grams once a day for 3 to 5 days for amebiasis, and nitazoxanide is given at 500 milligrams by mouth every 12 hours for 3 days in adults, with 100 milligrams for children 1 to 3 years old and 200 milligrams for children 4 to 11 years old.

The antibiotics recur across this book in roles far beyond their usual hospital uses, and two of them deserve special attention. Doxycycline is the treatment of choice for rickettsial disease at 100 milligrams by mouth twice a day for 7 to 14 days, for leptospirosis at 100 milligrams twice a day for 7 days and as a single 300 milligram dose for cholera, and it is also used for plague. It is generally avoided in young children, but when a serious rickettsial infection is the alternative, the risk of the infection outweighs the risk to the teeth. Azithromycin is given as a single 1 gram dose by mouth for cholera, at 500 milligrams once a day for 3 days for shigellosis and as a single dose of 20 to 30 milligrams per kilogram for yaws in children. Its main effects are nausea, abdominal pain and diarrhea, it is one of the safer antibiotics in pregnancy, and for both drugs the nurse checks the weight, the pregnancy status and the interval before the next dose.

Three more antibiotics complete the recurring set, and each has a specific niche in tropical practice. Ceftriaxone is given at 1 to 2 grams intravenously once a day in adults for severe bacterial infection, at 2 grams once a day for 7 to 14 days for typhoid fever when the organism is susceptible, and at 50 to 75 milligrams per kilogram per day in children, while a single 500 milligram intramuscular dose treats gonorrhea. Ciprofloxacin is given at 500 milligrams by mouth twice a day for 3 days for cholera and for 7 to 14 days for typhoid fever where the organism remains susceptible, and it can cause tendon pain and rupture, especially in older patients and those taking corticosteroids. Cotrimoxazole, the combination of trimethoprim and sulfamethoxazole, is given at 960 milligrams twice a day by mouth for common bacterial infections, at weight-based doses for childhood pneumonia and at 960 milligrams once a day to prevent opportunistic infection in people with HIV infection. It can cause rash, bone marrow suppression and a dangerous rise in potassium, so the patient is taught to report a rash at once. For all three the nurse confirms the indication, the weight, the renal function and the local resistance pattern before the first dose.

The antituberculous drugs are always used in combination, because a single drug selects resistance within weeks, and the standard regimen for drug-susceptible tuberculosis begins with 2 months of isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide and ethambutol, followed by 4 months of isoniazid and rifampicin. Isoniazid is given at 5 milligrams per kilogram per day in adults, usually 300 milligrams, and at 10 to 15 milligrams per kilogram per day in children, and it can cause a peripheral neuropathy that is prevented with pyridoxine, as well as liver injury. Rifampicin is given at 10 milligrams per kilogram per day in adults, usually 600 milligrams, and at 15 milligrams per kilogram in children, and it turns urine, sweat and tears orange, injures the liver and interferes with many other drugs, including antiretroviral therapy for HIV infection. Pyrazinamide is given at 20 to 25 milligrams per kilogram per day in adults and 30 to 40 milligrams per kilogram in children, and it causes liver injury, joint pain and a rise in uric acid, while ethambutol at 15 to 20 milligrams per kilogram per day can damage the optic nerve, so vision is checked before treatment. Multidrug-resistant tuberculosis requires a longer regimen with second-line drugs such as bedaquiline, linezolid and clofazimine, and the nurse who watches adherence and side effects is as important to the outcome as the prescription itself.

The antifungals in this book treat the deep mycoses that follow inhalation or implantation of a fungus, and the three that recur are itraconazole, ketoconazole and amphotericin B. Itraconazole is given by mouth at 200 milligrams once or twice a day for 3 months or longer for mild to moderate histoplasmosis and paracoccidioidomycosis, and the capsule is taken with food while the liquid preparation is taken on an empty stomach. It can injure the liver and the heart and it interacts with a long list of medicines, and where the laboratory can measure the level of the drug in the blood, that level guides the dose. Ketoconazole is given at 200 to 400 milligrams by mouth once a day for prolonged periods in some settings, but it can injure the liver, suppress the adrenal glands and disturb the hormones, so it has largely been replaced. Amphotericin B is given intravenously at 0.5 to 1.5 milligrams per kilogram once a day, or as a liposomal preparation at 3 to 5 milligrams per kilogram once a day, and it causes fever, chills, kidney injury and losses of potassium and magnesium. The nurse checks the liver enzymes, the renal function, the electrolytes and the drug interactions, and teaches the patient how and when to take each formulation.

Antivenom is unlike every other medicine here, because the dose is not calculated from the weight of the patient but from the amount of venom that must be neutralized. The dose is the same for a child and an adult, the drug is given intravenously for systemic envenomation and never intramuscularly, and the number of vials depends on the product, the species and the severity of the poisoning. Because antivenoms differ between regions and manufacturers, the nurse reads the label of the product in hand, and the national protocol and the poison center are consulted whenever there is doubt. The most feared reactions are immediate anaphylaxis and a delayed serum sickness with fever, rash and joint pain, so the patient is watched closely during the infusion, with adrenaline, oxygen and resuscitation equipment at the bedside. The antivenom is only one part of care, because the airway, the breathing, the circulation, the coagulation and the wound all need attention at the same time, and the nurse records the time of the bite, the product and lot and every reaction.

The unglamorous parts of a formulary decide whether a drug ever reaches the patient. Most of these medicines need protection from heat, light and moisture, and some need a cold chain verified daily with a thermometer rather than assumed from the presence of a refrigerator. Stock is rotated so that the earliest expiry is used first, and a drug that is expired, discolored or unlabeled is not a drug, whatever the box says. Counterfeit and substandard medicines are a real danger in many settings, and the nurse protects patients by ordering through the national supply system, checking packaging and batch numbers and reporting anything that seems wrong. The formulation matters in the field as much as in the prescription, because a tablet that cannot be divided accurately, a syrup without a measuring cup and an injectable without the correct diluent each turn a correct dose into an incorrect one. A stock-out is a clinical event rather than an administrative one, and it must be anticipated, reported and planned for with an alternative regimen in advance.

Monitoring is where the nurse turns a prescription into safe care, and it begins before the first dose with the weight, the allergies, the pregnancy and breastfeeding status, the renal and liver function and the exact indication. The baseline tests differ by drug, but the common set includes the blood count, the glucose, the creatinine, the liver enzymes and the electrolytes, and for some drugs an electrocardiogram or a vision check. During treatment the nurse watches for the specific adverse effect each drug is known to cause, such as the rash of benznidazole, the neuropathy of the trypanocides, the low blood sugar of pentamidine, the visual change of ethambutol, the hearing loss of the aminoglycosides and the kidney injury of amphotericin B. Every dose is verified against the current national guideline and the product label, and high-risk doses are calculated independently by two people, with the units written out in full before the arithmetic is done. The nurse documents the dose, the route, the time, the lot number and any reaction, teaches the patient what to expect and what to report, and arranges the follow-up test before the patient leaves. Where treatment is observed directly, the person who observes the dose records it honestly, because a signature that does not reflect a swallowed tablet is worse than no record at all.

To summarize, this appendix is meant to be used with your ears and your judgment rather than with your memory alone, because a dose recalled without its guideline is a dose that can harm. Every figure spoken here is what the World Health Organization, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control have published as current guidance, and every one is subject to revision as resistance shifts and new evidence arrives. The habit that keeps patients safe is to confirm the indication, weigh the patient, check the allergy, pregnancy and organ function, read the current national guideline, write the units in full and verify the calculation before the drug is given. The nurse who does that, who monitors the effect each drug is known to cause, who warns the patient about the rash or the dizziness or the orange urine and who documents the lot and the reaction is doing the work that turns a formulary into medicine. Guidance differs between countries and is revised without announcement, so a dose that was correct last year may be wrong today.
