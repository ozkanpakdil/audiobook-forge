# Chapter 41 — Emergency Obstetrics and Newborn Care

This chapter is about births that go well and births that do not, because across much of the tropical world the only skilled person at a delivery is a nurse. Around a quarter of a million women still die each year from causes connected to childbirth, and more than 2 million babies die within their first month of life. Those deaths concentrate in the poorest places, and most follow complications that a nurse with knowledge, drugs, and steady hands can prevent or survive. The World Health Organization counts the direct killers of birthing women as severe bleeding, pre-eclampsia and eclampsia, obstructed labor, infection, and the complications of unsafe abortion. Hear the warning before anything else: regimens are revised, guidance differs between countries, and every dose in this chapter reflects current World Health Organization recommendations, so verify the current guideline for your setting before you prescribe or act.

Preparation is a clinical skill, and it lives on the shelf. Keep the delivery pack complete in one place: clean gloves, soap and water or alcohol rub, a sterile blade, cord ties or clamps, gauze, and two warm cloths for drying the baby. Count weekly the drugs this chapter names, oxytocin, ergometrine, misoprostol, magnesium sulfate, calcium gluconate, tranexamic acid, dexamethasone, and intravenous antibiotics, and remember that oxytocin and ergometrine weaken in heat, so store them in the coolest corner of the drug room and replace stock that has survived a hot season. Check daily what cannot be improvised: a newborn bag and mask in two sizes, suction, a blood pressure cuff, a thermometer, a scale, a watch, a fetoscope, and a light that works when the power does not. Keep partographs, the delivery register, and the referral plan within reach of the bed, and know your blood situation before nightfall, who could donate, where the nearest screened blood waits, and what you will do if there is none.

Every emergency here is easier to see if you know normal labor first, because normal is the pattern that emergencies break. True labor is contractions that grow stronger, longer, and closer together, that do not settle with rest, and that steadily open the cervix. The first stage runs from those contractions to full dilation at ten centimeters, its active phase counted from about four to six centimeters, the second runs from full dilation to the birth, the third ends when the placenta is out, and the fourth covers the first two hours afterward, when most bleeding happens. When a woman arrives, take her story, her age, her earlier births, any past bleeding, fits, or operations, her antenatal visits and test results, and her tetanus immunization. Then examine her: feel the abdomen for the baby's position and whether the head has settled into the pelvis, listen to the fetal heart, time the contractions, record temperature, pulse, and blood pressure, and do a vaginal examination only when it will change what you do, making it answer how open the cervix is, how low the head lies, whether the waters have gone, and whether the scalp is swelling or the skull bones are overriding.

The partograph is the most useful piece of paper in this chapter: a graph of labor drawn against time. On it you plot the opening of the cervix in centimeters against the clock, alongside the fetal heart, the strength and frequency of contractions, and the mother's pulse, blood pressure, temperature, and urine. An alert line marks the progress a healthy labor should make, about one centimeter per hour, and an action line sits two to four hours behind it. If the plot crosses the alert line your vigilance rises, and if it reaches the action line labor is failing and you act, usually by referring, hours before instinct would tell you. In active labor, listen to the fetal heart at least every 30 minutes, count contractions over ten minutes at the same interval, and record the pulse each round, because the discipline of the partograph is that it makes you look, write what you saw, and then see what you wrote.

Through most of the first stage your work is presence, not intervention. Encourage her to drink by mouth, to pass urine every two hours, because a full bladder blocks descent and tires the uterine muscle, and to change position freely. Welcome a companion of her choosing, which measurably shortens labor, and watch for the warnings: bleeding before the birth, a fetal heart below 100 or above 180 beats per minute, meconium in the waters with an abnormal heart, fever of 38 degrees Celsius or more, and the plot drifting toward the action line. Waiting is right when observations stay normal and the plot keeps moving forward. Acting is right the moment a warning appears, and the action may be as simple as repositioning the woman and calling the referral center while there is still daylight and fuel.

The second stage announces itself: the cervix is fully open, the woman feels an involuntary urge to push, and the contractions are long and strong. Support her to push with the contractions in whatever position she finds strongest, to breathe and rest between them, while you check the fetal heart between contractions every five minutes or so. Do not hurry a normal second stage, and do not ignore a stalled one, because an hour of strong pushing with no visible descent means labor is failing, usually from a mismatch between the head and the pelvis. The signs of a baby in trouble are a heart rate that stays slow after the contraction ends, thick meconium, or a mother whose strength suddenly collapses. Decide the plan before you need it, who calls for help, who stays at the mother, and who receives the baby.

The birth of the head rewards gentle hands. Wash and glove, and let the head emerge slowly, ideally between contractions, one hand controlling the crown so it does not spring out and tear the perineum, the other supporting the tissue beneath. As the head is born, feel quickly for a cord around the neck: if it is loose, slip it over the head or under the body, and if it is tight, clamp it with two ties and cut between them before the shoulders come. The head then rests and turns to one side, telling you the shoulders are rotating to fit, so with the next contraction guide a gentle downward pull to free the anterior shoulder, then an upward one for the posterior, and the body follows easily. Routine episiotomy is not recommended by the World Health Organization, because a controlled natural birth tears less than fear suggests, and a cut is right only when the baby is distressed or a tear is heading toward the rectum.

The first minute after birth is shared work, because drying the baby and watching the mother happen at once. Dry the baby head to toe with a warm cloth, swap the wet cloth for a dry one, and lay the baby skin to skin on the chest with a hat and a cover, the rubbing itself enough stimulation for most babies to cry. Delay clamping the cord for one to three minutes, until it stops pulsating, unless the baby needs resuscitation, in which case clamp, cut, and move to the warm surface without delay. Cut with a clean instrument, leave the stump dry and open to the air, and put the baby to the breast within the first hour, because suckling releases the mother's own oxytocin and helps the uterus clamp down. Meanwhile someone is watching her: the color of her face, her blood pressure, and the trickle of blood that means the third stage is moving.

Manage the third stage actively, because that is where hemorrhage is prevented, and the physiology explains the steps. The placenta separates as the uterus shrinks, and the signs are a small gush of blood, a sudden lengthening of the cord, and a firm, globular uterus. Within one minute of the birth give 10 units of oxytocin by intramuscular injection, the uterotonic the World Health Organization lists first for this purpose, then wait for the signs of separation before touching the cord. Perform controlled cord traction only with trained hands: clamp the cord and hold it in one hand, rest the other just above the pubic bone to support the uterus, and pull the cord steadily downward and outward with a contraction, because traction without that counter support can turn the uterus inside out. Twist the membranes as the placenta comes so they trail out whole, then massage the top of the uterus firmly every 15 minutes for two hours, and if you have never been supervised in cord traction, your guideline permits oxytocin and massage alone, done honestly and on time.

Examine the placenta every time, as though a life hangs on it, because one does. Lay it flat, fit the cotyledons together like a jigsaw, look for a missing piece or torn vessels, and confirm the cord carries two arteries and one vein. Measure the blood loss deliberately: collect pads and swabs and weigh them if you have a scale, since one gram of added weight is about one milliliter of blood, and train your eye with measured amounts, because the distance between 500 and 1000 milliliters is the distance between watching and fighting. Then run the fourth stage as a schedule, not a feeling: every 15 minutes for two hours, feel the uterus, count the pulse, take the blood pressure, look at the bleeding, and check that she has passed urine. Most fatal hemorrhage occurs within the first one to four hours, so the quiet hour after a birth is a watch, not a rest, and the baby at the breast is doing pharmacology for you.

Postpartum hemorrhage means bleeding of more than 500 milliliters within 24 hours of a vaginal birth, and more than 1000 milliliters is severe; it is the leading direct cause of maternal death, responsible for roughly a quarter of such deaths worldwide. The mechanism dictates the response. At term, blood crosses the placenta at nearly 500 milliliters per minute, and when the placenta peels away, the flow is stopped not by clot but by muscle, interlacing uterine fibers strangling the vessels like living ligatures. If the uterus stays soft, a raw surface the size of a dinner plate bleeds into the vagina and the cavity, and a mother can lose a fatal liter of blood in fifteen minutes. Hold the causes as one sentence: the uterus is soft, the birth canal is torn, placental tissue is left inside, or the blood will not clot, and the soft uterus, called atony, causes about seven of every ten cases, which is why your first move is always the same.

Run the response as a sequence you could drill blindfolded. Shout for help, set a clock, rub the top of the uterus with your knuckles until it feels firm, empty the bladder with a catheter because no uterus contracts well against a full one, and put the baby to the breast. Give 10 units of oxytocin intramuscularly and run an intravenous infusion of 20 units in one liter of fluid at 60 drops per minute, doubling the rate until the uterus tightens, and never push oxytocin straight into a vein, because a bolus drops the blood pressure. If bleeding continues, add ergometrine 0.2 milligrams intramuscularly, never in a woman with high blood pressure or pre-eclampsia, and never while the placenta is still inside, because it can seal the uterus around it. Where no oxytocin exists, misoprostol 800 micrograms under the tongue is the substitute, and the shivering and fever of the first hour are expected effects, not allergy. Tranexamic acid, 1 gram intravenously over ten minutes, is part of World Health Organization guidance when bleeding is heavy: give it as early as possible within three hours of the birth, repeated once after 30 minutes if the bleeding has not stopped. Meanwhile run two wide cannulas with fluids, warm her, elevate her legs, and write the times as you work.

If the uterus is firm and bleeding still runs, the cause is not atony, and your hands must find it. With good light and a second pair of gloves, inspect in order the labia, the vaginal walls, and the cervix in a full circle, and repair what you see with absorbable sutures. If the uterus stays soft despite the drugs, hold bimanual compression: make a fist inside the vagina against the front of the uterus, press the other hand on the abdomen behind the fundus, and squeeze the uterus between them until the drugs catch up. The improvised balloon tamponade, described many times in humanitarian practice, fills the uterus from inside: push a urinary catheter into a condom, tie the base watertight with thread, insert it sterile into the uterus, inflate it with 250 to 500 milliliters of clean water, and leave it 12 to 24 hours with antibiotic cover. Built from two cheap items, that balloon has saved more lives in remote hospitals than any machine you are still waiting for.

A placenta that has not come within 30 minutes of active management is retained, and the clock is now against her. Give another dose of oxytocin, empty the bladder, encourage suckling, and repeat controlled cord traction only when the signs of separation appear, because dragging on a placenta that is partly stuck snaps the cord and can invert the uterus. If an hour has passed since the birth she needs manual removal, which asks a trained hand, sterile technique, pain relief, and usually antibiotic prophylaxis, and for most rural posts that means referral. An incomplete placenta is its own emergency, because a fragment left behind keeps the uterus soft and bleeding and can infect her days later. Whenever a woman leaves after a difficult third stage, send her intravenous line running, her notes in her hand, and her escort briefed on the sentence that matters at the other end.

Pre-eclampsia is the disease that kills quietly before it kills loudly. It is new blood pressure at or above 140 over 90 millimeters of mercury after twenty weeks of pregnancy, with protein in the urine, and it is severe at 160 over 110, or at lower pressures with the danger signs. The mechanism in plain words: the placenta implants too shallowly, its arteries stay narrow, and distress signals from it make the mother's vessels clamp down everywhere, so pressure climbs, the kidneys leak protein, the brain grows irritable, the liver swells and aches, and the lungs can flood. That is why the questions at every antenatal contact never change: severe headache, blurred vision or flashing lights, pain under the right ribs, swelling of face and hands, sudden weight gain, and the baby moving less. Take the blood pressure of every pregnant woman at every visit, even the quick ones, and dipstick the urine, because the headache she calls nothing at term may be her last words before a convulsion.

The drug that changed this disease is magnesium sulfate, and most low-resource programs use the intramuscular regimen carried in World Health Organization guidance. Give 4 grams intravenously, diluted in fluid, over five to ten minutes, and at the same time 10 grams deep intramuscularly, 5 grams into each buttock, mixed with a little lidocaine in the same syringe to make it bearable. Continue with 5 grams intramuscularly every four hours, alternating buttocks, for 24 hours after the birth or the last fit, and give each dose only if three checks pass: her knee reflexes are present, her breathing is at least 16 per minute, and her urine flows at least 30 milliliters per hour, which is why she needs a catheter and an hourly chart. Toxicity arrives in order, the reflexes go, the breathing slows below 16, and then come the flush, the drowsiness, and the arrest, and the antidote is calcium gluconate, 10 milliliters of a 10 per cent solution intravenously over five to ten minutes. If she fits again give 2 grams intravenously over five to ten minutes, because the wonder of magnesium is that it prevents fits in most of the women who would otherwise have them, and the discipline of magnesium is the counting, since the distance between cure and poison is a rate.

Blood pressure control is gentler than instinct suggests. Severe pressures, at or above 160 over 110 millimeters of mercury, come down with drugs the World Health Organization lists: hydralazine 5 milligrams intravenously, repeated every 20 minutes up to a total of 20 milligrams, or labetalol 20 milligrams intravenously over ten minutes followed by 40 and then 80 if needed, or, where no intravenous drug exists, nifedipine 10 milligrams by mouth repeated after half an hour. The aim is to leave the danger zone, not to normalize the number, because a sudden fall starves the placenta and distresses the fetus. Between emergencies, methyldopa 250 milligrams by mouth three times a day, or labetalol by mouth, holds the pressure as your guideline directs, and fluids are given with an open eye, because these women flood easily and a patient with wet lungs and scant urine has a failing kidney, not thirst. Hold the whole picture in view, because the only cure for pre-eclampsia is delivery of the placenta, so once she is stable plan the birth, and if it is likely to be preterm, between 24 and 34 weeks, give dexamethasone 6 milligrams intramuscularly every 12 hours, four doses, to mature the baby's lungs.

If she fits, your first acts are the airway and the drug: roll her onto her side, restrain nothing, force nothing into her mouth, suction only what blocks the throat, and start the magnesium. Eclampsia does not respect the delivery, because fits come before labor, during it, and up to 48 hours after birth, so a headache after delivery is still an emergency. Once she is conscious and stable she is delivered, and the magnesium continues for a full day after. Her dangers in the following hours are cerebral hemorrhage from unchecked pressure, lung edema from enthusiastic fluids, kidney failure, and placental abruption, the placenta tearing loose with the baby's blood loss hidden inside her, and the fetal heart is your window onto that last one. The women who die of this disease mostly die of delay, a headache minimized at one post and a fit arriving on the road.

Obstructed labor is labor that cannot succeed, and the partograph is how you meet it early. The head is too big for the pelvis, lies in a bad position, or a shoulder blocks the way, and the picture is strong contractions with a cervix that has stopped opening and a baby that has stopped descending. The clues gather one by one: the plot crosses the alert line and then the action line, the scalp swells into a soft bump, the skull bones override with molding you can feel growing hour by hour, the bladder will not empty by itself, the mother turns exhausted, dehydrated, and feverish, and the fetal heart slows again and again as the head is crushed. The last warning is a groove rising between the upper and lower parts of the uterus, a band you can see and feel, which means the wall is stretched toward tearing, and when you can put a hand on that groove, waiting is over.

Now the honest part: what to do when the operation that fixes obstruction is a day away by road. The delay changes the plan but not the nursing, because she still needs an intravenous line running, a catheter to watch her urine, nothing by mouth, position changes, and antibiotics if her waters have been open for many hours or she has fever, since sepsis travels with obstruction. The delay forbids the temptation: never give oxytocin to augment an obstructed labor, because the drug can rupture the uterus, and a rupture on a road turns a rescue into a funeral. If the head is low and the failure is in the second stage, an operator trained in the vacuum may finish the birth, but a vacuum pulled by an untrained hand tears the baby's scalp, so know your level and say it aloud. Arrange transport at the first crossing of the action line, with an escort who knows the story, a referral letter in her hand, a call made ahead, and the road driven in daylight where possible. Sometimes the baby has already died by the time the decision is made, and then every step serves the mother's survival, because the birth must still happen somewhere, and the grief needs a plan too.

Turn now to the newborn, and hold resuscitation as a drill, because its steps are few enough to practice until they happen by themselves. The first minute after birth decides more than the next hour, and the World Health Organization's basic newborn resuscitation course, like programs called Helping Babies Breathe, builds everything around that minute. Before every birth lay out a warm dry surface, two warm cloths, a bag and mask in newborn size, suction, and a working light, and squeeze the bag against your palm to prove it works. The sequence: dry the baby at once and thoroughly, swap the wet cloth for a dry one, position the head neutral with the neck slightly extended, clear the mouth first and then the nose only if they are blocked, and rub the back and soles to stimulate breathing, never probing deep in the throat of a baby who is breathing, because the reflex it triggers can stop breathing altogether. A baby who is crying, breathing easily, and holding good tone goes to the mother and needs nothing more, while a baby who is not breathing, or who is gasping, those slow seagull sighs, needs ventilation now.

Ventilation is the skill that saves, and oxygen is the afterthought. Take a mask that covers the mouth, chin, and nose without pressing the eyes, seal it with your fingers in a ring around the edges, lift the jaw toward it, and squeeze the bag about 40 times a minute, even and unhurried, watching for the chest to rise. If the chest does not move, reposition the head before blaming the bag, because a bad seal and a bad position explain almost every failure, and after a minute of good ventilation you reassess aloud: heart rate, breathing, color. If the heart rate stays below 60 beats per minute despite a moving chest, add chest compressions, three pushes to one breath at about 120 events a minute with two thumbs on the breastbone, done only by a person trained in them. Room air is the first choice for nearly all babies, and oxygen is added only if a baby stays blue in the center despite a rising chest, and only where oxygen exists. Reassess every minute and let the team hear the numbers, because the hardest honest sentence in this drill is that if after 20 minutes of good ventilation the heart has never started, the baby will not come back, and what follows is care for the parents.

Survival of a newborn in a cold clinic is a chain, and every link is free: a warm room, a hat, skin to skin, bathing delayed to at least the second day, and kangaroo positioning for the small one. Weigh every baby before its first long sleep, because a baby below 2500 grams is the one who dies on a mild night with a temperature of 35.5 degrees Celsius. The danger signs of the first days are fast breathing over 60 per minute, grunting, the chest sucking inward, a blue face, floppiness, refusal to feed, cold skin, and convulsions, and each is a referral sign. Give every baby vitamin K, 1 milligram intramuscularly once, as the World Health Organization's newborn guidance directs, keep the cord clean, dry, and uncovered, and feed within the first hour and often after, because a newborn's sugar is measured in hours.

Kangaroo mother care is the low-resource answer to the incubator. A baby born early or small cannot hold its temperature, because the skin is thin, the fat is absent, and the body surface is vast for the weight, and an incubator solves that with electricity, spare parts, and a technician, all of which a remote post lacks. The mother's chest solves it with biology, because her chest warms and cools with the baby's needs, a synchrony found in every study that has looked for it. The method is strict but simple: a stable baby below 2000 grams is placed upright between the breasts, head turned to one side, held by a wide cloth wrapped around both chests, wearing a hat, in continuous skin contact day and night, freed only for washing and the toilet, and fed breast milk alone while the family shares the holding so the mother sleeps. Done this way deaths fall, breastfeeding rises, infection drops, and discharge comes sooner, and the effect travels home, because the incubator is a person: this is the most beautiful economy in tropical medicine.

Documentation is the least glamorous part of this chapter and the part that makes the next birth safer, because notes are how a place learns. Write every delivery up during or immediately after it, never reconstructed later from memory. The record holds the woman's story and antenatal facts, the times of labor onset, arrival, rupture of the membranes, full dilation, the birth, and the placenta, the completed partograph, every drug with its dose, route, and clock time, the weighed or estimated blood loss, the state of the perineum, and for the baby the weight, temperature, condition at one and five minutes, resuscitation steps in order, vitamin K, and the first feed. The delivery register, kept in sequence, is both the district's data and your program's memory, and the counts of hemorrhages, fits, and resuscitations, read over months, tell you what to drill next, while referral letters travel with the woman, a copy stays behind, and a phone call follows the letter, because papers get lost on roads. Review the emergencies as a team, reading near misses aloud and asking not who erred but what the system needed, and write in ink, write legibly, and never write what you did not do.

Your role in this work is wider than your hands. You are the person who trains the traditional birth attendant to recognize danger signs and to come early rather than late, and who drills the cleaner and the midwife in the same sequence until it is muscle, and even a two-person team assigns its roles out loud before the emergency, one at the mother's head, one for the baby, with the phone numbers on the wall. Consent and dignity travel with the medicine: explain what you will do, welcome the companion she chooses, and honor the modesty her culture and faith expect, because a woman who trusts the post comes before the emergency, not after it. When a birth goes wrong the debrief is for you too, because a traumatic delivery lodges in the person who held the mask, and saying it aloud to a colleague, sleeping, and asking for supervision are clinical skills, not indulgences. Knowing your limits is the last tool in this chapter, and saying that this is beyond me while calling for help is the act that makes it safe for you to practice alone.

To summarize, a safe birth at a remote post is prepared long before the contractions start: a checked shelf, a partograph plotted honestly, and a team that has drilled hemorrhage, eclampsia, and the newborn minute until they happen by reflex. Recognize normal labor so you can recognize its failure, and act when the graph, the heart rate, or the mother herself tells you to. Manage the third stage actively with oxytocin, controlled cord traction in trained hands, and massage, and answer hemorrhage with the drilled order: rub the uterus, empty the bladder, give the uterotonics in their right sequence, tranexamic acid, compression, and the condom balloon, calling early for help and blood. Treat pre-eclampsia as the quiet emergency it is, run magnesium sulfate with its hourly checks of reflexes, breathing, and urine, lower only the dangerously high pressures, and remember that delivery is the cure. Read obstruction from the partograph before the uterus warns you with a groove, refer at the action line, and never augment an obstructed labor. Give the newborn the golden minute of drying, warmth, airway, and ventilation with room air, then keep the small baby warm on the mother's chest, the incubator that never fails. Every dose in this chapter reflects current World Health Organization guidance, but regimens are revised and guidance differs between countries, so verify the current guideline before you act, and write every birth down so that the next one is safer.
