# Chapter 15 — Soil-Transmitted Helminths

Soil-transmitted helminths are a group of parasitic worms whose eggs or larvae reach a new human host through soil contaminated with human feces, and they are among the most common infections of humankind. The group contains four worms that every advanced practice nurse in tropical medicine must know, and each one produces its own pattern of illness. They are the roundworm Ascaris lumbricoides, the two hookworms Necator americanus and Ancylostoma duodenale, the whipworm Trichuris trichiura, and the threadworm Strongyloides stercoralis. Together they infect an estimated 1.5 billion people, they are concentrated in the poorest communities of Africa, Asia, and Latin America, and they are a reason that children in those communities are shorter, lighter, and more anemic than they should be. None is transmitted by an insect, none requires an animal reservoir, and none is prevented by a vaccine. They are transmitted by poverty itself, by the absence of a latrine, by walking barefoot, and by the use of untreated human waste to fertilize crops. This chapter follows each worm in turn, then turns to the laboratory, the medicines, the campaigns that deliver those medicines to whole schools at once, and the nursing work that turns a deworming tablet into a lasting change.

Transmission is simple, and that simplicity makes these worms hard to eliminate. Ascaris and Trichuris are transmitted when a person swallows eggs that have matured in warm, moist soil, usually on unwashed hands, on raw vegetables, or on food prepared by someone whose hands carry the eggs. Hookworm and Strongyloides are transmitted when larvae that have hatched in the soil penetrate intact skin, most often the skin of bare feet, which is why these infections were once common among people who worked without shoes. The larvae wait in the soil for the warmth and moisture of a human foot, and they enter through hair follicles or small abrasions the person never noticed. Human waste used as fertilizer, a practice called night soil in some countries, concentrates the eggs and the larvae exactly where children play and where families grow food. The same sanitation failure that spreads these worms also spreads the bacteria and viruses that cause diarrhea, so a program that builds latrines and supplies clean water reduces several diseases at once. A latrine used consistently by every member of a household interrupts the cycle more effectively than any medicine, because a tablet treats the worms a person already carries, while a latrine prevents the next infection.

Ascaris lumbricoides is the largest intestinal worm that commonly infects people, and the adult female measures 20 to 35 centimeters in length. The adults live free in the lumen of the small intestine and do not attach to the wall. A fertilized female produces about 200,000 eggs a day, and those eggs pass in the stool and need 2 to 4 weeks in warm, shaded, moist soil before they become infective. The egg has a thick shell and is resistant, so it can survive in the environment for months or even years, which is why a household that stopped defecating in the yard may still be infected a year later. When a person swallows an infective egg, the larva hatches in the small intestine, penetrates the intestinal wall, and travels through the portal blood to the liver and then to the heart and the lungs. In the lungs the larva breaks into the air sacs, ascends the airway, is coughed up, and is swallowed, and it matures into an adult worm about 2 to 3 months after the egg was swallowed. This migration explains why the first illness a newly infected person notices is respiratory rather than intestinal.

The pulmonary phase of ascariasis appears 4 to 16 days after the eggs are ingested, and it produces cough, wheeze, breathlessness, low fever, and a patchy shadow on the chest radiograph that moves about as the larvae migrate. Sputum at this stage may contain eosinophils and occasionally larvae, and the blood shows a marked eosinophilia. The illness is usually mild and self-limited, and it is often mistaken for asthma or for a viral pneumonia, especially in a child who has never wheezed before. The intestinal phase is quieter but more dangerous, because a mass of adult worms can obstruct the bowel, and a bolus of worms in the terminal ileum produces colicky pain, vomiting, and distension that can progress to a complete obstruction needing surgery. The worms may also wander into the biliary tree or the pancreatic duct, causing biliary colic, infection of the bile ducts, or pancreatitis. Intestinal obstruction from Ascaris is a disease of young children, because a child's small intestine is narrow and because children carry the heaviest worm burdens. The classic patient is a child between 1 and 5 years of age with abdominal pain, vomiting, and a palpable mass, and the nurse must treat a reported worm in the stool or vomit as a diagnostic finding and ask about it directly. In a child who is passing worms and has a tender abdomen, the nurse should withhold food by mouth, secure intravenous access, and escalate promptly rather than giving another dose and waiting.

Hookworm infection begins with a step on contaminated soil, and the disease that follows is a study in slow blood loss. Two species cause it, Necator americanus and Ancylostoma duodenale, and Necator americanus is the commoner across much of the tropics, while Ancylostoma duodenale tolerates cooler climates and causes more blood loss per worm. Within minutes to hours after the larvae penetrate the skin, the person develops an itchy, raised, red rash at the site of entry, usually between the toes or on the sole, and this is called ground itch. The larvae then travel by the same route as Ascaris, through the blood to the lungs and up the airway, and they mature into adult worms in the small intestine, where each worm attaches to the lining and sucks blood every day for the several years that it lives. That daily loss is trivial in isolation and devastating in aggregate, because a child or a pregnant woman may carry hundreds of worms, and the result is iron deficiency anemia from chronic blood loss. The patient reports fatigue, breathlessness on exertion, and poor concentration, and the examination shows pallor, a rapid pulse, and in severe cases a murmur and swelling of the ankles. The laboratory shows a low hemoglobin, small red cells, a low ferritin level, and often a raised platelet count, with hidden blood in the stool. The differential diagnosis matters, because anemia in a tropical setting is not automatically hookworm disease, and the nurse who assumes so will miss malaria, tuberculosis, HIV, nutritional deficiency, and the anemia of chronic disease.

Trichuris trichiura, the whipworm, is a smaller worm whose shape explains its behavior. Its thin front end is threaded into the lining of the cecum and the colon, and its thicker back end lies free in the lumen, which gives it the look of a whip. The adult female lays eggs that pass in the stool, and the eggs need 2 to 4 weeks in moist soil before they become infective, so its cycle resembles that of Ascaris more than that of hookworm. Most infected people carry a light burden and have no symptoms at all, which is why the worm is often dismissed as a harmless finding on a stool report. Heavy infection, however, produces a distinctive syndrome of chronic bloody diarrhea, straining, and abdominal pain, and it is called Trichuris dysentery syndrome. Children with a heavy burden develop anemia, poor growth, and finger clubbing, and the chronic straining can cause rectal prolapse, one of the most treatable complications in tropical pediatrics. A rectal prolapse in a child in an endemic area should prompt a stool examination and a course of treatment, because the prolapse often resolves once the worms are cleared, and the nurse who assumes that surgery is the only answer has missed an infection that can be cured with a tablet.

Strongyloides stercoralis is the one worm in this chapter that can multiply inside a single human host, and that fact makes it the most dangerous. The infective larvae penetrate the skin as hookworm larvae do, travel through the lungs, and mature in the small intestine, but the female worms live embedded in the lining and produce eggs that hatch in the intestine itself. The resulting larvae are passed in the stool, but some of them mature within the bowel into infective larvae and penetrate the colonic wall or the skin around the anus, and this is autoinfection. Autoinfection allows a single exposure to establish a lifelong infection, and it explains why a person who walked barefoot once in a tropical village can still harbor the worm 40 years later. The worm is held at a low level by this internal cycle and by the immune system, and the person has few or no symptoms for decades. The larvae may also be found in the sputum, so a patient who is coughing and has a migrating rash may be told that the problem is asthma when the true cause is in the gut. An external cycle in the soil can also produce new infective larvae, so the organism can persist in a garden.

The hyperinfection syndrome is what happens when the balance between the worm and the immune system breaks, and the commonest trigger is a corticosteroid given for asthma, for an autoimmune disease, or as part of cancer treatment. The autoinfective cycle then accelerates out of control, the larval burden multiplies, and larvae migrate in enormous numbers through the lungs, the gut, and the bloodstream, carrying bacteria from the bowel with them. The result is a severe illness with cough, breathlessness, coughing of blood, abdominal pain, diarrhea, and septicemia, and the mortality is often greater than 50 per cent even with treatment. The tragedy is that the syndrome is preventable, because a blood test and a stool examination before the corticosteroid is started would have identified the infection. The rule is easiest to state as a question the nurse asks before any immunosuppressive plan begins. Has this patient ever lived, worked, or traveled for any length of time in a region where Strongyloides is endemic, and if so, has the infection been excluded? Screening consists of a stool examination by a method that detects larvae, a blood test for antibodies against the worm, and often more than one stool sample, because the sensitivity of a single test is low. A patient with a positive antibody test and a compatible exposure should be treated before the immunosuppressive drug is given, and the nurse's part is to make this screen visible in the record and to chase the result before the first dose.

Soil-transmitted helminths are distributed wherever poverty, poor sanitation, and warm moist soil coincide, and their burden falls most heavily on children. The World Health Organization estimates that about 1.5 billion people are infected with at least one of these worms, that more than 260 million preschool children and more than 600 million school-age children live in areas where the worms are transmitted, and that several hundred million women of reproductive age are also at risk. Infection is not evenly distributed within a community, because it is overdispersed, which means that a small proportion of people carry most of the worms and contribute most of the contamination. Heavy infection in childhood produces anemia, poor appetite, reduced absorption of nutrients, stunting, and impaired cognitive development, and the effect on school performance can be measured. In adults, hookworm infection reduces the capacity for physical work, which lowers agricultural productivity and household income, and hookworm anemia in pregnancy contributes to maternal death and to poor outcomes for the newborn. The World Health Organization measures this burden in disability-adjusted life years, a summary that combines years of life lost and years lived with disability, and the total remains large despite decades of control effort. The nurse who understands that the burden is concentrated in a minority of households will direct education and follow-up toward those households rather than toward the community as a whole.

Diagnosis begins at the bench, and the first tool is the stool examination. Direct microscopy of a wet preparation can show the eggs of Ascaris, Trichuris, and hookworm, but it is insensitive when the burden is light, so concentration and quantitative methods are used instead. The Kato-Katz technique is the standard quantitative method for these three worms. A measured amount of stool, usually about 41.7 milligrams, is pressed through a sieve, placed on a card with a template, covered with a strip of cellophane soaked in glycerine, and examined under the microscope, and the number of eggs counted is converted into eggs per gram of stool. That number is not merely a laboratory curiosity, because it classifies the intensity of infection as light, moderate, or heavy, and national programs use it to decide where to intervene and whether their interventions are working. The technique is inexpensive and needs only a microscope, a template, and cellophane strips, so it can be used in a district laboratory. Its weakness is that egg excretion varies from day to day, so a single sample can miss an infection or underestimate the burden, and the result must be read together with the clinical picture.

Strongyloides requires methods that look for larvae rather than eggs, and two such methods are standard. The Baermann method uses the warmth and moisture of a funnel of water. Fresh stool is placed on gauze inside a funnel, water at body temperature is added so that it just touches the sample, and the larvae, drawn to warmth and water, migrate downward through the gauze and collect in the stem of the funnel, where they can be drawn off and examined. The agar plate culture method places fresh stool on the surface of a nutrient agar plate, and the larvae that crawl across the plate carry bacteria with them, leaving visible tracks that can be seen the next day. Both methods need fresh stool and prompt processing, and both are more sensitive than a direct smear, but neither is perfectly sensitive. Serology detects antibodies against the worm and is useful for screening and for supporting a diagnosis when the stool is negative, but a positive result cannot distinguish a current infection from one treated years ago. The practical details are in the nurse's hands, because a specimen must be passed directly into a clean container, delivered the same day, and labeled with the exposures and the clinical question, and when the burden is light the nurse should arrange three samples on separate days rather than accept one result as final.

Treatment is simple, inexpensive, and highly effective, and three medicines carry almost the whole load. Albendazole is given by mouth at 400 milligrams as a single dose for Ascaris, for hookworm, and for light or moderate Trichuris infection. Mebendazole is given by mouth at 100 milligrams twice a day for 3 days, or as a single dose of 500 milligrams, and the 3-day course is preferred when the infection is heavy. For heavy Trichuris infection, which often coexists with heavy Ascaris infection, albendazole is given at 400 milligrams once a day for 3 days, and the longer course improves the cure rate substantially. Ivermectin is given by mouth at 200 micrograms per kilogram as a single dose for Strongyloides, and the dose is repeated after 2 weeks or after 1 month, because the autoinfective cycle may have released new larvae after the first dose. The two benzimidazoles, albendazole and mebendazole, are not interchangeable with ivermectin, and the choice depends on the worm that has been identified. For Strongyloides, ivermectin is the treatment of choice, and albendazole at 400 milligrams twice a day for 7 days is the alternative when ivermectin is unavailable or unsuitable. A patient treated with a single dose of albendazole who remains symptomatic should have the diagnosis and the species reconsidered rather than simply receiving another dose.

Strongyloides deserves one more warning, because the wrong management can kill. A patient with unrecognized strongyloidiasis who is treated with corticosteroids for an unrelated condition can develop hyperinfection within days, and the deterioration can be rapid. If the diagnosis is made in a patient who is already taking a corticosteroid, the corticosteroid should be reduced or stopped as quickly as the underlying condition allows, and treatment should start at once, often with ivermectin given daily until the stool is negative and then repeated. In disseminated disease, when larvae are found outside the gut and the lungs, treatment is prolonged, antibiotics are added, and the patient may need intensive care and mechanical ventilation. Pregnancy is the other situation that needs thought, because the first trimester is the period of organ formation and most guidance avoids deworming medicines during those weeks unless the need is urgent. Treatment is usually offered in the second and third trimesters, when the risk of the medicine is judged lower than the risk of untreated anemia, and albendazole and mebendazole are both used in that window in many national programs, while ivermectin is generally avoided. Lactation is less restrictive, because the amount of drug that passes into breast milk is small. A pregnant woman with hookworm infection and anemia should not be left untreated until after delivery simply because she is pregnant, because the anemia itself is dangerous to her and to the fetus.

All three medicines are well tolerated by the great majority of patients, and the common adverse effects are mild and brief. Abdominal pain, nausea, diarrhea, headache, and dizziness are reported after albendazole and mebendazole, and they usually appear within a day and settle without treatment. When a patient with a heavy worm burden is treated, the death of the worms can produce abdominal discomfort, and in heavy Ascaris infection the worms may be passed in the stool, which reassures the patient when it is explained in advance. Ivermectin may cause itching, a rash, joint pain, fever, and tender lymph nodes in patients who are co-infected with other parasites, and these reactions are usually mild. The serious adverse effects are uncommon but must be known. Ivermectin can cause severe neurological toxicity in a patient who is heavily infected with the parasite Loa loa, which is found in parts of Central and West Africa, which is why treatment in a co-endemic area must be planned with knowledge of the local guidance. Albendazole can raise the liver enzymes and, rarely, depress the bone marrow, so a patient given a prolonged or repeated course needs monitoring. Mebendazole is generally the safer of the two benzimidazoles for a long course, while albendazole is the more effective for a single-dose campaign.

None of the doses in this chapter should be given from memory. Antiparasitic regimens are revised as new trial evidence appears, guidance differs between countries, and the same infection may be managed with a single dose in one national program and a 3-day course in another. The World Health Organization publishes guidance on preventive chemotherapy for soil-transmitted helminths, and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention publishes guidance for clinicians in the United States, and the two documents are updated on their own schedules and are written for different populations. The current national guideline, the current product label, and the local program protocol must all be checked before a medicine is prescribed, dispensed, or given. The dose of ivermectin is calculated from the weight measured on the day of treatment, not from the weight recorded at an earlier visit. The nurse verifies the weight, checks the medicine and the strength on the label, confirms that the child is above the minimum weight for the medicine, and asks about a history of allergy or of a previous reaction before the tablet is swallowed. A dose that was correct at the last campaign may be wrong at this one, and the guideline is the authority rather than the habit of the unit. The same discipline applies to the record, because the child treated twice in one round or missed altogether is usually a recording failure.

Mass drug administration is the strategy that has changed the epidemiology of these worms more than any clinical advance. The World Health Organization recommends periodic treatment of entire at-risk groups without individual diagnosis, and the groups targeted are preschool children, school-age children, and women of reproductive age. The medicine is usually albendazole at 400 milligrams or mebendazole at 500 milligrams as a single dose, repeated once or twice a year depending on the intensity of transmission, with the aim of keeping the worm burden low enough that illness does not develop. In areas where transmission is low and sanitation is improving, repeated rounds can interrupt transmission altogether, and several countries have reached the point where these worms are no longer a public health problem. Mass drug administration is necessary and it is not sufficient, because repeated treatment without improvement in water, sanitation and hygiene produces rapid reinfection. The World Health Organization therefore couples preventive chemotherapy with improvements in water supply, latrine construction, and hygiene education, and it treats these as one program rather than as separate activities. Coverage is uneven, and the children who are absent from school on the day of the campaign are often those who need treatment most, while hesitancy based on rumors about side effects or fertility is best answered by a nurse who listens and replies plainly. Programs must also guard against drug resistance, which has been documented in animal parasites and is watched for in human worms, and this is one more reason to keep the dose adequate and the coverage high.

The logistics of a deworming campaign are the part a nurse is most likely to be asked to manage, and they reward careful planning. The tablets are stable at room temperature, so no cold chain is needed, and the dose for school-age children is often decided by height rather than by weight, using a dosing pole marked in centimeters. The tablets are chewable, and a child who cannot chew may be given a crushed tablet mixed with water, while a child who is severely ill or below the minimum weight for the medicine should be referred rather than treated in the campaign. The team records the number of children treated, the number who were absent, and the number who refused, because coverage determines whether the campaign succeeds, and a campaign that reaches 60 per cent of children will not reduce transmission. A teacher or a community health worker can be trained to give the tablets under supervision, and adverse events are reported through the national system. The nurse teaches families why the worms are passed in the stool, why a latrine prevents the next infection, why shoes protect the feet, and why hands must be washed before food is eaten and after the toilet is used. The teaching must name the specific practice that matters in that community, so in a farming community the nurse may discuss the use of fresh human waste as fertilizer and suggest composting or storage before the crop is harvested. The nurse should also explain that a child may pass a worm after treatment and that this is expected, so that the sight does not become a reason to refuse the next round.

One of the most important nursing tasks in this chapter is the proper investigation of anemia, because the habit of giving iron alone hides diagnoses and delays treatment. Anemia in a person who lives where these worms are transmitted may be caused by hookworm, by Trichuris, by malaria, by tuberculosis, by HIV, by a nutritional deficiency, by a hemoglobin disorder, or by several of these at once. The workup begins with a complete blood count, a blood film, and a ferritin level, and it continues with a stool examination, a malaria test, and an assessment of the diet. A ferritin level can be falsely normal or high when there is inflammation or infection, so it must be interpreted with the clinical picture and, when the question is important, with a marker of inflammation. The nurse should ask about menstrual loss, pregnancy, the duration of symptoms, and any bleeding, because these answers change the differential diagnosis. Iron should be given when iron deficiency is documented or strongly suspected, but it should be given with a plan for follow-up and for rechecking the hemoglobin, not indefinitely. The nurse who treats anemia with iron alone in a patient who has hookworm disease will see a partial response and then a relapse, because the daily blood loss continues, and the true cause will have been missed.

Monitoring after treatment is what separates a campaign from a clinical program. After a course of treatment the stool should be re-examined to confirm that the eggs have cleared, and for Strongyloides the follow-up should use a larvae-specific method and should continue for months, because the autoinfective cycle can restart. The hemoglobin should be rechecked after 4 to 8 weeks in a patient treated for hookworm anemia, because the marrow needs time to respond, and growth should be measured in children, with weight and height plotted on a growth chart. In a mass drug administration program the indicators are coverage, meaning the proportion of the target group treated, and the egg reduction rate in a sample of the population, and a falling rate suggests poor adherence, reinfection, or the beginning of drug resistance. Some patients must be referred rather than managed in the clinic, and the nurse should recognize them quickly. A child with a distended, tender abdomen, vomiting, and absent stool or gas may have an intestinal obstruction from Ascaris, and a patient receiving a corticosteroid who develops fever, breathlessness, coughing of blood, or confusion may have hyperinfection syndrome, and each of these needs urgent escalation. A transplant recipient or a patient about to start chemotherapy needs a documented screen for Strongyloides before the immunosuppression begins, and a migrant from an endemic area may carry a worm silent for decades, so a history of exposure should be recorded at the first visit and kept in the chart. A patient with advanced HIV may have more severe diarrhea and more frequent reinfection, so strongyloidiasis should be considered in that population.

To summarize, soil-transmitted helminths are a small group of worms that infect more than a billion people and cause anemia, poor growth, and impaired development, and they are transmitted by fecal contamination of soil and by walking barefoot. Ascaris lumbricoides migrates through the lungs, can obstruct the bowel and block the biliary tree, and is diagnosed by the Kato-Katz method with its egg count. Hookworm, caused by Necator americanus and Ancylostoma duodenale, enters through the skin, causes ground itch, and produces chronic iron deficiency anemia through daily blood loss. Trichuris trichiura causes dysentery and rectal prolapse in heavy infection, and Strongyloides stercoralis can persist for decades through autoinfection and can cause fatal hyperinfection after corticosteroids, which is why screening before immunosuppression is a rule. Diagnosis rests on stool examination with the Kato-Katz method for eggs and the Baermann method or agar plate culture for larvae, supported by serology for strongyloidiasis. Treatment with albendazole, mebendazole, or ivermectin is simple and effective, but the dose must be verified against the current national guideline and product label every time, because regimens are revised and guidance differs between countries. The nurse's work is to run deworming campaigns, to teach the specific practices that prevent reinfection, to investigate anemia properly instead of treating it with iron alone, and to monitor the patients and the programs until the worms no longer return.
