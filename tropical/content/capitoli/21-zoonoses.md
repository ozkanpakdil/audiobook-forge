# Chapter 21 — Zoonoses

A zoonosis is any infection that passes from an animal to a human being, and in tropical practice these are not curiosities at the edge of the syllabus but routine causes of fever, abortion, wound infection, and sudden death. The animals involved are the ones that live beside people, which means rodents in the walls and the fields, cattle and goats in the compound, dogs in the yard, bats under the roof, and the livestock and wildlife that share the water and the soil. The organisms travel by routes that seem unremarkable until someone traces them, such as urine in floodwater, milk that was never boiled, the placenta of an aborting goat, a bite on the calf, and the fine spray of a lambing pen. The principle that organizes this work is called One Health, the recognition that human health, animal health, and the health of the environment are one problem, and in a village clinic it is not an abstraction. It is the reason a case of human brucellosis is also a veterinary problem that will return next season unless the herd is addressed. This chapter takes the major zoonoses in turn, which are leptospirosis, brucellosis, rabies, anthrax, melioidosis, plague, and Q fever. The thread that holds them together is the exposure history, because a nurse who asks where the patient works, what the patient drinks, which animals the patient keeps, and what the patient was doing in the week before the fever began will recognize most of these diseases before the laboratory does.

Leptospirosis is caused by spirochetes of the genus Leptospira, and Leptospira interrogans is the species most often named in human disease. The organism is divided into hundreds of serovars that differ in their animal hosts and in the severity of illness they produce. The reservoir is the rodent, above all the rat, which carries the spirochete in the tubules of the kidney and sheds it in urine for life without ever appearing ill. Cattle, pigs, dogs, and many wild mammals do the same, and each of them adds a route of human exposure. The spirochete does not survive drying, but it survives for weeks in fresh water, damp soil, and mud, which is why the disease follows rain, flooding, and work done in wet ground. A person is infected when contaminated water or soil touches broken skin, the conjunctiva, or another mucous membrane, and the commonest settings are rice paddies, sugarcane and banana plantations, flooded streets, sewers, fish markets, slaughterhouses, veterinary practices, and freshwater swimming. The incubation period is usually 5 to 14 days, with a range of about 2 to 30 days, so a patient who fell into floodwater ten days before the fever began has handed the nurse the diagnosis.

The illness is biphasic, and the two phases are easy to miss if the nurse expects one continuous story. The first phase begins abruptly with fever, chills, severe headache, and muscle pain worst in the calves and the lower back, and it lasts about 4 to 9 days while the organism circulates in the blood. Conjunctival suffusion appears in this phase in a large proportion of patients, and it is the finding that should make the nurse think of leptospirosis rather than of a common viral conjunctivitis. The conjunctivae are red and congested, but there is no discharge, no crusting, and usually no pain, and the redness persists after the fever falls. A brief improvement may follow, and then the second phase begins with the return of fever, an aseptic meningitis, a rash, and sometimes an inflammation of the eye that can damage vision months later. Most patients, perhaps 90 per cent, have this milder form, which resolves over 5 to 7 days, though fatigue can persist for weeks. A minority develop severe leptospirosis, long called Weil's disease, with jaundice, acute kidney injury, bleeding from the nose, the gums, the gut, and the lungs, and inflammation of the heart muscle. Pulmonary hemorrhage is now a leading cause of death in severe disease and can develop within an hour in a patient who was talking normally, so a patient with jaundice, falling urine output, and a falling platelet count is a medical emergency who needs a facility with dialysis.

The laboratory confirms leptospirosis in different ways at different times, and the timing of the sample matters as much as the choice of test. During the first week the organism can be found by PCR of blood, and this is the most useful test early in the illness, while culture requires special media, grows slowly, and takes weeks to become positive. Antibodies appear after the first week and are detected by enzyme immunoassay or by the microscopic agglutination test, which remains the reference method, and a pair of samples taken two to four weeks apart that shows a rising level is the traditional proof. A single high level in a patient with the right exposure and the right illness is generally accepted as good evidence, but it must be read against the local background rate, because people in endemic areas often carry antibodies from past infection. Early antibiotic treatment can blunt the antibody response and produce a negative test in a patient who truly has the disease, so a negative serology never overrides a convincing clinical picture. The nurse's practical contribution is to draw the acute sample before the first antibiotic dose where possible, to label each sample with the date and the day of illness, and to arrange the second sample when the patient returns.

Treatment depends on severity, and the antibiotic is only one part of a supportive plan. Mild disease is treated with doxycycline at 100 milligrams by mouth twice a day for 7 days, or with amoxicillin or azithromycin where doxycycline is not appropriate, and these regimens shorten the illness and reduce shedding. Severe disease requires an intravenous antibiotic such as penicillin, ceftriaxone, or doxycycline in a facility where fluids, electrolytes, blood pressure, urine output, and renal function can be watched continuously. Supportive care is often what saves the patient, and it means careful fluid balance to avoid both dehydration and pulmonary edema, transfusion for hemorrhage, dialysis for kidney failure, and mechanical ventilation when the lungs fail. Antibiotic treatment can trigger a Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction within the first hours, with fever, rigors, and a fall in blood pressure, and a nurse who has been warned about it will recognize it and treat it rather than abandon the drug. Prevention rests on interrupting the path from rodent urine to human mucous membrane, which means rodent control, safe food storage, covering the skin and any wound when working in water or mud, wearing personal protective equipment in slaughterhouses and markets, and providing clean water and sanitation. Doxycycline at 200 milligrams by mouth once a week during short periods of intense exposure is used as chemoprophylaxis in some countries, though it never substitutes for keeping urine out of the water supply.

Brucellosis is caused by small bacteria of the genus Brucella, and the species that matter most to people are Brucella melitensis from goats and sheep, Brucella abortus from cattle, Brucella suis from pigs, and Brucella canis from dogs. The organism is shed in milk, urine, and above all the placenta and birth fluids of infected animals, and it survives in unpasteurized dairy products for weeks. Human infection follows one of three routes. The first is food, meaning raw milk, soft cheese made from raw milk, butter, cream, and ice cream, and this is the commonest route for the general population. The second is direct contact with an aborting animal, and the nurse should picture the shepherd who delivers a goat, the farmer who cleans the pen with bare hands, the abattoir worker who handles the uterus, and the veterinarian who assists a difficult calving. The third is inhalation, and Brucella is one of the most frequently reported laboratory-acquired infections in the world, which means that opening a culture plate and sniffing it to judge the growth is a genuinely dangerous act. The incubation period is usually 1 to 4 weeks but ranges from a few days to several months, and the disease is notoriously insidious.

Acute brucellosis is a fever with no obvious focus, and its most famous feature is its undulation. The temperature rises in the evening, falls with drenching sweats at night, and may return to normal for days before climbing again, and the sweat has a heavy musty smell that patients describe in the same words wherever they live. Weakness, loss of appetite, weight loss, headache, and pain in the muscles and joints accompany it, and the patient often looks less ill than the temperature suggests. The spine and the sacroiliac joints are the sites that give brucellosis away, and sacroiliitis produces low back and buttock pain that is worse at night and on movement, with tenderness directly over the joint. Spondylitis of the lumbar spine, arthritis of the peripheral joints, inflammation of the testis and epididymis, and enlargement of the liver and the spleen all occur. Endocarditis is the complication that kills, and it should be suspected in any patient with brucellosis and a new heart murmur, a prosthetic valve, or persistent fever despite treatment. The nervous system is involved in a small proportion of patients, producing meningitis and nerve palsies that require prolonged specialist treatment.

The diagnosis is made by culture, by serology, or by PCR, and each method has a weakness the nurse should know. Blood culture is definitive when positive, but the organism grows slowly and the laboratory must be told that brucellosis is suspected so that the culture is held longer than usual. Bone marrow culture is more sensitive in acute illness and is used when the diagnosis remains uncertain. Serology is the practical mainstay, and the standard agglutination test, the Rose Bengal test, and enzyme immunoassays are all used, with results read against local thresholds. Antibodies persist for months after cure, so serology cannot decide whether treatment has succeeded, and relapse occurs in about 5 to 10 per cent of treated patients, usually within six months. A patient who becomes febrile again after finishing treatment is therefore treated again rather than dismissed as having a new illness.

Treatment is long, and the length is the point. The standard regimen is doxycycline at 100 milligrams by mouth twice a day together with rifampicin at 600 to 900 milligrams by mouth once a day for 6 weeks, because either drug alone produces an unacceptably high relapse rate. An alternative is doxycycline for 6 weeks with streptomycin given intramuscularly for the first 2 to 3 weeks, and this combination is used in some countries for severe or complicated disease. Children under 8 years of age and pregnant women cannot receive doxycycline and are usually treated with trimethoprim with sulfamethoxazole, sometimes with rifampicin added, under specialist advice. The patient must be told at the first visit that the fever will settle within days but the medicine must continue for the full 6 weeks, because that single piece of information prevents most relapses. Rifampicin turns the urine and the tears orange, it interacts with many other medicines including antiretroviral therapy and oral contraceptives, and it can damage the liver, so liver function and blood counts are monitored during treatment. The nurse watches adherence, asks directly about missed doses, records the laboratory results, and arranges review at the end of treatment, because brucellosis treated for two weeks feels cured and comes back.

Rabies is caused by Rabies lyssavirus, a bullet-shaped virus whose genetic material is ribonucleic acid, and it belongs to a group of viruses transmitted in the saliva of infected mammals. The virus travels from the wound along the peripheral nerves toward the spinal cord and the brain, and it does not need to enter the bloodstream to do damage. This explains the two facts that govern the incubation period, which are that a wound close to the brain and a wound in a richly innervated part of the body produce a shorter incubation time, and that a wound on the foot can take months to produce illness. The incubation period is usually 1 to 3 months, but it has ranged from a few days to more than a year, and it may be shortened in children and in people bitten on the face or the hands. Dogs are responsible for the overwhelming majority of human deaths from rabies worldwide, which is why dog vaccination is the single most effective public health measure against the disease. Bats, monkeys, cats, and cattle also transmit it, and a bat in a room where a person was sleeping is treated as an exposure even when no bite can be found.

The illness begins with a prodrome that is easy to dismiss, and the nurse who knows it may be the only person who recognizes the disease in time. Fever, headache, malaise, and loss of appetite appear first, followed by a strange sensation at the site of the healed wound, which may be pain, tingling, burning, or itching that persists after the scar has closed. This local sensation is the most specific early feature of rabies and should be taken seriously in anyone with a recent animal bite. The furious form follows, with agitation, confusion, hallucinations, and muscle spasms, and with the two signs that give the disease its terrible reputation. Hydrophobia is a spasm of the muscles of swallowing and breathing when the patient sees, hears, or tries to drink water, and it is accompanied by aerophobia, the same spasm provoked by a draught of air. The patient is fully conscious during these spasms, and the autonomic nervous system becomes unstable with fluctuating heart rate, blood pressure, and temperature. The paralytic form ascends from the bitten limb without hydrophobia and is even more easily missed. Both forms progress to coma and death, which is the outcome in nearly every patient once symptoms begin, so care at that stage is palliative and the family needs an honest explanation.

Post-exposure care is a sequence, and every step has a deadline. The first step is immediate washing of the wound with soap and running water for at least 15 minutes, and this simple act, done before anything else, removes virus from the wound and saves lives. After washing, the wound is irrigated, an antiseptic such as povidone iodine or ethanol is applied, bleeding is controlled, and the wound is left open rather than sutured at once, because closing it can trap virus. Tetanus vaccination is updated, antibiotics are given for bacterial infection, and the wound is measured and recorded. Rabies vaccine is then given according to the exposure category, which depends on whether the skin was broken, whether the bite drew blood, and whether the exposure was a lick on intact skin, a scratch, or a bite. A bite that breaks the skin or contaminates a mucous membrane is treated with vaccine and with rabies immunoglobulin, and the immunoglobulin is infiltrated into and around the wound on the day the vaccine course begins. The vaccine schedules in current use include a five-dose course on days 0, 3, 7, 14, and 28, and a shortened course on days 0, 7, and 21 that gives a double dose on the first day. Immunoglobulin is given only once, on the day the course starts, and it is not repeated later.

The nurse's role in rabies is less about the medicine than about completion, and completion is where the failures happen. The patient who has been bitten is frightened, the vaccine may be expensive, the clinic may be far away, and the immunoglobulin may be out of stock, and each of these practical obstacles can end in a preventable death. The nurse should explain, in plain words, that the vaccine only works if the whole course is given, that a missed dose may mean restarting the count in some protocols, and that a patient who feels perfectly well after the first dose is not cured. A recall system is worth more than a lecture, and it means a written appointment card, a telephone reminder where that is possible, and a register that names the date of the next dose. The nurse also counsels the household about the animal and about reporting it to the veterinary service, and explains that a dog which remains alive and well ten days after the bite was not shedding virus in its saliva at the time of the bite. Pre-exposure vaccination is offered to people whose work or travel places them at continuing risk, including laboratory staff, veterinarians, animal handlers, and long-stay travelers to remote areas. Above all, the nurse never tells a patient that a small bite is nothing to worry about, because that sentence has killed people.

Anthrax is caused by Bacillus anthracis, a bacterium that forms spores able to survive in soil for decades, and the form of human disease reflects the route by which the spores enter. The natural hosts are grazing animals, especially cattle, sheep, and goats, and human cases follow contact with infected animals, their hides, their wool, their bones, or their meat. Cutaneous anthrax accounts for most human cases, and it begins as a painless itchy papule that becomes a blister and then breaks down into a depressed black eschar with striking swelling of the surrounding tissue and tender enlargement of the regional lymph nodes. The eschar itself is not painful, which is one of its most useful distinguishing features, and most patients recover with treatment although a small proportion progress to bloodstream infection. Inhalational anthrax follows the breathing of spores, which are carried to the lymph nodes in the middle of the chest and cause a hemorrhagic inflammation that widens the mediastinum, and it presents as fever, cough, chest pain, and breathlessness that can progress to respiratory failure and death within days. Gastrointestinal anthrax follows the eating of undercooked meat from an infected animal, and it causes fever, abdominal pain, vomiting, bloody diarrhea, and fluid in the abdomen, with a form that affects the throat and causes severe swelling of the neck. A fourth form has appeared among people who inject drugs, producing deep soft tissue infection and bloodstream infection that is difficult to recognize. The diagnosis is confirmed by culture and by PCR, treatment uses ciprofloxacin or doxycycline with combination therapy and an antitoxin for systemic disease, and post-exposure prophylaxis is given for 60 days after a known exposure.

Melioidosis is caused by Burkholderia pseudomallei, a bacterium that lives in soil and water in Southeast Asia and northern Australia, and it is one of the most under-recognized causes of severe infection in the tropics. Rice paddies, flooded fields, and muddy water are the classic sources, and the organism enters the body by inhalation, by swallowing contaminated water, or through a break in the skin. Diabetes is the strongest risk factor by a wide margin, so the nurse should think of melioidosis in any patient with diabetes, a high fever, and a history of working in wet ground. Alcohol use disorder, chronic kidney disease, chronic lung disease, and any condition that suppresses immunity also increase the risk. The disease can present as a sudden bloodstream infection that kills within days, as a pneumonia that resembles tuberculosis, or as abscesses in the liver, the spleen, the kidney, the prostate, the joints, the bones, or the brain, and it has earned the name of the great imitator for that reason. Children often present with an abscess of the parotid gland, a clue that is rarely seen in adults. The organism can also remain silent for years after exposure and then produce disease when the patient becomes diabetic or immunosuppressed, so a patient who lived in an endemic area decades earlier may still develop melioidosis.

Treatment is long and must be explained carefully at the outset, because the relapse rate after a shortened course is high. The intensive phase uses ceftazidime or meropenem given intravenously for at least 10 to 14 days, continued until the fever has settled and the patient has improved, which may take longer when abscesses are extensive. The eradication phase then follows, using trimethoprim with sulfamethoxazole by mouth for 3 to 6 months, with doxycycline and other agents used in some protocols. Every abscess that can be drained should be drained, because antibiotics penetrate pus poorly, and the nurse who notices a new swelling, a new joint, or new pain is doing the work that changes the outcome. Blood sugar must be controlled during treatment, because uncontrolled diabetes undermines the entire course. Monitoring includes renal function, blood counts, and potassium, since trimethoprim with sulfamethoxazole can raise potassium and damage the kidneys. The nurse should expect the patient to feel well long before the course ends and should say so in advance, because the long eradication phase is the part that patients abandon.

Plague is caused by Yersinia pestis, a bacterium maintained in rodents by fleas, and it has shaped human history more than any other zoonosis. When a flea feeds on an infected rodent and then bites a person, the bacteria enter the skin and travel to the nearest lymph node, which becomes inflamed, painful, and enlarged into the swelling called a bubo. Bubonic plague then presents with a sudden high fever, severe headache, and a bubo usually in the groin, the armpit, or the neck, and the patient is prostrated within hours of feeling well. If the bacteria reach the bloodstream the disease becomes septicemic plague, with bleeding, shock, and death, and if they reach the lungs, either from the blood or from inhaling droplets from another patient with pneumonia, it becomes pneumonic plague, which spreads from person to person and kills almost everyone untreated. The great pandemics of the past, including the Black Death of the fourteenth century and the pandemic that began in the nineteenth century and reached every continent, were caused by this organism, and only effective antibiotics brought the case fatality down from most patients to a small minority. The diagnosis is made by microscopy of a bubo aspirate, by culture, by PCR, and by rapid antigen testing, and treatment with streptomycin, gentamicin, doxycycline, or ciprofloxacin for 10 to 14 days cures the disease when it is started early. Because pneumonic plague spreads by droplets, patients must be isolated, contacts must be traced and offered preventive doxycycline, and the veterinary and rodent control services must be brought in at once.

Q fever is caused by Coxiella burnetii, a bacterium that is unusual in two ways that matter to the nurse. First, it forms a small variant that resists drying and heat and survives in dust for long periods, so infection can follow the breathing of contaminated dust rather than direct contact with an animal. Second, it is shed in enormous numbers in the placenta and birth fluids of infected sheep, goats, and cattle, so assisting at a birth, cleaning a lambing pen, or handling an aborted fetus is a high-risk exposure, and the organism can travel on the wind from a farm to a nearby town. The incubation period is usually 2 to 3 weeks. Acute Q fever presents as a fever with a severe headache, an atypical pneumonia, or an inflammation of the liver with raised liver enzymes, and most patients recover, although the illness can be prolonged and exhausting. Chronic Q fever is a different disease, appearing months to years later as an infection of the heart valves in patients with existing valve disease or vascular grafts and in patients whose immunity is suppressed. The danger in pregnancy is easy to understate, because infection during pregnancy can cause inflammation of the placenta, miscarriage, premature delivery, stillbirth, and low birth weight, and the infection may be entirely silent in the mother.

The diagnosis is made by serology and by PCR, with acute infection recognized by antibodies against the phase two antigen and chronic infection by high levels of antibody against the phase one antigen, and paired samples are sent when the diagnosis is suspected because a single sample taken at the wrong time can mislead. Acute Q fever is treated with doxycycline at 100 milligrams by mouth twice a day for 14 days. Chronic infection of the heart valves requires doxycycline together with hydroxychloroquine for at least 18 months, with a specialist involved from the beginning because the regimen is difficult and relapse is real. Pregnancy is the situation that demands the most caution, because doxycycline cannot be used, and treatment is usually given with trimethoprim with sulfamethoxazole under specialist supervision, with careful monitoring of the pregnancy and the newborn. Prevention is largely a matter of animal management, and it means safe disposal of placentas and birth products, vaccination of sheep and goats, pasteurization of milk, and the use of personal protective equipment by anyone who works with pregnant animals. The nurse who teaches a farming family to treat every abortion in a goat as a contaminated event is preventing cases of Q fever in that family and in the town down the road.

The zoonoses in this chapter share a laboratory problem, and the nurse usually decides whether the laboratory can solve it. Many of these organisms are dangerous to handle on a routine bench, and Brucella, Coxiella burnetii, Burkholderia pseudomallei, Bacillus anthracis, and Yersinia pestis all require the laboratory to be warned in advance so that the sample is processed with the right containment. A blood culture for brucellosis must be held longer than the standard period, a sample for Q fever must be handled as an aerosol risk, and a sample for anthrax must not be opened in the open laboratory. The nurse therefore labels each specimen with the suspected diagnosis and the date of onset, uses the correct container and transport medium, packages the sample correctly, and telephones the laboratory when the diagnosis is urgent. Surveillance closes the circle, because plague, anthrax, rabies, and brucellosis are notifiable in most countries, and a case reported accurately and quickly sets in motion a veterinary investigation, rodent and flea control, dog vaccination, milk hygiene, and the tracing of contacts who may need preventive treatment. The same system detects the outbreak that a single clinic would miss, such as a cluster of brucellosis in one village, a group of Q fever cases downwind of a farm, or several cases of leptospirosis after a flood. Prevention at the household level is concrete and teachable, and it means boiling the milk or using only pasteurized dairy products, washing the hands after touching animals, covering open wounds before working in water or soil, controlling rodents around the house and the food store, vaccinating the dogs, and handling placentas and aborted fetuses as infectious material.

No regimen in this chapter should ever be given from memory. Doses, durations, and the choice of drug are revised as new evidence appears and as national programs change their protocols, and the same infection is managed differently in Thailand, in Kenya, in Brazil, and in the United States, so the current national guideline and the current product label must be checked before any medicine is prescribed, dispensed, or administered. The World Health Organization publishes guidance that is updated on its own schedule, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention issues advice for clinicians in the United States, and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control publishes guidance for Europe, and none of these can substitute for the national protocol that governs the nurse's own practice. The dose must be calculated from the weight measured on the day, not from a weight remembered from a previous admission, and it must be adjusted for renal function, hepatic function, pregnancy, age, and the blood counts measured that week. Rifampicin, doxycycline, trimethoprim with sulfamethoxazole, hydroxychloroquine, and the intravenous agents used for melioidosis all carry interactions and toxicities that change as the patient changes, so the nurse reviews the latest laboratory results before the next dose and treats every new symptom as information that may alter the prescription. For medicines that require an exact dose and a second check, the protocol usually asks for a second person to verify the calculation and the preparation, and the nurse follows that requirement every time rather than only when the patient looks unwell. Monitoring is continuous rather than occasional, and it includes the temperature curve, the blood pressure, the urine output, the liver enzymes, the blood counts, the serum potassium, the blood sugar, and the patient's own account of how the medicine is being taken at home.

To summarize, the zoonoses are infections that pass from animals to people, and they are recognized at the bedside by an exposure history rather than by a laboratory result. Leptospirosis comes from rodent urine in water and soil, produces a biphasic fever with conjunctival suffusion, and in its severe form causes jaundice, kidney failure, hemorrhage, and pulmonary bleeding. Brucellosis comes from unpasteurized dairy products and from contact with aborting animals, produces an undulant fever with sacroiliitis and sweats, and requires 6 weeks of doxycycline with rifampicin because shorter courses relapse. Rabies comes from the saliva of an infected mammal, has an incubation period of 1 to 3 months, begins with pain or tingling at the healed wound, and is almost always fatal once hydrophobia appears, which is why immediate washing of the wound and completion of the vaccine course are the interventions that matter most. Anthrax, melioidosis, and plague are bacterial zoonoses with different routes and different presentations, and each is treatable when it is recognized early and treated for the full course. Q fever is a hazard of lambing and of contaminated dust, and it is especially dangerous in pregnancy. Across all of them, the nurse's work is to ask about animals, to wash and protect wounds, to secure the correct specimen and warn the laboratory, to check every dose against the current guideline and the measured weight, to monitor the adverse effects of long and toxic regimens, and to report the case so that the animal and environmental side of the problem is addressed. That combination of curiosity about the patient's world and discipline about the medicine is what prevents these infections from becoming deaths that no one saw coming.
