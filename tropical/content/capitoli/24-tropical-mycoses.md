# Chapter 24 — Tropical Mycoses

The fungal infections of the tropics are the least taught and the most delayed in diagnosis of all the diseases in this book. They live in soil, in guano, on plants and on decaying wood, and they enter the body by inhalation or through broken skin. Most people who meet them are never ill, because a healthy immune system contains them, and illness appears when the inoculum is large or the defenses are weakened. This chapter covers histoplasmosis, coccidioidomycosis, paracoccidioidomycosis, mycetoma, sporotrichosis, chromoblastomycosis and cryptococcosis. The guidance followed here is that of the World Health Organization, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control. Every dose below is current guidance rather than settled truth, because regimens are revised, guidance differs between countries, and the current guideline must be checked before prescribing.

Fungi that cause disease in humans fall into two groups. The dimorphic fungi grow as molds in cool soil and as yeasts in human tissue, and they cause histoplasmosis, coccidioidomycosis, paracoccidioidomycosis and sporotrichosis. Others grow in the environment and take hold only when the skin is broken or the immune system fails, which is the pattern of mycetoma, chromoblastomycosis and cryptococcosis. Because the exposure is often agricultural and repeated, the history is a diagnostic instrument that deserves the same care as the malaria travel history.

Histoplasmosis is caused by Histoplasma capsulatum, a dimorphic fungus that grows best in soil enriched by the droppings of bats and birds. It is found on every continent but concentrated in the river valleys of North America, in parts of Central and South America, in Africa, in India and in Southeast Asia. The classic exposures are entering a cave, cleaning a chicken coop, clearing a starling roost, demolishing an old building, cutting trees, and turning soil that has held birds for years. A bat cave is the most concentrated exposure of all, because the guano is deep and the air is full of spores. The fungus does not pass from person to person, so the history that matters is what the patient breathed rather than whom the patient met.

After an incubation of about 3 to 17 days, most infections produce either no illness or a brief flu-like fever with cough, fatigue and muscle aching that settles without treatment. The severity depends on how many spores were inhaled, so a person who enters a cave or cleans a silo can develop a severe pneumonia with high fever, breathlessness and chest pain. The radiograph may show patchy shadowing and enlarged lymph nodes in the middle of the chest, which can later form a mass that presses on the airways. A small number of patients develop a chronic cavity in the upper lung that resembles tuberculosis and must be distinguished from it. Others develop inflammation of the joints or of the covering of the heart, because the immune response rather than the fungus causes that damage.

Disseminated histoplasmosis is the form that kills, and it appears when the immune system cannot hold the organism. Advanced HIV with a low helper T cell count, treatment with corticosteroids or with tumor necrosis factor inhibitors, organ transplantation and infancy are the settings in which it is seen most often. The patient presents with weeks of fever, weight loss, drenching sweats, enlargement of the liver and spleen, and a falling blood count, so the picture can resemble a blood cancer or miliary tuberculosis. Ulcers of the mouth, the tongue and the gut, a rash, and an enlarged adrenal gland are also described. In an infant or a severely immunosuppressed adult the course can be so rapid that treatment must begin on suspicion before any test returns.

The diagnosis of histoplasmosis rests on one test that is fast and several that are slow, and the nurse usually controls which specimen is sent. The antigen test detects a molecule shed by the organism in urine, in blood and in fluid from the lung, and it can give an answer in hours, so a urine sample should be collected before the first dose. It is not perfectly specific, because similar antigens are produced by Blastomyces, by Paracoccidioides and by Talaromyces marneffei, a fungus of Southeast Asia. Antibody tests rise later, culture takes weeks and requires a laboratory with the right safety precautions, and a biopsy can show the yeast inside the cells of the affected tissue. Send the urine, the blood, the culture and the biopsy together, and do not withhold treatment while waiting.

Current guidance from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention describes two pathways, depending on how sick the patient is. Mild or moderate acute pulmonary histoplasmosis is treated with itraconazole, 200 milligrams by mouth three times a day for 3 days and then twice a day for 6 to 12 weeks, while some mild cases are simply observed. Severe pulmonary disease and disseminated disease are treated with liposomal amphotericin B, 3 milligrams per kilogram of body weight intravenously once a day for 1 to 2 weeks, followed by itraconazole, 200 milligrams by mouth twice a day. In disseminated disease the azole continues for at least 12 months, and in a patient with HIV it continues until the immune system recovers on antiretroviral therapy. Read those numbers as an illustration, verify them against the current guideline, and expect the durations and the alternatives to differ between countries.

Itraconazole is a difficult drug to take well, and most treatment failures in practice come from absorption rather than from resistance. The capsule needs gastric acid to dissolve, so it should be taken with food, and never with an antacid, a stomach acid blocker or a drug for reflux, because those reduce the amount that reaches the blood. The liquid formulation is better absorbed and is preferred when the patient cannot eat or is taking acid suppression. Itraconazole interferes with many medicines through the liver enzymes that handle drugs, including some statins, warfarin, rifampicin, phenytoin and several antiretroviral agents. It can also weaken the pumping of the heart, so it is used cautiously in heart failure, and it raises the liver enzymes, which should be checked before and during treatment. A drug level is often measured after 1 to 2 weeks to confirm that the dose is adequate.

Amphotericin B is the most effective drug in this chapter and the most demanding to give. The liposomal form is preferred because it carries the drug to the tissue with less damage to the kidney, and it is infused intravenously over 2 hours with fluids and careful observation. It commonly causes fever, shivering and nausea after the infusion, and those reactions are reduced by giving fluids, by slowing the infusion, and sometimes by premedication with acetaminophen and diphenhydramine. The serious effects are on the kidney, which wastes potassium and magnesium and reduces the filtering rate, so the creatinine, the potassium and the magnesium must be measured at least every 1 to 2 days, and a falling red cell count also develops. Stop and call the prescriber if the creatinine rises sharply, if the potassium cannot be maintained, or if the patient becomes short of breath.

Coccidioidomycosis is caused by Coccidioides immitis and Coccidioides posadasii, dimorphic fungi that live in the dry, alkaline soil of deserts. They are found in the southwestern United States, particularly Arizona and central California, in northern Mexico, in parts of Central America, and in scattered dry regions of South America. The organism waits in the soil as a mold that fragments into infective spores, and those spores become airborne when the ground is disturbed by a dust storm, by construction, by farming or by an earthquake. The incubation period is 1 to 3 weeks, and the risk is highest for people who work outdoors in these regions and for laborers who are moved into dusty land. It does not spread from person to person, so the diagnosis depends on knowing where the patient has breathed.

The illness is often called valley fever, and most people who inhale the spores have no symptoms or a mild illness that is mistaken for influenza. When it produces illness, the patient has fever, cough, chest pain, headache, fatigue and aching joints, and a rash appears in a minority. Two skin findings are characteristic, namely tender red nodules on the shins called erythema nodosum and a blotchy rash called erythema multiforme, and both signal a vigorous immune response rather than deep spread. The joints may swell and hurt so much that the illness is nicknamed desert rheumatism. A small proportion of patients, roughly 1 per cent, develop disseminated disease, which can involve the skin, the bones and the coverings of the brain, and that risk is higher in people of Filipino or African ancestry, in pregnancy, and in anyone whose immune system is suppressed.

Diagnosis is usually made by serology, which detects immunoglobulin M early and immunoglobulin G later, supported by culture and by PCR on a respiratory sample. Culture of this organism is dangerous to laboratory staff, so the laboratory must be told what is suspected before the specimen is handled. Uncomplicated pulmonary disease is treated with fluconazole, 400 milligrams by mouth once a day, or itraconazole, 200 milligrams twice a day, for 3 to 6 months, and many mild cases settle without treatment. Severe or disseminated disease is treated with liposomal amphotericin B, 3 to 5 milligrams per kilogram of body weight intravenously once a day, followed by an azole for a prolonged period. Infection of the coverings of the brain requires an azole at a higher dose for years, often for life, and needs specialist care. The azoles are avoided in the first trimester of pregnancy because they can harm the fetus, so amphotericin is used instead.

Paracoccidioidomycosis is caused by Paracoccidioides brasiliensis and Paracoccidioides lutzii, dimorphic fungi that live in the humid soil of Latin America. It is reported most often in Brazil, Colombia, Venezuela, Argentina and Ecuador, and it is a disease of rural workers who clear land, plant crops and handle soil and vegetation. The infection is acquired by inhalation, and it can remain silent for years before producing illness, so the exposure may lie far in the past. It affects men far more often than women, a difference attributed to the protective effect of estrogen on the conversion of the mold to the yeast form. Unlike the other mycoses in this chapter, it is a chronic disease that unfolds over months and is often mistaken for tuberculosis or for a cancer of the head and neck.

The patient usually presents with weight loss, fever, cough and breathlessness, together with painful ulcers of the mouth, the nose, the gums and the larynx that make eating and speaking difficult. The lymph nodes of the neck may enlarge and drain, the skin may show crusted lesions, and the adrenal glands are involved in a minority of patients, which can produce a quiet adrenal insufficiency that appears only under stress. Diagnosis rests on microscopy that shows yeasts with multiple buds arranged like the spokes of a wheel, on culture, and on serology. Treatment is itraconazole, 200 milligrams by mouth once a day for 6 to 12 months, with trimethoprim and sulfamethoxazole as an alternative when the azole is unavailable or unsuitable. Relapse is common when the course is too short, so follow the patient for at least 2 years and check adrenal function when the picture suggests it.

Mycetoma, also called Madura foot, is a chronic infection of the skin, the deeper tissues and the bone that begins when an organism is pushed through the skin by a thorn, a splinter, a stone or a sharp stem. It is a disease of people who walk and work barefoot on cultivated land, and it is reported most often in Sudan, in Mexico, in India and in Senegal. The infection grows slowly over months and years, and at first it causes a painless swelling that the patient ignores. In time it becomes a firm mass with nodules that open onto the skin and discharge through sinuses, and the discharge carries small grains whose color is a clue but never a proof. As it spreads, it destroys muscle, tendon and bone, and the foot becomes deformed and useless, though the pain may remain mild until the bone is involved. It produces disability, loss of livelihood and severe stigma, and the World Health Organization has targeted it for better recognition.

The single most important fact about mycetoma is that it has two families of causes, and the treatment is different for each. Fungal mycetoma, called eumycetoma, is caused most often by Madurella mycetomatis, responds poorly to drugs, and often requires surgery. Bacterial mycetoma, called actinomycetoma, is caused by bacteria such as Actinomadura madurae, Actinomadura pelletieri, Streptomyces somaliensis and Nocardia brasiliensis, and it responds to antibiotics, sometimes without any operation at all. Because the grains can look alike, the distinction rests on culture of the grains, on their microscopic appearance, and increasingly on molecular testing. A patient who is treated for years with an antifungal when the cause is bacterial loses time, money and tissue, and a patient treated with antibiotics when the cause is fungal may lose the foot.

Bacterial mycetoma is treated with antibiotics for months, and the combinations most often described are trimethoprim with sulfamethoxazole, sometimes with dapsone, or amikacin with a carbapenem in severe disease. Fungal mycetoma is treated with itraconazole, often 200 to 400 milligrams by mouth twice a day for a year or longer, and ketoconazole at 400 to 800 milligrams a day was used for many years before its liver and hormonal effects limited it. Practice in endemic countries adds surgery to remove the bulk of the diseased tissue, because a large fungal mass will not be cured by drug alone, and amputation is sometimes the only option for a destroyed foot. Imaging with ultrasound and magnetic resonance imaging shows how deep the disease has reached and whether the bone is involved. Ketoconazole, once the mainstay of fungal treatment, is now limited by liver damage and by its effects on testosterone and cortisol, so liver tests and hormone effects must be watched. Verify every dose and every duration against the current guideline, and confirm the culture result before committing a patient to years of a toxic drug.

Sporotrichosis is caused by species of the genus Sporothrix, which live in soil, in hay, in straw, in sphagnum moss and on the bark of plants. The classic story is a gardener or a farmer who is scratched by a rose thorn or a barberry thorn, and the organism enters through the puncture. It is also acquired by handling hay, by working with moss, by hunting armadillos, and from the scratch of an infected cat, which has produced large urban outbreaks in Brazil. Sporotrichosis is not transmitted from person to person. Because the exposure is so specific, the nurse who asks about gardening, farming, floristry, forestry and animal contact will often make the diagnosis before the laboratory does.

After an incubation of 1 to 12 weeks, a painless nodule appears at the site of inoculation and then breaks down into a crusted ulcer. In the most common form, new nodules appear along the lymph channels that drain the area, forming a chain of lesions up the arm or the leg, and the regional lymph nodes enlarge. A fixed form stays at the site of inoculation, a disseminated form spreads through the body in immunosuppressed patients, and inhalation produces a pulmonary form that resembles tuberculosis. The diagnosis is made by culture of the discharge or a biopsy, because the organism is hard to see on microscopy. Lymphocutaneous and fixed forms are treated with itraconazole, 200 milligrams by mouth once a day for 3 to 6 months, potassium iodide solution is an older and inexpensive alternative, and severe or disseminated disease is treated with amphotericin B. Corticosteroids should not be used, because they worsen the infection.

Chromoblastomycosis is a chronic infection of the skin and the tissue beneath it, caused by dark molds that live in soil and on decaying wood and plants. The organisms include Fonsecaea pedrosoi, Cladophialophora carrionii, Phialophora verrucosa and Rhinocladiella aquaspersa, and they are found across the tropics and subtropics, with heavy reporting from Madagascar, Brazil, Mexico, Venezuela, India and parts of Africa. The infection follows a thorn, a splinter or a piece of wood pushed into the skin, usually on the lower leg or the foot, in a person who works barefoot or in sandals among vegetation. The lesion grows slowly over years into a warty, cauliflower-like plaque with black dots and crusting, and it may itch, but it rarely spreads through the body. Secondary bacterial infection, odor, disability and stigma are common, and the patient often arrives after many years of ineffective home treatment.

Diagnosis is made by scraping the lesion or taking a biopsy and looking for the dark, thick-walled cells called muriform cells, which are the form the fungus takes in tissue and which are the diagnostic finding. Culture confirms the organism. Treatment is difficult and prolonged, and current guidance describes itraconazole at 200 to 400 milligrams by mouth once a day, terbinafine at 500 milligrams a day, and flucytosine, often in combination for 6 to 12 months or longer. Small lesions are sometimes treated by surgical removal, by freezing with liquid nitrogen or by local heat, and these physical methods are valuable because the drug alone often fails. Relapse is frequent, so follow the patient for several years after the lesion has flattened, and check the liver and the blood count during a long course. Because the disease is disfiguring and slow, the psychological and economic burden is as real as the physical one.

Cryptococcosis is caused by Cryptococcus neoformans and Cryptococcus gattii, yeasts surrounded by a thick capsule that protects them from the immune system. Cryptococcus neoformans is found worldwide and is associated with soil enriched by pigeon droppings and with old, weathered wood, while Cryptococcus gattii is linked to certain trees and to soil and causes disease in people who are not immunosuppressed, particularly on Vancouver Island, in the Pacific Northwest of North America and in parts of Australia. The organism is inhaled into the lung and can stay there, and it spreads through the blood to the coverings of the brain when the immune system fails. Cryptococcosis is the second most common serious fungal infection in people with advanced HIV, and it is a major cause of death in that group in Africa and Asia. It is not spread from person to person.

The usual illness is a meningoencephalitis that develops over 1 to 4 weeks with headache, fever, neck stiffness, nausea, sensitivity to light and a change in mental state. The features that make it dangerous are the raised pressure inside the head and the swelling of the brain, which can cause blindness, hearing loss and death within days. Some patients have no fever and no neck stiffness, and a patient with HIV may look deceptively well, so the diagnosis is easily missed. The antigen test on serum and on cerebrospinal fluid is the key investigation, because it is fast, it is sensitive, and a rapid version can be done at the bedside in many programs. A positive serum antigen test in a person with advanced HIV and a low helper T cell count identifies disease before symptoms appear, which is why screening is recommended. Culture and microscopy with India ink staining are used where the antigen test is unavailable.

The World Health Organization recommends a short induction course of liposomal amphotericin B, 3 to 4 milligrams per kilogram of body weight intravenously once a day, with flucytosine, 25 milligrams per kilogram by mouth four times a day, for 1 week, followed by a second week of fluconazole, 1200 milligrams by mouth once a day. Consolidation then uses fluconazole, 400 to 800 milligrams by mouth once a day for 8 weeks, and maintenance uses fluconazole, 200 milligrams once a day, continued until the immune system recovers on antiretroviral therapy. Where flucytosine is unavailable, amphotericin B with fluconazole is used, and where amphotericin is unavailable, high-dose fluconazole alone is the fallback. Raised pressure inside the head is managed by draining cerebrospinal fluid through repeated lumbar puncture, and it must be treated even when the antifungal is working. Antiretroviral therapy is usually started 4 to 6 weeks after antifungal treatment begins, because starting it earlier increases the risk of immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome, the paradoxical worsening that follows immune recovery.

The defining practical problem of this chapter is that these diseases are diagnosed late, and the reason is not clinical ignorance alone. The antigen tests are not licensed or stocked in many countries, culture takes weeks and needs a laboratory with a mycology capacity that most district hospitals do not have, and biopsy and histopathology may be days of travel away. The drugs are worse, because liposomal amphotericin B, flucytosine and itraconazole are expensive, available only in referral centers, and subject to stockouts that can interrupt a course. This is why fungal disease earns the name of a neglected disease, even though the organisms are everywhere. The nurse who documents a failure of access, who escalates it, and who starts whatever treatment is available is doing as much for the patient as the clinician who names the organism.

Logistics decide outcomes here as much as pharmacology, and the nursing role in logistics is central. Keep a written stock record of every antifungal your clinic holds, know the expiry dates, rotate the older supply first, and know the referral pharmacy that can supply what you lack. Amphotericin B needs a controlled temperature and protection from light, the reconstituted drug must be used within the time stated on the label, and the liposomal form must not be frozen. Flucytosine and itraconazole come in limited pack sizes, so a patient beginning a year of treatment may need a plan for refills, transport and money before the first dose is given. Specimens for antigen testing need the correct container, prompt transport and a laboratory that will accept them, and the nurse usually controls that chain. Write the plan in the notes so that the next nurse can continue it.

Monitoring during antifungal treatment is a set of ordinary observations repeated with discipline. Record temperature, heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate and weight at every visit, because weight change reflects both the disease and the dose calculation in a child. Check the liver enzymes, the kidney function, the potassium, the magnesium and the blood counts on the schedule the guideline sets, and respond to a rising creatinine or a falling potassium before the patient becomes ill. Ask about vision, hearing and headache at every visit when the brain has been involved, because raised pressure and nerve damage can progress quietly. Ask directly about missed doses, nausea, stomach pain, rash and sensitivity to sunlight, since patients often stop a toxic medicine without saying so. In a long course, also screen for depression and for loss of income, because the illness and the treatment both consume the patient's life.

Adherence over years is a different problem from adherence over days, and it needs a different approach. The patient must understand why the medicine continues after the fever has gone, what happens if it stops, and which symptoms must bring them back, and that conversation must be repeated at several visits rather than delivered once. Simplify the schedule where possible, link refills to a date the patient can remember, and use the family, the community health worker and the treatment supporter as partners rather than as police. Address the costs of transport, food and lost work explicitly, because a patient who cannot afford the journey is not a patient who has refused treatment. Use a pill box or a written card for a patient with low literacy, and check the card at each visit. When a dose is missed, ask why before assigning blame, because the answer usually reveals a barrier that can be fixed.

Chronic wound care is the nursing skill that this chapter demands most, because mycetoma and chromoblastomycosis can last for decades. Clean the wound gently with clean water or saline, remove dead tissue, and choose a dressing that manages the heavy discharge and the odor without macerating the surrounding skin. Measure the lesion and record the size, the number of sinuses and the character of the grains at each visit, so that slow improvement becomes visible. Protect the foot from further injury with appropriate footwear, and involve a foot specialist or an orthopedic colleague early when the bone is involved. Treat pain, because the pain of a long-standing infection is underrecognized, and treat the odor, because it drives stigma and isolation. Ask about work, family and mood, and connect the patient to a support group or a rehabilitation service where one exists.

The nursing role in tropical mycoses can be stated as a series of concrete habits. Take an exposure history that includes caves, guano, chicken coops, dusty land, construction, farming, gardening, thorns, barefoot work and animal contact, and record it in the patient's own words. Collect the right specimen before the first dose, label it clearly, and track it until the result returns. Verify every dose against the current guideline, checking the weight, the concentration on the label and the maximum dose before administration, and question any order that does not match. Monitor the parameters described here, teach the patient what to report, and organize the refills that make a long course possible. Prevent what can be prevented by teaching footwear, gloves, protective clothing and, where dust is heavy, a mask. Finally, treat stigma as a clinical problem, because a patient who is ashamed of a discharging foot will stay away until the foot is lost.

To summarize, the tropical mycoses are diseases of exposure, immunity and access, and each of those words points to something the advanced practice nurse can act on. Histoplasmosis follows bat and bird guano, coccidioidomycosis follows desert dust, paracoccidioidomycosis follows humid Latin American farmland, mycetoma follows a bare foot on thorny ground, sporotrichosis follows a rose thorn, chromoblastomycosis follows a splinter, and cryptococcosis follows a failing immune system. Most are diagnosed late because the antigen tests, the cultures and the drugs exist in few places, and that delay costs tissue, vision, hearing and life. The drugs are effective but unforgiving, so itraconazole, ketoconazole, fluconazole, flucytosine and amphotericin B each require a monitoring plan and a teaching conversation. Treatment often runs for months or years, which makes adherence, refills, wound care and the reduction of stigma part of the cure. Name the source of every dose, check the current guideline before prescribing, verify the weight and the concentration before administering, and remember that the nurse's history and follow-up are frequently what turn a chronic and disfiguring illness into a manageable one.
