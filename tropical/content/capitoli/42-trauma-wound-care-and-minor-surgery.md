# Chapter 42 — Trauma, Wound Care, and Minor Surgery

Trauma reaches the remote clinic before any other emergency does. Road traffic injuries, falls from heights, burns from open cooking fires, and machete or animal wounds arrive together, and the nearest surgeon may be a full day of travel away. In that geography, the advanced practice nurse is not the assistant to a trauma team; you are the trauma team, and the decisions usually fall to whoever holds the dressing tray. This chapter walks through the skills that save the most: the primary survey, the control of bleeding, the judgment of which wound to close and which to leave open, abscess drainage, debridement, burns, splints, chest drains, and the prevention of tetanus and rabies. Where a dose or a schedule appears, it reflects current published guidance, and you must verify the current version before you act.

The primary survey exists to prevent one specific error: letting a dramatic wound distract you while the patient quietly dies of something you never checked. The order never changes, and it is worth saying aloud until it is automatic: airway, breathing, circulation, disability, exposure. Before any of it, stop at the edge of the scene and look for what creates second victims, which means moving traffic, leaking fuel, unstable structures, a live wire, or an angry crowd. Once the scene is safe, work in the fixed order, because a blocked airway kills in minutes and a crushed leg, however terrible, does not. Talk as you work, since a patient who answers in clear sentences has, at that moment, a working airway and a working brain, and that single observation is the fastest triage tool you own. If helpers are present, assign one to hold the head still, because any blow violent enough to cause visible injuries is violent enough to break the cervical spine.

The airway comes first, and the maneuver matters more than any equipment. If the voice is hoarse, the breathing gurgles, or the patient is unconscious, open the airway with a jaw thrust, which lifts the tongue forward without tilting the neck, and keep the head held still with hands while you check breathing. Look at the chest, listen at the mouth, and feel air on your cheek, spending seconds, not minutes. A hole in the chest that sucks air with every breath is sealed first with an occlusive dressing taped on three sides, so that air can escape but not return. An unconscious patient who is breathing goes onto the side, rolled as one unit with the spine protected, because a relaxed airway left flat will flood. Simple positioning, the recovery position, and clearing debris save more airways in remote settings than any device that is not on the shelf.

Breathing assessed, move to circulation, and in trauma that word means bleeding. Let your eyes lead your hands: scan the scalp, the chest, the abdomen, both flanks, the thighs, and the ground beneath, because a patient lying in a spreading pool has already lost more than a liter. A fast, thin pulse with cold, pale, anxious skin means shock until proven otherwise, and the bleeding that causes it is usually on the outside, which is the good news, because you can reach it. Disability is a rapid check of consciousness, pupil size, and one-sided weakness, telling you whether the brain is injured along with the body. Exposure means undressing enough to find every wound, then covering the patient again at once, because a cold trauma patient bleeds worse. Only after these five steps comes the secondary survey, a head-to-toe examination with the mechanism, the allergies, the medications, the last meal, and the events, taken from witnesses when the patient cannot speak.

Hemorrhage control deserves its own minutes, because bleeding is the most preventable cause of trauma death. Direct pressure stops most external bleeding by pressing the vessel walls together so a clot can form, and it works only when it is truly direct. Lay a thick pad of clean cloth on the wound, press hard with the heel of your hand, and do not lift the pad to peek, because every peek destroys the clot that is forming. If the pad soaks through, add layers on top rather than removing them. Hold real pressure for a full ten minutes when the situation allows, since impatience, not technique, is the usual reason for failure.

Some wounds cannot be pressed against a bone, and these are the ones that need packing: the groin, the armpit, the neck, the buttock, and the deep thigh. Packing fills the cavity with gauze or clean cloth firmly enough to create pressure from inside, squeezing the bleeding vessel's walls shut. Fill from the depth upward, layer by layer, pressing each layer down before adding the next, so that the cavity is filled rather than plugged at its mouth. If hemostatic gauze, the kind carrying a clotting agent, is on the shelf, it goes against the deepest bleeding point first, held with pressure for the time stated on the package, usually about three minutes. Plain gauze works too, but a deep thigh wound can swallow several meters of bandage before the bleeding slows. Leave the pack in place for at least a day before opening and cleaning the wound again, and that delay is a deliberate part of the treatment, not neglect.

The tourniquet is for the limb wound that floods despite pressure, meaning amputations, mangling injuries, and major vessel wounds, and for the trapped patient who must be freed or carried. Place it 5 to 7 centimeters above the wound, never over a joint, and tighten until the bleeding stops and the pulse beyond it disappears, which takes real force and hurts. A commercial tourniquet is best, but a folded cloth about 5 centimeters wide, twisted tight with a stick as a windlass, works, while wire, string, or a narrow cord cuts skin without stopping the flow. Write the time on the forehead or on the tourniquet, and once bleeding is controlled, do not loosen it in the field without training, since each loosening restarts the bleeding and wastes the clot. The complications are real, with pain, nerve injury, and limb loss after two hours or more, so a tourniquet is a bridge to repair, never the repair. Tell the receiving clinician about it and its time, and teach the technique widely in your community, because most rural bleeding deaths happen before any clinic is reached.

Once the patient is stable, every wound gets the same four questions before anything is cleaned. How did it happen, because glass on clean skin, a machete through mud, and a tooth over a knuckle carry different risks. How long ago, because bacterial counts in a wound double every hour or so in a warm climate, and time is the wound's worst enemy. How dirty, meaning soil, saliva, river water, dead tissue, or a foreign body left inside, remembering that a puncture drives contamination deep where you cannot reach. And what lies beneath the skin at that spot, which you test by asking the patient to move everything the wound could cross and by checking pulses, sensation, and strength beyond it. A wound crossing a joint, opening a tendon sheath, exposing bone, made by a bite, or sitting over a fracture belongs to surgical hands, and it is better to transfer a stable patient with an open tendon than to close over one.

The decision to close a wound is a decision about bacteria. A freshly cut, clean wound closed within the first hours heals quietly, because living tissue can defend the small numbers present. A contaminated wound closed tightly seals bacteria inside a warm, airless space with no drainage and no defense, which is the exact recipe for a closed abscess, and in a tropical climate the recipe succeeds with terrible reliability. That is why not closing is often the correct answer in remote practice, and it is a mark of experience rather than defeat. Classic teaching allows primary closure within about 6 hours for clean wounds, and up to 24 hours on the face, but the clock matters less than the wound itself. A wound that looks clean and freshly made can usually be closed, and a wound that looks angry never should be, whatever the clock says.

Delayed primary closure works like this: clean the wound thoroughly, leave it open under a loose dressing, and look again after 48 to 72 hours. If it is clean then, with no fever, spreading redness, or pus, bring the edges together with a few sutures, and it will heal as though it had been closed on the first day. If it is not clean, it declares itself while still open, and you have lost nothing but a short wait. Healing by secondary intention means letting the wound fill with new tissue from its floor, contracting slowly over weeks, and it is the natural path for punctures, bites, and heavily contaminated wounds. Keep such wounds clean and dressed, protect them from flies, and tell the patient honestly that healing will take weeks and leave a scar, which is a fair price for avoiding an infection under a closed skin. The face, with its rich blood supply, forgives closure more readily, while the lower leg, where swelling and poor venous return slow everything, forgives almost nothing dirty.

Whatever you decide about closing, irrigation comes first, and its lesson is that the volume of clean fluid removes bacteria and debris mechanically, so that nothing you pour into a wound adds as much as it removes. Irrigate with normal saline where it exists, or with boiled and cooled water, or with any water fit for drinking, since studies have found no meaningful difference in infection rates among them, and a liter flushed through a wound beats any antiseptic. Use a 20 to 60 milliliter syringe with a cannula, aiming a stream at moderate pressure across the wound and out again, letting the dirty fluid run off the lowest edge into a basin. Avoid jet-like pressure, which can drive contamination deeper and splash blood toward your eyes. Antiseptics belong on the intact skin around the wound, not in it: alcohol and hydrogen peroxide damage the living tissue you are trying to save, and povidone-iodine inside the wound should be dilute and brief if used at all. Wear gloves and eye protection, wash hands between patients, and remember that the wound must be protected from your bacteria as much as from the patient's.

Debridement is the removal of what cannot live: crushed fat, gray muscle that does not bleed when cut, fragments detached from any blood supply, and tissue already decayed. Dead tissue is not a bystander but food for bacteria, a barrier to antibiotics, and the reason small wounds become large infections. Under good local anesthesia, lift the dead material with forceps and cut it away in small increments, working until bright bleeding appears from every edge, which is the reliable sign of living tissue. Color is a weaker guide than bleeding and contractility, since viable muscle is red and twitches when touched, while dead muscle is dull and still. Stop when the wound is clean, not when it is pretty, and be conservative over tendons, joints, and nerves, which deserve the chance to recover if they have any attachment at all. Wounds needing repeated formal debridement, or debridement that would open a joint or expose major vessels, belong to surgical hands, and your work is to clean, dress, stabilize, and arrange the transfer.

Nothing in wound care justifies skipping anesthesia, because the pain of repair is itself a reason patients stay away. Lidocaine 1 per cent, which is 10 milligrams per milliliter, infiltrated through a fine needle just under the skin at the wound edges, blocks the sodium channels that carry pain, with a maximum safe dose of 3 milligrams per kilogram of body weight without epinephrine and 5 milligrams per kilogram with it. Injecting slowly through the wound edge rather than through intact skin hurts less, and warming the solution helps too. Inflamed tissue defeats lidocaine because infection makes tissue acidic and the drug cannot reach the nerve, which is why anesthesia around an abscess is so unrewarding. Watch for systemic toxicity as you infiltrate, with numbness of the tongue and lips, ringing in the ears, slurred speech, confusion, and at worst a seizure, and stop injecting at the first sign. For fingers and toes, a ring of plain lidocaine at the base anesthetizes the whole digit and keeps you out of a painful wound bed. The limits given here follow widely published guidance, but verify the current one before you infiltrate, since recommendations are revised from time to time.

Suturing by voice begins with materials. Nonabsorbable sutures, most commonly nylon or polypropylene, are for skin: they hold until the wound has healed, and are then removed. Absorbable sutures, such as polyglactin, are for the layers beneath the skin, for fascia, muscle, and the inside of the mouth, dissolving over weeks so that nothing must be fished out later. Silk handles pleasantly but provokes tissue reaction and is a poor choice in contaminated wounds. Sizes are spoken by number, with 3-0 or 4-0 for skin on trunk and limbs, 5-0 for the face and hands where scars should be fine, and 2-0 for the scalp and for repairs under tension. The needle is curved: skin takes a cutting needle, whose sharp edges pass through tough hide cleanly, while deeper layers take a round-bodied tapered needle that spreads rather than slices. Sutures come packaged sterile with the needle already attached, and a stocked, resterilized instrument set, autoclaved where power allows or boiled for a full 15 minutes where it does not, is worth more to your patients than any antiseptic on the shelf.

The simple interrupted suture is the stitch to master, and it is best spoken as a cycle. Grip the needle a third of the way back from its point, enter the skin about 5 millimeters from the wound edge, and drive the point downward and forward through the full thickness, letting the needle travel its own curve rather than forcing it. Emerge on the cut surface at the depth where you will enter the other side, because matched depth is what makes the edges meet level. Cross the wound, take the matching bite, and roll the needle out through the skin 5 millimeters beyond, so that the two bites and the wound floor form one symmetric loop. Tie with the instrument in your hand, placing the first throw double, flattening the knot to the side of the wound rather than on the line, and cinch until the edges just touch, never tighter, because strangled skin swells and dies. Cut the tails to about half a centimeter, place the next stitch 5 to 10 millimeters along, and keep checking your work, since the edges should sit slightly raised and meeting at the surface.

Two mattress techniques solve what the simple stitch cannot. The vertical mattress takes a deep bite far from the edge and a shallow bite close to it on the same side before crossing, closing dead space underneath and everting stubborn edges, and it is the stitch for wounds that keep springing apart. The horizontal mattress spreads tension across two bites on each side, useful over a joint or on a shin where skin cannot be brought together without help, and both mattress stitches are spaced more widely and released earlier if the skin blanches, because their tension is their purpose and their danger. Not every wound needs a needle at all: shallow, clean, low-tension wounds do well with wound adhesive or with adhesive strips anchored across the cleaned edges, and every stitch you avoid is pain spared and a supply preserved. Removal timing follows the skin's healing rate, with face sutures out at 5 to 7 days, scalp and trunk at 10 to 12, limbs at 12 to 14, and over joints up to 14. Cut the stitch at the skin and pull the clean end, never the knotted end, through the track, so that surface bacteria are not dragged into the wound.

An abscess is a walled-off collection of pus, most often seeded by Staphylococcus aureus, the common skin bacterium, entering through a hair follicle or a break in the skin. The signs are heat, tenderness, and swelling, but the sign that matters is fluctuance, the soft give of fluid under your fingers when one presses and another rests on the far side of the mass. The overlying skin becomes shiny and thin, sometimes with a pointing white head, and the patient guards the part and walks differently depending on where it sits. Antibiotics alone fail for a mechanical reason: the cavity has no blood supply, so no drug reaches the pus, and the pressure inside is what makes the pain throb in time with the pulse. Fever and spreading redness mean containment is failing. A superficial abscess that is clearly fluctuant and away from major structures is yours to drain, and drainage is the treatment, complete in itself.

There are times the knife is the wrong instrument. An abscess still in its early hot, hard, red stage, with no give under the fingers, is in the cellulitis phase, and cutting it releases no pus while opening clean tissue to infection, so warm compresses, rest, an antibiotic active against skin bacteria, and patience until fluctuance appears are the correct care. A swelling you cannot confidently feel, a deep or multiloculated abscess, or one in the palm, the neck, the breast, or the groin, where a blind cut can open a vessel, a nerve, or a duct, belongs to imaging and surgical hands, and the rural move is antibiotics and transfer. When you drain, anesthetize the skin over the dome or field-block around it, because injecting into the cavity both hurts and fails. Incise with a number 11 blade along the natural creases, make the opening wide at its base, express the pus gently, sweep a hemostat once through the cavity to break the inner walls, and irrigate. Pack loosely with gauze for 24 to 48 hours, then begin warm soaks twice a day, and leave the wound open, because an abscess heals by secondary intention, and closing it over is how it comes back.

Burns are assessed by depth and extent, and depth decides everything. A superficial burn, like most sunburn or a brief flash, is red, painful, blanches under pressure, and heals within a week because only the outer layer of skin is hurt. A superficial partial-thickness burn reaches into the dermis: it is blistered, moist, pink, extremely painful, still blanches, and heals in two to three weeks with good care. A deep partial-thickness burn is mottled red and white, less painful because the nerve endings themselves are injured, no longer blanches, and takes more than three weeks or needs grafting. A full-thickness burn destroys the whole skin, leaving a leathery, waxy, or charred surface that is dry, painless, and unable to heal from within. The trap is the deep burn that looks survivable, so treat any burn of uncertain depth as the deeper one and reassess at 48 hours, since depth declares itself over two days.

Extent is measured as per cent of total body surface area, counting only partial and full-thickness burns, because superficial burns lose no meaningful fluid. The rule of nines gives the adult: 9 per cent for the head, 9 for each arm, 18 for the front of the trunk, 18 for the back, 18 for each leg, and about 1 per cent for the groin. The patient's own palm, fingers included, is a portable 1 per cent measure for scattered burns. Children carry proportionally more of their surface on the head and less on the legs, so their numbers differ, and if children are common in your clinic, learn the pediatric version. Suspect a burned airway with any facial burn, singed nasal hair, hoarseness, or a fire in an enclosed space, and treat it as an urgent transfer even when breathing is still comfortable, because the airway narrows over hours. First aid is cooling with clean running water for 10 to 20 minutes if within three hours of the burn, never ice, keeping the rest of the patient warm, and gently explaining that ash, egg, and toothpaste belong on the table, not in the wound.

Fluid needs in the first hours are calculated, not guessed, because both under-resuscitation and over-resuscitation kill. The Parkland formula, in its classic form, gives 4 milliliters of Ringer's lactate per kilogram of body weight for each per cent of body surface area burned, with half of that volume in the first 8 hours counted from the moment of the burn, and the remainder over the following 16 hours. The formula is only a starting point, and the patient then tells you the truth: pass a catheter for any large burn, and keep the urine at half a milliliter per kilogram per hour in adults and 1 milliliter per kilogram per hour in children. Concentrated dark urine means more fluid, and a clear flood with puffy eyelids means less. Refer burns over 10 per cent of the surface in adults and over 5 per cent in children, burns of face, hands, feet, genitals, or joints, any full-thickness burn, any airway burn, and every chemical or electrical burn, after cooling, fluid, analgesia, and tetanus care have begun. Give morphine for a significant burn in small titrated doses without hesitation, because the pain is severe and its memory lasts. The volumes above follow standard trauma teaching, but verify the current guidance your program uses before writing a fluid plan.

Fracture care in the field is splinting, and the reasons are worth hearing: immobilizing the bone ends stops the fragments sawing into muscle and nerve, quiets pain dramatically, slows the hidden bleeding a broken femur can hold, and keeps a closed fracture closed. A splint must cross the joint above and the joint below the break, and it must be padded, because a hard splint on prominent bone makes wounds of its own. Improvised splints are everywhere: sticks or poles laid along the limb and wrapped with cloth, cardboard folded into a gutter, a folded blanket for padding, a sling for arms and collarbones, sandbags to hold a leg at rest. A femur may need traction, and traction without training has caused real harm, so if you have not been taught it, splint, give analgesia, and transfer. Gentle longitudinal traction to straighten a grossly deformed limb is reasonable when the distal pulses are absent, since restoring circulation outranks skin, and the alignment is then held while the splint goes on. Before and after every splint, perform the same neurovascular check: feel the pulse beyond the injury, press the nail bed and watch the color return in under two seconds, and ask the patient to move the fingers or toes and report any change in sensation, comparing with the other side whenever unsure.

An open fracture is an orthopedic emergency with an infection clock running, because the broken bone has been in contact with the dirt of the road. Do not push the bone end back under the skin unless it threatens to pierce the skin or the limb beyond it is pulseless, since reducing it in the field drags contamination inward; cover the bone with a clean, preferably sterile moist dressing, splint in the position found, and give antibiotics early. Current surgical guidance favors a first-generation cephalosporin such as cefazolin 1 gram intravenously, with added coverage for soil organisms, such as metronidazole, for heavily soiled wounds, and the exact regimen must be verified against the current guideline before you give it. Assess tetanus prophylaxis on every open fracture without exception, and keep the patient nil by mouth from the start, because early surgery is likely. Your documentation should state the time of injury, the antibiotic time, the wound's appearance, and the distal findings, because the receiving surgeon will build decisions on your notes. A suspected pelvic fracture after a crush or a high fall, with pain on pressing the hips or blood at the urethral opening, is stabilized by binding the pelvis snugly with a folded sheet at the hip level, since the binder reduces the space available to bleed into.

The chest drain is the one invasive procedure in this chapter that demands supervised training before you attempt it alone, and what follows is written to make you a safer decision-maker and monitor, not a self-taught operator. The situations demanding a drain are tension pneumothorax, a large pneumothorax with breathlessness, and a hemothorax filling the chest with blood. A tension pneumothorax is a one-way tear in the lung: air enters the chest with each breath and cannot leave, pressure collapses the lung and then pushes the heart and the great veins aside, and the patient dies because the veins are squeezed shut and no blood returns. The signs are severe breathlessness, distended neck veins, absent breath sounds with a drum-like chest on one side, and shock, with the windpipe pushed away from the affected side appearing late. Treat it first with a needle, not a tube: a large-bore needle thrust into the second intercostal space at the mid-clavicular line, or the fifth space just in front of the armpit, with the hiss of escaping air confirming the diagnosis and buying time. After the needle, a drain always follows, because the needle is a bridge, not a treatment. A small pneumothorax in a comfortable patient is watched with rest and oxygen if available, not drained.

Placing a drain, when you are trained and equipped, follows a fixed anatomical path. The safe entry is the fourth or fifth intercostal space in front of the mid-axillary line, roughly level with the nipple or just below, and the tube passes just above the upper border of the rib below the space, because each rib carries its nerve, artery, and vein in a groove along its underside. Clean widely, drape with whatever clean barrier exists, and infiltrate generous local anesthesia into skin, rib edge, and pleura, since the pleura is exquisitely sensitive. Make a 2 to 3 centimeter incision, spread the muscles bluntly with a hemostat down to the pleura, and avoid stabbing through with the trocar, which is how lungs and vessels get injured. Pass a drain of fitting size, commonly 28 to 32 French in adults for blood and smaller for air, direct it upward and forward, secure it to the skin with a stout suture, and dress the site airtight. Where any imaging exists, a chest radiograph confirms the position, and where none exists the clinical signs do: breath sounds returning, neck veins settling, the patient speaking in longer sentences. Never let a chest drain be your first unsupervised procedure, and never let anyone talk you into it before you have been proctored through several.

The underwater seal is not a ritual but a one-way valve made of water. The tube from the chest runs down to a bottle kept below chest level, its end sitting 2 to 3 centimeters under the surface of sterile water or clean saline: when the patient breathes in, the water column inside the tube rises, and when air or blood leaves the chest, it bubbles out below the surface. Water allows air out and blocks its return, whatever the patient does, which is the whole point. Keep the bottle below the chest always, because lifting it above floods tube water back into the chest, and laying it flat empties the seal. The improvised version, long described in humanitarian surgical manuals, is a clean bottle with a tight lid pierced by two holes, a long tube reaching 2 to 3 centimeters under the water, and a short tube open to the air as a vent, taped at every joint, with a one-way flutter valve as the substitute when movement cannot be avoided. Monitor by looking and listening: the water column should swing with each breath, steady bubbling means a continuing air leak, and the rule that saves lives is never to clamp a bubbling drain, because clamping turns the leak back into a tension pneumothorax. Record the drainage volume and character every hour early on, and more than 1500 milliliters of blood immediately, or more than 200 milliliters per hour for several hours, means surgical bleeding and an urgent conversation about transfer. Remove the drain when the lung is re-expanded, the leak has stopped for a day, and drainage is minimal, asking the patient to hold the breath and bear down while you pull the tube and close the pre-placed purse-string suture.

Every wound, burn, and puncture gets two questions in the same breath: tetanus and rabies. Tetanus is caused by the bacterium Clostridium tetani, whose spores sit in soil and dust in every village and enter through wounds so small they are forgotten, germinating in dead tissue and releasing a toxin that travels to the nerves and produces the terrible spasms of lockjaw. Once the spasms begin, the patient needs intensive care that most of the world does not have, which makes prevention the entire strategy. The scheme taught by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, whose current version you must verify before acting, is this: for a clean minor wound, a patient with a complete series needs a booster only if more than 10 years have passed; for a dirty wound, meaning soil, saliva, feces, puncture, dead tissue, or burn, a booster is needed if more than 5 years have passed; and a patient whose series is unknown or incomplete needs both tetanus toxoid and passive protection with human tetanus immunoglobulin, commonly 250 international units into the muscle, a portion infiltrated around the wound. Guidance differs between countries and is revised from time to time, so check the current national schedule rather than trusting memory. Record exactly what was given, on which date, and into which site. Where vaccination coverage is thin, assume nothing, because adults may have no childhood series at all, and a woman who recalls being vaccinated long ago may mean she once received a single dose.

Rabies is untreatable once symptoms begin and entirely preventable before them, which makes any mammal bite an emergency of the same rank as a hemorrhage. The wound care is the first treatment: wash with soap and running water for a full 15 minutes, then apply a virucidal agent such as povidone-iodine if available, and washing alone removes a large share of the risk. The World Health Organization grades exposures into categories: category one, touching or feeding an animal or a lick on intact skin, needs only washing and reassurance; category two, a nibble on uncovered skin or a scratch without blood, needs washing and vaccine; category three, any bite or scratch that breaks skin, a lick on broken skin, or saliva on mucous membranes, needs washing, vaccine, and rabies immunoglobulin. The vaccine is given into the deltoid muscle on day 0, day 3, day 7, and once between days 14 and 21 by the schedules in common use, and regimens differ between countries and products, so the first dose is never delayed while the animal is being sought. Immunoglobulin, when the category demands it, is infiltrated into and around the wound as far as volume allows, with the remainder injected at a site distant from the vaccine, never in the same syringe and never in the same limb. Human rabies immunoglobulin is dosed at 20 international units per kilogram of body weight, and the product you stock dictates the schedule, so verify the current protocol of the World Health Organization and of your national program before giving the series. Take the animal's story seriously, since an animal that behaved strangely, that cannot be observed, or that has died raises your urgency rather than lowering it.

Consent and pain control are not the wrapping around these procedures; they are part of the procedures, and they run through every step. Before you touch a wound, tell the patient what you found, what you plan to do, what it will feel like, and how long it will take, and obtain agreement, through an interpreter when languages differ, and document that you did. An unconscious patient is covered by the emergency exception, a child by the caregiver's agreement in simple honest words, and a patient who refuses a reasonable procedure receives your explanation, the risks stated plainly, and a documented refusal with a witness. For pain, climb a simple ladder: ibuprofen 400 milligrams by mouth every eight hours with food for mild wounds, and morphine 5 to 10 milligrams by mouth every four hours, or in smaller intravenous doses where you are trained and equipped, for burns, fractures, and chest drains. Local anesthesia is the analgesic of the procedures themselves, and a patient who does not flinch under your needle gets better repairs than one who is fighting you. Watch the adverse effects you can cause: ibuprofen can injure the kidneys of a dehydrated patient, and morphine slows breathing, so check the respiratory rate and depth after every opioid dose and keep the reversal agent on the shelf if you can stock one. Pain that returns after you leave is a reason patients never come back, so send every wound home with a spoken and written plan for the next two days of analgesia, and verify these doses against current guidance before prescribing.

The unglamorous work decides whether any of this is possible. A trauma kit you can carry contains what you cannot improvise: sterile gloves in several sizes, gauze and elastic bandages, a suture set with needle holder, forceps, scissors, and scalpel, local anesthesia, syringes and needles, adhesive tape, a tourniquet, a blood pressure cuff, a light you can aim, and a marker for writing times. Stock deep what you use daily and single what you rarely use, rotate everything by expiry date, and audit the kit on a fixed weekday, because midnight on the roadside is the wrong moment to learn the needle holder is missing. Sterilization is a quiet discipline: autoclave when power allows, and otherwise boil instruments at a rolling boil for 15 minutes, then store them dry in closed containers, and never call an instrument sterile if it has been lying loose in a drawer. Irrigation alone can consume liters, so plan water like a drug, in covered containers refilled on a schedule, with a basin beneath the wound and a safe way to dispose of the dirty fluid. Lighting, waste, and sharps handling belong to the trauma response, because a needle that pierces a colleague after a night procedure opens a wound chapter of its own. Keep the checklist inside the bag and let any team member audit against it without asking permission.

Monitoring is what turns procedures into care. After a tourniquet, the time is recorded and the limb reassessed on arrival; after packing, the wound is reassessed at 24 hours by a planned revisit, not when the pain returns; after splinting, pulses and sensation are rechecked at one hour and again the next day, since swelling under a splint is silent until it is dangerous; after a chest drain, bottle height, swinging, drainage, and breathing are charted every hour early on; after a burn, hourly urine output is your gauge of whether the fluid plan is right. Every closed wound is inspected at 48 hours, and every wound left open gets its first dressing change at the same interval, because the second day is when infection declares itself. Teach the family the signs that mean immediate return: spreading redness, throbbing pain, fever, pus, a bad smell, red streaks running toward the armpit or groin, and new numbness beyond the wound. Watch also for the harms of your own treatments, such as the numb tongue after too much lidocaine, the pale skin under a too-tight mattress stitch, the patient who cannot pass urine after morphine, or the diarrhea that follows a careless antibiotic course. Then set the follow-up date in writing, with the day and the place, because a plan that lives only in your head does not reach a patient walking three hours home.

Documentation is clinical work and legal work at once. In the record, write the mechanism and time of injury, the findings with a simple sketch of the wound, the care given with times, the tetanus and rabies status and what was given for each, the analgesia and its effect, the consent conversation, the instructions spoken aloud, and the follow-up plan, then sign and date it. In road traffic injuries and assaults your notes may become evidence, so record facts and measurements, not opinions about fault. Registers serve surveillance, because a rise in dog bites feeds the rabies program and a cluster of burns from one stove design informs prevention, and the numbers you keep are the numbers the district can act on. Your role as an advanced practice nurse is to hold all of this at once, and it extends outward: teach bleeding control and tourniquet use to teachers and drivers, teach families dressing changes and the danger signs, and train community health workers to clean simple wounds and to recognize what must travel. Knowing what to refer is a greater skill than knowing how to cut, and the honesty to say so protects patients more than any procedure.

To summarize, trauma care in a remote setting begins with looking before touching, and the primary survey of airway, breathing, circulation, disability, and exposure protects the patient from the dramatic wound while they die of a quiet one. Bleeding is stopped with direct pressure first, then packing, then a tourniquet with its time written down, and every wound is judged by its mechanism, its age, its contamination, and what lies beneath it. Clean wounds close, contaminated wounds wait or heal open, liters of clean irrigation beat any antiseptic, dead tissue must be cut away, and the simple interrupted suture, placed with matched bites and removed on time, is the spine of repair, with mattress stitches for the wounds that spring apart. Abscesses are drained only when they fluctuate, burns are sized by depth and surface and resuscitated by formula then titrated to urine, fractures are splinted above and below with neurovascular checks before and after, and the chest drain, when truly needed, works through a water seal you can improvise and must never clamp while it bubbles. Every wound asks about tetanus and rabies in the same breath, with schedules verified against the current guidance of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the World Health Organization before you act. Consent, analgesia, monitoring, honest records, and teaching carry the same weight as the procedures, and the nurse who holds the whole of this, not only the cutting, is the reason patients in remote places survive their injuries.
