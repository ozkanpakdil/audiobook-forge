# Chapter 59 — Data, Registers, and Reporting

In a clinic at the end of a long road, the most important object on the shelf may be the register, the bound book in which every visit is written down. It is the clinic's memory, because no single nurse can hold a year of patients in mind, and the woman who returns after 2 months expects to be known rather than to start again. It is also the nurse's defense, because a record written at the time of the visit is the only durable proof that a reasonable decision was made with the information then available. The register is not paperwork that competes with care, and treating it that way is the first error; it is part of care, and it takes its value from the same discipline you bring to a dressing or a dose. In this chapter we will treat the register, the tally, the monthly report, and the notification as clinical instruments, each with a purpose you can explain to a colleague and a method you can teach to a health worker. None of them saves a life by itself, and all of them change what happens to the next patient.

A patient record exists for 3 reasons at once, and holding all 3 in mind keeps the writing honest. The first is continuity, so that the next clinician, who may be you in 3 weeks or a stranger covering your leave, can reconstruct the illness and continue the plan without repeating it. The second is communication inside the team, because the health worker who gives the injection, the assistant who runs the test, and the nurse who reviews the patient all work from the same page. The third is accountability, which includes the legal sense that the record may one day be read by a supervisor, a court, or a family asking what happened. A record that serves only the third purpose becomes defensive and empty, and a record that serves only the first becomes a private note. Write for all 3, and write as though the patient will read it, because one day the patient may.

Every record begins with identity, and identity errors are the quiet cause of serious harm. Write the patient's name as the patient gives it and as the family confirms it, with the spelling checked aloud, because a name written from assumption may belong to someone else. Add the age or the date of birth, the sex, the village or the neighborhood, the nearest recognizable landmark, and the name of a next of kin who can be found when the patient cannot be. Where the clinic uses a unique number, write it in the same place on every page so that the pages of one person stay together. Note the language spoken and the interpreter used, because a consultation conducted through a third person carries a risk that the record should reveal. If 2 patients share a name in a small community, use a second identifier such as the mother's name or the year of birth rather than trusting the name alone.

Then write the complaint and the history in the order in which they unfolded, because a record that is not in time order cannot be reasoned through later. Start with the reason for the visit, in the patient's own words where you can keep them, and then give the duration of each symptom and what preceded it. Compress the structured exposure history into the facts that matter here, such as travel, water contact, animal contact, a bite, a recent funeral, or a new sexual partner, and say when each exposure occurred rather than only that it occurred. Record the treatment already taken, which includes medicines bought without a prescription, traditional remedies, and injections given by a neighbor, because these change what you will see and what you will give. Note the allergies, the chronic conditions, the drugs taken every day, and for a woman of childbearing age whether she is pregnant or breastfeeding. The history you leave out is the history that later seems obvious.

The findings belong in the record as measurements rather than as impressions, and a number you did not measure should not be written as though you had. Write the temperature in degrees Celsius, the pulse and the respiratory rate per minute, the blood pressure, and the weight in kilograms, because every pediatric dose and every nutritional judgment depends on that weight. Describe the examination in a fixed anatomical order, so that a reader can see what you looked at and notice what you did not, and say plainly which parts of the examination you could not complete and why. Describe a rash by its distribution and character, a wound by its size in centimeters, its depth, its discharge, and its smell, and a chest by what you heard rather than by the diagnosis you concluded. Add the results of any test done at the bedside, with the value and the unit written in full words, and note whether the result was a screening finding that still needs confirmation. A clear finding can be argued with; a vague one cannot.

The treatment and the plan close the record, and they are the part that the next reader consults first. Write what was given during the visit with the dose, the route, the frequency, and the duration, and record the time of administration, because a drug given twice by two well meaning clinicians is a real harm. Write what was prescribed to be taken at home, who will administer it, and whether the family understood, noting the language used and any teaching given. Set the follow-up date and, more importantly, write the danger signs that should bring the patient back sooner, since a plan without a threshold for return is only half a plan. Add 1 or 2 sentences of reasoning that state what you think is happening and why you chose this course, because that sentence turns a list of actions into a clinical decision. Finally, record what the patient refused or what you could not provide, along with the alternative you offered, so that the gap is visible rather than hidden.

All of this has to fit inside the minutes that a busy visit allows, and the discipline of brevity is a clinical skill rather than a clerical one. Use a standard form or a register with fixed columns and write in the same order every time, so that the eye of the next reader knows where to look without searching. Write during the visit or immediately after it, in the few minutes while the facts are still exact, rather than reconstructing a whole day from memory at night. Invent no abbreviations of your own, because a shorthand that saves you 3 seconds can cost a colleague 3 minutes, and a misread abbreviation has caused wrong drugs to be given. Write legibly and in dark ink that survives damp and copying, and correct a mistake with a single line through it, your initials, and the date, never with correction fluid and never by erasing. If a page is full, continue on a numbered continuation sheet and say so on the original page.

The register itself is a physical object with a supply chain behind it, and the logistics of that chain decide whether the record exists at all. Count your forms and books before they run out and order them with the same lead time you use for drugs, because a clinic with no register paper stops recording, and a gap in the sequence is a gap in the memory. Keep the current register in a dry, locked place away from insects, rodents, and the roof leak, and keep past volumes together in a bound sequence that can be found by year. Protect the record from fire and flood by storing a duplicate of the most important pages elsewhere, and copy or photograph the pages periodically where a camera is available. Decide who holds the key and who may write in the book, and write that rule down. A register that cannot be found is the same as a register that was never written.

Electronic registers and mobile data collection have real advantages, and they also fail in ways that paper does not. A telephone application that works without a connection and synchronizes later can reduce the transcription errors that come from copying a paper tally into a computer, and it can produce a report without a second counting. But such a tool depends on a charged battery, a working device, a signal at some point, and a person who understands the program, and it introduces a new risk of losing months of data in one cracked screen. Enter the data on the device only if you also keep the clinical record in a form that survives the device, and export or print a copy at regular intervals rather than trusting the application's distant server. Protect the device with a password and keep it with the register rather than in a pocket that goes home. Treat the electronic tool as an addition to the paper system until it has proved itself for a full year.

The tally is the daily bridge between the clinical record and the monthly report, and it is built at the moment of the visit rather than assembled afterward. Each consultation is counted once, with a mark or a figure in the category that fits, so that the sheet at the end of the day already holds most of what the report will need. The categories should follow the questions you actually have to answer, such as new visits, follow-up visits, children younger than 5 years, referrals, admissions, deaths, and the specific diagnoses your program must track. A tally made from memory at the end of the month is a guess dressed as a count, and it will disagree with the register in ways that no one can explain. Keep the tally sheet with the register page of the same date, so that any number can be traced back to the visit that produced it. The minute the tally costs each day is repaid many times when the report is due.

The monthly report is the register made into a summary, and it should be assembled from the tally and the register rather than from recollection. It usually carries the number of consultations, the breakdown by age and sex, the diagnoses or syndromes seen, the treatments given, the referrals made, the deaths recorded, and the supply problems that affected care. Whatever its local shape, its numbers must be internally consistent, so that the parts add to the whole and the same patient is not counted in 2 places. Write the report on a fixed day of the following month, check it against the register, and keep a copy in the clinic as well as sending it upward. A report that contains a number you cannot defend when asked is worse than a report with a gap that is honestly explained. The report is a clinical document rather than a formality, and it should be signed by the person who prepared it.

The rule that a number must be the same when it is counted twice is the simplest test of a reporting system, and it catches more errors than any sophisticated check. If your tally says that 42 children were treated for diarrhea and your register contains 38 such entries, one of the two is wrong, and the discrepancy is a signal to investigate rather than to average. Recount a sample of the register, perhaps 10 per cent of the month's pages chosen at random, and compare the result with the tally for those same pages, because a sample exposes a systematic error that a total can hide. Look for the common causes, which are a visit tallied twice, a visit tallied in the wrong category, a page that was never tallied, and a patient seen by 2 clinicians on the same day. Write down what you found and what you changed, so that the same error does not return next month. A system that reconciles its own numbers builds the confidence it needs when it reports something alarming.

Every count depends on a definition, and where the definition is unwritten, 2 people will count differently and both will be sincere. Decide and write down what counts as one visit, whether a child seen twice in one month is 2 visits or one episode, whether a referral is a consultation at your clinic, whether a death in the community is your death, and which age group a child belongs to on the day after a birthday. Post these counting rules beside the register in the language of the clinic, and teach them to everyone who writes in the book, including the health worker who covers your day off. Where a national program supplies case definitions, use those definitions rather than inventing your own, because a local definition that cannot be compared with the district is nearly useless. When the rule is genuinely unclear, choose the interpretation that matches the district report and record that choice. Consistency, not elegance, is what makes a series of monthly numbers worth reading.

Some diseases carry a legal and professional duty to report, and that duty is not discretionary, however busy the clinic is. Notifiable diseases are the conditions that public health authorities must know about quickly, because a single case may be the first sign of an outbreak that can still be stopped. The list is set by national law and differs between countries, and it usually includes diseases such as cholera, measles, poliomyelitis, plague, yellow fever, viral hemorrhagic fevers, and certain forms of meningitis, with others added or removed according to the local situation. Some must be reported immediately, by the fastest means available, and others within a week or a month through the routine report. The duty belongs to the clinician who suspects the case, not only to the laboratory that confirms it, and a report based on a clinical suspicion is expected rather than premature. Reporting is part of the care of the community, and failing to report is a failure of practice rather than merely a paperwork lapse.

To report well you must know the list that matters where you are, and that list is not the same as the one you learned elsewhere. Ask the district health office for the current notifiable disease list, the case definitions that go with it, and the person to whom each report must go, and keep that information posted where you write. Where the country uses the integrated disease surveillance and response approach, the case definitions and the reporting forms come from that system, and the thresholds that trigger an alert are written into it. The International Health Regulations of the World Health Organization add a further layer, since some events must be assessed and reported beyond the national level, but your own duty is discharged by the national route. Keep a copy of the list in the local language as well, because the person who first sees the patient may not read the official version. A posted list that no one has read is decoration, so review it with the staff at the start of each season.

The path a report travels is worth understanding, because a report that stops at the clinic does nothing. The first step is usually a telephone call or a message from you to the district officer, made as soon as the suspicion exists, followed by the written form with the details. The district assembles the reports from its clinics, verifies what it can, and passes the summary upward to the province and then the national level, where the response is decided and the laboratory work and the supplies are arranged. A week of delay at any step is a week in which the outbreak grows, and the outbreak that reaches the district a week early is the one that is contained. Keep a log of what you reported, to whom, by what means, and on what date, because the log is both your protection and the district's record. When you send a specimen, record the identifier and the date, and follow the result back to the patient rather than waiting to be told.

Once the register is reliable, it becomes a surveillance instrument, which means it is no longer only a count but a way of noticing change. Surveillance works by comparing what you see now with what you usually see, so you need a baseline, which is simply the accumulated memory of ordinary weeks. Plot your weekly count of the syndromes that matter, such as fever, diarrhea, and respiratory illness, and watch the line rather than the single point, because one busy Tuesday is noise and 4 rising weeks are a signal. Many programs set an alert threshold and an epidemic threshold, often by comparing the current week with the average of the same weeks in previous years, and the crossing of a threshold is a prompt to investigate rather than a diagnosis. With small numbers, a rise from one case to 3 should make you ask a question rather than declare an epidemic. The value of the weekly count is that it buys you time, and time is the only thing an outbreak cannot give back.

A small program cannot track everything, so it chooses a few indicators and follows them honestly and continuously. The most basic is the number of consultations, split into new and follow-up visits, which describes the workload and the population the clinic actually reaches. A second is the share of patients who are children younger than 5 years, which tells you whether your service is reaching the group at greatest risk. A third is the number of deaths recorded, and the case fatality of the conditions you treat, which together say something about severity and about the quality of care. Programs add others as their purpose requires, such as the proportion of confirmed cases treated according to the current guideline, the completion of referrals, the doses of vaccine given, the women who attend antenatal care, the defaulters traced, and the days on which a tracer drug was out of stock. Each indicator needs a numerator and a denominator that you can explain to a visitor, and an indicator whose denominator is unknown should not be reported at all.

Each of those numbers carries a meaning that depends entirely on its denominator and its context, and reading them without that context produces confident nonsense. A rise in consultations may mean an epidemic, or a new road, or a new nurse whom the community trusts, or the end of the harvest when families have time to travel. A high case fatality may mean severe disease arriving late rather than poor treatment, and a low one may mean that only the mildest patients reach you. A fall in the proportion of children treated may reflect a stock-out rather than a change in illness, and the stock-out is the finding that matters. When you compare your clinic with another, compare the definitions and the catchment before you compare the results, because a difference between 2 clinics is often a difference between 2 record keepers. Use the indicators to ask a question, and answer that question with the register, the community, and your own eyes.

Data quality rests on 4 unglamorous properties, and you can check all 4 with a pencil and a little time. Completeness means that the fields you need are filled in, and a blank is not neutral, because it will be read later as a zero or as an absence of disease rather than as an absence of information. Timeliness means that the record was written near the visit, since a register completed at the end of the month loses the detail that made it useful. Accuracy means that what is written matches what happened, which you test by comparing the tally with the register and the register with the patient's memory on return. Plausibility means that the values could be true, so you look for the impossible and the improbable, such as a man recorded as pregnant, a child whose weight fell by half in one week, or a month in which every patient had the same diagnosis. The habit that catches most of these is simply reading what was written, page by page, which is the one quality activity that costs nothing and is most often skipped. Correct the errors you find, and then correct the habit that produced them.

Monitoring does not end with counting cases, because the harm a treatment causes is also data that the system must hold. Record every suspected adverse drug reaction, every adverse event following immunization, every device or test failure, and every treatment failure that suggests resistance or a spoiled batch, with the drug, the batch number if it is written on the vial, the timing, and the outcome. These reports travel by their own route, usually to a national pharmacovigilance center, and they matter even when a single event seems trivial, because one report can be the first sign of a problem that affects thousands of doses. The nurse at the bedside is the person most likely to notice the rash, the jaundice, the sudden fever, or the child who deteriorated after an injection, and no program can detect these without that nurse writing them down. Record also the quieter monitoring that keeps care honest, such as adherence, defaulter tracing, and the follow-up of a patient referred onward. The same discipline applies, which is that what is not recorded did not happen as far as the system can tell.

The analysis that a nurse can do with a pencil is more powerful than most people expect, and it begins with a simple count by month. Write the monthly figures in a row, look at the row, and ask whether the shape is what you expected, because the eye notices a break in a line before the mind notices a difference between 2 totals. Compare the same month with the same month of the previous year to remove the effect of season, and compare the rainy season with the dry season if that is the pattern that matters where you work. Divide one number by another to make a proportion you can understand, such as the share of children who completed their treatment, and write the proportion as a sentence rather than leaving it as a pair of numbers. Then look for the outlier, the month that does not fit, and ask what was different, because the answer is often a broken refrigerator, a closed road, a new health worker, or a real outbreak. None of this requires a computer, and all of it requires honesty about the limits of what you counted.

Data becomes valuable only when it returns to the people and the place it came from, and the return is a clinical act. When your weekly count crosses a threshold, the response is to investigate, to send a specimen, to alert the district, to stock the drug, or to organize an outreach, and the record of what you did is as important as the count that prompted it. Tell the staff the numbers each month, because a health worker who never sees the result of the recording will stop recording carefully. Tell the community as well, in plain language and without exposing any individual, because a village that hears its own figures can help interpret them and will trust the clinic that shares them. Put a simple chart on the wall where the staff can see it, and mark the actions that followed, so that the system shows its own usefulness. A report that rises and never returns is a tax on the clinic, and people stop paying taxes they cannot see.

Behind every tally is a person whose story was borrowed, and that fact governs how all of this must be handled. A register contains names, diagnoses, pregnancies, infections, and sometimes the facts that could expose a patient to stigma, violence, or arrest, including a diagnosis of the human immunodeficiency virus, the cause of acquired immunodeficiency syndrome. Keep the register locked, decide who may read it and for what purpose, and refuse the casual request from a visitor, a relative, or an official who has no clinical or legal reason to see a name. When you report upward or to a partner, report counts and proportions rather than identities, and if a case series or a study requires names, obtain proper consent and approval before collecting them. Removing the name is not enough, since a village, an age, and a rare diagnosis together can point to one person in a small community. The trust that lets a patient answer your questions honestly is the same trust that requires you to keep the answers safe.

The record is also the nurse's defense in the professional and legal sense, and it is more reliable than memory in every setting. It shows what you knew, what you measured, what you offered, what you advised, and what the patient refused, and that sequence is the substance of a defense when a bad outcome is questioned years later. In remote places the law may be far away and the supervisor may never visit, and it is precisely there that the written record becomes the proof of competent practice. Record the consent you obtained, the instruction you gave, the referral you arranged, and the death or the stillbirth with the time and the people present, because these are the events that produce questions. Never alter a record after the fact to make a decision look better, since an honest correction with a date is credible and a fabrication discovered later destroys everything. The certificate of death and any report the law requires are clinical duties, and they should be completed with the same care as the treatment itself.

The nurse usually becomes the designer and guardian of the recording system, whether or not that role was ever assigned, and it is a leadership task as much as a clinical one. Teach every health worker to write in the register at the time of the visit, supervise the writing with the same attention you give to procedures, and audit a sample of the pages each month rather than waiting for a crisis. Make the system fit the visit, so that the form asks for what you can actually obtain in the minutes available, because a form that demands the impossible is filled with invention. Protect the time the recording needs, and resist the addition of new columns that serve only a distant office and tell you nothing about your patients. When a report must be produced for a program or a donor, use the register as the single source rather than maintaining a second parallel book. The nurse who keeps the register honest is doing public health, and it is worth saying so aloud to the staff.

Reporting to programs, ministries, and donors carries its own temptations, and honest numbers are the only durable strategy. Different audiences want different summaries, and the pressure to report success can quietly turn a count into a hope, which is why a program that reports only good news has no way to learn and no way to improve. Keep one source of truth, which is the register and its tally, and derive every report from that source so that the same month never produces 2 different totals. Where several partners each demand a separate form, negotiate one shared report or accept that the duplication is a real cost, and say so to the people who impose it. Record the failures as plainly as the successes, including the stock-outs, the deaths, the patients lost to follow-up, and the targets missed, because those are the facts that justify the next budget and the next drug. A small clinic that is known to report truthfully is trusted in an emergency, and trust is worth more than any favorable figure.

The case definitions, the notification list, the reporting thresholds, and the treatments you record are not matters of local taste; they come from current national guidance and from the recommendations of the World Health Organization, whose documents are revised as the evidence changes. A regimen that was correct when this chapter was written may already have been replaced, and a threshold drawn from an old manual will produce an old response. This is why every recording system needs a small shelf or folder of the current guidelines, reviewed at least once a year and sooner when a national circular arrives. When a new guideline reaches you, read it with the staff, change the forms if the case definitions have changed, and note the date of the change in the register, so that a later reader can see why the numbers shifted. The details in this chapter are illustrative, and the current guideline must be checked before you prescribe, notify, or count. That habit protects the patient, the report, and you.

To summarize, the register is the clinic's memory and your defense, and it deserves the same discipline as any other clinical instrument. Write for every patient the identity, the complaint, the findings, the treatment, and the plan, in the minutes the visit allows, and write it at the time rather than from memory. Build the tally as you go and the monthly report from the tally and the register, and make the numbers agree when they are counted twice, because a system that reconciles itself is a system that can be trusted. Report the notifiable diseases on the day you suspect them, follow the path the report travels, and watch the weekly count for the change that buys the district a week. Choose the few indicators your program can follow honestly, protect the quality of what you write, and record the adverse effects and the failures as carefully as the successes. Do the simple analysis a pencil allows, return the numbers to the staff and the community, and act on what they show. Remember that behind every tally is a person whose story was borrowed, and keep it safe, then verify the current guideline before you act on anything this chapter has described.
