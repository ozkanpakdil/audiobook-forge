# Chapter 17 — Cestodes: Neurocysticercosis and Echinococcosis

This chapter covers two tapeworm groups whose danger lies in the larval stage rather than the adult worm. Taenia solium, the pork tapeworm, causes two separate diseases in one person, and the genus Echinococcus builds a fluid-filled sac that grows silently for years and can kill within minutes if it ruptures. Both are neglected, both are preventable through latrines and meat inspection, and both require the nurse to think in years rather than days. You will meet them even in wealthy countries, because migration and travel carry them into neurology clinics and emergency departments everywhere.

A cestode is a flatworm with no gut of its own, absorbing nutrients through its outer surface. Taenia solium lives as a long ribbon in the human small intestine, its head bearing four suckers and a ring of hooks that grip the wall. Behind the head a neck produces a continuous chain of segments called proglottids, each a reproductive unit, and the chain can reach 2 to 7 meters. The terminal segments are gravid, packed with eggs, and they detach and pass out, and an untreated worm survives for years.

A gravid proglottid holds tens of thousands of eggs, and the thick shell lets each egg survive weeks to months in a cool, moist environment. A passed segment may crawl out of the anus and be found on the skin or clothing, sometimes the only complaint the patient makes. When a pig swallows eggs from human feces, the egg hatches and releases a six-hooked embryo called an oncosphere, which penetrates the gut wall, travels in the blood, and forms a larval cyst called a cysticercus in muscle. When a person eats undercooked pork containing those cysts, the scolex attaches and a new adult worm matures about 2 to 3 months later.

The far more dangerous route is swallowing the egg rather than the larva. When a human being swallows Taenia solium eggs, the oncosphere penetrates the intestinal wall and travels through the bloodstream exactly as it would in a pig, forming cysticerci in human tissue and most seriously in the brain. The eggs come from human feces, not from pork, so a person who eats no meat can still develop the disease. Eggs reach the mouth on contaminated hands, food, or water, and a carrier can contaminate himself or herself. Taeniasis is the adult worm in the intestine, while cysticercosis is the larval stage in human tissue, and their treatments differ completely.

Taeniasis is usually quiet. Most carriers have no symptoms and discover it only when they notice a moving segment in the stool or on the bedding. Others report mild abdominal discomfort, nausea, a change in appetite, loose stools, or itching around the anus. Weight loss is uncommon and the patient does not look ill, though the blood count may show mild eosinophilia.

A carrier sheds eggs for years, exposing every household member and neighbor who shares a latrine or water source. In endemic communities the person with the adult worm is the engine of neurocysticercosis, so finding and treating carriers interrupts transmission at its source. When you diagnose taeniasis, ask who else lives in the house and who prepares the food. Hygiene instruction given at that moment reaches the whole household at once.

Neurocysticercosis is the presence of larval cysts in the central nervous system, and it is one of the commonest causes of acquired epilepsy in the world. The World Health Organization counts it among the neglected tropical diseases, with the greatest burden in Latin America, sub-Saharan Africa, and parts of Asia where pigs roam freely and sanitation is incomplete. Nurses in wealthy countries now see it in migrants and long-term travelers. It is often missed because nobody thinks of a pork tapeworm when a patient arrives with a first seizure.

After penetrating the intestinal wall, the oncosphere is carried to the brain, meninges, ventricles, spinal cord, eye, muscle, and subcutaneous tissue. Cysts develop over weeks to months and may stay viable for years. How much disease they cause depends on where they land and on the host immune response rather than on how many eggs were swallowed. A single cyst in a quiet area of cortex may never announce itself, while a cluster at the base of the brain can be life-threatening.

A cyst passes through stages that explain both symptoms and treatment. In the vesicular stage the larva is alive and the fluid clear, and the surrounding brain shows little inflammation because the parasite suppresses the local immune response. In the colloidal stage the parasite is dying, the fluid turns turbid, and the immune system floods the area with edema and inflammation that cause symptoms. In the granular nodular stage the cyst collapses into a scar, and in the calcified stage it becomes a hard nodule that can remain for life. Most suffering comes from the middle stages, which is why killing the parasite can make a patient worse before better.

The location of the cysts determines the clinical picture. Parenchymal cysts cause seizures, headache, and focal weakness. Cysts in the subarachnoid space, especially the racemose form that grows like a cluster of grapes at the base of the brain, can become enormous, raise intracranial pressure, inflame blood vessels, and cause strokes. Cysts inside the ventricles obstruct the flow of cerebrospinal fluid and cause hydrocephalus, sometimes abruptly. Spinal cysts compress the cord, causing weakness, sensory loss, and loss of bladder control, and cysts in the eye can destroy vision and require surgery rather than drugs.

Seizures are the signature of the disease, because inflamed cortex, edema around a dying cyst, and old calcified scars all irritate the brain. A seizure may be focal, with twitching of one limb, unusual sensations, or impaired awareness, or it may generalize with loss of consciousness and convulsions. A first seizure in a person 20 to 40 years old who has lived in an endemic area should raise the question at once. Repeated untreated seizures can become a chronic epilepsy lasting decades, carrying stigma and loss of employment as well as medical risk.

Hydrocephalus turns a manageable illness into an emergency. A ventricular cyst can block the narrow channels through which cerebrospinal fluid circulates, and meningeal inflammation at the base of the brain can leave a scar that prevents fluid absorption. The patient develops headache that is worse on waking, vomiting, neck stiffness, blurred or double vision, and swelling of the optic discs, followed later by slow thinking, unsteadiness, and incontinence. These signs can build over days or weeks, so any patient with known cysticercosis who develops them needs urgent imaging and referral rather than a wait and see approach.

Imaging is the center of diagnosis. Computed tomography shows calcified lesions well, because calcium appears as bright dense dots, and it also shows cysts, edema, and hydrocephalus. Magnetic resonance imaging is better at distinguishing a living cyst from a dying one, at showing the scolex, and at finding cysts in the ventricles, the subarachnoid space, and the spinal cord. A viable cyst often appears as a rounded fluid collection with a bright dot inside, sometimes described as a hole with a dot, and the dot is the scolex.

Treatment begins with a principle that prevents harm: not every lesion requires an antiparasitic drug. A patient whose only finding is a calcified lesion with controlled seizures needs an anticonvulsant and nothing else, because there is no living parasite to kill. A patient with viable parenchymal cysts generally does benefit, but only with corticosteroids and usually with an anticonvulsant. Patients with cysts in the ventricles, the subarachnoid space, or the eye are managed individually and often surgically, because location, not mere presence, decides the approach.

When antiparasitic treatment is indicated, the choice lies between albendazole and praziquantel, and the dose must come from current guidance rather than memory. A widely used regimen is albendazole at 15 milligrams per kilogram per day by mouth divided into two doses for 10 to 14 days. An alternative is praziquantel at 50 milligrams per kilogram per day by mouth divided into three doses for 15 days, and some protocols use both drugs together. These regimens are revised as evidence accumulates, national recommendations differ, so the World Health Organization guidance, the current national guideline, and the product label must all be checked before the first dose. Name the source you followed in your documentation.

The reason corticosteroids are not optional is the most important safety concept in this chapter. The antiparasitic drug kills the cyst, and a dying cyst can no longer hide from the immune system, so the brain mounts an intense inflammatory response around it. That inflammation produces edema, raises pressure, causes headache, worsens seizures, and can be fatal when many cysts die at once. The treatment therefore causes the very complication it is meant to prevent, and corticosteroids suppress the response while the parasite is being killed. A patient given albendazole or praziquantel without corticosteroid cover, and without the ability to recognize and treat a flare, is exposed to avoidable danger.

Corticosteroids are started before or together with the antiparasitic drug and continued long enough to cover the period when cysts degenerate. Dexamethasone and prednisone are both used, with dexamethasone preferred when there is significant edema or raised intracranial pressure, given by mouth in divided doses and then tapered according to symptoms and imaging. In extensive subarachnoid disease, corticosteroids may be the main treatment and antiparasitic drugs withheld entirely. Dose and duration must be verified against current guidance, because practice varies with the burden and location of the cysts.

Anticonvulsant treatment is the other pillar, because seizures most threaten safety and social life. Many clinicians begin an anticonvulsant when a viable cyst is treated, since the treatment itself can provoke seizures. Levetiracetam is often preferred because it does not interact with the antiparasitic drugs, while carbamazepine and phenytoin lower the blood levels of albendazole and praziquantel and may reduce their effectiveness, and valproate is avoided in people who could become pregnant. How long to continue after lesions calcify is genuinely uncertain, so the decision must be individualized and reviewed with a neurologist where possible.

Monitoring during treatment is continuous nursing work rather than an occasional blood test. Before treatment, record a baseline neurologic examination, seizure history, blood pressure, blood glucose, and weight, and confirm that the corticosteroid has been prescribed rather than merely considered. During treatment, assess for worsening headache, vomiting, drowsiness, new weakness, visual change, and any increase in seizure frequency, because these may signal rising intracranial pressure from the inflammatory flare. Blood glucose must be checked when corticosteroids are used, and liver tests and blood counts followed during prolonged albendazole therapy. Document everything, because the next decision often depends on a trend rather than a single value.

Adverse effects must be anticipated and explained in advance. Albendazole commonly causes abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, headache, and dizziness, and it can raise liver enzymes, so liver tests are checked at baseline and during treatment. Rarely it suppresses the bone marrow, producing a fall in white cells or platelets, which is why blood counts are monitored, and it can cause temporary hair loss, so it is avoided in early pregnancy unless the illness justifies the risk. Praziquantel causes dizziness and drowsiness that make driving unsafe for the rest of the day, and its level falls with rifampicin, carbamazepine, phenytoin, and dexamethasone. Corticosteroids bring high blood sugar, high blood pressure, fluid retention, infection, and mood change, and must never be stopped abruptly after a long course.

Surgery and shunting are required for some patients and are not a failure of medical care. Ventricular cysts are often removed by neuroendoscopy, which can also restore the flow of cerebrospinal fluid. Hydrocephalus that cannot be relieved by removing the cyst is treated with a shunt that diverts fluid from the brain to the abdomen, or by a procedure that opens a channel at the floor of the third ventricle. Shunts fail, and failure presents with the return of headache, vomiting, drowsiness, or fever, so the patient and family must be taught these warning signs before discharge.

The logistics are as demanding as the medicine. Patients need imaging before treatment and again afterward, which may mean travel to a referral hospital and an overnight stay, so neurosurgical referral pathways must be identified in advance rather than improvised during a crisis. Albendazole, praziquantel, dexamethasone, and anticonvulsants must all be in stock at the same time, and the patient must be able to return for follow up, which depends on transport, work, and childcare. Cost is a barrier, and a nurse who knows the supply chain can prevent interruptions in treatment.

Treatment of the intestinal tapeworm is simpler but carries a trap. Niclosamide is given by mouth as a single dose of 2 grams for adults, with tablets chewed thoroughly or crushed and swallowed with water, and it kills the worm by preventing it from absorbing glucose. Praziquantel as a single dose of 5 to 10 milligrams per kilogram is an alternative and is highly effective, and albendazole at 400 milligrams per day for 3 days is a further option. Because niclosamide is poorly absorbed, it has the advantage of not acting on cysts elsewhere in the body.

That advantage matters because of the trap. If a patient has undiagnosed neurocysticercosis and is given praziquantel for the intestinal worm, the drug can attack the brain cysts and provoke seizures, headache, or raised intracranial pressure. In endemic areas, therefore, ask about seizures and chronic headache before treating taeniasis, and consider imaging first where it is available. Where imaging is not available, some programs prefer niclosamide for exactly this reason. This is one of the clearest examples of a drug that is safe for one disease being dangerous for another that the same patient may be hiding.

Prevention of cysticercosis rests on pig husbandry, sanitation, and meat safety, and each is a place where nursing education has measurable effect. Pigs must not roam freely and must not reach human feces, because a pig that eats infected stool becomes the next link in the chain. Keeping pigs in pens away from latrines and using manure as fertilizer rather than letting animals scavenge are changes communities can make. Meat inspection at slaughter identifies heavily infected carcasses, and pork must be cooked thoroughly until it is no longer pink and the juices run clear, while freezing at minus 10 degrees Celsius for 10 days also kills the larvae where cold storage exists.

Sanitation belongs to the same conversation. Latrines that are used consistently, kept clean, and sited away from wells and streams reduce the number of eggs reaching the environment, and hand hygiene with soap after defecation and before food preparation interrupts the route that carries eggs to the brain. Water sources should be protected from runoff, and produce grown in contaminated soil should be washed and cooked. Because the carrier is the source, finding and treating carriers in a community benefits everyone. This is the one health approach in practice, linking human health, pig health, and the environment through a single parasite.

Echinococcosis is caused by a different tapeworm with a different cycle, but it shares the theme of a larval stage that grows quietly in human tissue. Two species matter. Echinococcus granulosus causes cystic echinococcosis, in which the larva forms a well-defined fluid-filled sac called a hydatid cyst, most often in the liver or lung. Echinococcus multilocularis causes alveolar echinococcosis, in which the larva forms an infiltrating mass that behaves like a slow-growing cancer of the liver.

The adult worms are tiny, only a few millimeters long, and live in the intestine of the definitive host. In the cystic form the definitive host is the dog and the intermediate hosts are sheep, goats, cattle, and other grazing animals. The adult sheds eggs in dog feces, and the grazing animal swallows them while feeding on contaminated pasture. The oncosphere penetrates the gut wall, travels in the blood, and forms hydatid cysts in the animal's liver and lungs. The cycle closes when a dog eats the raw organs of an infected animal, which is exactly what happens when home slaughter leaves offal accessible to dogs, and human beings are infected by swallowing eggs from dog feces and are a dead end in the cycle.

The hydatid cyst grows slowly, which is why the disease presents late. A cyst may enlarge by only a few millimeters to a few centimeters in a year, so a person infected in childhood may not develop symptoms until middle age. About 60 to 70 per cent of cysts form in the liver and about 20 to 30 per cent in the lungs, with the remainder in kidney, spleen, bone, and brain. A cyst has an outer laminated layer, an inner germinal layer that produces brood capsules and microscopic protoscolices, and highly antigenic fluid, making it a self-contained parasite factory.

Many cysts cause no symptoms for years and are found incidentally. A liver cyst may produce a dull ache in the right upper abdomen, a sense of fullness after small meals, nausea, and a palpable mass, and a cyst pressing on a bile duct causes jaundice. A lung cyst causes cough, chest pain, and shortness of breath, and it may bleed into the airway. The most feared complication is rupture, because the fluid is intensely allergenic: rupture into the abdomen, bloodstream, or pleural space can cause anaphylaxis with low blood pressure, hives, airway swelling, wheezing, and collapse, which can be fatal within minutes. Spilled protoscolices can also seed the peritoneum and produce dozens of new cysts, a condition called secondary hydatidosis that is very difficult to treat.

Because rupture is both the danger and a possible consequence of our own interventions, the nurse must be prepared for anaphylaxis whenever a hydatid cyst is manipulated. Epinephrine must be drawn up and available, intravenous access established, airway equipment ready, and oxygen at hand before any aspiration or operation, and many teams insist that a clinician skilled in airway management and resuscitation be present. Elective puncture of a cyst for diagnosis alone is contraindicated. If fluid must be removed, it is done as a planned therapeutic procedure under imaging guidance, with albendazole cover and full resuscitation equipment in the room.

Diagnosis begins with imaging. Ultrasound is the first line for abdominal disease because it is portable, inexpensive, and informative, and it shows whether a cyst is a simple unilocular fluid collection, contains daughter cysts within itself, has a detached membrane floating inside, or has become solid and calcified. The classification used by the World Health Organization describes these appearances to stage the cyst and guide treatment. Computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging are used for deep, complicated, or uncertain lesions and for surgical planning, and chest radiography shows lung cysts as rounded opacities. Blood tests for antibodies support the diagnosis but are neither fully sensitive nor fully specific, eosinophilia is unreliable, and aspirating a cyst to make the diagnosis is not acceptable.

Albendazole is the main drug for cystic echinococcosis, both alone and around any intervention. A commonly used regimen is 10 to 15 milligrams per kilogram per day by mouth divided into two doses taken with food, because absorption improves with a fatty meal. Treatment may be continuous or given in cycles of 28 days on treatment followed by 14 days off, and it usually continues for 3 to 6 months around surgery or percutaneous treatment, or longer for inoperable cysts. These regimens are revised and national recommendations differ, so the guidance of the World Health Organization and the current national protocol must be checked before prescribing. Blood counts and liver tests are checked at baseline, every 2 weeks during the early weeks, and then monthly, because albendazole can cause hepatitis and bone marrow suppression as well as nausea, abdominal pain, and hair loss.

Percutaneous treatment uses puncture, aspiration, injection, and reaspiration, and it has become the preferred approach for many liver cysts. Under ultrasound guidance a needle or catheter is placed into the cyst, some fluid is aspirated, a protoscolicidal solution such as hypertonic saline is injected and left in contact with the germinal layer long enough to kill the parasite, and the fluid is then reaspirated. The patient takes albendazole before and after the procedure to reduce the risk of secondary cysts if fluid leaks. The technique is unsuitable for superficial cysts that could leak into the peritoneum, cysts that communicate with the bile ducts, and cysts that are inaccessible or already ruptured. Careful patient selection, skilled imaging, and readiness for anaphylaxis are what make it safe.

Surgery remains necessary for very large cysts, cysts pressing on vital structures, cysts with a biliary communication, cysts containing many daughter cysts, ruptured or infected cysts, and cysts that have failed other treatment. The aim in every operation is to remove the parasite without spilling its contents, so the field is packed with pads soaked in a protoscolicidal solution, the cyst is handled gently, sharp dissection into it is avoided, and fluid is aspirated in a controlled way. Removing the intact cyst with its membrane, sometimes with a rim of liver tissue, is the most complete approach when feasible. After surgery albendazole is continued, biliary leaks are drained, and the patient is followed with imaging.

Follow up in echinococcosis is measured in years. Imaging is repeated at planned intervals to confirm that a cyst is shrinking, stable, or recurring, and serology is followed as a supportive trend rather than a verdict. Calcification suggests the parasite has died but is not proof, and a calcified cyst can still obstruct a bile duct or become infected. Recurrence is common enough that a recall system with dates, transport arrangements, and a way to reach the patient is essential, and in many programs follow up is the weakest part of the whole cascade. The nurse who maintains that system does work no other member of the team is positioned to do.

Alveolar echinococcosis is a different and worse disease caused by Echinococcus multilocularis, whose life cycle runs mainly between foxes and small rodents, with dogs and cats sometimes acting as definitive hosts, across the northern hemisphere. Instead of a smooth sac, the larva grows in small vesicles that infiltrate surrounding tissue, invade blood vessels and bile ducts, and spread to the lungs and brain, behaving like a slow-growing cancer. Early infection causes no symptoms, and by the time jaundice, right upper abdominal pain, weight loss, and fatigue appear the mass is often extensive. Untreated, it is generally fatal, and even with treatment it requires long-term, sometimes lifelong, albendazole.

Management combines surgery, prolonged drug treatment, and honest discussion of prognosis. Radical surgical resection offers the best chance of control when the disease is confined and the patient can tolerate the operation, and liver transplantation is used in selected patients with advanced disease. For most patients albendazole continues for many years or for life, because the parasite cannot reliably be eradicated and stopping treatment risks regrowth, and other measures may relieve complications such as biliary obstruction. Nursing care here is long term: supporting adherence to a drug taken indefinitely, monitoring liver tests and blood counts, managing symptoms, helping the patient plan around appointments, and talking plainly about what is known and what is not.

Across both forms of echinococcosis the nursing role has a consistent shape. You assess the exposure history, including dogs, sheep, foxes, slaughter practices, and where the patient grew up. You prepare the patient for imaging and procedures, and you make sure anaphylaxis equipment and resuscitation support are ready before any cyst is touched. You verify that albendazole cover has been prescribed around interventions, that doses and durations match current guidance, and that blood counts and liver tests are scheduled. You teach the patient how to take the drug with food, why the cycles are structured as they are, which symptoms to report, and why follow up must continue even when they feel well. You also record which guideline was used, so that the next clinician can reconstruct the reasoning.

The same discipline applies to neurocysticercosis. Confirm the diagnosis with imaging where possible before starting antiparasitic treatment, and confirm that corticosteroids are prescribed with the antiparasitic drug and that an anticonvulsant is in place or available. Look for cysts in the eye, the ventricles, and the subarachnoid space, because those change the plan. Establish a monitoring routine for headache, vomiting, drowsiness, seizures, vision, blood glucose, liver tests, and blood counts. Confirm that the patient can return for follow up and that the drugs are available. Then document the source of every regimen, because doses in this field are revised often and the guideline, not any individual memory, is the authority.

Prevention for echinococcosis follows the same one health logic, with dogs at the center. Dogs must not be fed raw offal, and home slaughter must be done where organs can be disposed of safely rather than left for dogs to eat. Periodic treatment of dogs with praziquantel reduces the adult worm burden and therefore the number of eggs reaching the environment, and this is a recognized control measure in endemic areas, while handwashing after contact with dogs, washing produce, and protecting water sources reduce human exposure. Education must be specific rather than general, because telling a family to improve hygiene changes little, while showing them that the pig must be penned away from the latrine, that pork must be cooked until it is no longer pink, that hands must be washed with soap after handling a dog, and that the child with a new seizure needs to be seen rather than hidden changes a great deal.

To summarize, Taenia solium causes two separate diseases that must never be confused: the intestinal tapeworm, acquired by eating undercooked pork, and neurocysticercosis, acquired by swallowing eggs from human feces, which produces seizures, hydrocephalus, and chronic epilepsy when larval cysts settle in the brain. Antiparasitic treatment with albendazole or praziquantel is given only when living cysts warrant it, is always paired with corticosteroids because killing the cysts inflames the brain, is supported by anticonvulsants, is coordinated with imaging, and is verified against current guidance before the first dose. Intestinal taeniasis is treated with niclosamide or praziquantel, but praziquantel must be used cautiously when hidden brain cysts may be present, and the tapeworm carrier must be treated because that person is the source of future brain disease. Echinococcosis follows a dog and sheep cycle for Echinococcus granulosus, producing hydatid cysts that grow slowly in liver and lung, threaten anaphylaxis if they rupture, and are managed with albendazole, percutaneous puncture and aspiration with injection and reaspiration, or surgery, while Echinococcus multilocularis produces a worse, infiltrating, often lifelong disease. The nurse's enduring contributions are the exposure history, the pre-treatment safety check, readiness for anaphylaxis, monitoring for drug toxicity and rising intracranial pressure, long-term follow up, and prevention education that names the specific practice in plain language.
