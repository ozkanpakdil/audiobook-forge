# Chapter 49 — Triage and Referral

Triage is the discipline of ranking, not the ceremony of sorting that the word sometimes suggests. The triage question is never whether a patient is sick, because in a remote clinic nearly everyone in front of you is sick in some way. The question is narrower: who needs help in the next five minutes, who can wait an hour, and who can safely wait a day. Ask it in those words and the room stops being a crowd and becomes an order.

Triage is not a doorway ritual that ends once the patient sits down. It is a continuous state of attention, repeated at every handover and after every intervention, because the patient who could wait an hour at eight in the morning may not be able to wait an hour at ten. The nurse is almost always the person who performs it and repeats it, since the clinician may be in the operating room, on the radio, or asleep after a night of call. When triage fails, it usually fails not because knowledge was absent but because nobody was assigned to keep looking.

The rapid assessment begins before you touch anyone, from the doorway of the room. Look at the patient's color, posture, and work of breathing, and listen for the sounds that carry, such as stridor, grunting, or the wet gurgle of an airway filling with vomit. Airway danger is announced by drooling, a muffled voice, gurgling, or a hand held to the throat, and burns around the mouth warn that swelling will worsen over hours. Never lie a patient with airway obstruction flat, and never force a look with a tongue depressor in a child who is drooling.

Breathing failure is more often a matter of effort than of rate, and the nurse who counts only the respiratory rate will miss it. Look for the muscles between the ribs and above the collarbone pulling inward, for the child whose head bobs with every breath, and for the patient who has stopped speaking in full sentences. Circulation declares itself through cold hands, a mottled knee, capillary refill longer than three seconds, and a fast thready pulse, and the blood pressure falls only after all of these. Severe distress, blue lips, exhaustion, or a falling level of consciousness means oxygen, positioning, and immediate referral.

Consciousness is the fourth pillar, and any change in it is a red flag until proved otherwise. Assess it with a simple ladder: alert, responding to voice, responding only to pain, and unresponsive. Check the pupils, check the blood sugar when a meter is available, and look for a stiff neck, because a first seizure or a seizure lasting more than five minutes requires treatment now and referral afterward. Children compensate beautifully and then fail within minutes, and the integrated management of childhood illness of the World Health Organization teaches danger signs any trained worker can find: inability to drink, vomiting everything, convulsions, and lethargy.

Obstetric triage puts the mother first and the baby second, and it is the one setting where two patients share a single decision. Heavy vaginal bleeding, a blood pressure of 160 over 110 or higher, severe headache with blurred vision, convulsions, and absent fetal movement are all red flags, as is a presenting part that is not the head. The woman who has bled and is now cold, restless, and thirsty needs treatment for shock before she needs a diagnosis, so place her on her left side, secure an intravenous line, and move. Trauma triage marches through the primary survey, so splint the fractures, cover the wounds, keep the patient warm, and move, because the interval from injury to definitive care is the strongest predictor of survival.

The focused assessment with sonography in trauma looks for free fluid in the abdomen and around the heart, but its absence changes nothing about the order of your actions. Fever is not a category by itself, but fever with a danger sign is one of the loudest alarms in tropical practice. In a malaria endemic area, a positive rapid diagnostic test confirms infection but does not tell you how sick the patient is, and a negative test does not exclude malaria in a severely ill patient whose blood film has not been examined. Treat the sickest patient for the most likely lethal cause without waiting for perfect proof, and verify the current national guideline before prescribing, because the criteria for severe malaria are revised.

Many facilities translate the ranking into colors, because a color can be seen across a room and understood by a family. Red means attention now, usually within minutes; yellow means attention soon, within an hour, and a patient who must be watched; green means a patient who can walk, wait, and be sent home with advice. Write the category, the time it was assigned, and the time of the next review beside every patient, and treat a missing category as a red one. Labels are often improvised, and that is acceptable if the meaning is shared, such as colored cards or a seating order along a bench.

When more than one patient arrives at once, triage changes from a queue into an allocation of scarce hands, and the arithmetic becomes brutal. Assign the first trained person to stand at the door and do nothing but ranking, because the greatest error in a surge is that everyone begins treating the first patient they meet. Sort by the likelihood of survival with the resources available: the patient who needs one intervention you can give goes first, the patient who needs an operation you cannot perform may have to wait, and the patient who is already dying may receive comfort only. Vital signs are the backbone of both triage and monitoring, and their value comes from repetition rather than from any single reading. Record the respiratory rate, the pulse, the level of consciousness, the oxygen saturation when a meter exists, and the last urine passed, with the time beside each value. One set of numbers is a snapshot, while a set taken every 15 minutes is a trajectory, and the trajectory tells you whether treatment is working. The patient who is waiting is still a patient, so name a watcher for every shift and tell that person to look at the breathing, the color, and the alertness of everyone in the waiting area at least every 15 to 30 minutes.

There are times when waiting is the correct clinical decision, and the skill is to know which waits are safe. A child with diarrhea who is drinking and playing, an adult with a mild fever and no danger sign, and a woman in early labor with a normal examination can all be observed, provided the observation has a schedule and a person attached to it. A safe wait always has a defined interval for reassessment, a defined set of findings that would end it, and a plan for what happens if the patient worsens at night. Referral is the central clinical act of remote practice, and it deserves the same seriousness as prescribing. Three questions organize it: does this patient need care that only a higher level can provide, can this patient survive the journey, and is there anything at the destination that will change the outcome. A referral that answers yes to the first question and no to the second is a decision to kill the patient politely, and one that answers yes to the first two and no to the third spends a family's savings for nothing. Ask all three every time, and write down how you answered them.

Certain conditions oblige you to evacuate, and the list is short enough to memorize. It includes an airway that is threatened or already blocked, respiratory failure that does not improve with first treatment, shock that persists after intravenous fluid, a surgical abdomen, an open fracture, a head injury with a falling level of consciousness, a seizure that will not stop, severe malaria with any danger sign, meningitis, severe pneumonia in a child with danger signs, a woman with severe pre-eclampsia or hemorrhage in pregnancy, and a newborn who is not feeding or is breathing fast. The purpose of the list is speed, because when the diagnosis is on it you stop deliberating and start preparing the journey. Just as important is the discipline of treating in place, because every unnecessary transfer consumes a vehicle, a driver, a nurse, and a family's money, and it exposes the patient to real risk. A patient can usually be treated where you are when the diagnosis is one you manage with confidence, when treatment is adequate by mouth or by injection, when monitoring is available, and when a deterioration would still leave time to travel. Uncomplicated malaria, simple diarrhea with mild dehydration, an uncomplicated urinary infection, and a closed fracture that can be splinted all fit this description. The test is not whether you would prefer the patient to be seen by a doctor, since you would always prefer that; the test is whether the journey adds more benefit than risk.

There is a small but real group of patients for whom the journey is the greater danger. A patient whose airway is obstructed and unprotected, a patient in profound shock who is still bleeding, a woman in advanced labor with an imminent delivery, and a patient actively convulsing should not be loaded into a vehicle until the immediate threat is controlled. A patient with severe shortness of breath who desaturates when moved, and a patient with severe anemia whose heart is already failing, may die in the first minutes of travel rather than at the destination. Sometimes the honest conclusion is that moving the patient will kill them before the operating room does, and that care must be delivered where they lie, with the family present and the reasoning recorded.

There is another group for whom transfer changes nothing because the destination has no treatment that will alter the course. Advanced cancer with no available chemotherapy, end stage organ failure with no dialysis, and a catastrophic brain injury with no ventilator all belong here, and sending such a patient on a painful journey to a place that will confirm what you already know transfers distress rather than treating disease. Honest palliative care in place, with pain relief and symptom control, is a legitimate and often superior plan. The World Health Organization is explicit that comfort is a treatment and not a surrender, and its guidance on pain relief and opioid dosing is revised and differs between countries, so verify the current national guideline before you prescribe.

Palliative transfer is a distinct plan with its own logic, and it is chosen when the patient cannot be made comfortable where they are but will not receive curative treatment at the destination. Whatever the reason, the transfer is made for comfort, so the preparations differ: a plan for pain and breathlessness, an escort who understands that the goal is not resuscitation, and a letter stating clearly that the patient is for comfort only. The most important sentence in that letter tells the receiving clinician not to begin an aggressive search you have already completed. Palliative transfer is not a lesser referral, it is a referral with a correctly stated objective.

The referral decision is a comparison of two risks, the risk of staying and the risk of going. The risk of staying is the probability that the untreated condition deteriorates, multiplied by the harm that deterioration would cause. The risk of going is the probability that the journey itself produces harm, multiplied by the severity of that harm. Assessing transport risk begins with the question of who will actually drive, so ask how long the journey takes in the worst case, whether a river or a ferry must be crossed, and whether the receiving facility will still be receiving when the patient arrives.

Stabilization before transport rests on a single observation: the first hour of the journey is the most dangerous hour, because in that hour the patient leaves the monitoring and the hands that were keeping them alive. Everything you can do in the clinic should be done before the wheels turn, not attempted on a rough road. Secure the airway or position the patient so that it stays open, obtain intravenous access and start fluids if shock is present, give the first dose of the definitive drug, stop the seizure, splint the fracture, warm the patient, and treat the pain. The measure of a good stabilization is that every line is taped, the next dose is drawn up, and nothing essential remains to be done during the first hour.

Some specific actions repay the time they take. In a convulsing patient, stop the seizure before loading the vehicle, because a seizure in transit is how patients die on the road. In a child with severe dehydration, begin the rehydration plan of the World Health Organization and, when shock is present, give the fluid that the current guideline specifies for that child's weight, remembering that a malnourished child needs slower and smaller volumes. In severe malaria give the first parenteral dose before departure, in suspected bacterial meningitis give the first antibiotic, and in severe pre-eclampsia give magnesium sulfate according to the current national guideline, because every regimen named here is revised and differs between countries.

Doses deserve to be spoken carefully, with the source named and the instruction to verify. For an adult with severe malaria, current World Health Organization guidance uses intravenous artesunate at a weight based dose given at zero, 12, and 24 hours and then once a day. For a seizure that will not stop, an intravenous benzodiazepine such as diazepam or lorazepam is given at a weight based dose, and for severe pre-eclampsia magnesium sulfate is given by the regimen in the national guideline. Every one of these numbers has been changed by a guideline revision at some point, so verify the current edition and confirm the weight before you calculate.

Fluid resuscitation is where good intentions and physiology sometimes part company, so the remote nurse needs a defensible plan. In a child with shock and no severe malnutrition, current guidance calls for a bolus of an isotonic crystalloid at a weight based volume, repeated and reassessed, with attention to the signs of overload that may follow. In a child with severe acute malnutrition, fluids are given more slowly and in smaller volumes, because the malnourished heart tolerates a rapid load poorly. In an adult with hemorrhage, the goal is to stop the bleeding and to keep the circulation sufficient to preserve consciousness and urine output rather than to fill the patient to a normal blood pressure, so record the volume given and the response.

The documentation that travels with the patient is often the only voice the patient has at the destination, and it should be written for a stranger who is tired and busy. Include who the patient is, with age, sex, and a contact for the next of kin; what happened, in a short narrative; what you found, including the vital signs and the important negative findings; what you have already given, with drug names, doses, routes, and times; and what you are asking for. Add the allergies, the weight, and the pregnancy status, and keep a duplicate or photograph the sheet with consent. Sign it, date it, and print your name and the facility, because an unsigned note is only a rumor.

Communication with the receiving facility before departure is one of the highest yield actions in the whole process, and it is frequently skipped. A call that says who is coming, what the problem is, what the patient needs, and when the patient will arrive allows the receiving team to prepare a bed, hold blood, alert a surgeon, and keep oxygen ready. Use a structured order of speech so the critical facts survive a bad connection: identify yourself and the patient, state the problem in one sentence, give the vital signs, say what you have done, state what you need, and confirm the estimated time of arrival. Ask a direct question and wait for a direct answer, namely whether the facility will accept this patient and whether the service needed is available now.

Communication fails in predictable ways, and the remote nurse should plan for each one. The network may be down, the only phone may have no credit, the receiving clinician may be in the operating room, and the family may have no way to call ahead. Build redundancy before you need it: know the alternate number, keep a written card with the referral criteria and the direct line of the destination, and agree on a fallback plan for a patient who arrives unannounced. Use whatever channel exists, including a community radio, a passing driver, or a health worker traveling the same way, and always send the written letter with the patient even when the telephone call succeeded.

The escort is part of the treatment, and choosing the escort is a clinical decision rather than an administrative one. A patient who needs oxygen, an intravenous infusion, or repeated doses of a drug needs an escort who can deliver those things, while a patient who needs only comfort may travel with a family member who has been taught what to watch for at the gate. Give the escort a written summary of the diagnosis, the treatment already given, the doses due during the journey, and the signs that require a stop. The transport kit holds a bag and mask, suction, oxygen if the facility has it, intravenous cannulas and fluid, the drugs the patient is likely to need, an anticonvulsant, a source of glucose, dressings, a splint, and a torch, and it is checked at the start of every shift and restocked after every use.

Monitoring during the journey is a matter of position, warmth, and repeated observation. Keep the airway open, keep the patient warm, and keep the injured part still, and check the breathing, the color, and the level of alertness at intervals written on the referral sheet. A patient carried on the back of a motorcycle or in the bed of a truck is exposed to cold, dust, and vibration, so wrap the patient and pad the stretcher, and check any intravenous line for patency and for swelling at every stop. If the patient deteriorates, the general rule is to stop, treat what is treatable, and then decide whether to continue or turn back.

Death on the road is a known event in remote practice, and it is often preventable, which is why it deserves to be named rather than avoided. The common mechanisms are an airway lost in a patient who was laid flat, aspiration of vomit, respiratory failure that progressed without oxygen, hemorrhage that continued because the source was never controlled, and a drug dose that was miscalculated. Review every death in transit as a case, without blame but with honesty, and ask whether the referral was necessary, whether the patient was stable enough, whether the escort was equipped, and whether the destination was right. Transfer carries its own adverse effects, and the nurse should anticipate them the way she anticipates the side effects of a drug. Immobility produces pressure injury, and a long journey on an empty stomach produces nausea and aspiration. Drug reactions travel too, and an allergic reaction that begins in transit is far harder to treat than one that begins in a room with a tray of drugs. Give an antiemetic when nausea is likely, check the pulses beyond every plaster before departure, and send the drugs that treat the complications you can foresee, because a referral is not complete until the risk of the journey itself has been reduced.

Sometimes the family refuses the journey, and the response is neither to bully nor to abandon. Ask what the refusal means, because a father may be unable to leave the harvest, a mother may have no one to keep the other children, and a family may believe that a person who dies away from home cannot be buried properly. Explain what you believe will happen without treatment, offer whatever you can offer in place, and write down that the offer was made, the reason given, and the plan for follow up. If the patient is a child or cannot decide, the ethical weight shifts, so involve a supervisor, a community leader, or the local authority, and keep the door open, because a family treated with respect often returns.

Referral is not a single step but a ladder of levels, and the remote nurse should know every rung. The health post refers to the health center, the health center to the district hospital, and the district hospital to the regional hospital, and each rung has different capabilities in surgery, blood banking, imaging, and intensive care. When travel is impossible, a consultation by telephone or a stored photograph sent to a distant colleague can change management without moving the patient, so ask for it early and record the advice and the name of the person who gave it. A referral that never closes is a loose end in the patient's care and in the clinic's records. Ask the receiving facility to send back a short note with the diagnosis, the treatment given, and the plan, and give the family a written request for that note to carry. When the patient returns, enter the outcome in a register, because the register is how a clinic learns whether its triage decisions were right, and follow up the patients who were sent away and never returned. Count the patients seen, the number assigned to each category, the number referred, the number who arrived, and the number who died, and review the trend monthly with the team.

The logistics of movement decide more referrals than any clinical judgment, and the nurse who manages them is doing clinical work. Know in advance where the vehicle is, who can drive it, whether it is fueled, and who authorizes its use at night, because a referral that waits three hours for a signature is a referral that waited too long. Keep a written agreement with the district office and the ambulance service that sets out how a vehicle is requested, how fuel is reimbursed, and what happens when the ambulance is already out. Identify the alternatives before you need them, such as a motorcycle with a trailer, a boat, or a stretcher carried by four people, and establish a hard rule about night travel on unsafe roads.

The supplies that make referral possible must be kept for referral and not consumed by routine work. Reserve a small stock of the drugs used in stabilization, a cylinder of oxygen if you have one, intravenous fluids, and the dressings and splints for the journey, and label them for that purpose. If the district hospital needs blood, ask in advance how a donor is found and how long cross matching takes, since a patient with severe anemia may need to wait for a relative to arrive. Check the oxygen supply and the delivery device before the emergency, keep the referral kit at the point of departure rather than in a locked store, and tell your supervisor when the reserve is running low rather than after it is gone.

The nursing role in triage and referral is one of the few places in medicine where the nurse holds the whole decision and must own it. In many remote facilities the nurse is the most senior person present at night, and the triage category, the decision to travel, and the letter are hers to make and to sign. That authority is legitimate only when it is supported by training, by standing orders that a medical officer has approved, and by a clear channel to a senior clinician by telephone. Escalate early, document the advice you received and the name of the person who gave it, and never let a fear of looking foolish delay a call for help.

The moral weight of these choices should be acknowledged honestly rather than papered over. The nurse who chooses which of two patients receives the last unit of blood, who tells a family that the journey is futile, or who watches a patient die on the road after deciding to travel carries something that no protocol can relieve. It helps to name the feeling accurately, because this is moral distress and not incompetence, and it arises when the right action is unavailable rather than when you have failed to find it. Talk about the difficult decisions with a colleague, a supervisor, or a mentor, and write the reasoning down at the time, because a documented decision can be defended and learned from while an undocumented one is remembered as a private failure. Telling the truth to the patient and the family is part of the referral, not a separate courtesy. Explain what you think is wrong, what you can and cannot do, why you are recommending the journey, what it will cost in money and time, and what you expect to happen at the other end. Consent for transfer should be explicit, but the signature is worthless unless the conversation was real, so record what was said and give the family a copy of the transfer note.

To summarize, triage is the discipline of ranking patients by the urgency of their need, and it is repeated rather than performed once, so that the patient who could wait an hour is examined again before that hour ends. Look for the red flags by system, the airway that is narrowing, the breathing that costs effort, the circulation that fails before the blood pressure does, and the consciousness that is fading, and remember that children compensate and then collapse. Use color categories, improvised labels, and a deliberately generous threshold for urgency, accepting that over-triage is the safer error. The referral decision weighs the risk of staying against the risk of travel, and it separates those who must go, those who can be treated in place, those for whom the journey is the greater danger, and those for whom comfort is the honest goal. Stabilize before the wheels turn, because the first hour is the most dangerous, name the drug and the dose and the guideline they came from, and verify the current national or World Health Organization regimen before you prescribe.
