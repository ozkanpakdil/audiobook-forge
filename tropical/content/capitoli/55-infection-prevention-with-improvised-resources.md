# Chapter 55 — Infection Prevention with Improvised Resources

A clinic can infect a patient as surely as it can cure one. In a remote post that truth is a daily arithmetic rather than a slogan. Every hand at the door, every instrument on a tray, and every bucket drawn from the well carries some of the clinic's own microbial life. Managed well, those surfaces interrupt disease, and managed poorly the clinic becomes an amplifier that sends patients home with something new. Infection prevention is therefore a mindset before it is a set of supplies. It rests on the conviction that the next infection is preventable with what is already in the building. The guidance here follows the World Health Organization and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, with the field manuals of Doctors Without Borders. Every concentration, temperature, duration, and drug named below is current guidance rather than settled truth, so verify the current guideline and your national protocol before acting on any of it.

To see why improvisation is not the same as laxity, follow the chain every infection travels. A pathogen needs a reservoir, a way out, a mode of travel, a way in, and a susceptible host. The clinic concentrates all five links in a single building, which is why it can amplify what it receives. The reservoir is the infected patient, the dirty instrument, or the standing water. The exit is a wound, a cough, a stool, a catheter, or a needle. Contact on hands is the dominant route in most small clinics, while droplets travel a short distance and airborne particles can cross a whole room. Once you can name the route, you improvise the right barrier instead of the most familiar one.

Hand hygiene is the single highest-yield practice available to you. No piece of equipment in the clinic comes close to it. The World Health Organization built its campaign on the observation that most transmission is preventable at the moment of contact. Clean the hands before touching a patient and before any clean or aseptic procedure. Clean them after any risk of contact with blood or body fluid. Clean them after touching a patient and after touching anything in that patient's surroundings. Those are the five moments, and they follow the sequence of an ordinary clinical encounter rather than a list of pathogens.

The technique of washing matters as much as the decision to wash. Wet the hands, apply soap, and work the lather over the palms, the backs, between the fingers, the thumbs, the fingertips, and the wrists. Continue for 40 to 60 seconds, then rinse under running water. Running water is the point, because a shared basin hands the next person a stronger dose of whatever the last person left. Dry the hands on a clean cloth or paper used once and then laundered. Remove rings with stones or ridges, keep the nails short, and never wear artificial nails, because soap does not reach the space beneath them. If the water fails, a jerry can with a tap or a tippy tap of can and string still beats a bowl.

When soap is scarce or the queue is long, an alcohol-based hand rub made on site is the workhorse. The World Health Organization publishes two standard formulations a careful nurse can prepare locally. The first is ethanol at 80 per cent by volume with glycerol at 1.45 per cent and hydrogen peroxide at 0.125 per cent. The second is isopropyl alcohol at 75 per cent with the same glycerol and hydrogen peroxide. The alcohol kills, the hydrogen peroxide guards against spores in the stored product, and the glycerol protects the skin from drying. Apply the rub to dry hands and work every surface for 20 to 30 seconds until it evaporates. Do not use it on hands visibly soiled with blood, stool, or dirt, because alcohol cannot penetrate organic material and washing must come first.

Treat production of the rub as a pharmaceutical process rather than a kitchen chore. A weak batch is invisible and gives false confidence. Use clean utensils, measure the alcohol honestly, and mix in a washed and dried vessel. Decant into small bottles that are dated and labeled with the batch number. Record who prepared each batch and the volumes used, and keep the record where it can be reviewed. Store the bottles away from sunlight and open flame, because alcohol vapor is flammable, and never reuse a food or drink bottle that a child might mistake. Where soap truly runs out, ash mixed with water loosens fat and dirt and is accepted by the World Health Organization, though it is harsh on broken skin.

Gloves supplement hand hygiene and never replace it, and their rationing is a clinical decision. Wear gloves whenever you expect contact with blood, body fluid, mucous membrane, broken skin, or a contaminated surface. Wear sterile gloves for any procedure entering sterile tissue or the bloodstream. Clean examination gloves are adequate for most patient contact and for handling soiled linen. Change gloves between patients and between tasks on the same patient. Clean the hands immediately after removing them, because the glove carries the same organisms as the bare hand. The difference between clean and sterile is about the risk of introducing organisms into a site that cannot defend itself, and the nurse who can explain it can defend a supply decision.

The commonest abuses of gloves are the ones that spread the most infection. A single pair worn from one patient to the next, rinsed under a tap, or wiped with alcohol is a vehicle rather than a barrier. Gloves that are too large slip and tear, and gloves worn over rings puncture more easily. Prolonged wear macerates the skin and causes the contact dermatitis that makes staff abandon the practice altogether. Short, task-specific use with frequent hand hygiene is the goal. If a shortage forces a choice, protect the sterile procedure first, then the exposure to blood and body fluid. Reprocessing examination gloves is discouraged and is a last resort: only visibly intact gloves, washed, soaked in 0.5 per cent chlorine, rinsed, dried, and inspected against the light, documented as a supply failure rather than praised as ingenuity.

Before an instrument reaches a sterilizer, decide what level of reprocessing it needs. A critical item enters sterile tissue or the bloodstream, such as a scalpel, a suture needle, or a catheter, and it must be sterile. A semicritical item touches mucous membrane or non-intact skin, such as a speculum or a laryngoscope blade, and it needs high-level disinfection at minimum. A noncritical item touches only intact skin or the environment, such as a blood pressure cuff or a floor, and it needs cleaning and a low-level disinfectant. Naming the category out loud avoids both the dangerous shortcut and the wasteful one. Cleaning always comes first, and it is the step improvised clinics skip most: blood and pus protect organisms from disinfectants and block steam, so scrub every hinge and serration with detergent, rinse with clean water, and dry before packaging.

Sterilization by steam under pressure is the standard improvised settings reach for, and the pressure cooker and the small autoclave work on the same principle. Steam carries more heat energy than dry air at the same temperature, and it releases that energy when it condenses on a cooler instrument. That is why moist heat kills far faster than dry heat. Pressure raises the boiling point so the steam exceeds 100 degrees Celsius, and the sealed chamber holds it against the load long enough for the kill. A pressure cooker with a weight, an intact gasket, and a rack above the water is a genuine sterilizer. A common standard is 121 degrees Celsius at 15 pounds per square inch for 30 minutes for wrapped instruments. Loads must not be packed tightly, because steam must circulate and air must escape, and timing begins only when the working pressure is reached.

The indicator on the outside of the pack tells you less than most people believe. Tape changes color when heated, and a chemical indicator changes color after heat, steam, and time. Neither proves that the center of a dense load reached the killing temperature. Only a biological indicator, resistant spores processed with the load and then cultured, can demonstrate that the cycle truly sterilized, and it belongs at defined intervals and after any repair. Where it is unavailable, the honest fallback is process control: record the temperature, pressure, time, and load in a signed logbook and read the gauge yourself. Packaging and storage then decide whether the instrument is still sterile, so wrap in material that passes steam but blocks dust and never in sealed plastic. Dry the packs, store them in a closed cupboard away from the damp wall and the sink, date every pack, and reprocess any pack that is dropped, torn, or damp.

When sterilization is impossible, high-level disinfection carries most of the benefit for semicritical items. Boiling fully immersed instruments at a rolling boil for at least 20 minutes kills bacteria, viruses, fungi, and most parasites. It does not reliably kill bacterial spores and does not leave the item sterile, so boiled instruments must be used promptly and stored dry and covered. Keep the pot dedicated to this purpose, scrub it between uses, and never cook in it. Boiling suits specula and forceps but not a scalpel that will enter tissue, because surviving spores can cause tetanus and gas gangrene. Chlorine is the other improvised route: 0.5 per cent, which is 5000 parts per million, disinfects cleaned instruments and blood spills with a contact time of about 10 minutes. Mix it fresh each day and never mix it with acid or ammonia, because the mixture releases a gas that injures the lungs.

Other high-level disinfectants each have a place and a hazard. Glutaraldehyde at 2 per cent suits heat-sensitive equipment but irritates the skin, eyes, and airways and has caused occupational asthma, so it needs a well-ventilated area, gloves, eye protection, and a closed container. Hydrogen peroxide is less irritating, though it corrodes some metals and bleaches fabric. Alcohol at 70 per cent is an excellent surface and skin disinfectant but a poor instrument sterilant, because it evaporates before the contact time is complete. Whatever agent you choose, the contact time decides whether the process worked, and shortening it converts a disinfectant into a rinse. Scalds from boiling water and from opening a pressurized cooker too early are common injuries. Store chemicals below the level of food and medicines, locked away from children, and treat every new rash or wheeze as an occupational problem.

Isolation is an idea before it is a room, and the idea is to separate the patient who can transmit from the one who is vulnerable. The World Health Organization organizes isolation around the route of transmission. A patient with an airborne infection therefore needs different separation from a patient with diarrhea or a resistant wound. In a small clinic the separation comes from distance, a barrier, dedicated equipment, and the discipline of staff who move between two sides. The purpose is to interrupt a specific route, not to punish or frighten the patient. Ventilation is the improvised answer to airborne transmission and costs only attention. Open windows on opposite sides to create a cross flow, place a coughing patient near the window or on a verandah, and let the wind decide where others wait.

The improvised isolation room is assembled from plastic sheeting, string, tape, and a clear plan. Choose a room at the end of the building with its own door, or a corner that can be closed off with plastic hung from a wire. Hang a screen at the entrance and place a chair, a table, and a handwashing point just outside the boundary. Mark the boundary with tape or paint, so that the cleaner and the visiting relative can see where the clean side ends. Dedicated staff and equipment keep the isolation from leaking back into the ward. Assign one or two nurses and one cleaner to the isolated patient, and reserve a thermometer, a cuff, a stethoscope, and a bedpan for that patient alone. Keep gloves, aprons, and disinfectant at the entrance, because a barrier that must be fetched is a barrier that will be skipped.

Some infections change the whole plan, and tuberculosis and measles are the clearest examples because they travel on air. Separate a coughing patient with weight loss at the entrance and give a surgical mask where masks exist. Move that patient by the shortest route to the best-ventilated space, keeping others at a distance rather than seated beside them. Moving the waiting area outdoors under a shade tree is often the most effective intervention in a clinic with no mechanical ventilation. Keep children with measles out of the general waiting area entirely, because the virus lingers in a closed room after the patient has gone home. Viral hemorrhagic fever raises the standard to its highest level and demands a single room, a dedicated equipment set, and a limited number of trained staff. It also demands a supervised sequence for putting on and taking off personal protective equipment, and the World Health Organization publishes the detailed guidance that no improvised version should replace.

Barrier nursing in an ordinary clinic is improvised from cheap materials used consistently. A plastic apron cut from heavy sheeting protects the front of the body from splashes and can be washed and reused. Boot covers made from sturdy plastic bags tied above the ankle keep the feet out of contaminated fluid. A cloth mask tied with four strings protects those nearby from a cough but does not protect the wearer from fine airborne particles. It is source control rather than a respirator and must be changed when it becomes damp. Eye protection can be improvised from clear goggles or a face shield cut from a plastic bottle. Hands are covered with gloves changed between tasks, and the barrier is only as good as the moment it is removed, so the order of removal and the hand wash afterward matter more than the material.

Traffic flow turns a collection of barriers into a system. Divide the patient area into a contaminated zone, a clean zone, and a transition space where equipment is put on and taken off. Move in one direction wherever possible, from clean to contaminated. A nurse carrying a sterile dressing should not meet a nurse carrying a soiled one in the same doorway. Place handwashing points at every boundary. Keep clean supplies on a shelf that is never touched with a contaminated glove, and keep waste and soiled linen moving outward. Where the building is too small for three zones, define the corners and mark the floor, because the discipline of the staff is what makes the imaginary lines real.

An outbreak in a single building forces decisions a large hospital never has to make, and cohorting protects the most people with the least space. Cohorting means placing patients infected with the same organism together and apart from the uninfected. The number of staff, equipment sets, and contaminated surfaces then stays as small as possible. When cases grow, the whole building may become one cohort, and the clinic must decide how to receive any new patient who arrives without the infection. That decision belongs with the district health authority and the community rather than the nurse alone. It determines who gets care and where, so it must be written down with its reasoning. The alternative is a ward in which every bed is a new exposure, and an afternoon of planning can prevent that.

Waste is where the clinic's organisms leave the building, and separation must happen the moment waste is created. Soiled dressings, used gloves, and blood-stained gauze belong in a closed, lined container that is lidded and never overfilled. Sharp items belong in a puncture-resistant container kept within arm's reach of the person using them. Food waste, paper, and wrappers stay separate, so the infectious stream remains small enough to burn or bury safely. Build the sharps container from a rigid bottle or sealed tin with a small opening and a lid, fill it no more than three quarters full, and seal and label it. Never recap, bend, or carry a used needle across the room, because most injuries happen in that short walk. Label every bin, require gloves and an apron of whoever empties it, and design the burn pit so it never contaminates the water table.

Linen and environmental cleaning are quiet sources of infection that a busy clinic forgets. Carry soiled sheets in a bag that does not leak and never shake them out in the ward. Wash them in hot water with detergent, add chlorine where the fabric tolerates it, and dry them in direct sunlight where the climate allows. Where laundry is done by hand at a shared tap, teach the sequence and watch it done. Cleaning deserves a written order of work, because the sequence prevents the cleaner from carrying contamination from the toilet to the delivery room. Clean the least contaminated areas first, then the ward, then the toilets and the soiled utility area, changing cloth and water between zones. Remove visible blood with absorbent paper, disinfect with a stronger solution, dispose of everything used safely, and dry the mop and bucket afterward.

When a needle-stick or a splash happens, the first minutes matter and the wrong reflex makes things worse. Wash the wound gently with soap and running water and let it bleed a little. Do not squeeze, scrub, or apply bleach, alcohol, or a caustic agent, because those measures injure tissue and have not been shown to reduce transmission. If blood or fluid splashes into the eye, the mouth, or the nose, flush with clean water or saline for several minutes with the eyelids held open, and never put disinfectant in the eye. Remove contaminated clothing, wash the exposed skin, and tell someone immediately, because a hidden exposure cannot be treated. Record the time, the device, the depth, whether the source is known, and whether the worker has been vaccinated against hepatitis B. Test the source with consent where the source is known, test the worker at baseline, report the exposure the same day, and review it at the next staff meeting.

Post-exposure drugs for bloodborne viruses must start quickly, because their value falls with every hour of delay. For exposure to the human immunodeficiency virus, the World Health Organization recommends tenofovir disoproxil fumarate at 300 milligrams, lamivudine at 300 milligrams, and dolutegravir at 50 milligrams, once daily for 28 days. Begin as soon as possible and no later than 72 hours after exposure, with the greatest benefit when it begins within a few hours. Some countries still use efavirenz at 600 milligrams in place of dolutegravir, and some programs adjust the regimen for the source patient's treatment history. The choice must therefore follow the current national guideline rather than memory. Because these regimens and thresholds are revised and differ between countries, verify the current guidance and the drug supply before you prescribe or dispense any of them. A stock that has expired is the same as no stock at all.

Hepatitis B and tetanus add two further arms to the same protocol. A worker who is not immune to hepatitis B needs the vaccine series started immediately, with hepatitis B immunoglobulin where available and ideally within 24 hours of exposure. A wound contaminated with soil, saliva, or feces requires an assessment of tetanus risk, and an out-of-date worker needs a booster or a full course according to the national schedule. Immunity should be documented before employment, so the decision after an injury does not depend on a frightened memory at midnight. The cheapest exposure protocol in the world is the one that vaccinated the staff before the needle was ever used. Every worker who handles blood, including cleaners and laboratory staff, belongs in that program. Staff who are pregnant, immunosuppressed, or living with a chronic illness need an individual assessment of the tasks they can safely perform.

The drugs cause adverse effects the exposed worker must be warned about, or the course will be abandoned in silence. Nausea, headache, fatigue, dizziness, vivid dreams, insomnia, and loose stools are common in the first days and usually settle. They are frightening when they arrive unannounced at three in the morning. Dolutegravir and the older drugs can affect the liver and, less often, the kidneys, so jaundice, dark urine, a rash, or severe abdominal pain needs urgent review. Adherence support is part of the treatment, because a course stopped at day ten protects far less than one completed for 28 days. Give the drugs discreetly, name a person to contact, and write the follow-up appointment down. Testing follows the intervals the national guideline specifies, commonly at six weeks, three months, and six months, and the worker should know what each test means before it is taken.

None of these systems work where no one may challenge a senior person, and hierarchy is where safety most reliably dies. The aide who notices that the surgeon did not change gloves or that a sharps container is overflowing must be able to say so without fear. Build that permission deliberately by asking the most junior person first what they saw. Thank people in public for raising a problem and treat every report as information rather than accusation. The senior clinician should model the behavior by being seen to wash hands and to change a decision when the evidence changes. Monitor hand hygiene at random moments, count exposures and sharps injuries, review the sterilization log for gaps, and watch surgical site infections and newborn sepsis. Track soap, rub, and glove consumption against patient contacts, because falling use with a stable case load is an early warning.

Feed the results back at a regular team meeting in plain language, with the specific change the data suggests. Documentation makes all of this visible and should survive a busy week. Keep a sterilization log of date, load, temperature, pressure, time, and initials. Keep an exposure register, a hand hygiene observation sheet, an infectious waste record, and a training record for every member of staff. Write the isolation precautions in the chart with the date they started and the reason. The records exist so the clinic can learn from its own past, not to satisfy an inspector. A culture in which the aide corrects the doctor is not a loss of authority but the only authority that keeps patients alive.

The supply line determines whether any of this is possible, and the nurse must manage it as carefully as any drug. Identify the small list without which infection prevention collapses: soap, alcohol, gloves, chlorine, sharps containers, and sterilization indicators. Set a minimum stock level for each that triggers a reorder before the last box is opened. Rotate stock by expiry date, keep the store dry, off the floor, and locked, and count critical items at fixed intervals rather than during a crisis. Where local production is possible, such as the rub or the sharps containers, make it a routine task with a named owner and a written recipe. When a supply fails, report it upward in writing and record the compromise in care that followed. A shortage that is never documented is a shortage that will be repeated.

The community is part of the system, and rules about visitors must be negotiated rather than imposed. Families provide food, washing, reassurance, and the practical care no understaffed clinic can supply. The goal is not to exclude them but to teach them where to stand, when to wash, and what not to touch. Explain the plastic screen, the separate entrance, and the mask in words that connect to the family's own understanding of sickness. Where a family cannot read, use demonstration and return demonstration rather than a printed sheet. A rule the community helped to write is a rule the community will help to enforce, and that trust is what makes an isolation ward acceptable rather than feared. Improvisation begins where the resource ends and ends where safety ends, so a pressure cooker that cannot sterilize a surgical load, plastic sheeting that cannot create a negative-pressure room, and a cloth mask that cannot protect during an aerosol-generating procedure must all be named and escalated rather than stretched.

To summarize, the clinic can infect as surely as it can cure, and infection prevention is the discipline that keeps the building on the side of healing. Clean hands at the five moments, made possible by soap, running water, or a locally prepared alcohol rub, prevent more infection than any other single act. Gloves supplement hand hygiene and are chosen by the category of the task, and cleaning before sterilization is the step that makes every later step work. Sterilization by steam under pressure, boiling, or chemical high-level disinfection must be selected by where the instrument will go, monitored with indicators and a log, and protected by proper packaging and storage. The improvised isolation room, the ventilation, the dedicated staff and equipment, the zoning, and the cohorting contain an outbreak within one building, while barrier nursing made from plastic, string, and discipline protects both the patient and the nurse. The needle-stick protocol, the post-exposure drugs, and the hepatitis B and tetanus measures are a clock that starts at the moment of injury. Their doses and intervals must be checked against the current World Health Organization and national guidance, which are revised and differ between countries. What you cannot improvise you must name and escalate, because the boundary of safe improvisation is exactly where the next infection begins.
