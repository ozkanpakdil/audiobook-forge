# Chapter 23 — Neglected Bacterial and Skin Diseases

The skin is the largest and most public organ. In much of the tropical world it carries disease that never reaches a record. The conditions in this chapter are bacterial or parasitic. They are neglected because they disable and disfigure rather than kill. Trachoma blinds slowly over decades. Yaws and scabies deform and isolate, and tungiasis makes every step painful. Podoconiosis swells the leg of a person infected by nothing but soil. Each is preventable and treatable, and each is wrapped in stigma.

Trachoma begins with Chlamydia trachomatis, an intracellular organism. Its variants labeled A, B, and C have adapted to the conjunctiva. Transmission is not sexual. Infection passes eye to eye on fingers, on shared cloths, and on flies. The bacterium provokes inflammation the immune system cannot fully resolve. Because immunity is incomplete, the same eye is infected repeatedly. Each episode leaves more scar. Repeated infection is the engine of trachoma.

Inflammation first produces lymphoid follicles on the tarsal conjunctiva. That tissue lies against the eye when the lid closes. Repeated episodes merge the follicles and lay down fibrous scar. The scar contracts and distorts the lid margin. It turns the upper lid inward, a condition called entropion. The lashes then rest against the cornea. This is trichiasis, and every blink drags stiff hairs across the eye. The result is pain, tearing, and light sensitivity.

Repeated abrasion leads to ulceration and bacterial superinfection. The cornea scars and becomes opaque. A person who reaches that stage is blind or nearly so, usually in both eyes by middle age. Risk factors are almost entirely environmental. Trachoma clusters where water is scarce and sanitation is poor. In such places faces are rarely washed. Households that walk far for water ration it, and the face is last. Fly density rises where animal dung and human waste lie near living space.

Test acuity in each eye if you can. Count the lashes that touch the globe. Record which eye and how many. Control rests on surgery, antibiotics, facial cleanliness, and environmental improvement. The four are not alternatives, and successful programs deliver all four together. Surgery corrects the inturned lid. Antibiotics reduce the community reservoir of infection. Facial cleanliness reduces transmission within households.

The operation is done under local anesthesia as an outpatient procedure. For a few lashes, epilation, or plucking, relieves symptoms briefly. Lashes regrow within weeks, so epilation is not a substitute for surgery. Your role is to identify who needs referral. Prepare the patient for the procedure. Provide wound and suture care afterward. Ensure the right patient reaches the operating table. Antibiotic treatment is where community and individual meet.

Current guidance from the World Health Organization gives azithromycin as a single dose. The dose is 20 milligrams per kilogram by mouth, maximum 1 gram. This equals topical tetracycline ointment applied twice a day for 6 weeks. The topical form remains useful for young infants. It is also useful when azithromycin is unavailable. Oral azithromycin has one great advantage. The course is completed in front of the person who gives it. That removes the adherence problem entirely.

For an individual case, treat the whole household at once. Reinfection from a family member is the usual cause of failure. Record the weight used, the dose given, and the date. Records are what let a program prove coverage. Mass treatment is the population-level use of the same antibiotic. The World Health Organization sets a threshold, usually around 10 per cent. Where active trachoma in children exceeds it, the community is offered azithromycin once a year. The program continues until prevalence stays below the threshold.

Coverage is the critical variable. Treating most of a community but not enough leaves transmission intact. Community drug distributors and teachers usually carry out distribution. Doses are measured by weight or height using a dosing pole. Children out of school and adults who work away are most easily missed. Registers and house-to-house sweeps are part of the method. Facial cleanliness and environmental improvement need behavior change and infrastructure. Face washing competes with carrying water for drinking and cooking.

Programs therefore improve water access as well as teach hygiene. Fly control includes safe disposal of animal and human waste. Keeping livestock away from living areas also helps. Latrine promotion reduces fly breeding and fecal contamination. These interventions are slow and political. Without them, trachoma returns when campaigns stop. Progress is measured with population-based prevalence surveys, not clinic counts. Programs track active inflammation in children and trichiasis in adults.

Those figures decide when mass treatment can stop. Surveillance then continues for several years. The World Health Organization validates elimination as a public health problem when criteria are met. Many countries have reached that point. The last cases are hardest because they occur in the poorest and most remote communities. Your monitoring role includes confirming that every person with trichiasis has been offered surgery. A program that stops at antibiotics leaves those losing sight without help.

Before leaving trachoma, a word about every regimen in this chapter. The doses here are drawn from published guidance. The sources are the World Health Organization, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control. That guidance is revised as new evidence appears. National protocols also differ, especially for pregnancy and young infants. Verify the current guideline before you prescribe or dispense. Never rely on a dose remembered from years ago. Present every regimen as what current guidance says, not as settled truth.

Write the source and the date beside the dose in your notes. Yaws is caused by Treponema pallidum pertenue, a spirochete. It is a close relative of the organism of venereal syphilis. It is transmitted by direct skin contact rather than sexually. It flourishes in warm, humid, rural settings where children run barefoot. It is a disease of childhood, with most cases before age 15. Infection requires a break in the skin and an infectious lesion. Crowding and poor hygiene facilitate spread.

Unlike syphilis, yaws is not passed from mother to fetus. It does not attack the nervous system or the heart. A diagnosis of yaws must never imply sexual transmission. The first lesion appears at the site of inoculation after roughly 3 to 5 weeks. It begins as a papule that enlarges and ulcerates. It is painless or only mildly tender, with a raised edge and moist base. It is often called the mother yaw. It usually sits on the leg or foot of a child.

It teems with treponemes and is highly infectious to other children. It heals spontaneously over weeks to months and leaves a scar. Secondary yaws follows weeks to months later. The organism spreads through the blood and lymph. The eruption is a crop of papular, macular, or nodular lesions. They are often covered with a yellow crust and favor warm surfaces. The palms and soles are frequently involved and crack painfully.

Latent infection can reactivate. Humans are the only host, with no animal reservoir and no vector. That is what makes elimination theoretically possible. Diagnosis is usually clinical in an endemic area. A child with a typical ulcerating lesion and a compatible history is treated at once. Laboratory confirmation is not required before treatment. Where laboratory support exists, dark-field microscopy can show the spirochete. It needs fresh material, a good microscope, and an experienced observer.

Serology is more practical. A non-treponemal test such as the rapid plasma reagin test shows activity. A treponemal test provides confirmation. The polymerase chain reaction, which you may know as PCR, can detect treponemal DNA from lesion swabs. Serology cannot distinguish yaws from venereal syphilis. Interpretation therefore depends on the clinical picture and the epidemiologic setting. Distinguishing the two depends on age, site, and history rather than a single test. Venereal syphilis is transmitted sexually, appears in adults, and produces genital lesions.

Venereal syphilis can affect the nervous system, the heart, and the fetus. Bejel, also called endemic syphilis, spreads among children in dry, hot, arid regions. It usually begins as oral lesions. Yaws occurs in humid tropical areas and begins on the legs and buttocks of young children. It is the only one of the three targeted for global elimination. All three respond to penicillin and azithromycin. The distinction is epidemiologic rather than a matter of different drugs. It still matters, because telling a family that a child has a sexually transmitted infection has consequences no laboratory result can undo.

Treatment of yaws has been transformed by a single drug. Current guidance gives azithromycin as a single oral dose. The dose is 30 milligrams per kilogram, maximum 2 grams. This cures the great majority of cases. Benzathine penicillin remains the older standard. It is used where azithromycin is unavailable or the patient cannot take an oral medicine. The adult dose is 1.2 million units given intramuscularly as a single injection. Younger children receive half that dose.

Because treatment is a single observed dose, adherence is not the obstacle it is in tuberculosis or HIV care. A whole village can be treated in a day. The drug is generally well tolerated, though gastrointestinal upset is common. Allergic reactions occur rarely, so check the national protocol before prescribing. The one serious threat to elimination is resistance to azithromycin. A mutation in the bacterial gene the drug targets has been detected in several countries. It can cause treatment failure and, if it spreads, undermine the campaign. Programs monitor and investigate treatment failures.

They test for the mutation when laboratory capacity allows. The operational response has been total community treatment. Everyone in a defined area receives a single dose. This reaches latent infections that would otherwise seed new cases. It requires very high coverage and careful dosing by weight. Resistance surveillance and high coverage are two halves of one strategy. After treatment, the patient should be examined again. Primary and secondary lesions usually heal within weeks.

Scabies is caused by the mite Sarcoptes scabiei variety hominis. It is about one third of a millimeter long and barely visible. The female burrows into the outermost skin layer, the stratum corneum. She lays eggs and deposits feces along a tunnel. The itch comes from the immune response, not from the mite itself. Symptoms take weeks after a first infestation. A previously infested person reacts within days because the immune system is primed. The mite survives away from human skin for a limited time.

Transmission requires prolonged skin-to-skin contact. That is why scabies spreads in households and institutions. A brief handshake does not usually transmit it. Ordinary scabies is dominated by intense itching that is worse at night. The lesions are small, red papules and vesicles. Burrows appear as fine, wavy, grayish lines a few millimeters long. They are most easily seen in finger webs, wrists, armpits, waist, buttocks, and genitalia. In infants the palms, soles, and scalp may be involved.

Excoriations from scratching are common and may obscure the primary lesions. The rash is often atypical and the burrow is easy to miss. Scabies is therefore mistaken for eczema, allergy, or insect bites. In an endemic area, treat an itchy rash in more than one household member as scabies. The most important complication is bacterial superinfection of scratched skin. Group A streptococcus and Staphylococcus aureus enter through the excoriations. They produce impetigo, cellulitis, and abscesses. In tropical settings this pathway contributes to post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis.

That kidney disease follows skin infection rather than sore throat. The link is well established in Pacific and Australian Indigenous communities. There, scabies control has reduced the incidence of kidney disease. Crusted, weeping skin lesions in a child may therefore signal future kidney damage. Ask about dark urine, facial swelling, and reduced urine output. Diagnosis of scabies is usually clinical. A typical itchy rash with a compatible distribution is enough to treat.

The practical rule is to treat when suspicion is reasonable. Treat the household rather than wait for laboratory proof. Crusted scabies, also called Norwegian scabies, behaves differently. It occurs when the immune response fails to control the mite. It is seen in advanced HIV infection and in people taking corticosteroids. Other immunosuppressive drugs carry the same risk. The very old and those with severe neurologic disease are also at risk. The skin may harbor thousands to millions of mites.

The lesions are thick, scaly plaques that can look like psoriasis or eczema. Itching is often minimal or absent. That is why the condition is missed until an outbreak occurs. The enormous mite burden makes it far more contagious than ordinary scabies. Even brief contact can transmit it. Any patient with a widespread scaly eruption in an institution should be considered infectious. The same applies to anyone with a suppressed immune system. Institutions are where scabies becomes an emergency.

Nursing homes, hospitals, prisons, orphanages, and boarding schools can sustain outbreaks. Dozens of residents and staff may be affected. Once crusted scabies is introduced, the environment becomes a source. Shed skin scales carry mites that survive long enough to infest others. Control requires treating every resident and every staff member at the same time. This is done whether or not they have symptoms. Treatment is repeated as the drug requires. Environmental cleaning matters, including laundering bedding and clothing.

Staff who are not treated become reinfested and reintroduce the mite. Treatment of ordinary scabies rests on two drugs. Permethrin is a topical insecticide that kills the mite on contact. The 5 per cent cream is applied from the neck down. It is left for 8 to 12 hours and then washed off. In infants the scalp, neck, and face are also treated, avoiding the eyes and mouth. Permethrin does not reliably kill the eggs. The application must therefore be repeated after 7 days.

Temporary burning, stinging, and itching can occur. These are often mistaken for treatment failure. Wash bedding and clothing used before treatment in hot water and dry hot. Sealing them in a plastic bag for several days also works. Permethrin is safe in pregnancy and in young infants when used as directed. Ivermectin is the second drug, taken by mouth as 200 micrograms per kilogram. It is repeated after 7 to 14 days because it does not kill the eggs. It is convenient and useful in mass treatment.

It helps patients who cannot apply topical therapy properly. It is effective in crusted scabies when combined with a topical agent. It should not be given to pregnant women in most guidance. Children weighing less than 15 kilograms should not receive it. Breastfeeding mothers in the first weeks are also excluded. These restrictions must be checked against current recommendations. A separate caution applies where Loa loa, the eye worm, is co-endemic. There, ivermectin can cause a severe neurologic reaction.

All household members and sexual contacts should be treated on the same day. This applies even without symptoms. Launder clothing and bedding used before treatment in hot water. Set them aside long enough for the mite to die if hot water is unavailable. Fumigants and sprays are not recommended. They do not reach the mite in the skin and may be harmful. Itching can persist for 2 to 4 weeks after successful treatment. Warn patients not to restart treatment for this reason.

Reassess persistent itching or new burrows as failure or reinfection. At the population level, scabies is increasingly managed with mass treatment. In communities with high prevalence, programs have used community-wide ivermectin. It is given alone or with permethrin. Trials show it reduces scabies and severe bacterial skin infection. The World Health Organization has added scabies to its strategy for skin-related neglected tropical diseases. Coverage must be high. The caution about Loa loa applies wherever ivermectin is distributed broadly.

Your part is to explain the medicine and to weigh and dose accurately. Record who received what. Make sure the youngest and oldest are not missed. Tungiasis is caused by Tunga penetrans, the sand flea. It lives in dry, sandy soil in parts of Latin America, the Caribbean, and sub-Saharan Africa. The adult female flea is the parasite. The male and immature stages live in the soil and feed on mammals. Dogs, cats, pigs, and rats are among the hosts.

The female attaches to human skin, usually on the foot. She burrows into the epidermis and enlarges as her eggs develop. Over about 2 weeks her abdomen swells into a nodule. It is white or yellowish with a dark opening at the center. She expels eggs through that opening and then dies in place. The flea jumps poorly and cannot fly. It therefore attacks the body parts closest to the soil. That is why the feet bear the burden.

The lesion is a firm, pea-sized nodule with a central black dot. It may be single or multiple. Clusters appear on the sole, heel, toes, and around the nails. Itching and a sensation of movement are common early symptoms. Pain develops as the nodule presses on nerve endings. Walking becomes painful and children may limp or refuse to walk. Inflammation around the lesion may be out of proportion to its size. Look carefully between the toes and under the nails.

Lesions there are easily missed and often most symptomatic. Confluent lesions on a toe can deform it and eventually cause autoamputation. The chief complications are secondary bacterial infection and tetanus. The opening in the skin gives bacteria direct access. Abscess, cellulitis, and lymph channel inflammation are common with many lesions. Tetanus is a real risk in unvaccinated people. Check and complete tetanus vaccination status in every patient. Chronic infection can destroy soft tissue and bone.

That leads to deformity and loss of the toe. Children and older adults carry the heaviest burden. Treatment is extraction of the flea with a sterile needle or forceps. Remove the intact flea without rupturing it. Rupture spills eggs and antigenic material into the wound. That increases inflammation and infection risk. Clean and dress the cavity and give tetanus prophylaxis according to history. Use antibiotics when there is cellulitis or an abscess.

Dogs and cats are reservoirs, so veterinary treatment is part of an integrated approach. Community education is essential. The condition is so familiar in some places that it is accepted as part of childhood. Podoconiosis most often masquerades as lymphatic filariasis. Understanding why it is not infectious is the key to managing it. The name means dust of the earth. The cause is prolonged barefoot contact with irritant soil. Fine volcanic clay particles are the specific culprit.

They occur in highland regions of Ethiopia, Rwanda, Uganda, Kenya, Cameroon, and Tanzania. Particles are typically less than 2 micrometers. They pass through the skin of the sole and enter the lymphatics. Chronic inflammation and years of exposure produce fibrosis and obstruction. The leg becomes firm rather than pitting. It does not respond to pressure as early edema does. There is no infectious agent, no mosquito, and no person-to-person transmission. A genetic susceptibility appears to influence who develops the disease with the same exposure.

Those features distinguish podoconiosis from filarial disease. Pain is common and walking becomes difficult. Lymphatic filariasis is caused by Wuchereria bancrofti, Brugia malayi, or Brugia timori. It is transmitted by mosquitoes and typically produces leg lymphedema and hydrocele in men. It is identified with a rapid antigen test or by finding microfilariae in blood. Podoconiosis occurs above about 1,000 meters where filarial vectors are less common. It has no hydrocele, spares the groin, and has a negative antigen test. The two conditions can coexist in the same region.

Young women are especially affected because the swelling is believed to reduce marriage prospects. Children may be withdrawn from school when a parent becomes disabled. The economic consequence extends to the next generation. Health education that names the true cause is therefore a clinical intervention. It must state that podoconiosis is not infectious and not a curse. It must show that the disease is preventable and manageable. Management is simple, inexpensive, and effective. It does not require surgery or long drug courses.

The foundation is daily foot hygiene with soap and clean water. Dry the feet carefully, especially between the toes. An emollient such as petrolatum keeps the skin supple and prevents cracking. Antifungal or antibacterial powder may be needed for macerated skin. Compression with elastic bandages or custom garments reduces swelling. Elevation of the leg when resting helps as well. Well-fitting shoes protect the feet from further soil exposure and injury. Treat acute attacks of cellulitis with antibiotics according to World Health Organization guidance and local protocols.

Attend to analgesia and rest. Surgery has no useful role in reversing established lymphedema. Protect patients from operations that promise a cure they cannot deliver. Prevention is a matter of footwear. Shoe distribution in endemic highlands, coupled with education, has been associated with reduced incidence in children. Protecting children's feet now prevents disability in middle age. A shoe that is uncomfortable or expensive will not be worn. Programs therefore work to make footwear affordable and culturally acceptable.

Integrating podoconiosis into campaigns that already distribute shoes is cost-effective. These five conditions were once addressed by separate programs. That separation was one reason each remained neglected. The current approach is integrated skin disease management. A single examination of the skin screens for several conditions at once. A community health worker can be trained to recognize several signs. These include a burrow, an embedded flea, a scaly plaque, an inturned eyelid, and a hard swollen leg. Most of these diseases can be identified in a short encounter.

Treatment can then be delivered together. Sometimes a single medicine serves, since azithromycin treats both trachoma and yaws. Integration reduces cost and reduces the number of visits. It reduces the chance that one condition is missed. Programs use simple algorithms and picture-based job aids. Community health workers and nurses can then classify common skin conditions. They decide what to treat locally and what to refer. A basic kit contains gloves, a bright light, a measuring tape, a magnifier, dressings, and a small formulary.

Ask permission before examining intimate areas and explain why it is necessary. Examine the whole skin surface in a logical order. Include the scalp, finger webs, nails, soles, spaces between the toes, and genital area. Ask about itching at night and household members with the same problem. Ask about barefoot walking, animals in the home, and previous treatments. Record what you actually see rather than what you expect. These diseases coexist, so one diagnosis does not exclude a second. Wound care is a large part of the nursing work here.

The principles are the same as anywhere else. Clean the wound with clean water or saline. Remove debris and dead tissue. Dress the wound with a material that keeps it moist and protected. Avoid macerating the surrounding skin. For embedded fleas and the lesions of yaws and tungiasis, remove the organism. Prevent bacterial infection and let the skin heal from below. Signs of spreading infection, increasing pain, fever, or foul discharge require assessment for antibiotics.

Referral may be needed. Offer pain control, because these lesions are often more painful than their size suggests. Untreated pain drives patients away from care. Verify and complete tetanus vaccination for any penetrating or contaminated lesion. Document each dressing, the wound appearance, and the pain score. Improvement or deterioration is then visible on the page. Reducing stigma is not a soft skill that follows the clinical work. In these diseases it often makes the clinical work possible.

The words you choose matter. Describe trachoma as an infection, scabies as a mite, tungiasis as a flea, and podoconiosis as a disease of soil and bare feet. Say plainly that none is a curse, a punishment, or a sign of uncleanliness. Counsel families together where possible. Treating a household as a unit improves cure and removes blame. Correct the myth of contagion for podoconiosis. Explain that yaws is not a sexually transmitted infection.

Both misunderstandings destroy lives. Support patients excluded from school or work with clear clinical letters. State the diagnosis and its management. Where culturally appropriate, involve community leaders and teachers. Community belief determines whether people come forward for treatment. Monitoring and recognition of adverse effects are core nursing responsibilities. Azithromycin for trachoma or yaws commonly causes nausea, abdominal discomfort, and loose stools. These effects are usually mild and brief.

Serious allergic reactions, including anaphylaxis, are rare but must be anticipated. Every treatment point should have a plan and the supplies to respond. Permethrin can cause local burning or stinging. Ivermectin can cause dizziness, headache, and, where Loa loa is present, a serious neurologic reaction. That requires immediate medical attention. Benzathine penicillin carries the risk of anaphylaxis. Take a penicillin allergy history and give the injection where resuscitation equipment is available. In mass treatment, a simple system for recording and reporting adverse events is essential.

The nurse usually notices the pattern first. Never dismiss a complaint as coincidence in a campaign. The logistics of these programs are unglamorous and decisive. Store azithromycin tablets and suspension dry and within expiry. The drug does not require a cold chain. It does require protection from heat and humidity. That is a real challenge in a tropical clinic. Dosing poles and scales must be available and calibrated.

Dosing by height and weight is accurate only if the tools are accurate. Community registers, treatment cards, and tally sheets must be printed, distributed, and collected. The numbers must reconcile with the medicines consumed. Schedule distribution around planting, market days, and travel. A campaign that asks people to stay home can cost income they cannot afford to lose. Agree referral pathways before the campaign begins. They must cover surgery, severe skin infection, and adverse events. Two numbers dominate success or failure.

The first is coverage, and the second is the retention of adverse event reports. Both are built by nurses who plan the practical details. They train and supervise the distributors and keep the records that prove what was done. Coverage is counted against the eligible population, and every adverse event report is reviewed rather than filed away.

To summarize, trachoma, yaws, scabies, tungiasis, and podoconiosis are neglected bacterial and skin diseases that disable and stigmatize long before they kill. Trachoma is repeated infection by Chlamydia trachomatis that scars the upper lid and drags lashes across the cornea, and it is controlled with surgery, azithromycin, facial cleanliness, and environmental improvement. Yaws is a childhood treponemal infection cured by a single dose of azithromycin, and its distinction from venereal syphilis and bejel rests on age, site, and epidemiology rather than serology alone. Scabies is a mite infestation treated with permethrin or ivermectin together with every household contact, and crusted scabies in an immunocompromised patient is a highly contagious trigger for institutional outbreaks. Tungiasis is an embedded sand flea causing pain and secondary infection, and podoconiosis is a non-infectious lymphedema of volcanic clay soil routinely mistaken for filariasis. Across all of them, your skin assessment, wound care, and accurate dosing matter, and so do your attention to adverse effects and your program logistics. The regimens in this chapter come from guidance that changes and differs between countries, so verify them against the current national protocol every time you prescribe.
