# Chapter 53 — Evacuation Planning and Communication

An evacuation is not an event that begins when the patient deteriorates. It is a system that either exists before that moment or does not exist at all, and the difference between the two is measured in hours of a patient's life. In a remote post the plan is part of the treatment, because the distance to surgery, to blood, to a working ventilator, and to a clinician who can pass a breathing tube is fixed by geography and cannot be negotiated at the bedside. This chapter treats evacuation as a designed system with named decision-makers, agreed thresholds, prepared equipment, tested routes, and disciplined communication, and it treats communication as the other half of that same system. The guidance in what follows follows the World Health Organization, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and the field manuals of Doctors Without Borders. Every threshold, dose, and interval below is current guidance rather than settled truth, and guidance is revised and differs between countries, so verify the current guideline and your own national protocol before you act on any of it.

The reason the plan must exist before the emergency is arithmetic. A patient in shock loses ground continuously, and the journey consumes time that cannot be recovered, so the hours spent waiting for a decision are hours of perfusion lost. When the crisis arrives, the clinician's attention is fully occupied by the airway, the bleeding, and the blood pressure, and there is no spare capacity to invent a route, find a driver, negotiate a price, or discover that the radio battery is flat. A plan made in advance is how a small team buys the one resource it cannot manufacture, which is time. A plan made in advance is also how disagreement is settled before it becomes a fight at the side of a dying patient.

The first element of the plan is a named decision-maker for every hour of the day and night. The clinic protocol should state who may authorize an evacuation, who may authorize it at night, and who may authorize it when the senior nurse is the only clinician on the post. In most remote settings the nurse initiates the process and a designated medical officer, clinical officer, or district health officer confirms it, but initiation must never wait for a confirmation that may take hours to reach the mountain. Write the escalation order on the wall, with names and telephone numbers, and update it the day anyone changes post. A plan that depends on one irreplaceable person is not a plan but a hope.

The criteria themselves are the heart of the system, and they are written as physiological triggers rather than as diagnoses. A trigger is a finding that the post cannot manage and that will probably kill the patient if it is not managed elsewhere, and it must be specific enough to recognize at three in the morning. Typical triggers include an airway that cannot be kept open, breathing that is failing despite oxygen, shock that persists after two boluses of fluid, a seizure that does not stop, a fever with neck stiffness and a falling level of consciousness, a hemoglobin so low that the heart is struggling, a hemorrhage that continues after the first measures, a ruptured uterus, a retained placenta with bleeding, eclampsia, a labor that has stopped progressing with signs of obstruction, a perforated viscus, an open fracture, a snake bite with spreading paralysis or coagulopathy, and any patient whose condition the post cannot monitor safely overnight. The list should be short enough that everyone remembers it and long enough that it covers the emergencies the post actually receives, which is a judgement that only local experience can make.

Alongside the triggers belongs a written statement of what stability means before departure, because the phrase is often used loosely. In this chapter it means that the airway is secure or can be managed, that breathing is supported as far as the post allows, that the circulation has been started on a plan the escort can continue, that glucose and temperature have been corrected, that the first dose of the drugs that must not wait has been given, and that the receiving facility has been told the patient is coming. It does not mean that the patient is well, and it never means that every abnormality has been reversed. Waiting for full stability in a facility that cannot create it is one of the commonest ways a remote evacuation fails.

Consent and authority must be settled in the same document, because the patient who is unconscious cannot sign and the family who is frightened may refuse. The protocol should state who may consent for a child, who may consent for an adult without capacity, and how a refusal by a competent adult is recorded, witnessed, and reviewed. Where an evacuation is clearly life-saving and consent cannot be obtained in time, the clinician acts in the patient's best interest and writes the reasoning in full, with the time and the names of those consulted. Every one of these decisions belongs in the record, not because a lawyer may read it, but because the next clinician needs to know what was decided and why.

The destination is chosen before the emergency, and it is chosen by capability rather than by distance. The nearest hospital may be a health center that cannot operate, cannot transfuse, and cannot ventilate, and sending a surgical patient there costs a day that the patient does not have. The protocol should name the facility for each category of emergency, the level of care it can provide, the route to reach it, the person to call before departure, and the alternative when the first choice is full or unreachable. Two or three destinations, each with a defined trigger, is usually enough. The decision is not the nurse's alone, but the nurse is almost always the person who must make it first.

Stabilization matters more than speed, and the reason is physiological. Movement is a stress on a patient whose reserves are already spent, and jolting, cold, pain, hunger, and fear all raise the work of the heart and the consumption of oxygen. A patient who is rushed to a vehicle with an unsecured airway, an empty circulation, and an untreated low blood sugar will often arrive worse than they left, or will not arrive at all. Ten minutes spent on the airway, on glucose, on warmth, on a splint, on a second intravenous line, and on a plan is usually recovered many times over on the road. The exception is a condition whose only treatment is the operating table, such as a ruptured uterus or an uncontrolled hemorrhage, and there the balance shifts toward movement as soon as the first life-saving measures are in place.

The airway and the breathing are prepared first, because they fail fastest. Suction, a bag and mask with a good seal, oral airways in several sizes, and oxygen with tubing that actually fits the cylinder are the minimum kit, and the escort must be able to use every piece of it alone. A patient who cannot protect the airway should travel in the lateral position unless a spinal injury forbids it, and a patient with a reduced level of consciousness should never travel unobserved on the back of a vehicle. Where the post has no oxygen, the honest answer is that the patient may need a facility closer than the ideal one, and that trade-off should be written into the protocol. These priorities follow the emergency and trauma guidance of the World Health Organization, and the local version of that guidance is the one to check before you rely on it.

The circulation is prepared next, and the preparation is as much organizational as medical. Two short, wide cannulas are better than one long, narrow one, because flow depends far more on the fourth power of the radius than on the length, and a child who cannot be cannulated needs an intraosseous needle placed without delay rather than a fifth attempt at a vein. Fluid is started in a volume the escort can repeat safely, and the plan for the next hour is written down rather than remembered. Blood, where it exists, is the treatment for hemorrhagic shock that fluid cannot replace, and the referral must say so plainly when the receiving facility is expected to supply it. Over-resuscitation is its own danger, because a patient with a leaking heart or a malnourished child can be pushed into pulmonary edema by the very fluid meant to save them, so the target is a patient who is warm, alert enough to be reassured, and passing urine, not a patient whose blood pressure has been driven to a normal number at any cost.

The drugs are drawn up and labeled before departure, because a syringe prepared on a jolting road is a syringe prepared badly. The pre-referral treatment of severe malaria in a child is a single rectal dose of artesunate at 10 milligrams per kilogram, given when a reliable injection cannot be given promptly, and this is the World Health Organization recommendation for pre-referral care. For suspected severe bacterial infection the pre-referral antibiotic is ceftriaxone at 100 milligrams per kilogram given intravenously or intramuscularly, and a convulsing patient may be given diazepam at 0.5 milligrams per kilogram rectally when there is no intravenous access. Hypoglycemia accompanies severe malaria, malnutrition, and sepsis, so carry glucose at 10 per cent and be ready to give it intravenously at 5 milliliters per kilogram to a child. For eclampsia and severe pre-eclampsia the drug is magnesium sulfate, given intravenously as 4 grams over 20 minutes and then by infusion, with the respiratory rate and the tendon reflexes watched closely for toxicity. Every one of these regimens is revised from time to time and differs between national programs, so verify the current guideline and the current national protocol before you prescribe or administer any of them.

Adverse effects are part of the transport plan and not an afterthought, because the road removes the safety net that a ward provides. Diazepam can depress breathing, especially in a child who has already received another sedative, so the escort must watch the chest and be ready to support ventilation. Magnesium can cause loss of the tendon reflexes, respiratory depression, and cardiac arrest, and its antidote, calcium gluconate, should be in the bag whenever magnesium is. Ceftriaxone can cause an allergic reaction, and a patient with a known severe allergy needs a different antibiotic chosen in advance. Fluids can overload a malnourished child or a patient in heart failure, and glucose given too fast or in too strong a concentration can injure a small vein. Write these possibilities on the escorts' instruction sheet, in plain language, with the action to take for each.

Monitoring during transport is what turns a journey into a continuation of care rather than a gap in it. The escort should record the level of consciousness, the breathing rate, the pulse, the blood pressure where a cuff and a working stethoscope are available, the temperature, the urine output if a catheter is in place, and the pain, at intervals set by the patient's condition and never longer than every thirty minutes in a seriously ill patient. Oxygen saturation measured with a finger probe is valuable when the probe is reliable and the battery is charged, but a cold hand or poor perfusion will lie, so it never replaces the clinical examination. The record is kept on a clipboard that travels with the patient and is handed to the receiving clinician, because the pattern of change matters more than any single number. A deterioration that is written down becomes a decision; a deterioration that is only remembered becomes a story.

The escort is chosen with the same care as the destination. Where a nurse can travel, a nurse travels, and where no nurse can be spared, a trained health worker or a relative is prepared for the role in advance rather than recruited at the roadside. The escort must know the patient's diagnosis, the drugs already given and their times, the plan for the next dose, the position the patient should travel in, the signs that mean deterioration, and the exact words to use on the radio. The person who stays with the patient is also the person who holds the hand, and that continuity matters to the patient in a way that no monitor can measure. Never send a seriously ill patient alone.

The vehicle options each trade speed against capacity, and the choice is made by condition rather than by preference. A four-wheel drive vehicle is the default in most remote programs, a motorcycle with a trailer reaches places no car can, a boat is the only road where the road is the river, and an aircraft is the fastest and least available of all. The plan names the first choice, the second choice, and the conditions under which each is used. What matters is not the vehicle in the clinic yard, but the vehicle that will actually be there at two in the morning, with fuel, with a driver, and with its lights working.

The four-wheel drive vehicle is the workhorse, and its value lies in what has been done to it before the emergency. A mattress or a foam pad, straps or a canvas cot fixed to the floor, a bracket for the oxygen cylinder, a torch that is clipped in place, a container for sharps, a bucket, a blanket, tarpaulin against rain, and a spare wheel with a working jack are the difference between a vehicle and an ambulance. The driver should be a named member of staff with a second driver named as backup, and both should be reachable by radio or telephone. Fuel discipline is part of clinical safety, so the tank is filled at the end of every day and not when the emergency begins. Spare parts, a working pump, and a mechanic who can be found at night belong in the plan, because a breakdown two hours from the destination is a clinical event.

The motorcycle with a trailer is the vehicle of last resort and the only vehicle on many footpaths. It can carry a patient who can sit or lie on a padded platform, and it can cross a bridge, a narrow track, or a hillside that no car will manage. Its costs are real: the patient is exposed to rain, dust, and cold, the ride is rough, and the escort has little room to work. The patient should be strapped in securely, wrapped against hypothermia, shielded from the rear wheel, and positioned so that the airway is not compressed by the angle of the trailer. Helmets for the driver and the escort are not negotiable, and a slow, deliberate ride beats a fast, spectacular one.

The boat is the ambulance of the river, and it carries its own set of dangers. A patient on a stretcher must be secured to the hull or the deck, never held by hand, and the weight must be distributed so that the boat does not list. Rain, spray, sun, engine noise, and the smell of fuel are all part of the journey, and the patient should be covered, shaded, and positioned with the head slightly raised unless shock requires otherwise. Life jackets for the patient and the escort, a bailer, a spare fuel line, and a torch are minimum equipment, and no crossing should be made at night on an unlit river without local knowledge. The river gauge, the current, and the weather are read before departure, not after.

The aircraft exists mostly in planning documents, and the nurse should treat it as a resource that must be summoned long before it is needed. Air evacuation requires an airstrip or a landing zone within reach of the patient, a weight limit that includes the escort and the equipment, a certified pilot, fuel, daylight or night capability, and an authorization that usually comes from a ministry, an insurer, or a humanitarian organization rather than from the clinic. The request must be made early, because the aircraft may be hours away and the weather may close the strip. The written plan should name the requesting authority, the radio frequency or telephone number, the information required for the flight, and the landing zone with its measurements and its obstacles. Even where the aircraft never comes, knowing the procedure prevents the paralysis that comes from assuming there is no answer.

The journey itself is where planning becomes skill, and positioning is the first skill. A patient in shock travels lying flat with the legs raised, unless breathing difficulty or heart failure forbids it, and a breathless patient travels sitting up as far as the stretcher allows. A pregnant woman beyond the middle of pregnancy should lie on her left side, or have the uterus displaced to the left by hand, because the weight of the uterus on the great vein can drop the blood pressure dramatically. A patient who is vomiting or unconscious travels on the side, and a patient with a head injury travels with the head of the stretcher slightly raised and the neck supported. Every transfer from bed to stretcher, from stretcher to vehicle, and from vehicle to trolley is a chance to lose an airway, a drip, or a fracture alignment, so the moves are planned aloud and counted before they begin.

Comfort is not decoration; it is treatment. Pain raises the heart rate, the blood pressure, and the demand for oxygen, and it makes a frightened patient fight the very support meant to help. Paracetamol at 15 milligrams per kilogram by mouth every six hours is safe for most patients and is often enough, and an opiate may be needed for severe pain but should be given with the same attention to breathing that any sedative requires, and its dose verified against the current guideline before it is drawn up. Warmth preserves clotting and comfort, so the patient is covered and the vehicle is not ventilated to the point of cold. Hearing is the last sense to leave and probably the last to be comforted, so the escort should keep speaking to the patient, explaining each step, in the patient's own language, at a normal volume. If the patient is conscious, ask before you move, and let them know when the worst of the bumps is coming.

The deterioration plan is prepared before the vehicle leaves, because a decision made at speed on a dark road is a decision made badly. The plan states what will be done if the breathing worsens, if the bleeding restarts, if a seizure begins, if the patient becomes unrousable, or if the vehicle breaks down, and it states the point at which the escort should stop and use an intermediate facility rather than press on. The escort must have the authority to stop, to give the next dose already prepared, and to call for advice, and must have the numbers to call. Turning back is sometimes the right decision, and a plan that treats it as failure will produce worse outcomes than one that names the conditions for it.

Documentation travels with the patient and is the nurse's clearest legal and clinical protection. The referral note should carry the patient's name and age or estimated age, the working diagnosis, the relevant history, the examination findings, the drugs given with their doses and times, the fluids given, the response, the allergies, the results of any test performed, the time of departure, the name of the escort, and the telephone number of the post. A copy stays in the clinic register, because the day may come when the outcome must be reviewed and the post will otherwise have no record at all. The radio log with its times belongs in the same register, and so does the name of the person who authorized the transfer. Write the note once, legibly, and hand it over rather than reciting it from memory.

Roads and seasons decide the route, and the route decides the prognosis. The same journey that takes two hours in the dry season can take a day in the rains, when a ford becomes a torrent, a track becomes a swamp, and a bridge may have gone. The clinic should keep a seasonal map of its routes, with the dry-season road, the wet-season road, the crossing points, the alternative tracks, and the times each one takes in each season. The river crossing in the rainy season is the real discharge planning, because a patient who is safe in the clinic today may be unreachable on the day the emergency comes. Ask the drivers and the traders, who know the water better than any map, and revise the notes whenever a route changes.

Route reconnaissance is the unglamorous work that makes the emergency simple. Someone from the post should drive, ride, or walk each route at least once, record the time it takes, note the fuel and the water, mark the places where a vehicle can turn around, identify the checkpoints and the villages that can offer help, and find the mobile telephone positions and the radio relay points along the way. The receiving hospital should be visited, not merely telephoned, and the visit should confirm the entrance that is open at night, the ward that receives referrals, the blood bank, and the name of the person to ask for. None of this is clinical work, and all of it determines whether clinical work succeeds.

Radio communication is the spine of the system, and high frequency radio remains the only reliable tool across long distances in terrain where telephone coverage comes and goes. The post needs a licensed set, a charged battery, a spare battery, an antenna that has been tested, an agreed frequency, and a daily schedule for listening. The discipline is the part that saves lives: the radio is switched on at the appointed hour, the operator answers, and silence is treated as an event rather than as convenience. Every message should be brief, spoken slowly, repeated back for confirmation, and written in the log with the time. A radio that no one listens to is worse than no radio, because it creates false confidence.

Satellite communication extends the system beyond the reach of the relay and the mast, and it needs the same discipline. A satellite telephone or a satellite messaging device should be kept charged, credited, and stored where it can be found in the dark, with a written procedure for its use and a spare battery or power source. The device should be tested on a schedule, because the failure is usually discovered at the moment of need: a dead battery, an expired subscription, or an antenna that has never been pointed at the sky. A short text message sent through the satellite device is often easier to deliver than a voice call and should carry the essential facts in the agreed order. The clinic that relies on a single channel has no communication plan at all.

The scheduled check-in is the mechanism that turns silence into action. Every vehicle, every boat, and every escort leaves with a departure time, an expected arrival time, and a check-in time, and the post records them. If the check-in does not arrive within a defined margin, the post begins a defined sequence: call the radio, call the satellite device, call the receiving hospital, call the driver's other number, and then alert the agreed search or rescue contact. Everyone on the team must know that sequence before departure, and the person who stays behind must have the authority to start it. The plan is judged not by how it works when nothing goes wrong but by what happens when the check-in fails.

The discipline of protocols is what keeps communication reliable under stress. Messages follow an agreed order: who is speaking, where they are, what the patient's problem is, what has been done, what is needed, and what the caller can do while waiting. The operator confirms receipt of every critical message and repeats numbers and drug doses back. The protocol names the language and the level of detail, because a clinician who has never met the patient needs the facts in a predictable sequence. Write the message format on a card and keep it beside the radio, and rehearse it until it is automatic. Under pressure, people do not rise to the occasion; they fall to the level of their training.

The medical call that must reach a doctor is a specific skill, and it must be planned to survive a failed first attempt. The protocol should name the on-call clinician, the backup, the hours of availability, the number for each, and the escalation path when a call is not answered within a set time. The caller should have the written case summary in hand, the specific question stated in one sentence, and a pencil ready to record the answer. The advice received must be written in the patient's chart with the time and the name of the adviser, and the loop must be closed by reporting back what was done and what happened. Where no doctor can be reached, the senior nurse acts on the standing protocol and records that no advice was available, which is an honest record and a useful audit finding.

The agreements with the nearest hospital are written before they are needed, and a written agreement is worth more than a good intention. The agreement should name the facility, the clinical referral pathway, the person who accepts referrals at each hour, the beds and services that are guaranteed, the blood supply, the surgical and obstetric cover, the feedback that will be sent back to the referring post, and the process for settling costs. A copy should hang on the clinic wall, and both sides should review it once a year. The purpose is not to create bureaucracy but to remove the argument from the moment of crisis.

The relationships behind the agreement are what make it function in practice. The surgeon who recognizes your voice on the telephone will find a bed; the same surgeon who has never heard of your clinic may not. Visit the receiving team, learn their names, ask what makes a referral easy for them, and make it easy: call ahead, prepare the note, send the relevant results, and follow up with a short report on what happened to the patient. Thank people specifically, and take criticism seriously, because the reputation of a remote post is built one referral at a time. A relationship that is cultivated in the quiet months is what answers the telephone at two in the morning.

Costs are discussed in advance, with the family and with the employer, and the conversation must be honest. The fuel, the driver, the boat, the aircraft, the hospital fee, the blood, the operation, and the food for the escort may each fall on someone, and it is usually the family who is asked to pay at the worst possible moment. The clinic should know in advance what the program covers, what the family must find, whether a waiver or a loan fund exists, and who can authorize it quickly. A family that must sell a goat or a season's harvest to move a child is a family that may delay the decision until it is too late. The ethical position is clear: no patient should be bankrupted by the transport that saves them, and where the system falls short, the nurse should say so plainly and record it as a systems failure rather than a private misfortune.

The family travels with their own fears and must be part of the plan rather than an obstacle to it. Explain where the patient is going, why, how long it should take, who is allowed to travel, what to bring, what will happen on arrival, and when there will be news. If a relative is to be the escort, that person needs the same preparation as a health worker, including the instruction sheet, the drugs, and the numbers to call. If the patient is a child, the mother or the usual caregiver should travel whenever possible, because a child who is held settles and a child who is alone does not. When the outcome is likely to be death, say what can be said with honesty and without cruelty, and let the family be present if they wish to be.

Special populations need adjustments that are easy to forget. A pregnant woman in the last months of pregnancy should be positioned on her left side, monitored for bleeding and for the onset of labor, and accompanied by someone prepared for a delivery on the road. A newborn needs warmth above all, with skin-to-skin contact, a hat, and a covered transport cot, and needs glucose and feeding continued. A child with severe acute malnutrition is at risk of hypothermia and of fluid overload, so the fluid plan and the warming plan are written before departure. An older patient on treatment for a chronic disease may need the regular medicines carried and the doses continued, and a patient with a mental illness may need a calm, familiar person beside them. The escort should know these differences, because a plan that treats every patient identically fails the ones who are most fragile.

Security on the road is part of the clinical plan, and the nurse should not pretend otherwise. Night journeys, checkpoints, unlit tracks, and the risk of theft or harassment all belong in the assessment, and the program should state whether escorts travel armed, whether a second vehicle accompanies, which routes are considered safe after dark, and what to do at a checkpoint. The escort should carry identification, a letter explaining the medical purpose, and enough fuel and money to reach the destination and return. Where travel at night is considered unsafe, the plan says so, and the alternative is agreed in advance rather than improvised by a frightened driver. The safest clinical decision is worthless if the team does not arrive.

Training and drills are what convert a written plan into a working one. The team should rehearse an evacuation at least twice a year, with the bag packed, the radio on, the vehicle loaded, and the timeline recorded, and the drill should include the awkward parts: the failed call, the flat battery, the blocked road, the patient who deteriorates halfway. The equipment must be checked on a fixed schedule, because expired drugs, a perished oxygen seal, a dead torch, and a radio on the wrong frequency are all found in a drill rather than at the bedside. The nurse is usually the person who owns this check, and the sign-off should be dated and kept. Preparedness is a habit maintained by ordinary people on ordinary days.

The debrief after every evacuation is how the system learns. Within a few days, the team should sit down and walk through what happened: the time the problem was recognized, the time the decision was made, the time the vehicle left, the time of arrival, the drugs given, the complications, the communication that worked, and the communication that failed. The family and, where possible, the receiving clinician should contribute, and the lessons should be written into the protocol rather than trusted to memory. Adverse events, near misses, and delays belong in the same review, reported without blame and without the fear that reporting is a confession. A system that reviews itself improves; a system that only endures does not.

The legal and professional record completes the picture, because distance does not dissolve responsibility. The register should hold the referral, the consent, the refusal where it occurred, the drug administration, the radio log, the names of those involved, and the outcome on return. Where the patient dies, the record should show what was done, what was advised, and what was not available, written factually and without self-justification. The nurse's scope of practice, the standing orders that authorize treatment before referral, and the delegation to health workers should be documented and current, and should be checked against the national regulations before they are relied on. The record protects the patient first and the clinician second, and it is written with the same care as the treatment itself.

To summarize, an evacuation is a system built in calm weather for use in a storm, and its parts are a named decision-maker, written triggers, a stabilization standard, prepared drugs, tested routes, defined vehicles, disciplined radio and satellite communication, scheduled check-ins, a written agreement with a receiving hospital, an honest plan for costs, and a family that is included rather than managed. It is also a system that is rehearsed, audited, and corrected, because a plan that has never been tested is a document rather than a capability. The nurse who plans these things in advance is the nurse who, when the emergency comes, has time to think about the patient instead of the paperwork, the driver, the fuel, and the battery. Stabilize before you travel, monitor on the road, hand over in writing, and verify every dose against the current guideline before you give it, because guidance changes and a plan is only as good as its last review.
