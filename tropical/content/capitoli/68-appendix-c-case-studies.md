# Appendix C — Case Studies with Nursing Management Plans

This appendix turns the substance of the earlier chapters into ten composite cases, each built from patterns that advanced practice nurses meet in tropical settings rather than from any single person's record. Every case here is a composite, no case describes a real patient, and no identifying detail belongs to anyone living or dead. Each case follows the same shape, moving from a short narrative to the nursing management, then to the priority nursing problem, the monitoring that matters, and the point at which the nurse must escalate. Doses appear throughout, because a tropical medicine course without them is useless, but every dose is spoken as current guidance and never as settled truth. Regimens are revised, national programs adapt them differently, and the current guideline must be checked before anything is prescribed, so treat each number as a prompt to verify rather than as a substitute for the source.

Before the first case, fix the habits that govern all of them. Confirm the patient's weight in kilograms, because most pediatric doses are calculated per kilogram and a weight guessed from age is a weight guessed wrong. Write the calculation down, have a second nurse repeat it independently, and compare the result with the current guideline rather than with memory. Check that the unit on the label matches the unit you intended, because a concentrated solution can turn a correct number into a tenfold error. Recheck kidney and liver function when it is available, since failing organs change the interval as much as the dose. Logistics belong to the plan as much as the drug does, so each case names what the clinic must actually stock, who can transport a deteriorating patient, whether screened blood is available, and whether the family can return for follow up. The World Health Organization, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control publish revisions on different schedules, and the national program where you work usually has the last word.

A 3 year old child arrives at a district clinic with 3 days of fever, repeated vomiting, and a convulsion seen at home. The child is drowsy, does not localize pain, breathes deeply, and has cool hands with slow capillary refill. The family returned 2 weeks ago from a region where malaria is transmitted all year, and the blood film shows Plasmodium falciparum, the species responsible for the most severe form of malaria, at high density. The bedside glucose is dangerously low, so this is severe malaria with hypoglycemia and acidosis, and the risk is greatest in the first hour.

Give 5 milliliters per kilogram of 10 per cent dextrose intravenously at once, then run a continuous infusion and recheck the glucose after 30 minutes, because one bolus is often not enough in a child whose parasites consume glucose. Current guidance from the World Health Organization lists intravenous artesunate as the treatment of choice, at 3 milligrams per kilogram per dose below 20 kilograms and 2.4 milligrams per kilogram per dose above that weight, given at the start, at 12 hours, at 24 hours, and then daily. Verify that dose against the national guideline and have a second nurse repeat the calculation independently, because a reconstituted powder invites a dilution error. Intramuscular artemether and intravenous quinine are the listed alternatives, and their regimens must be read at the time rather than recalled. Continue the drug intravenously until the child can swallow, then finish a full course of artemisinin-based combination therapy.

The priority nursing problem is impaired glucose regulation with impaired consciousness, and the monitoring that matters is glucose, breathing pattern, urine output, and level of consciousness. Measure glucose every 30 minutes until it is stable and then every hour, and record deep breathing as a sign of acidosis rather than as anxiety. Inspect the intravenous site often, because extravasation is easy to miss on a dark limb. Escalate at once for another convulsion, a glucose that falls again after a bolus, deepening breathing, or no urine within 6 hours, and arrange referral for transfusion if pallor deepens or the hemoglobin falls below the threshold in the national guideline.

A 34 year old man attends a travel clinic 10 days after returning from 3 weeks in West Africa with fever, headache, and muscle aches and no localizing symptom. He took no chemoprophylaxis and recalls mosquito bites despite using repellent. His temperature is 38.9 degrees Celsius and his pulse is 104 beats per minute, and the examination is unremarkable apart from mild dehydration. Two thick and thin blood films and a rapid diagnostic test are reported as negative for malaria parasites. He asks whether he can go home with paracetamol and return if he feels worse.

A single negative film does not exclude malaria, because the parasite density can be low, the reading can fail, and some parasites no longer make the protein that a rapid diagnostic test detects. Current guidance from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention asks that films be repeated every 12 to 24 hours for a total of 3 sets over 48 to 72 hours before malaria is set aside, and that severe illness still be examined by microscopy after a negative rapid test. The differential includes dengue, typhoid, leptospirosis, rickettsial infection, acute HIV, and hepatitis, and blood cultures should be drawn before any antibiotic is given. Avoid empirical antibiotics in a well patient with fever, because they blur the diagnosis and select for resistance. Give fluids by mouth, treat the fever with paracetamol, and record the full exposure and sexual history.

The priority nursing problem is risk of missed or delayed diagnosis, and the monitoring that matters is the temperature curve, the pulse pressure, the urine output, and any new rash, bleeding, confusion, or breathlessness. Explain the plan in plain words and give it in writing, because a traveler who feels well may not return. Make the appointment for the second film before he leaves and record a telephone number for the family. Escalate immediately for confusion, bleeding gums, dark urine, a falling platelet count, or shock, and arrange admission rather than another outpatient visit. Fever in a returning traveler is malaria until repeated films prove otherwise.

A 27 year old woman presents on the fifth day of illness with fever that has just broken, severe abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, and a rash over the trunk and limbs. Her platelet count has fallen from 140,000 to 60,000 per microliter over 2 days, and her hematocrit has risen by 18 per cent above her baseline. She is restless, her hands and feet are cool, and her pulse pressure has narrowed. There is no bleeding and no breathlessness yet. This is dengue entering the critical phase, which usually begins as the temperature falls and lasts 24 to 48 hours.

The critical phase is a problem of leaky capillaries, so plasma escapes into the chest, the abdomen, and the tissues while the blood cells stay behind, and a rising hematocrit with a falling platelet count is the signature. Current guidance from the World Health Organization uses crystalloid at 5 to 10 milliliters per kilogram per hour for the first 24 to 48 hours in a patient who can still drink, adjusted by the hematocrit and the urine output rather than by the platelet count alone. Give paracetamol for pain, never give nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs or aspirin, and never give an intramuscular injection when the platelets are low. Monitor the hematocrit, the vital signs, the pulse pressure, the urine output, and the signs of fluid overload every hour. Fluid algorithms differ between the World Health Organization, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and national programs, so verify the volume and the rate against the guideline in use.

The priority nursing problem is risk of shock from plasma leakage with possible bleeding, and the narrowest monitoring is the pulse pressure, the hematocrit, and the urine output. Measure the hematocrit every 4 to 6 hours during the critical phase and keep an accurate fluid balance. Watch for restlessness, cold extremities, and a falling pulse pressure, which are the earliest signs of compensated shock. Escalate at once for shock, for a hematocrit that falls with bleeding, for a hematocrit that stays high with a fast pulse, or for breathlessness with a rising fluid level. Recovery begins with reabsorption, so expect a falling hematocrit with a good urine output and reduce the fluid rather than continue it.

A 41 year old farmer is brought to a rural health center 2 hours after a bite on the ankle from a snake he describes as brown with a triangular head. The ankle is swollen and bleeding from the fang marks, and he has vomited twice. A bedside whole blood clotting test shows that the blood has not clotted after 20 minutes. His blood pressure is 96 over 60 millimeters of mercury and his pulse is 118 beats per minute. This is a viper bite with coagulopathy, and the systemic effects may worsen over the next several hours.

The coagulopathy comes from venom that consumes clotting factors, so the blood cannot clot and the patient bleeds from the wound, the gums, the gut, and the intravenous sites. Current guidance from the World Health Organization describes antivenom as the only treatment that reverses this, and the initial dose varies by region and by species, so it must be read from the national guideline and never recalled from memory. Give the antivenom intravenously over about 1 hour, diluted as the product directs, with epinephrine and airway equipment at the bedside, because anaphylaxis is common and a skin test does not predict it. Verify the product and the dose against the guideline with a second nurse, and record the batch number and the time. Avoid incision, suction, tourniquets, and ice, and never give the antivenom intramuscularly.

The priority nursing problem is risk of hemorrhage and of anaphylaxis, and the monitoring that matters is the wound, the gums and venipuncture sites, the vital signs, the urine output, and the clotting test repeated every 6 hours. Keep the limb still and at the level of the heart, and avoid intramuscular injections for tetanus prevention and for analgesia. Treat pain with paracetamol, because nonsteroidal drugs add to the bleeding risk. Escalate for continued bleeding, a clotting time that stays prolonged, a falling blood pressure, breathlessness, or swelling that spreads up the limb within hours, and arrange transfer to a facility that stocks antivenom and screened blood.

A 24 year old woman who is 22 weeks pregnant presents with 3 days of fever, chills, and headache, and a blood film shows Plasmodium falciparum, the parasite of severe malaria, at a moderate density with no criterion of severity. She is alert, she is not vomiting, and her fetus is moving normally. She is anemic, with a hemoglobin of 8.4 grams per deciliter, and she has had no antenatal care until now. She lives in a district where malaria is transmitted throughout the year. This is uncomplicated falciparum malaria in the second trimester, and the risk is that placental infection will cause anemia, poor fetal growth, and preterm delivery.

Current guidance from the World Health Organization treats uncomplicated falciparum malaria in the second trimester with an artemisinin-based combination therapy given by mouth for 3 days, and artemether with lumefantrine is the combination most widely used, taken twice a day with food that contains fat. Verify the exact product and the weight band against the national guideline, have a second nurse check the tablet count, and observe the first dose so that vomiting is recognized and the dose repeated. Add folic acid and iron for the anemia, treat the fever with paracetamol, and recheck the hemoglobin after treatment. If any criterion of severity appears, change to intravenous artesunate at once, because parenteral artesunate is recommended in every trimester of severe disease. Never give primaquine or tafenoquine during pregnancy.

The priority nursing problem is risk of maternal anemia and fetal compromise, and the monitoring that matters is the temperature, the hemoglobin, the fetal heart rate, and the signs of preterm labor. Count fetal movements and listen to the fetal heart at every visit, and record any uterine activity. Escalate for vomiting that prevents oral treatment, a falling hemoglobin, vaginal bleeding, contractions, reduced fetal movement, or any criterion of severe malaria, and involve the maternity team early. Arrange follow up films and a repeat hemoglobin, and give an insecticide-treated bed net with instruction on its use.

A 36 year old man with HIV and a helper T cell count of 60 cells per microliter begins treatment for pulmonary tuberculosis, and antiretroviral therapy is added 2 weeks later. He improves at first, then in the third week he develops high fever, painful swollen lymph nodes in the neck, and increasing breathlessness. His chest radiograph shows worsening shadowing, and his sputum is again negative for acid-fast bacilli. He is alert and his blood pressure is stable. This deterioration during immune recovery is the pattern of the immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome rather than of treatment failure.

The syndrome arises when recovering immunity recognizes a heavy load of mycobacterial antigen and mounts an exaggerated inflammatory response, so the patient worsens as the immune system improves. Current guidance from the World Health Organization and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention is to continue both treatments and to treat the inflammation rather than to stop the drugs, and corticosteroids are used for severe paradoxical reactions under a defined regimen and taper. Verify the dose and the taper against the current guideline, and exclude an alternative explanation such as drug resistance, a new infection, or a drug reaction before accepting the diagnosis. Monitor the liver enzymes, because tuberculosis drugs and antiretroviral drugs both stress the liver, and watch for a rash from the antiretroviral drug. Never stop antiretroviral therapy without senior advice.

The priority nursing problem is impaired gas exchange with risk of airway obstruction from the swollen nodes, and the monitoring that matters is the respiratory rate, the oxygen saturation, the temperature, the size of the nodes, and the liver enzymes. Position the patient upright, give oxygen, and record the exact time of every dose so that adherence can be assessed. Watch for stridor, difficulty swallowing, or a rapidly enlarging node, because these signal compression of the airway. Escalate for worsening breathlessness, a falling oxygen saturation, confusion, jaundice, or a rash with fever, and involve the tuberculosis program and the HIV team together rather than separately.

A 58 year old man who grew up in a rural area of Southeast Asia is about to start high dose corticosteroids for an autoimmune condition. He has had intermittent abdominal bloating and episodic itchy skin for years but feels well otherwise. Screening before treatment includes a stool examination and a blood test for Strongyloides stercoralis, the threadworm that can persist for decades, and the blood test is positive. He has no fever, no diarrhea, and no breathlessness at present. This is chronic strongyloidiasis, and the danger is that corticosteroids will trigger overwhelming infection.

The worm has a cycle that reinfects the same host, so a person can carry it silently for decades, and corticosteroids suppress the immune control that keeps the cycle contained, which allows massive multiplication and dissemination to the gut, the lungs, and the bloodstream. Treat before the corticosteroids begin, and if those drugs are already under way, treat at the same time rather than waiting. Current guidance from the World Health Organization lists ivermectin at 200 micrograms per kilogram by mouth once a day for 1 to 2 days for uncomplicated infection, with a longer course for disseminated disease, and albendazole at 400 milligrams by mouth twice a day for 7 days as an alternative. Verify the weight, the dose, and the duration against the national guideline and have a second nurse check the calculation. Confirm cure by repeated stool tests, because a single negative test does not prove that the infection has cleared.

The priority nursing problem is risk of disseminated infection once immunosuppression begins, and the monitoring that matters is the temperature, the respiratory rate, the stool pattern, the skin, and the white cell count. Teach the patient to report fever, cough, breathlessness, abdominal pain, or a new rash at once rather than at the next appointment. Record the date that treatment started and the date that corticosteroids are due to start, and tell the prescribing clinician if the order is reversed. Escalate for fever with breathlessness, a rash spreading over the trunk, abdominal pain with distension, or confusion, because disseminated strongyloidiasis is a medical emergency with a high death rate when it is missed.

A 45 year old woman who migrated 12 years ago from a rural region of Bolivia is told that her blood donation tested positive for Trypanosoma cruzi, the parasite that causes Chagas disease. She feels well, works full time, and has no chest pain, palpitations, or difficulty swallowing. On examination she has a normal pulse and no edema. She is frightened by the result and asks whether she is infectious to her family. This is chronic Chagas disease detected in the indeterminate phase, and the priority is confirmation and a cardiac assessment.

The infection is transmitted by triatomine bugs in the Americas, and it can also pass from mother to child, in blood products, and in an organ transplant, but it is not spread by casual contact, so sharing a home does not put her family at risk. Confirm the result with a second and different test, because a single positive donation test is not a diagnosis. Current guidance from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the World Health Organization lists benznidazole at 5 to 7 milligrams per kilogram per day by mouth in 2 divided doses for 60 days, and nifurtimox at 8 to 10 milligrams per kilogram per day in 3 or 4 divided doses for 60 days, and the choice depends on availability, age, and pregnancy. Verify the dose and the duration against the current national guideline and have a second nurse check the calculation, because the course is long and side effects are common. Warn her that treatment often causes rash, nausea, and tingling in the hands and feet, and that it is not given during pregnancy.

The priority nursing problem is lack of knowledge about a chronic infection that carries a cardiac risk, and the monitoring that matters is the heart rhythm, the symptoms of heart failure, and the swallowing pattern. Obtain an electrocardiogram and arrange a cardiac evaluation, because a conduction abnormality may appear years after infection. Screen her children for the infection, since mother to child transmission is possible, and arrange testing for anyone in the family who may donate blood. Escalate for fainting, palpitations, chest pain, breathlessness on exertion, or difficulty swallowing, and refer to a cardiologist or a gastroenterologist as the local service allows.

A 30 year old man presents with a large pale patch on his forearm that has been numb for 6 months, and his ulnar nerve is thickened and tender. He has begun to lose strength in his hand and cannot grip a cup firmly. This is leprosy, also called Hansen disease, and the tender nerve indicates a type 1 reaction, in which the immune system attacks the nerve itself. The reaction is an emergency for the nerve, because the damage can become permanent within days. He has no other illness and takes no regular medication.

The nerve is the organ at risk, so treatment aims to stop the immune attack while continuing the antibacterial drugs. Current guidance from the World Health Organization treats multibacillary leprosy with rifampicin, dapsone, and clofazimine, and treats a nerve reaction with prednisolone at 40 to 60 milligrams by mouth each day, tapered slowly over 12 to 20 weeks. Verify the dose and the taper against the national guideline and have a second nurse check the tablet count, because the taper is long and a missed step causes relapse. Teach the patient that the antibacterial drugs may darken the skin, that urine and sweat may turn red, and that clofazimine causes a harmless change in skin color. Give analgesia for nerve pain, avoid drugs that are toxic to nerves when an alternative exists, and report the case to the national leprosy program.

The priority nursing problem is risk of permanent nerve damage and disability, and the monitoring that matters is the strength of the hand, the sensation in the patch, the size and tenderness of the nerve, and the closure of the eye. Test grip strength and sensation at every visit and record the findings on a simple drawing that the next nurse can read. Teach the patient to protect the numb hand from burns and cuts and to inspect the feet every day. Escalate for worsening weakness, a new patch, increased nerve pain, or a foot ulcer, because these signal an uncontrolled reaction that needs a change in treatment. Address the stigma directly, because fear of the diagnosis delays care more than the drugs do.

A 4 year old child is brought to a treatment center during an outbreak with profuse watery diarrhea and vomiting for 12 hours. The child is lethargic, the eyes are sunken, the skin pinch returns slowly, and there has been no urine output. The pulse is fast and weak and the extremities are cold. A stool specimen is sent for culture, but the picture is severe dehydration from cholera, caused by the bacterium Vibrio cholerae, and treatment must begin before the result returns. The outbreak has already affected several households that share the same water source.

Severe dehydration is corrected with fluid given intravenously, and current guidance from the World Health Organization gives 100 milliliters per kilogram over 3 hours for a child, with the first 30 milliliters per kilogram given in the first hour and the remainder over the following 2 hours. Verify the volume and the rate against the national guideline and against the child's weight, and check the calculation with a second nurse, because both the total and the speed matter. Give oral rehydration salts as soon as the child can drink, and continue breastfeeding if the child is still nursing. Add zinc at 20 milligrams by mouth each day for 10 days, or 10 milligrams each day for an infant below 6 months, and give a single dose of azithromycin at 20 milligrams per kilogram by mouth where the guideline recommends an antibiotic. Record every stool and every vomit, and note the time of each.

The priority nursing problem is deficient fluid volume with risk of shock, and the monitoring that matters is the pulse, the capillary refill, the skin pinch, the urine output, and the number of stools. Weigh the child on arrival and again after rehydration, and use the change as a measure of the fluid given. Watch for overhydration as well, because a fast infusion in a small child can cause breathlessness. Escalate for a weak pulse, no urine after the first hour, continuing profuse diarrhea, drowsiness, or difficulty drinking, and notify the outbreak team so that the water source can be investigated. Teach the family to prepare oral rehydration salts with clean water and to bring the child back at once if the diarrhea returns.

To summarize, these ten composite cases repeat the same lessons in different bodies, and the repetition is deliberate. A negative test never replaces a clinical assessment, a dose is never given from memory, and the nurse who measures the glucose, the urine output, the pulse pressure, or the skin pinch is often the one who sees the turn before anyone else. Each case named a priority nursing problem, the monitoring that mattered, and the threshold for escalation, and each one showed that logistics, adverse effects, and the family's ability to return are part of the clinical plan rather than an afterthought. The World Health Organization, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control revise their guidance, national programs adapt it, and the current version must be checked before any regimen described here is prescribed. Every case in this appendix is a composite, and none of them describes a real person. Carry the pattern rather than the number, verify the number every time, and the patient in front of you will be safer for it.
