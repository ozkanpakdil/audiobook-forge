# Chapter 62 — Security and Field Safety

A clinic is useful only for as long as it is open, staffed, and trusted, and those three conditions are exactly what insecurity destroys. Security in this chapter does not mean fortification or armed response. It means the ordinary discipline that keeps a nurse, a patient, and a vehicle out of the situations where harm becomes likely. A clinic that is secure is not a clinic with high walls; it is a clinic whose team has thought carefully about where the risks lie and has arranged the day so that most of them never arrive. The guidance that follows draws on the security management system of the United Nations, the World Health Organization, the International Committee of the Red Cross, and the field manuals of Doctors Without Borders. Security guidance is revised as conflicts change, and practice differs between countries and between agencies, so verify the current guidance and your own organization's security plan before you rely on any threshold or route named here.

Begin with the recognition that the most senior clinician on site is usually the person responsible for security, whether or not the job title says so. In a remote post the nurse often decides whether a journey happens, whether a patient is transferred, whether the gate is opened after dark, and whether the team stays or leaves. Each of those is a clinical decision wearing the clothes of an administrative one, because the answer determines who receives care and who does not. This is why security belongs to the same professional competence as triage or drug dosing rather than to a separate department far away. A nurse who understands the mechanism of risk can argue for the safer plan with evidence instead of fear. And the person who can explain the risk is usually the person who is listened to.

Assessment begins before arrival, and it begins with a map of actors. You need to know who holds authority, who holds influence, and who holds weapons in the district where you will work. That map includes the ministry of health and its district officer, the local government administrator, the traditional authority and the religious leaders, the police, the army, any armed group that controls the roads, the market association, the teachers, the youth association, and the women's groups that organize much of daily life. Each of these actors can protect your work or obstruct it, and each has a different reason for doing either. Ask who has been helpful to previous clinics and who has not, and ask why. Write the map down, name a contact for each entry, and revise it every few months, because the actors change faster than the buildings do.

The physical geography decides what is possible, and it must be read season by season. Learn which roads are passable in the dry months and which become rivers in the rains, where the bridges are weight-limited, where the culverts wash out, and where the tarmac ends. Learn the distance and the driving time to the referral hospital in the best conditions and in the worst, because the difference is often a factor of three rather than a fraction. Find the alternate routes and check them with your own eyes rather than on a screen, since a route that exists on a map may pass through a checkpoint that will not let you continue. Identify the airstrip, its length, its surface, and who controls access to it. Note the hours of daylight across the year, because that number sets the boundary of every journey you will plan.

The history of the place is the part of the assessment that documents never contain. Ask what happened here in the last election, during the last epidemic, and after the last dispute over land or cattle or water. Ask when the last attack on a clinic or a health worker occurred, what preceded it, and whether anyone was ever held responsible. Ask about the grievance that people still mention in the market, because that grievance is the soil in which a future incident grows. Ask about the previous organization that worked here and why it left, since the answer is often instructive rather than flattering. Listen especially to the driver, the cleaner, the translator, and the community health workers, who move through places that outsiders never see. What they know is operational intelligence, and it costs nothing but respect to collect it.

The assessment is never finished, and treating it as a form completed at the start is one of the commonest mistakes in field practice. Security is a moving picture, so you monitor it the way you monitor a patient: at a defined interval, against defined signs, with a named person responsible for noticing change. The signs that matter include a new checkpoint, a new weapon in the market, a change in the tone of a conversation with an official, a rumor that repeats, a staff member who reports being followed, and a sudden silence from a source who usually speaks. Record the signs in one place, with dates, and review them at the weekly team meeting rather than in a crisis. A pattern is visible in a written record and invisible in memory. Monitoring is therefore the bridge between the assessment you made and the decision you must make tomorrow.

Road travel is where most field teams meet harm, and vehicle discipline is the cheapest protection available. Before every departure, walk around the vehicle and look at it rather than trusting the driver's reassurance, because the driver may be tired or eager to leave. Check the fuel level against the distance and add a reserve of at least a quarter of a tank beyond what the journey requires. Check the engine oil, the coolant, the brake fluid, the tire pressures including the spare, the jack and the wrench, the lights, the horn, the wipers, and the first aid kit. Confirm that the communication device works, that its battery is charged, and that the credit or subscription is current. Confirm that every occupant has a seat belt that fastens and that the load, including any patient, is secured so that it cannot become a missile. Initial the log, because a check that leaves no trace is a check that will quietly stop happening.

Speed is the variable that converts a mistake into a fatality, and the arithmetic deserves to be understood before it is legislated. The energy of a moving vehicle rises with the square of its speed, so doubling the speed does not double the harm; it multiplies it roughly fourfold. A loaded four-wheel drive with a high center of gravity rolls over at a lower speed than the same vehicle empty, and a roof rack loaded with supplies raises that center further. Set a ceiling for your own team that is slower than the legal limit and slower again on gravel, in the rain, and at night if night travel is unavoidable. Brief the driver before departure on the ceiling and on the reason for it. Nobody in the vehicle, including the most senior clinician, should ever ask the driver to go faster, because the request transfers the risk to everyone without transferring the responsibility.

The rule against driving at night saves more field lives than any other single rule, and it should be treated as close to absolute. Darkness removes the pedestrian, the animal, the unlit truck, the washed-out culvert, and the unmarked checkpoint from your view until they are inside your stopping distance. Darkness also changes the behavior of people at checkpoints and on the road, and it makes a breakdown far harder to see and to avoid. Plan the day backward from the journey so that you arrive well before dusk, and build in a margin for the delay that a flat tire or a broken bridge will certainly cause. If the journey cannot be completed in daylight, the correct decision is usually to leave earlier, to travel in two stages with a night stop, or not to travel at all. The nurse who cancels a journey because of the clock is practicing medicine, not avoiding it.

Every journey needs a breakdown plan, because a mechanical failure is a certainty and a surprise only if you have not planned for it. Decide in advance who will be called first, what information they will need, and how the message will reach them if the radio or the telephone has no signal. Carry water, food, a shade, a spare container of fuel, a basic tool kit, and a means of repairing the tire yourself, because the recovery vehicle may be a day away. Decide whether the team stays with the vehicle or walks out, and make that decision in advance, when it can still be made calmly, rather than at the roadside in the heat. Never split the group in a way that leaves one person alone, and never abandon the vehicle on a main road where it can be looted or where it becomes a hazard. Tell the receiving post the make, the color, the plate number, and the last known position before departure. A plan that exists only in the head of the driver is not a plan.

The checkpoint is the ordinary interface between your team and armed authority, and preparation removes most of its danger. Keep a single folder, in the vehicle and never in the luggage, containing the identity documents of every occupant, the vehicle registration and insurance, the driving permit, the travel authorization, the passenger manifest, the cargo list, and the documentation for any medicines or samples being carried. Medicines and controlled substances need a written justification and the correct permits, because an unexplained box of ampoules invites a suspicion that no explanation at the barrier can dispel. Keep the documents clean, current, and legible, and keep photocopies in a second place in case the original folder is confiscated. Know which documents each kind of checkpoint is entitled to see, and know the difference between a demand that is lawful and one that is not. A folder in order is not a formality; it is the difference between a two-minute stop and an hour of negotiation.

Demeanor at the barrier matters as much as the paperwork. Slow the vehicle, switch off the radio or the music, keep both hands visible on the wheel, and greet the person in charge before offering any document. Nominate one person, usually the driver or the team leader, to speak, and let that person do all of the speaking so that two voices never contradict each other. Stay calm, stay polite, and do not argue about the law at the barrier, because the argument will be lost there and won in writing later. Do not offer a bribe where your organization forbids it, and do not carry anything, for anyone, that you have not inspected yourself, because a favor asked at a checkpoint is often a trap. Learn the shape of the refusal that keeps everyone alive: the order to take a road you have been told is unsafe, the demand to carry a passenger or a package of unknown contents, the instruction to hand over a patient or a sample, and the pressure to drive after dark. A refusal delivered calmly, without moralizing, with an alternative offered in its place, is among the most valuable sentences a field nurse can learn.

A crowd behaves as a fluid rather than as a collection of individuals, and it must be handled with the respect you would give to water. Never drive a vehicle into a crowd, because the vehicle stops being a vehicle and becomes the thing the crowd is against. If you meet a demonstration, stop at a distance, turn the vehicle so that you can leave, keep the engine running, and wait rather than negotiate or push through. Do not photograph, do not shout, and do not single out anyone in the crowd, because attention is what turns curiosity into hostility. If the clinic is near a demonstration, close the gate early, keep patients inside, and wait for the crowd to disperse on its own, since most crowds are temporary and most confrontations are created by the attempt to end them quickly. The demonstration is almost never about you, and the sooner you behave as though it is not, the sooner it will pass.

Firearms in a community change the meaning of every dispute, and the clinic must define itself as a place apart from them. Negotiate and publish a simple rule that no weapon enters the patient area, whether carried by a soldier, a police officer, a hunter, or a neighbor. Enforce it consistently, because an exception granted once becomes the rule thereafter. Provide a place at the gate where weapons can be left, and be realistic about who has the authority to enforce the rule, since a nurse cannot disarm an armed man and should not try. Where an armed actor insists on entering, your objective is to protect the patients and the staff and to document what happened, not to win the argument at the door. Make the neutrality of the clinic visible through its practice: treat the wounded from every side, keep political symbols off the wall, and never use the clinic as a platform. A clinic known to be even-handed is a clinic less often attacked, and that reputation is built in ordinary weeks rather than in crises.

Every journey must exist on paper before it exists on the road. Leave a journey plan with a colleague who is staying behind, and include the names of everyone traveling, the vehicle and its plate number, the destination, the route, the purpose, the departure time, the expected arrival time, and the check-in times along the way. The person staying behind is not a clerk but a named monitor whose job during your absence is to watch the clock and to act on what it says. Agree the check-in interval before departure, and keep it short enough that a missed check-in is meaningful rather than routine. Keep a board in the office that shows who is out, where they are, and when they are due back, and update it by hand if the power and the network are unreliable. A journey that is invisible to the rest of the team is a journey that cannot be rescued.

The missed check-in is the signal that converts concern into action, and the response must be written before it is needed. Define the margin, so that a check-in is late by fifteen minutes in one setting and by two hours in another, depending on the terrain and the network. When the margin passes, the monitor starts a fixed ladder: try the radio, try the telephone, try the satellite device, call the destination post to see whether the team arrived, call the driver's alternate number, and then alert the designated security contact and the organization's office. Give the monitor the authority to climb that ladder without seeking permission from someone who is asleep, and name a deputy for the days the monitor is away. Do not wait for reassurance, and do not accept a rumor as a check-in. Record every attempt with its time and its result, because that record is what a search or an investigation will use, and because it is the only way the plan can be improved afterward.

Neutrality is a working tool rather than a moral position, and it must be understood precisely if it is to protect anyone. In humanitarian practice neutrality means that you do not take sides in a hostilities, impartiality means that you treat according to need, humanity means that you relieve suffering wherever you find it, and independence means that your decisions follow need rather than the instruction of any party. These four principles are the reason an armed group may allow a clinic to treat both its own wounded and the wounded of its opponent. Neutrality does not require silence about what you witness, and it does not require you to pretend that all parties behave identically. It does require that the clinic's actions, and not only its statements, are even-handed. The credibility of that even-handedness is the clinic's actual armor, and it is built slowly and lost quickly.

Neutrality has limits, and the field worker should know them before the day they matter. A party to a conflict may decide that a clinic is aligned, whether because of where it is, whom it has treated, or what it has said, and no amount of correct practice can guarantee otherwise. When a threat becomes targeted rather than incidental, the options narrow to negotiation through a trusted intermediary, temporary suspension of activities, relocation of staff, or withdrawal. Each of those decisions belongs to the organization's designated security authority rather than to the individual nurse, and the point of naming that authority in advance is so that the decision can be made quickly and defended later. Prepare the handover while you still have room to negotiate: brief the ministry, the district officer, and the community leaders on what will stop and what will continue. Write the decision, its reasoning, and its date, because memory is unreliable in a crisis and the record is the institution's protection. Leaving is not a failure of courage when the alternative is a clinic that has become a target endangering patients and staff alike.

The evacuation of staff is a different problem from the evacuation of a patient, and the two plans should never be confused. A medical evacuation moves a sick patient toward a higher level of care, while a security evacuation moves staff away from a threat, sometimes leaving the clinic standing and sometimes leaving the country. Field practice usually distinguishes three postures: hibernation, in which the team stays in place behind a locked gate until the situation clears; relocation, in which the team moves to a safer part of the country; and evacuation, in which the team leaves entirely. Each posture has a defined trigger, a defined authority who calls it, an assembly point, and a route, and none of those should be improvised on the day. Everyone must know the trigger and the assembly point without consulting a document. The plan must also say who remains responsible for the patients and the medicines when the team leaves, because a clinic abandoned without a handover becomes a supply depot for whoever arrives first.

The grab bag is the physical form of the evacuation plan, and it should be packed for the realistic scenario rather than the dramatic one. It holds identity documents and copies, local currency in small notes, a printed contact list, a charged communication device with its charger, water and food for the first day, a headlamp and spare batteries, a change of clothes, a light rain shell, basic toiletries, and any personal medicine that cannot be bought locally. The medical component typically includes a small wound kit, an analgesic, an antimalarial for treatment and for prophylaxis, an antibiotic for common infections, oral rehydration salts, and a small supply of any drug that a chronic condition requires. In current World Health Organization and national guidance, adult malaria prophylaxis with doxycycline is commonly 100 milligrams by mouth once a day, and an antibiotic such as ciprofloxacin for a simple infection is commonly 500 milligrams by mouth every twelve hours, while an antimalarial treatment course follows the national first-line combination. Doxycycline commonly causes nausea and makes the skin burn more easily in strong sun, and ciprofloxacin can cause tendon pain and uncommon disturbances of the heart rhythm, so each must be monitored and stopped if an adverse effect appears. These regimens are revised and differ between countries, so verify the current guideline before you pack or take any of them.

Documents deserve their own paragraph because their absence causes more delay and more danger at a barrier than any other single failure. Keep the passport, the visa, the work permit, the professional license, the vaccination record, and any authorization to carry medicines current, and begin the renewal process months before the expiry rather than weeks. Carry a paper copy of every essential document in a second bag, and store an electronic copy with a trusted colleague abroad as well as on your own device. Carry a small card in your wallet with your blood group, your allergies, your chronic conditions, your regular medicines, and two emergency contacts who can be reached from the country where you work. Write the contact details in a form that works without a charged telephone, because a number stored only in a dead device is not a contact. The nurse who carries the team's documents should check them at the same monthly interval as the grab bag, since a permit that expired quietly is indistinguishable at a barrier from a permit that never existed.

A plan that has never been rehearsed is a document rather than a capability. Rehearse the security plan as you would rehearse a resuscitation: with a short scenario, a time limit, and a person assigned to watch what actually happens. A tabletop exercise in which the team talks through a checkpoint, a road closure, a medical evacuation, and a relocation costs an hour and reveals every unspoken assumption in the plan. A physical drill in which everyone walks to the assembly point in the dark with the grab bag teaches things that no discussion can, including how long it takes, who cannot find the key, and which gate is locked. After each rehearsal, write down the failures and assign each one to a named person with a date, then rehearse the corrected plan at the next interval. The rehearsal is uncomfortable, and that discomfort is the entire point, because it is cheaper to be embarrassed in an exercise than surprised in an incident.

The most likely threat to a field nurse is not a gunman but an accident, and the mundane causes deserve more of your attention than the dramatic ones. Road traffic crashes dominate the statistics of field injury, followed by falls, burns, electrical injuries, and animal bites. Fatigue is the great multiplier in all of them, so protect sleep on the nights before a long drive and refuse to drive when you have not slept. The same discipline applies to the ordinary equipment of the post: the ladder used as a bench, the exposed wiring, the generator with its hot exhaust, the fuel stored beside the kitchen fire, the cooking gas cylinder that is never leak-tested, and the oxygen cylinder that is not chained upright. Keep a fire extinguisher where it can be reached, check it on a schedule, and keep every exit unlocked from the inside at all times. A clinic can lose a staff member to a preventable fall in exactly the week it is preparing for an armed attack, and the fall is far more probable.

Some of those accidents carry a clinical consequence that must be managed at the moment it happens. A dog bite or a scratch from a suspect animal requires immediate and thorough washing of the wound with soap and clean running water for at least 15 minutes, because washing removes virus before it can enter the nerve. After washing, apply an antiseptic, do not suture the wound tightly, and arrange rabies post-exposure vaccination without delay, together with rabies immunoglobulin for a category of exposure that requires it, following the current World Health Organization and national regimens. A deep puncture, a wound contaminated with soil or manure, or a fracture sustained in the field also needs tetanus assessment and the appropriate vaccine or immunoglobulin, again according to the current guideline. A needle-stick or a splash to the eye or the mouth starts a clock for human immunodeficiency virus post-exposure prophylaxis and for hepatitis B, and that clock is best carried in writing inside the grab bag. Every one of these regimens is revised and differs between countries, so confirm the current protocol before you act, and record the exposure, the time, the treatment given, and the follow-up date in the register.

The security guard is the first person a patient meets and the first line of the clinic's defense, and the post deserves to be designed rather than improvised. Select the guard in consultation with the community, so that the person at the gate is known, accountable, and unlikely to be coerced by a neighbor. Give the guard a written contract, a clear job description, an identifying badge, and a wage that is paid on time, because an unpaid guard is a guard who leaves or who supplements the income in ways the clinic cannot see. Train the guard in what the role actually requires: how to greet and register visitors, when to refuse entry, when to call for help, how to use the radio, how to write the night log, and what not to do, which includes not making arrests and not carrying a weapon the organization has not authorized. Visit the gate at night yourself, at irregular intervals, because a supervisor who never comes at night will never know what happens at night. Respect the guard as a colleague, since a person treated as furniture will behave accordingly, and the gate is the beginning of every clinical story that ends well.

Security is ultimately a relationship with the community rather than a set of barriers, and the community usually knows about a threat before the clinic does. Build the channels through which that knowledge can travel in both directions: the community health workers, the traditional birth attendants, the women's groups, the market leaders, the teachers, and the religious congregations. Make it easy and safe for a neighbor to warn you, and make it clear what you will and will not do with the information, because a clinic believed to report names to the police may lose the very warnings that keep it safe. Reciprocate by sharing what you can: the vaccination dates, the outbreak news, the reason a road is closed, and the honest explanation when the clinic cannot help. A community that has a stake in the clinic will defend it more effectively than any wall, and that stake is built by years of consistent, respectful presence. Where the community helped to write the rules at the gate, the community also helps to enforce them.

Every security measure has adverse effects, and a serious team monitors them as carefully as it monitors a drug. A gate that closes too early, a journey cancelled too readily, or a curfew applied too broadly leaves patients without care, and the harm falls hardest on the people who can least afford to travel. The staff carry their own adverse effects: the fatigue of permanent vigilance, the guilt of leaving a patient behind, the irritability that follows months of restraint, and the numbness that follows an incident never discussed. Monitor these signs the way you would monitor a fever, and record them in a security log that includes incidents, near misses, threats, and the decisions taken in response. Review the log at a fixed interval with the whole team, look for trends rather than isolated events, and act on the pattern before it produces an injury. Documentation in this domain is not bureaucracy; it is the memory that a frightened team does not have, and the evidence a distant office needs in order to send help.

The nurse's role in field security is partly clinical and partly moral, and the two cannot be separated. You are the person most likely to notice that a colleague has stopped sleeping, that a driver has been drinking, that a patient's relative has made a threat, or that a journey is being planned for reasons of pride rather than need. You are also the person who must sometimes say out loud that the clinic is beyond its limit and that a specific plan is unsafe, and that sentence is a professional act rather than an act of disloyalty. Support the colleague who is sent home, the family of a staff member who was hurt, and the whole team after an incident, because the days that follow an event are when people most need a structured debrief and are least likely to ask for one. Protect your own sleep, your own food, and your own contact with the people who remind you why the work matters. And remember that refusing an unsafe journey is not abandoning a patient, because a nurse who is dead or captured treats no one.

To summarize, security is not a wall around the medicine but the condition that lets the medicine happen at all, and it belongs to the nurse's competence rather than to a distant office. The work begins with an assessment made before arrival and continued forever afterward: the map of actors, the roads and the seasons, the history of the place, and the signs that are monitored, written down, and reviewed. The journey is protected by a vehicle check, a sane speed, a rule against driving at night, a breakdown plan, and a journey plan left with a named colleague who acts when the check-in is missed. The checkpoint is passed with papers in order, one calm voice, and the practiced refusal that keeps everyone alive, while crowds are met with distance and the clinic is kept as a place apart from weapons and from politics. Neutrality, impartiality, humanity, and independence protect the clinic until they fail, at which point the designated authority calls hibernation, relocation, or evacuation, and the grab bag, the documents, and the rehearsal make that call survivable. The most likely harm remains the ordinary accident, which is why the seat belt, the ladder, the wiring, the fire extinguisher, and the sleep before a long drive matter more than the dramatic scenario, and the same mundane care covers the animal bite, the deep wound, and the needle-stick, whose vaccination and drug regimens must be checked against the current World Health Organization and national guidance, which are revised and differ between countries. The security guard, trained, paid, respected, and supervised, together with the community that helped to write the rules, will protect the clinic better than any fortification. And the nurse who plans for the worst, monitors honestly, documents carefully, and says out loud when a plan is unsafe is the person who keeps the clinic open, staffed, and trusted for the patients who need it.
