# Tropical Medicine — content briefs

One section per chapter. Each writer covers every bullet of its own section, in
whatever order reads best aloud. Read `PLAN.md` first: the style contract there
overrides anything here.

---

## Chapter 1 — Scope and History of Tropical Medicine: Neglected Tropical Diseases, the Global Burden, and One Health

Definition of the field and why the geographic name is misleading; the colonial and
military origins, sleeping sickness campaigns, the Rockefeller Foundation, and how the
discipline moved from geography to poverty as the real variable; what makes a disease
"neglected" and how the World Health Organization's list of roughly twenty conditions
is assembled; the global burden expressed with real figures and explained through
disability-adjusted life years; why the big three of malaria, tuberculosis and HIV
attract money while the neglected diseases do not; One Health and the human, animal and
environmental interface; control, elimination and eradication as three different goals;
what the COVID-19 pandemic did to these programs; and why the nurse sits at the point
where all of it is delivered.

## Chapter 2 — Epidemiology and the Determinants of Tropical Disease

Incidence, prevalence, case fatality and attack rate explained for a clinician who
needs them at the bedside; the basic reproduction number and herd immunity in plain
language; temperature thresholds and vector survival; rainfall, breeding sites and
seasonality; altitude and the malaria line; why poverty is the fundamental determinant
rather than climate; housing, crowding, and the absence of clean water and sanitation;
migration, displacement and camps; urbanization and the Aedes problem in slums;
deforestation, dams and irrigation schemes creating new habitat; the effect of climate
change on the range and season of transmission; extreme weather and outbreaks;
El Niño cycles; where surveillance data comes from; and the structural determinants a
nurse can name but not fix alone.

## Chapter 3 — Vectors and the Ecology of Transmission

What a vector is and what makes one competent; mosquitoes by genus with their diseases
and their biting habits, Anopheles at night, Aedes by day, Culex in between; sandflies
and leishmaniasis; tsetse flies and African trypanosomiasis; triatomine bugs and Chagas
disease; ticks and the rickettsioses, borreliosis and Crimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever;
snails and schistosomiasis; blackflies and onchocerciasis; fleas and plague; the
difference between biological and mechanical transmission; the extrinsic incubation
period and why it matters for control; endophilic and exophilic behavior; breeding site
ecology; water-borne and soil-borne transmission for the helminths; how interrupting
each point in a transmission cycle translates into a real intervention; and why the
insecticide-treated bed net works on biology rather than on obedience.

## Chapter 4 — The Advanced Practice Nurse in Tropical Medicine: Scope, Task-Sharing, and Ethics

The scope of advanced nursing practice and how it differs across countries; task-shifting
and task-sharing, with the evidence that nurses deliver outcomes comparable to physicians
for defined conditions; standing orders, protocols and clinical algorithms as the
instruments that make this safe; working where there is no physician; the realities of
low-resource practice, unreliable power, cold chain, reagents, transport and staffing;
the ethics of duty to care, resource allocation, dual loyalty and research participation;
cultural humility as distinct from cultural competence; working with interpreters;
traditional healers as collaborators rather than obstacles; personal safety and security;
documentation and supervision when nobody is checking; mentoring local staff; and
burnout, moral distress and what actually helps.

## Chapter 5 — Travel and Migration History: The Structured Exposure History

Why the history is the diagnostic instrument and the examination is secondary; the
structured sequence, destination, dates, duration, season, altitude, rural or urban,
type of accommodation, food and water, animal contact, insect avoidance, sexual history,
tattoos and piercings, transfusions and injections, vaccinations and prophylaxis
adherence; the logic of incubation periods and how the return date anchors the whole
differential; geographic risk mapping; why visiting friends and relatives is the highest
risk category of traveler; the migrant and refugee history, country of origin, transit,
camp, duration, and the difference between what a chart asks and what a person will
tell; how to ask about adherence without accusation; the exposures patients do not
volunteer; documenting so the next clinician can use it; and the common traps that make
a tropical diagnosis get missed in an emergency department.

## Chapter 6 — The Febrile Returning Traveler

Fever as the most frequent presentation and malaria as the diagnosis to exclude first;
initial triage and the vital signs and bedside findings that change the plan, mental
status, respiratory rate, capillary refill, rash, bleeding, urine output; the red flags
that demand immediate action; the differential organized by incubation period, days for
dengue, chikungunya, typhoid, rickettsial disease and influenza, weeks for malaria,
typhoid, leptospirosis, hepatitis, acute HIV and brucellosis, months for tuberculosis,
leishmaniasis, filariasis, amoebic liver abscess and schistosomiasis; the physical
findings that shift probability, eschar, conjunctival suffusion, splenomegaly, relative
bradycardia, jaundice, lymphadenopathy; what must happen in the first hour; when to
treat empirically before the diagnosis is certain; the timing and repetition of malaria
films; and the nurse's role in triage and in noticing deterioration between rounds.

## Chapter 7 — The Syndromic Approach to Tropical Fever

Fever with rash and its differential, including dengue, chikungunya, measles,
rickettsial disease, the rose spots of typhoid and meningococcal disease; fever with
jaundice, including hepatitis A and E, leptospirosis, severe malaria, yellow fever and
biliary sepsis; fever with neurological signs, including cerebral malaria, meningitis,

eosinophilia and what causes it, the soil-transmitted helminths, schistosomiasis,

lesions, ulcers, nodules, plaques, migratory swellings and the eschar; lymphadenopathy
and splenomegaly as clues; the chain from syndrome to organism to test to treatment; and
the danger of anchoring on the first plausible diagnosis.

## Chapter 8 — Diagnostics Where Resources Are Limited

Specimen collection and the timing that makes or breaks a test; malaria thick and thin

their practical constraints; serology and paired sera, cross-reactivity and the dengue

nurse's influence on specimen quality; and why a negative test is not always absence of
disease.

## Chapter 9 — Malaria

The five species that infect humans and why Plasmodium falciparum dominates;
the life cycle from sporozoite to liver stage to blood stage to gametocyte, told as a
sequence because the drugs act at different points; incubation and the unreliability of
the classic fever pattern; uncomplicated presentation; the criteria for severe malaria,
impaired consciousness, acidosis, hypoglycemia, severe anemia, renal impairment,
pulmonary edema, bleeding, shock and high parasite density; diagnosis and parasite
density; treatment with artemisinin-based combination therapy and the parenteral
artesunate regimen for severe disease, including pre-referral rectal artesunate; the
resistance picture, chloroquine long lost and partial artemisinin resistance in
Southeast Asia; chemoprophylaxis options and the adherence problem; special populations,
pregnancy by trimester, infants, children and the glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase
issue for primaquine and tafenoquine in vivax malaria; relapse against recrudescence;
integration with vector control; and the nursing priorities, glucose monitoring, fluid
balance, drug timing, temperature control and neurological checks.

## Chapter 10 — Arboviral Diseases

Dengue, its four serotypes, the significance of a second infection, plasma leakage and
the warning signs of abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, bleeding, lethargy and fluid

inflammatory drugs are avoided; chikungunya and its arthralgia, chronicity and neonatal
risk; Zika and congenital infection, Guillain-Barré syndrome and sexual transmission;
yellow fever, its vaccine and its severe form with jaundice and bleeding; West Nile and
neuroinvasive disease; Japanese encephalitis, its vaccine, its burden in children and
its sequelae; tick-borne encephalitis; Rift Valley fever, Mayaro and Oropouche as
emerging concerns; the Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus ecology; and the nurse's role
in recognizing the transition into the critical phase and in infection control.

## Chapter 11 — Leishmaniasis

The Leishmania species and the sandfly vector; the three clinical forms and why the
species determines which one appears; visceral leishmaniasis with fever, splenomegaly,
pancytopenia, wasting and post-kala-azar dermal leishmaniasis; cutaneous leishmaniasis
and its chronicity and scarring; mucocutaneous disease and its destruction of the nose
and palate; diagnosis by aspirate microscopy, the rK39 rapid test and PCR; treatment
with liposomal amphotericin B, miltefosine, sodium stibogluconate and paromomycin, with
their toxicities including cardiac, pancreatic and renal effects and the teratogenicity
of miltefosine; HIV co-infection and the relapse problem; the elimination program in
South Asia; and the nursing work of toxicity monitoring, adherence and wound care.

## Chapter 12 — Trypanosomiasis: Chagas Disease and Human African Trypanosomiasis

Chagas disease from Trypanosoma cruzi and the triatomine bug, the acute phase, the
indeterminate decades-long phase, and the cardiac and digestive disease that eventually
appears; congenital transmission and its screening implications; transmission by
transfusion and by contaminated food; treatment with benznidazole and nifurtimox, who
benefits and who should not be treated; the global dimension as migration carries the
infection out of Latin America. Then human African trypanosomiasis, the two subspecies
and their different courses, the tsetse fly, the chancre, the hemolymphatic stage, the
sleep disturbance, and the neurological stage; staging by cerebrospinal fluid
examination; treatment with pentamidine, suramin, eflornithine combined with
nifurtimox, and fexinidazole as the oral advance; and the remarkable progress toward
elimination alongside the nursing work of lumbar puncture support and adverse effect
surveillance.

## Chapter 13 — Filarial Diseases

Lymphatic filariasis, the worms Wuchereria bancrofti and Brugia species, the mosquito
vectors, the lymphangitis and hydrocele and progressive lymphedema, the acute
adenolymphangitis attacks, and the mass drug administration with ivermectin, albendazole
and diethylcarbamazine; the practical care of a lymphedematous limb, skin hygiene,
elevation, prevention of entry wounds and treatment of bacterial infection. Onchocerciasis
from Onchocerca volvulus and the blackfly, the subcutaneous nodules, the dermatitis and
the river blindness, ivermectin and community-directed treatment, and the elimination
achieved in the Americas. Loiasis from Loa loa and the deerfly, the Calabar swelling and
the eye worm, and the serious problem of ivermectin in co-endemic areas. The nurse's
role in campaigns, in adverse event monitoring and in long-term disability care.

## Chapter 14 — Rickettsioses and Other Vector-Borne Bacteria

Scrub typhus from Orientia tsutsugamushi and the chigger mite, the eschar, the fever and
the respiratory failure, treated with doxycycline; the spotted fever group including
Rocky Mountain spotted fever, Mediterranean spotted fever and African tick bite fever;
murine typhus; Q fever; bartonellosis with its two faces of Oroya fever and verruga
peruana; the Borrelia, both the Lyme group and the relapsing fevers transmitted by ticks
and by lice; anaplasmosis and ehrlichiosis; the diagnostic difficulty since serology
arrives after the decision, and the resulting principle of empirical doxycycline; the
question of doxycycline in children and in pregnancy; and the nursing role in
recognizing the eschar and in not waiting for confirmatory serology.

## Chapter 15 — Soil-Transmitted Helminths

Ascaris lumbricoides and its migrations through the lung, its intestinal obstruction and

Trichuris trichiura and the dysentery and rectal prolapse in heavy infection;

## Chapter 16 — Schistosomiasis and Food-Borne Trematodes

The Schistosoma species and their geography; the snail intermediate host and the

the circulating cathodic antigen test and ultrasound; treatment with praziquantel, its

intestinal flukes; and the nurse's role in health education that names the specific food
and the specific practice.

## Chapter 17 — Cestodes: Neurocysticercosis and Echinococcosis

Taenia solium and the two distinct diseases it causes, the intestinal tapeworm from
undercooked pork and the neurocysticercosis that follows when eggs are swallowed; the
seizures, hydrocephalus and chronic epilepsy that result; why treatment of
neurocysticercosis with albendazole or praziquantel must be paired with corticosteroids

husbandry and sanitation in prevention. Then echinococcosis, the dog and sheep cycle of

a different and worse disease; and the nurse's role in long-term follow-up and in
preventing needle-track and surgical spillage.

## Chapter 18 — Intestinal Protozoa

Amoebiasis from Entamoeba histolytica, the cyst, the invasive disease with bloody

## Chapter 19 — Enteric Infections and Fluid Management

Typhoid and paratyphoid fever from Salmonella Typhi, the stepped fever, relative

## Chapter 20 — Mycobacterial Diseases: Tuberculosis, Drug Resistance, and Leprosy

## Chapter 21 — Zoonoses

## Chapter 22 — Viral Hemorrhagic Fevers

## Chapter 23 — Neglected Bacterial and Skin Diseases

## Chapter 24 — Tropical Mycoses

## Chapter 25 — Envenomation and Tropical Injuries

## Chapter 26 — Pregnancy, Neonates, and Children

## Chapter 28 — Nutrition and Tropical Medicine

## Chapter 31 — Vaccination for Tropical Settings

## Chapter 34 — Mass Drug Administration and Elimination Programs

## Chapter 35 — Surveillance, Outbreak Response, and Notification
