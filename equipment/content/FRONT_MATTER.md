# Hospital Equipment — A Complete Guide to Devices, Machines, Tools and Consumables

This guide is a spoken tour of the hardware of medicine. It begins at the hospital bed and ends at the incinerator, and it passes on the way through the infusion pumps, the ventilators, the imaging rooms, the laboratory benches, the operating theatre, the dialysis unit, the sterilization department, and the storeroom. It covers the machines that cost more than a house and the consumables that cost less than a cup of tea, because a clinic fails as often for want of a five-cent item as for want of a scanner.

Every chapter describes the same subject twice. The first description is the hospital-grade device, the one installed in a well-resourced institution, with its dedicated power, its piped gases, its service contract, and its supply of single-use parts. The second is the field variant, the portable, robust, hand-operated, battery-powered, or improvised version used in a mobile clinic or a frontier hospital, the version that must survive dust, heat, and a supply line measured in months. The same clinical need is met by very different machines depending on where the patient lies, and knowing both is the difference between a plan that works and a plan that assumes a hospital that is not there.

Because this guide is spoken rather than printed, there are no photographs in it. Instead, every device is described in words chosen so that you can picture it: its size, its shape, the parts you touch, the sound it makes, the way it fails. Where a machine has a distinctive appearance or a characteristic alarm, the description is there. Where a number matters, such as a pressure, a temperature, an energy setting, or the length of a sterilization cycle, the number is spoken and then qualified, because settings differ between devices, manufacturers, and countries, and the manual that came with the machine and the protocol of your institution always outrank a recording.

The seventy-eight chapters are organized in fourteen parts, followed by five appendices: a spoken atlas that walks through each department in the order you would walk through it, quick-reference tables of the sizes and colors and gauges that are hardest to remember, the checklists for a crash cart and a department, a glossary of devices and short forms, and a set of troubleshooting walk-throughs for the alarms you will meet most often. The appendices are meant to be returned to, and the chapter that covers the device explains it in full.

This guide is educational, and it assumes a working knowledge of anatomy, physiology, and clinical practice. It does not replace the manufacturer's instructions, the training provided with a device, the biomedical engineering department, or the law and standards of the country where you work. What it offers is the understanding that lets a nurse, a clinical officer, or a technician look at a machine, know what it is doing, know what it needs, and know when to stop using it.
