# Appendix E — Troubleshooting Flowcharts for Common Alarms

An alarm is a comparison made by a machine, not a diagnosis and not a fault report. Every alarm has three parts: a sensor that measures something, a threshold that a person has set, and an annunciator that turns the crossing of that threshold into sound and light. The meaning lies in the comparison rather than in the noise, so the first question is never which button silences it but which measurement crossed which limit. This appendix tells these alarms as spoken sequences rather than as diagrams, and one rule governs every sequence: patient safety precedes equipment diagnosis, so the patient is examined before the machine is touched. Every threshold, limit, and cycle value named here must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The patient monitor is the most familiar alarm in the hospital and the least respected, and that disrespect is itself a hazard. Each channel turns a body signal into a voltage and compares it with limits a person has entered, so the electrocardiogram, called an ECG after this first mention, reads the electrical field of the heart. The characteristic sounds are a single beep for a limit violation, a repeating two-tone chime for a lead that has come off, and a lower continuous tone for a low battery. The checks go in a fixed order: speak to the patient, inspect the electrode and the skin beneath it, follow the lead wire to its connector, read the limits against the protocol, and only then consider the device. The device must be stopped and help called when the tracing is unreadable, when the alarm repeats after the electrode and cable are replaced, when the readings disagree with a manual pulse, or when the patient has changed rather than the screen. The common operator error is to widen the limits or silence the alarm instead of correcting the cause, and limits and battery runtime must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The ventilator measures two things at once, the pressure it must generate and the volume it delivers, and their relationship describes the link between machine and lungs. Pressure rises when the path to the small air sacs narrows, as it does with secretions in a tube, a patient biting the tube, water in the circuit, a wet filter, or air collecting around a lung. Volume falls short when gas escapes between machine and patient, as it does at a loose connection, a deflated cuff, or an open chest drain. The high-pressure alarm is therefore the alarm of obstruction and the low-pressure alarm is the alarm of disconnection, and the disconnection alarm is the more dangerous because a circuit that has come apart delivers nothing and sounds a longer, more urgent pattern. The checks go in one order only: look at the patient and the chest, confirm that the chest rises and that breath sounds are present on both sides, follow the circuit outward for a disconnection or a kink, suction the tube and check the cuff, read the settings against the prescription, and only then test the machine. The error that kills is the search for a fault on the screen while the patient is disconnected, so the self-inflating bag is attached first, help is called when ventilation cannot be restored or when the machine will not complete its self-test, and every pressure limit, tidal volume, respiratory rate, and positive end-expiratory pressure setting must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The infusion pump alarms for pressure and for air because those are the two ways it can hurt a patient while appearing to work. Its mechanism pushes fluid at a controlled rate against a resistance it can measure, so a pressure sensor watches the force needed to keep the flow and an ultrasonic sensor watches the tubing for gas. An occlusion alarm means the resistance has risen, which happens when a cannula has moved out of the vein into tissue, when a clamp is closed, when tubing is kinked, or when the bag runs dry; an air alarm means a bubble has been detected, and its sound is a faster and higher tone than the repeating beep of an occlusion. The checks go in the order of the patient first: look at the cannula site for swelling, pain, or blanching, because an extravasation is the emergency, then feel the tubing for a closed clamp, then inspect the drip chamber, then reseat the tubing and clear the alarm, and only then consider the pump. The device must be stopped and help called when the site is swollen, when the patient complains of pain, when the alarm returns after being cleared, or when the fluid is a drug whose interruption is itself dangerous. The operator error that does the most damage is to silence the alarm and leave, or to remove tubing without closing the roller clamp, which turns a controlled infusion into free flow, and every occlusion setting and air-detector sensitivity must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The dialysis machine alarms for conductivity and for temperature because both faults are invisible in the blood line and both reach the patient directly. A pump draws blood through a filter while a second circuit mixes purified water with concentrated salts to make a dialysate whose conductivity reflects its electrolyte content. A conductivity alarm means the proportioning has drifted, as it does when a concentrate container runs dry, when water pressure falls, when a mixing pump fails, or when a sensor is fouled with scale, and low conductivity is the dangerous direction because a dilute dialysate can destroy red cells. A temperature alarm means the dialysate is outside its narrow band, its sound is a separate tone from the repeating conductivity alarm, and an over-hot dialysate can also destroy red cells while a cold one causes shivering. The checks begin at the patient: stop the ultrafiltration and the blood pump, look for shaking, chest pain, or a change in color, take a manual blood pressure, then read the displayed conductivity and temperature, then confirm that concentrate remains and that water is flowing, and then check calibration and the last maintenance date. The device must be stopped and help called when the patient has symptoms, when the alarm persists after the concentrate and water are checked, or when the machine reports a failure of its own proportioning system, and the operator error to avoid is resetting the alarm without testing the dialysate; every conductivity limit, temperature limit, and alarm threshold must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The autoclave fails differently from every other device here, because its alarm usually arrives after the cycle rather than during it. Saturated steam under pressure gives up a large amount of latent heat when it condenses on a cooler surface, and a combination of temperature, pressure, and time kills microorganisms. A cycle failure may mean the chamber never reached its temperature, that air was not fully removed and left a cool pocket, that the door gasket leaked, that the load was packed too tightly for steam to penetrate, or that the steam supply was wet; the sound is a buzzer or repeating beep at the end of the cycle with a failure code. The checks go in order: confirm first that the load has not been released, because a failed cycle means the contents are not sterile, then read the printed record for the actual temperature and time, then inspect the gasket for cuts, then examine how the load was arranged, then check the water and steam supply, and only then run a test cycle. The device must be stopped and help called when a biological indicator fails, when the same fault recurs after a correct load and a clean gasket, or when the machine reports a fault in its own control system, and the load is then reprocessed while the machine is taken out of service. The operator error that causes the most harm is to release the instruments anyway, and every temperature, pressure, and hold time, including the common cycle of 134 degrees Celsius for a few minutes and the longer cycle at 121 degrees Celsius, must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The refrigerator alarm is a temperature excursion, and its importance comes from the fact that the product inside degrades invisibly. Cold does not stop time but slows it, so a vaccine, a blood product, or a protein drug loses potency as a function of temperature and duration, and a short warm period may waste a shelf without changing its appearance. The alarm sounds as a beeping tone from a panel or a data logger and means that the air temperature at the sensor has crossed a limit, usually because of an open door, a failed compressor, a blocked condenser, an interruption of power, or a refrigerator packed too tightly for cold air to circulate. The checks go in order of the product first: close the door, read the temperature and the logger history, move the stock to a working refrigerator if the excursion continues, then examine the door seal, the condenser coils, the power supply, and the probe placement, and only then consider repairing the unit. The device must be stopped and help called when the temperature remains outside its range, when the compressor is silent or hot, or when the excursion has lasted longer than the protocol allows, and the affected stock is quarantined until a named person decides whether it may still be used. The characteristic ranges are 2 to 8 degrees Celsius for a pharmaceutical refrigerator and minus 20 degrees Celsius or colder for a freezer, and the operator error that ruins a cold chain is to keep food inside and open the door constantly; these ranges must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The pulse oximeter alarms for poor signal rather than for a value, and that alarm is a statement about the measurement rather than about the patient. Oxygenated and deoxygenated hemoglobin absorb red and infrared light differently, so the probe shines light through perfused tissue, measures the pulsatile part of the transmitted light, and computes a ratio that becomes a percentage. If the pulse is weak, if the tissue is cold, if the probe is loose, or if the light path is blocked, the ratio loses its meaning, and the sounds are a low-pitch tone for a low saturation and a more urgent tone for a poor signal. The checks begin with the patient: look at the hand for warmth, color, and swelling, confirm that the probe is not on an arm whose blood pressure cuff is cycling, move it to another finger or to the ear or toe, remove nail polish, shield it from bright light, keep the patient still, and only then suspect the cable or the device. The device must be stopped and help called when the reading is low and the patient looks unwell, when the signal cannot be recovered by moving the probe, or when the monitor shows a flat line with no pulsation, and the assessment then continues on the evidence of breathing, color, and conscious level. The operator error that misleads is to accept a good number from a bad signal, which is the false reassurance a poor-signal alarm was designed to prevent, and every alarm limit and signal-quality threshold must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The oxygen concentrator alarms for low concentration, and this failure is a slow decay rather than a sudden event. A pair of zeolite sieve beds adsorbs nitrogen from compressed air while the other bed releases it, so the gas leaving the machine is enriched in oxygen, and performance depends on the sieve material, on the compressor pressure, and on the cleanliness of the incoming air. A low-concentration alarm means the delivered gas has fallen below the set fraction, which happens when the intake filter is clogged with dust, when the compressor is worn, when the sieve beds are saturated with moisture, or when the machine runs at an altitude whose thin air reduces its output. The checks go in order: look at the patient first and confirm breathing and color, then check that tubing and cannula are connected and unkinked, then clean or replace the intake filter, then confirm the flow setting, then listen for the rhythmic puff of the sieve valves, and only then consider the internal sieve. The device must be stopped and help called when the patient becomes breathless, when saturation falls, when the alarm persists after the filter and tubing are checked, or when an oxygen analyzer confirms a low fraction, and the patient is then supported from a cylinder while the concentrator is investigated. The operator error most common is to place the machine against a wall or under a blanket so that its intake cannot breathe, and every flow setting, alarm threshold, and filter service interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The field variant of these machines keeps the same logic in smaller boxes and gives up automation for portability. A portable monitor offers fewer channels, a shorter battery life, and a tougher case, so it loses the central station and the archived trend and demands that the clinician watch the patient and write observations with a time beside each one. A transport ventilator may measure only pressure and show nothing beyond a gauge and a battery light, while the self-inflating bag has no alarm except the hand that feels the compliance of the lung and the eye that watches the chest, and it gives up the waveform and the automated self-test. The field infusion is a bag, a length of tubing, a roller clamp, and a nurse who counts drops, so it loses the pressure sensor that would catch an extravasation and the air detector that would catch a bubble, and it demands a visible drip chamber, a site inspected every hour, and a rate written on a label; every field limit, setting, and inspection interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The field substitutes for the remaining devices trade recorded proof for human attention. Peritoneal dialysis or continuous renal replacement therapy, shortened to CRRT after this first mention, replaces dialysate monitoring with pressure sensors or with the cloudy drained fluid of infection, so it demands a trained eye, a strict record of volumes, and a temperature check before every exchange. A pressure cooker or small benchtop autoclave has no printout and no failure code, so the cycle depends on a thermometer, a timer, and indicator tape that changes color only if steam reached it, and it demands a written log with the date, the load, the temperature reached, and the initials of the operator. A pharmaceutical refrigerator in the field is read by a person rather than by an alarm, and a portable oximeter and a concentrator on a battery or solar panel demand that the clinician reassess the patient and check the delivered gas rather than trust a number; the central sterile services department, or CSSD, is the partner of every clinic that reuses instruments, and every field range, temperature, and hold time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Alarms are repaired by maintenance, and maintenance is the unglamorous half of every sequence in this appendix. Electrodes dry out, lead wires break at the strain relief, gaskets harden, filters clog, batteries lose capacity, probes lose their optical window, and sensors drift out of calibration, and none of these failures announces itself until an alarm fires under a sick patient. The consumables that keep alarms meaningful include electrode pads, oximeter probes in each size, carbon dioxide sampling lines and water traps, ventilator circuits and filters, pump cassettes and dedicated tubing, autoclave gaskets and indicator tape, refrigerator loggers and thermometers, and concentrator filters. Cleaning belongs to the same discipline, because a probe wiped with the wrong agent clouds its window, a connector washed with a dripping cloth pushes fluid into the electronics, and a sensor fouled with dried gel or blood reads confidently and wrongly; the operator check before every use is to look at the machine, look at the patient, confirm that the two agree, and confirm that the alarm is audible rather than muted, and calibration intervals, cleaning agents, disinfection contact times, and replacement schedules must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Alarm fatigue is the hazard that turns every sequence in this appendix into theory, and it deserves to be named plainly. When a monitor alarms all night for reasons that never matter, staff learn that the alarm is noise, and the one alarm that matters is silenced with the rest, usually because a default limit is too wide or too narrow or a delay is so short that ordinary movement triggers it. The defenses are the deliberate setting of limits at the start of a shift, the review of which alarms fired and which were true, and the habit of saying aloud what is being silenced and why. A log that records the time, the alarm, the check performed, and the outcome is what lets a recurring fault be seen as a pattern rather than as a series of accidents, and the electronic health record, shortened to EHR after this first mention, is where that pattern becomes visible. Every device that alarms repeatedly without a patient cause is a device to remove from service rather than a device to tolerate, and alarm limit policy, silence duration, and the criteria for removal must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

To summarize, an alarm is a comparison and not a verdict, and the sequence that answers it always begins the same way: look at and speak to the patient before touching the machine. The hospital-grade device surrounds that step with printed records, self-tests, calibrated sensors, central displays, and maintenance schedules, while the field variant strips those away and asks instead for a hand on the bag, an eye on the chest, a written log, and a spare cylinder that is always full. The sounds differ, the thresholds differ, and the checks differ in their details, but the order does not: patient first, then the path from the patient to the device, then the settings, then the machine, and finally the point at which the device is stopped and help is called. The unglamorous work of filters, gaskets, batteries, probes, and calibration is what keeps an alarm worth hearing, and the discipline of setting limits deliberately is what keeps it from becoming noise. Every number in this appendix is a starting point for a local decision rather than a substitute for one, and each must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.
