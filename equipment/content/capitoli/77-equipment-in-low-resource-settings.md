# Chapter 77 — Equipment in Low-Resource Settings

This is the chapter where the two halves of this book meet, because equipment in a low-resource setting is the same machine asked to work without the invisible system that surrounds it in a large hospital. A device is never a self-contained object but the visible end of a chain that begins with energy, clean water, gas, sterile supplies, spare parts, documentation, and a trained person. The physical principle is a continuous flow in which energy enters, work is performed, and consumables are used, and if any one link is broken the flow stops whether or not the machine looks intact. A ventilator with no oxygen, a sterilizer with no power, and an analyzer with no reagent are all, in the only sense that matters, broken. Understanding the chain rather than the box is the skill this chapter teaches, and it explains why the same need is met by very different hardware depending on where the patient lies. The chapter therefore describes the subject twice, first the hospital-grade system inside its maintained envelope and then the field variant that survives on what is physically present.

A machine is an island of order in a world that tends toward disorder, and every function it performs costs energy and material that must be replaced. Seals harden, lubricants dry, filters load, batteries lose capacity, calibration drifts, and metal corrodes, and these processes continue whether the device is used or merely stored. Maintenance is therefore not a repair after failure but the routine replacement of the small consumable parts of order before they are exhausted. A device that is not maintained rarely fails suddenly; it declines quietly, and the decline is usually invisible to whoever reads the number on its display. A machine without a maintenance plan is already in a slow failure, and the plan must name who does the work, with which parts, at what interval, and with what record. All maintenance intervals and calibration frequencies named in this chapter must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The hospital-grade half of this book describes devices inside a support envelope that someone is paid to maintain. The building supplies stable electrical power, piped medical gases and vacuum, filtered air, purified water, drainage, steam, and a sterile processing department that returns instruments clean and packaged. A stores system holds consumables and rotates them before expiry, a biomedical workshop calibrates and repairs, a service contract brings the maker's technician, and the clinical staff are trained and supervised. The device is designed for exactly this envelope, and its tolerances for temperature, humidity, dust, and voltage are stated on the assumption that the envelope will hold. When it holds, the machine is reliable, and that reliability is a property of the system rather than of the box. The field variant is defined by what it loses from this list rather than by what it is.

The envelope is a set of physical conditions, and each one carries a number in the device documentation. Ambient temperature, relative humidity, airborne dust, altitude, supply voltage, supply frequency, and the depth and duration of power interruptions are all specified, and a device moved outside its stated range may fail at once or merely become inaccurate. A machine rated for a temperate clinic can overheat in a tropical room where the air is hot and wet, because heat cannot leave a cabinet when the surrounding air is already warm and moisture condenses on cold surfaces. Dust settles on cooling fins, blocks filters, and coats circuit boards, while humidity corrodes contacts and creates leakage paths across insulation. The stated operating temperature, humidity range, voltage tolerance, and frequency tolerance of every device must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The hospital-grade chains for consumables and maintenance are the parts clinicians notice least and depend on most. Single-use items arrive in sealed packaging with a lot number and an expiry date, are stored in a controlled store, and are issued against a requisition, while reagents, electrodes, and sensors are kept within their temperature range and watched with a log. Preventive service is scheduled at intervals taken from the maker's documentation, calibration is performed against traceable reference standards, and a device that fails a test is removed from service rather than returned to the ward with a warning. The whole apparatus depends on trained biomedical technicians, on spare parts held in stock, and on purchasing that treats the cost of ownership rather than the purchase price as the real cost. Remove the technicians, the standards, or the parts, and the service program becomes a folder of unsigned forms.

A low-resource setting is not defined by geography or poverty alone but by the thinness of that support envelope. It is a place where power is intermittent, where clean water is carried rather than piped, where the nearest calibrated reference standard is a long journey away, and where a consumable may take months to arrive if it arrives at all. The same ward may hold a modern device and a broken generator, and the device will be judged by the generator. The term covers a rural health post with a solar panel, a district hospital with a failing grid, a mobile clinic on a truck, and a field hospital set up after a disaster, and the common feature is that the machine must survive on what is physically present. In such a setting the clinical question and the logistical question become one, because a treatment that cannot be repeated next week is not a treatment the clinic can honestly offer.

Equipment donation is the largest single source of unusable hardware in low-resource settings, and the reason is that the gift is usually chosen by the giver rather than the receiver. A well-meaning institution, a maker clearing a product line, a charity, or a surplus program assembles devices that are no longer wanted, and the receiving clinic accepts because refusing appears ungrateful. The machines arrive without manuals, without spare parts, without the consumables that fit them, and sometimes without a single person trained to use them. They may be built for a different voltage, frequency, gas connector, or language, and the incompatibility is discovered only when the crate is opened. The result is not care but the appearance of care, paid for in storage space, staff time, and the false confidence that the need has been met.

Walk into the back room and you see the anatomy of a failed donation. Along one wall stand ventilators with cracked circuits and missing sensors, their screens dark, beside infusion pumps whose proprietary tubing no longer exists and dialysis machines whose water treatment was never installed. On the floor are centrifuges with seized bearings, sterilizers with corroded chambers, X-ray units whose tubes have expired, and an ultrasound console whose probe cable was cut by a door. In the corner are boxes of consumables for machines that left the clinic years ago, their packaging faded and their dates long past, and a shelf holds manuals in a language no one reads. Every object in that room consumed money, freight, and hope on the way in, and it stands there because no one asked the questions that would have stopped it.

Incompatibility is physical, and it begins with electricity. A device built for a supply of one frequency will run fast or hot on another, and a motor, a compressor, or a centrifuge designed for the wrong waveform can overheat, vibrate, or fail. A device built for one nominal voltage may be destroyed by a surge or may refuse to start on a supply that sags, and the plug on its cord may not fit the socket at all. Gas connectors differ between countries, so a machine that needs a particular oxygen inlet may be impossible to connect without an adapter that no one stocks. Consumables are often proprietary, which means the device is useless without an item that only the maker supplies, and the supply route may be closed by cost, distance, or policy. Frequencies, voltages, connector standards, and consumable specifications must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before any donated device is accepted.

Refurbished equipment occupies the middle ground and carries a middle risk. Refurbishment can be genuine, with worn parts replaced, software updated, performance tested, and a warranty given, and it can also be a coat of paint over a machine whose internal wear is unknown. A refurbished device has a history that cannot be fully verified, so its remaining life, its calibration, and its failure mode are estimates rather than facts. Proprietary software locks, activation codes, and dealer-only service tools can make an otherwise sound machine impossible to repair locally, and a device whose maker has withdrawn support may never receive another spare part. The purchase price is lower than new, but the total cost of ownership may be higher because the first repair needs an imported assembly that takes months to arrive. The condition, the warranty, the availability of parts, and the service arrangements must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before purchase.

Needs assessment is the discipline that prevents the dead machine room, and it is performed before the crate is opened rather than after. The first question is clinical: which patient, with which condition, will this device treat, and is that condition actually common here? The second is operational: who will operate it, who will be on duty at night, and who will be accountable when it fails? The third is logistical: is the power compatible, is the consumable affordable for years, is a spare part obtainable, and is a technician within reach? The fourth is institutional: does the device duplicate something already present, and does the clinic have the space, security, and budget to keep it running? If any answer is no, the correct decision is to decline the device and, where possible, to ask instead for training, consumables, or a service contract.

Refusing a gift is difficult because it feels like refusing help, and the courage to do it is a clinical skill rather than a social failing. The refusal is easier with a written policy, adopted before any donation arrives, that states what the clinic can accept and what it cannot, so that the decision belongs to the institution rather than to the individual on the loading dock. The policy names the required voltage, frequency, gas connectors, language of the manual, availability of consumables, and the presence of a service plan, and it requires a signature from the person who will maintain the device. A polite refusal can be paired with a specific alternative, such as a request for a different device, for spare parts, or for the funds to train a technician. The person who accepts an unsupported device has, in effect, agreed to maintain it forever without the means to do so.

Robustness begins with temperature, because heat is the commonest killer of electronics in the tropics. A device dissipates its waste heat into the surrounding air, and the rate of transfer depends on the difference between the temperature of its components and the temperature of the room, so a hot room narrows the margin and a blocked vent removes it. Battery chemistry is temperature sensitive too, because heat accelerates the permanent loss of capacity while cold reduces the available current, so a battery that reads full in the afternoon may refuse to start a compressor in the early morning. Dust and humidity compound the problem, because dust absorbs moisture and moisture carries dust into places it should not reach; particles insulate cooling surfaces, clog filters, abrade bearings, and bridge contacts, while condensed water on a circuit board causes leakage currents and corrosion. A device intended for a hot, dusty climate should be rated for the ambient temperature, humidity, and dust it will actually meet, and those ratings must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The practical defenses are shade, ventilation, sealed enclosures with clean filters, a cover when the device is idle, and the habit of never opening a case in a dusty room.

Electrical supply in a low-resource setting is rarely a steady wave at the nominal voltage. It sags when a large load switches on, surges when that load drops, flickers as connections arc, and may change frequency as a generator governor hunts, and a supply shared with welding equipment, pumps, and air conditioners carries disturbances a hospital-grade device was never designed to tolerate. A transformer or a motor responds with heat, vibration, and torque that vary with the square of the voltage, which is why a compressor or a centrifuge is more vulnerable than a simple lamp. Electronic devices are protected by a voltage stabilizer, a surge protector, or a line conditioner, and each of these is itself a device that can fail and must be maintained. The acceptable voltage range, the frequency range, and the type of protection required must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A power interruption tests the whole chain, and the behavior of a device during the gap between the loss of mains power and its restoration decides whether a patient is harmed. Some devices simply stop, which is safe if the clinician knows and dangerous if the clinician does not; others switch to an internal battery, continue for a defined period, and then stop with an alarm; others restart automatically and may resume in an unexpected state. An uninterruptible power supply holds a battery and an inverter and carries the load for a short time, long enough to finish a critical action or move the patient to another supply. A generator takes several seconds to start and settle, and a device that cannot tolerate that gap needs a battery between it and the wall. The runtime, the transfer time, the battery test interval, and the alarm threshold of any backup supply must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The field variant of this chapter is the clinic that has accepted the chain is thin and has chosen its equipment to match. Its power comes from a solar panel, a battery, and an inverter, with a small generator as a last resort, and its devices are chosen for low current draw and tolerance of a sagging supply. Its oxygen comes from a concentrator driven by that power or a cylinder refilled at a distant plant, and its suction comes from a foot pump or a battery pump when the wall has no vacuum. Its sterilization is a pressure cooker or a small electric autoclave when power allows, and its laboratory is a set of tests that need no cold chain and no analyzer. The field clinic gives up speed, throughput, and the ability to run several complex procedures at once, and in exchange it gains the ability to keep working when the grid does not. What it demands instead is routine: batteries charged, filters cleaned, consumables counted, and every machine tested before the patient arrives.

The consumable is the true constraint on any device, because it must be bought again and again while the machine is bought once. A device is a lock and its consumable is the key, and when the maker controls the key the clinic's ability to treat patients depends on a supply route it does not own. The recurring cost of consumables, reagents, electrodes, tubing, filters, and test strips usually exceeds the purchase price over the life of the device, and a budget that covers only the purchase has funded a demonstration rather than a service. The practical test before buying anything is to ask whether the consumable can be obtained next month, next year, and after the donor has gone home. Where the answer is uncertain, the clinician should prefer a device whose consumable is generic, locally produced, or reusable under a defined protocol. The cost, availability, shelf life, and storage conditions of every consumable must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is adopted.

Clinics that cannot rely on a single supplier develop strategies, and each carries a risk. Standardization on a few device families reduces the variety of consumables that must be stocked and makes training and repair easier, but it also creates dependence on the one supplier chosen. Generic consumables may fit and perform correctly at a fraction of the price, or they may fit loosely, leak, or deliver the wrong volume, and the difference is often invisible until a patient is harmed. Reuse of items labeled for single use happens wherever supply is short, and it is safe only when the item can be cleaned, disinfected or sterilized, inspected, and confirmed to function, and when the protocol is written and followed. Reprocessing a catheter, a filter, or a sensor beyond its designed life introduces infection, inaccurate measurement, and sudden failure, and the decision must never be left to improvisation at the bedside. Reuse protocols, cleaning agents, sterilization cycles, and maximum reuse counts must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Cleaning and reprocessing are the unglamorous core of safe equipment use, and they compete for the same scarce inputs as everything else. Cleaning removes visible soil and the organic film that protects microorganisms, and it must precede disinfection or sterilization, because a sterilizer cannot penetrate a crust of dried blood. Disinfection with a chemical depends on the concentration, the contact time, and the temperature, and a reused bath that has become dilute or dirty may look correct while doing nothing. Water is the hidden input of this work, because hard water leaves scale that harbors soil and blocks fine channels, water carrying organic matter recontaminates what has just been cleaned, and water of the wrong acidity corrodes metal. Sterilization by steam depends on saturated steam under pressure reaching every surface for a defined time, and many hospital cycles use 134 degrees Celsius for a short hold or 121 degrees Celsius for a longer one, values that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A chemical indicator and a biological indicator are the evidence that the cycle worked, and a sterilizer with no indicator, no log, and no maintenance produces confidence rather than sterility. Every disinfectant concentration, contact time, rinse water quality, sterilization temperature, pressure, and holding time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Training is the difference between a machine that is used and a machine that is merely present, and it must be planned at the same time as the purchase. The operator manual explains the controls, the setup, the meaning of the alarms, and what to do when one sounds, and it is useless if it exists only in a language the staff do not read or in a file no one can open. The service manual is a different document and contains the maintenance schedule, the calibration procedure, the spare parts list, the circuit descriptions, and the fault codes; it must be obtained at purchase, because years later the maker may no longer supply it and the local technician will need it more than ever. Training must be repeated, because staff move and memory fades, and a single session at installation does not survive a change of shift, let alone a change of staff. A written quick guide in the local language beside the machine, and one named person responsible for training, keep the knowledge alive after the trainer has left. The training required and the interval for refresher training must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Local repair capacity is the most valuable and most neglected asset in the system, because a clinic that repairs its own equipment is independent in a way no stock of spare machines can match. The technician needs tools, a multimeter, a test load, reference standards, a workspace, light, a computer, and access to the internet where it exists; more importantly, the technician needs the service manual, a stock of the parts that wear, and the authority to take a device out of service. Training a technician on a specific machine is far cheaper than importing a technician for every fault, and a person who repairs a device in the clinic learns its behavior in a way no course can teach. The alternative to local repair is cannibalization, in which one machine is dismantled to keep another alive, and while this can be a legitimate last resort it is also how a fleet of four devices becomes a fleet of one. The spare parts to hold in stock, the test equipment required, and the documentation needed for repair must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before purchase.

The failure modes of a low-resource setting are often quieter than those of a well-resourced one, and quiet failure is the more dangerous kind. A device may run but drift out of calibration, so that its readings are systematically wrong while the machine appears healthy; a flowmeter may stick, a scale may read low, a thermometer may lag, and a pump may deliver less than its display promises. A device may fail intermittently, working in the morning and stopping in the afternoon heat, which leads to the false conclusion that it has been repaired when it is merely between failures. A device may be missing a small part no one can name, such as a washer, a fuse, a battery cover, or a sensor cap, and that trivial absence stops a substantial machine. A device may also fail because its consumable is exhausted, which the operator mistakes for a fault and which no amount of repair will solve. The characteristic alarms here include the low battery alarm no one can hear, the alarm whose message is in a foreign language, and the alarm silenced so often that the staff no longer register it, and every alarm threshold must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The operator's check before every use is short, and it is the only thing standing between a hidden fault and a patient. It begins with the power source: that the plug is seated, the cable is not frayed, the supply is live, and the battery holds enough charge for the expected task, followed by switching the device on and confirming that it completes its self test without an unexplained error. Next comes the physical condition of the device and its accessories, the tubing, seals, cuffs, probes, and connectors, which must be intact, clean, and correctly fitted. Then the consumables are checked for correct type, intact packaging, and an unexpired date, and the settings are compared with the clinical requirement rather than with the last patient's convenience. The alarms are tested where the device allows it, because an alarm that cannot sound is worse than no alarm at all, and the result is written in a log the next shift can read. When any check fails, the device must be taken out of service, tagged, and reported rather than used with a private mental reservation.

Some equipment survives in a low-resource setting, and the reasons are consistent enough to serve as a design brief. The survivors are simple, with few settings and few parts, so a fault is visible and a repair is possible with common tools; they are mechanical where the function permits it, because a hand pump, a gravity infusion set, a manual resuscitator, and a spring-loaded scale have no electronics to fail and no battery to charge. They are modular, so a failed component can be replaced instead of the whole device, and they share consumables with other machines so that the store carries one kind of tubing rather than six. They tolerate a wide temperature range, a dusty room, and a sagging supply, and they accept generic consumables that can be bought in the local market. This is also the case for owning less, because every extra machine consumes power, space, security, training, consumables, and the technician's attention, and attention is the scarcest resource of all. A clinic that owns three complex devices and cannot maintain any of them treats fewer patients than a clinic that owns one device and maintains it flawlessly, since a device that is present but unreliable is worse than an absent one because it invites decisions based on false information. The proper test of a purchase is not what the device can do in a demonstration but what it will do in this clinic on an ordinary Tuesday.

Judgment at the point of purchase is the discipline this chapter is trying to teach, and it reduces to a few questions asked honestly and in advance. What clinical problem will this device solve, and how often does that problem occur here? Who will operate it, who will clean it, who will calibrate it, and who will repair it when it fails? What does it consume, where does that consumable come from, and can the clinic pay for it for the next five years? What does it need from the building, in power, water, gas, air, or space, and does the building actually provide it? What happens during an outage, and what is the plan if the device stops in the middle of a procedure? The answers define the total cost of ownership, which is the purchase price plus the consumables plus the training plus the spare parts plus the eventual disposal, and it is this total, not the price on the invoice, that decides whether the clinic can afford the device at all.

To summarize, equipment in a low-resource setting is the same medicine practiced against a thinner support envelope, and the machine is only the visible part of a chain that includes energy, consumables, documentation, spare parts, and trained people. The hospital-grade half of every chapter assumes that chain, and the field half rebuilds it from solar power, batteries, hand pumps, generic supplies, and local skill. Donated and refurbished equipment fails when it is incompatible, unsupported, or unrepairable, and the answer is a needs assessment before acceptance and the courage to refuse a gift that would cost more than it gives. Robustness means tolerance of heat, cold, dust, humidity, and a swinging voltage, and behavior during an outage that the clinician can predict. Consumables are the true constraint, maintenance and cleaning are the true work, and the operator's check before every use is the true safeguard. The equipment that survives is simple, modular, repairable, and modest in its demands, and the wisest clinic is often the one that owns less and keeps it working.
