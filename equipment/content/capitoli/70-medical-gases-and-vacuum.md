# Chapter 70 — Medical Gases and Vacuum

Every hospital depends on services that no patient ever sees, and medical gases and vacuum are the most invisible of them all. A gas is drawn from a source, pushed through pipes, delivered at a keyed outlet beside the bed, and removed again through a second network that pulls rather than pushes. The physical principle behind the whole system is a difference in pressure, because a gas always moves from a region of higher pressure toward a region of lower pressure, and every regulator, valve, and pump exists only to create and control that difference. Suction obeys the same physics, because it is not a pulling force but simply a pressure lower than the atmosphere, so the ordinary pressure of the room pushes air, fluid, and debris toward the machine. Understanding this one idea makes the rest of the chapter legible, because supply, distribution, delivery, and removal are four versions of the same principle. This chapter describes its subject twice, beginning with the piped system of a large hospital and then rebuilding the same functions in a frontier clinic from cylinders, a concentrator, and a suction pump with a battery.

A gas is compressible, and that property is what makes storage and transport possible. When a fixed quantity of gas is squeezed into a smaller volume at a steady temperature, its pressure rises in proportion, and when the pressure is released the gas expands again toward the pressure of the atmosphere. This is why a steel cylinder no larger than a forearm can hold many hundreds of liters of oxygen, and why the gas leaves it at a pressure that would burst a thin tube if it were not reduced first. Flow through a pipe is driven by the pressure difference between its ends and opposed by friction against the walls, so a long, narrow, or kinked line delivers less than a short, wide, straight one. A regulator reduces the high pressure in the cylinder to a low working pressure, and a flowmeter turns that pressure into a measured volume for the patient. Every pressure and flow named in this chapter must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, because the numbers differ between systems and between countries.

A modern hospital stocks five gases, and each has a role that follows from its physical and chemical behavior. Oxygen raises the partial pressure of oxygen in the alveolus, which restores the gradient into the blood, and it is used wherever the tissues are short of oxygen. Medical air is filtered, dried, and tested atmospheric air, used to drive ventilators, to blend with oxygen, and to run pneumatic tools, because it avoids washing nitrogen out of the lungs. Nitrous oxide is an anesthetic and analgesic gas with a faint sweet smell, used in operating theatres and, mixed with oxygen, for pain relief in labor and minor procedures. Carbon dioxide is used to inflate the abdomen for laparoscopic surgery and the colon for endoscopy, because it is absorbed quickly and does not support combustion. Nitrogen is an inert gas used to drive surgical power tools, to purge pipelines and vessels, and to provide a controlled atmosphere in some laboratory and cryogenic applications. The purity and identity of every one of these gases must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Gas purity is a clinical requirement rather than a marketing detail, because a trace of oil, water, or another gas can harm a patient or destroy a machine. Medical gases are manufactured, filtered, dried, and analyzed against defined limits for moisture, oxygen content, carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide, and particulate matter, and each batch is certified before it leaves the filling plant. A cylinder or a pipeline carries the identity of its gas, established by the label, the connector, and the certificate rather than by smell or appearance. The most dangerous failure in the whole system is a cross-connection, in which a pipe or a cylinder delivers the wrong gas to a patient or a device, because the error is silent and the consequence can be fatal. It can arise from a wrongly labeled pipe, a pigtail fitted to the wrong cylinder, a manifold assembled by an untrained person, or a regulator forced onto the wrong valve. The result may appear as a ventilator that alarms for no reason, an oxygen saturation that will not rise, or a sedated patient who does not wake, so the clinician must suspect the gas supply whenever the clinical picture does not fit. The defense is a chain of verification in which every cylinder is identified before connection, every manifold is checked after a change, and every new or altered outlet is tested with an analyzer.

The cylinder is the oldest and most portable container of medical gas, and it is a pressure vessel first and a clinical object second. It is drawn from seamless steel or aluminum, tested at a pressure well above its working pressure, and stamped near the neck with the gas, the filling pressure, the water capacity, the test date, and the owner. The valve at the top is a metal block with an outlet port, a stem, and a safety device that releases gas if the internal pressure rises beyond a set limit, which must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The connection to a regulator is protected by a pin index system on small cylinders and a diameter index safety system on larger ones, so that a regulator built for one gas cannot be seated on a cylinder of another. A compressible washer sits between valve and regulator, and a worn, missing, or greasy washer defeats the interlock and invites a leak or a fire. If a regulator does not seat easily, the operator must stop and find the reason rather than force it.

Cylinders come in a family of sizes, from small units carried by hand to tall ones that stand beside a bed, and the size is chosen by how much gas a task needs and how far it must travel. The label on the shoulder names the gas, and the body may carry a color, but color conventions differ between countries and suppliers, so the printed label is the authority and the color is only a first hint. A cylinder must be stored upright and secured with a chain, a strap, or a bracket, because a full cylinder is a heavy vessel of stored energy that can become a missile if the valve is sheared off. The valve cap must be in place whenever the cylinder is moved, the cylinder must never be lifted or rolled by the valve, and travel is made on a trolley, in a rack, or in a padded carrier secured to the vehicle. Heat raises the internal pressure and can open the safety device, so a cylinder must never be left in a closed vehicle in the sun or beside an oven. A cylinder that is corroded, dented, or overdue for its hydraulic pressure test must be quarantined and returned to the supplier rather than refilled, and because a cylinder has no alarm of its own, the gauge and the written record are the only warning that the supply is falling.

The large hospital rarely fills its pipeline from cylinders, and instead stores oxygen as a cold liquid in a bulk tank outside the building. Oxygen becomes a pale blue liquid at about minus 183 degrees Celsius, a temperature that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and in that state it occupies a small fraction of the volume it will occupy as a gas. The tank is a double-walled vessel with a vacuum between the walls, and the small amount of heat that leaks in boils a little of the liquid, builds pressure, and pushes gas into the pipeline through an evaporator. The installation includes a pressure builder, a safety relief valve, a contents gauge, and a filling connection that only a trained supplier may use. The hazards are cold and concentration, because spilled liquid causes frostbite and the boiling gas enriches the surrounding air, so the area is fenced, ventilated, and kept clear of vehicles and ignition sources. A bulk system carries low-level and low-pressure alarms, and their thresholds must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Where a pipeline is impossible or too expensive, oxygen is made on site from the air itself by pressure swing adsorption. The active material is a synthetic zeolite, a mineral with an enormous internal surface of tiny pores that adsorbs nitrogen in preference to oxygen. A compressor pushes filtered air into one of two sieve beds until that bed is loaded with nitrogen, while the oxygen-rich gas that passes through is collected in a receiver, and then the beds swap roles so that the flow never stops. A small bedside concentrator delivers a stream of about 90 to 96 per cent oxygen, a value that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and a hospital-scale plant of the same principle can fill a pipeline when it is built with dryers, receivers, monitors, and a backup supply. The plant depends on electricity, clean intake air, and serviceable compressors, so it is only as reliable as its power and its maintenance. Dust clogs the intake filter, altitude reduces the total oxygen delivered at any given flow, and a discharged battery stops the machine without warning, so its alarms for concentration, pressure, and temperature are the clinician's only notice that the product is failing.

Medical air is made in the hospital by compressing the atmosphere, and the engineering is dominated by what must be removed from it. A compressor draws outside air through a filter, compresses it, cools it, dries it, and passes it through a series of filters that trap oil aerosol, water droplets, and particles before it enters the receiver and the pipeline. The drying step matters because water in a pipeline corrodes the pipe, feeds microorganisms, and can block a ventilator or freeze in a cold line. The compressor itself is a hazard, because a worn machine can carry oil mist into the air and a poorly placed intake can draw in vehicle exhaust and raise the carbon monoxide level. A dental or workshop compressor must never be connected to a medical air outlet, and the medical air plant must never be used to fill breathing cylinders. Its alarms for low pressure, high dew point, and carbon monoxide are the backbone of its safety, and each threshold must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Nitrous oxide and carbon dioxide are supplied in cylinders and through pipelines, and each brings a hazard that follows from its chemistry. Nitrous oxide supports combustion as vigorously as oxygen, so it must be stored apart from grease, oil, and flammable gas and handled with the same discipline. It diffuses into any closed space faster than nitrogen leaves it, so it can expand a loop of bowel, a pneumothorax, or the cuff of a breathing tube, and it can cause diffusion hypoxia at the end of an anesthetic. Carbon dioxide is heavier than air and can collect in low places, and when it is insufflated into a body cavity it can enter the bloodstream and cause a gas embolism or a dangerous rise in carbon dioxide in the blood. Chronic exposure to leaking anesthetic gas is an occupational hazard for theatre staff, which is why scavenging and ventilation are part of the equipment rather than an afterthought. Both gases must be identified by label and connector, never by color or smell, and all concentrations, flows, and exposure limits must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The pipeline is the hospital's circulatory system, and its material and workmanship decide whether the gas arrives clean. Medical gas pipe is usually copper or stainless steel that has been cleaned and capped for oxygen service, because a trace of oil left inside a pipe becomes fuel in an oxygen-rich stream. Joints are brazed or welded under a flow of inert gas so that no oxide scale forms inside, and the pipe is supported, sloped, and protected from vibration and thermal movement. The system is divided into zones by shut-off valves so that a fire or a leak can be isolated without cutting the whole hospital, and the valves are labeled, lockable, and known to the engineering staff. The pipe is labeled with the gas name at intervals and at every valve and wall, and colored bands are applied according to a national standard, but a label can fade or be applied wrongly during a repair, so color is a first hint and never proof. Pressure falls along a pipeline as flow rises, so the diameter and the layout are chosen to keep the delivery pressure at the bedside within its design range, often near 50 pounds per square inch, a value that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The terminal unit is the point where the invisible system becomes a device in the hand. It is a gas-specific socket built so that only the matching connector can be inserted, using a diameter index safety system and a shaped probe, and it opens only when the connector is fully seated and locked. Some outlets supply oxygen, some supply air, some supply vacuum, and their faces look alike, so the keying is the only protection against a wrong connection. A flowmeter, a vacuum regulator, or a humidifier hangs from the outlet, and the operator must check that it is seated, that gas flows when the valve is opened, and that the reading moves freely. A terminal unit that leaks, hisses, or refuses to lock must be taken out of service and reported, because a forced connector damages the keying and creates a future cross-connection. If a regulator does not seat easily, the operator must stop and find the reason rather than force it, because the resistance is information rather than an obstacle. All flows, pressures, and suction levels set at the bedside must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A piped system carries alarms because its failure is silent and its consequences are immediate. A master alarm sits in the engineering office or at the plant, and an area alarm panel sits at the entrance to each ward or department, and both watch the pressure of each gas, the vacuum level, and sometimes the oxygen concentration. A low-pressure alarm sounds when the supply falls below its set threshold, and a high-pressure alarm sounds when it rises above the design limit, and both thresholds must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The characteristic alarm is a repeating electronic tone with a flashing lamp and a printed message naming the gas, and a vacuum alarm is often a slower pulse with a different pitch so that the two are not confused. The correct response is never to silence the alarm and continue, but to establish whether patients are receiving gas, to move them to cylinders or portable suction if the supply is failing, and to call the engineer. The alarm does not always mean that the supply has failed, because a closed zone valve, a leaking terminal unit, or a maintenance isolation produces the same reading, and a silenced alarm that is not investigated is the common operator error in this system.

Where a pipeline is fed from cylinders, the cylinders stand in a manifold, which is a header that connects a bank of them to the supply line through a common pipe. A manifold usually has a primary bank and a reserve bank, and an automatic changeover valve senses the falling pressure of the primary bank and switches to the reserve without interrupting the flow. A non-return valve prevents gas from the reserve bank from flowing back into the empty primary bank, and a pressure switch triggers an alarm when a bank is nearly empty so that the change can be made in daylight rather than at night. The pigtails that join each cylinder to the header have their own check valves, and the operator changes a bank by closing the empty cylinders, removing the pigtails, fitting full cylinders with new washers, and opening them slowly. The common error is to leave both banks open or to replace a cylinder while the reserve is already empty, which stops the supply with no warning at the bedside. The changeover pressure, the alarm threshold, and the pigtail torque must all be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The vacuum system is the mirror image of the gas system, and it begins with a pump that removes air from a closed space. A vacuum is not a substance and not a pulling force, but simply a pressure below that of the atmosphere, and it exists because the pump moves molecules out of the receiver faster than they leak back in. The clinical effect comes from the pressure difference between the room and the patient's airway or wound, so the ordinary pressure of the room pushes fluid and debris along the tubing toward the collection canister. Several kinds of pump are used, including rotary vane, liquid ring, dry claw, and diaphragm pumps, and each has its own maintenance and its own failure mode. A receiver, which is a large tank on the suction side of the pump, stores vacuum so that a sudden demand does not drop the level at the bedside, and a filter and a trap protect the pump from liquid and debris. Vacuum levels and flows must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A central vacuum plant has more parts than most staff ever see, and each part earns its place. The pumps are duplicated so that one can be serviced while the other runs, and they exhaust to the outside air, because the gas they remove may carry microorganisms, anesthetic vapor, or droplets. A receiver and a condensate trap collect liquid before it reaches the pump, and a bacterial filter and a set of inlet filters protect both the pump and the pipeline. Valves allow a section of pipe to be isolated, and a monitoring panel watches the vacuum level and the running hours of each pump. The maintenance routine includes changing pump oil or checking the dry pump seals, cleaning or replacing filters, draining condensate, testing the alarms, and recording the running hours so that a worn pump is rebuilt before it fails. The plant carries alarms for low vacuum, high temperature, and pump failure, and a blocked filter or a closed isolation valve is the common error that reduces suction at the bedside long before any alarm sounds.

At the bedside, central vacuum is tamed by a regulator that lets the clinician choose how strongly the suction pulls. The regulator is a valve with a gauge and a control knob, and it is connected to a collection canister that holds what is removed, with a shut-off float that seals the canister when it is full so that fluid cannot enter the pipeline. The suction pressure is set in millimeters of mercury, and the level depends on the task, with a gentle range for a tracheal tube and a stronger range for a large wound drain, and every level must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The tubing must be wide enough and short enough to allow the flow the task requires, and the catheter or tip must be chosen for the airway or the wound. The characteristic alarm in this setting is often not a sound but a rising float or a falling gauge, and an overfilled canister, a loose lid, or a kinked tube is the commonest reason that suction seems weak. The hazards are tissue injury from excessive vacuum, infection from a contaminated canister, and fluid reaching the central pump, so the operator must never leave a catheter in place with the vacuum applied.

Testing is what separates a safe gas system from a dangerous one, and it is done by trained staff with calibrated instruments. The pipework is pressure tested for strength and for leaks, purged with oil-free gas or nitrogen to remove air and debris, and then tested for cross-connection at every outlet. Purity is measured for oxygen concentration, moisture, carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide, and particulate matter, and the results are recorded against the design specification. A verified identity label and a dated certificate are attached to each outlet, and any outlet that fails is locked and repaired before a patient can be connected. The maintenance program repeats parts of this work at defined intervals, checks the alarms and the changeover valves, services the pumps and compressors, and tests each cylinder before it is refilled. Oxygen concentrators and medical air plants are serviced on their own schedules, with filters, sieve beds, dryers, and analyzers treated as consumables with a finite life. Every test pressure, purge volume, purity limit, and test interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The consumables of a gas and vacuum system are small, cheap, and decisive, and their absence stops the system as surely as a broken pump. They include the washers and seals for cylinder connections, the filters and traps for vacuum lines, the humidifier chambers and sterile water for oxygen, the suction canisters and tubing, the flowmeter tubes and floats, and the labels and tags that identify a checked cylinder. Reusable items such as regulators, flowmeters, and suction regulators must be cleaned and disinfected between patients, while tubing, canisters, chambers, and filters are usually single-patient items discarded when soiled or at the interval the maker specifies. The store must hold a reserve of these items, and the reserve must be rotated so that nothing expires on the shelf. A washer reused until it is hard and cracked, or a suction canister refilled instead of replaced, is a failure waiting for a patient. Cleaning and disinfection methods, replacement intervals, and expiry dates must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Cleaning in this chapter has two meanings, because the equipment must be clean both for the patient and for the gas it carries. Externally, regulators, flowmeters, terminal units, and suction regulators are wiped with a cloth dampened with a compatible disinfectant, never immersed, and allowed to dry before use. Internally, the danger is water and biological material, because a humidifier chamber, a suction canister, or a vacuum trap that is refilled rather than replaced becomes a reservoir of microorganisms. Suction tubing and canisters that are reused must be emptied, cleaned, and disinfected or sterilized by a validated method, and the exhaust filter on a vacuum pump must be changed on schedule. Staff who handle suction, condensate, and used tubing must wear personal protective equipment, and hand hygiene follows every contact. The disinfectant, contact time, and sterilization cycle for each item must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Fire is the hazard that makes medical gases different from every other supply, and the rules that prevent it are absolute. Oxygen and nitrous oxide are oxidizers rather than fuels, but they lower the temperature at which other materials ignite and make any fire burn hotter and faster, and a patient's clothing, bedding, and hair in an enriched room can flare with terrifying speed. Oil, grease, and petroleum jelly must never touch a cylinder valve, a regulator, a flowmeter, or the face of a patient receiving oxygen, and alcohol-based hand rub must dry completely before the hands go near a mask. Smoking, open flames, and electrical sparks are forbidden wherever these gases are stored or used, and cylinders must be kept away from radiators, heaters, and direct sun. The exhaust of a vacuum pump and the vent of a bulk tank must be sited so that they cannot draw gas or vapor back into the building. A fire in an oxygen-enriched space demands immediate removal of the oxygen source if that can be done safely, alerting of the fire response, and evacuation, and the decision to close a zone valve belongs to the engineering staff who know the system.

In a mobile or frontier clinic the entire infrastructure disappears, and the same clinical needs must be met from what can be carried, charged, and repaired. The field version gives up the pipeline, the bulk tank, the manifold, the central vacuum, and the engineering department, and in exchange it demands discipline, calculation, and a maintenance routine performed by the clinician. Oxygen usually comes from a portable concentrator charged by a solar panel and a battery, with one or two cylinders held as the reserve for resuscitation and transport. The field supply chain is the real machine, and it begins long before the patient arrives, because cylinders must be ordered, carried upright, stored in the shade and off the ground, chained against a wall, and checked on arrival for label, pressure, damage, and test date. Refilling is done by a cascade from a larger cylinder or by a supplier visit, and a cylinder that is refilled without a new washer and a leak test is a hazard waiting to happen. The concentrator demands clean intake filters, a shaded position, a hard level surface, and a battery that is cycled and charged according to plan, and its output should be checked with an oxygen analyzer whenever one is available. The reserve cylinder must be treated as untouchable except for resuscitation, and the stock must be counted at every shift so that the reserve is real.

Field suction is built from a pump, a canister, and a conscience about cross-infection. A battery-powered suction unit works well for airway clearance and for small wound drainage, but its capacity is limited, its battery must be charged from the same solar system as the oxygen concentrator, and its filter and trap must be emptied and cleaned after every use. A foot pump or a hand pump has no battery at all and is the fallback when power fails, and a large syringe with a catheter is the last resort for a newborn airway, though it must never replace a functioning machine when one exists. The patient's own cough, positioning, and chest physiotherapy remain the most reliable forms of airway clearance and must be used before any machine. The field check before every use follows the same order as the hospital check, because the operator must confirm the gas or vacuum, inspect the cylinder or battery, test the seal or the suction, set and read the level, and keep the reserve ready. The demands of the field are a routine that no engineer will perform for the clinic, so filters are cleaned, batteries are cycled, tubing is inspected, and a written log is kept by the clinical team itself. All suction levels, filter intervals, and charging times must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

To summarize, medical gases and vacuum are a single system of pressure, identity, and removal, and its parts are only as safe as the checks around them. A gas is stored as a compressed or liquefied fluid, moved through labeled and keyed pipework, delivered at a terminal unit, and monitored by alarms whose thresholds and meanings must be known before they sound. The gases differ in chemistry and in use, and each brings its own hazard, from the fire risk of oxygen and nitrous oxide to the expansion and embolism risk of nitrous oxide and carbon dioxide. The vacuum system is the same physics reversed, with pumps, receivers, traps, and filters protecting a bedside regulator that must be set to the task. Testing, purging, identity verification, cleaning, and maintenance are not optional extras but the substance of the discipline, and the field variant replaces infrastructure with logistics, a battery, and a written routine. Whatever the setting, the operator must identify the gas, calculate the supply, set and confirm the level, watch the patient, and keep a reserve that is genuinely in reserve.
