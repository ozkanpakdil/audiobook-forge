# Chapter 28 — Nuclear Medicine and PET

Nuclear medicine images radiation that begins inside the patient rather than a beam aimed through the patient, and that inversion explains nearly everything about the machines that follow. A radionuclide is an unstable atomic nucleus that emits a particle or a photon as it reaches a more stable state, and when it is attached to a compound the body handles in a particular way the combination is called a radiopharmaceutical. Such an agent is a tracer rather than a drug, because the quantity given is far too small to have any pharmacological effect, yet its photons escape through the overlying tissue to a detector outside the body. The detector records the position and the number of those photons, so the picture maps where the tracer concentrated rather than showing a shadow of what stood in the beam, which is why these images are grainy compared with a radiograph. Every tracer, activity, and imaging time named in this chapter must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A nucleus can decay in several ways, and the choice of radionuclide follows from the manner of decay and the energy of what comes out. In one common mode a nucleus rearranges its own protons and neutrons and emits a photon of pure electromagnetic energy, called a gamma ray, whose predictable energy can be counted and located from outside the body. In positron emission a proton becomes a neutron and a positron, and the positron travels a few millimeters through tissue before meeting an electron, after which the two annihilate and produce two photons of 511 kiloelectron volts flying apart almost exactly opposite to each other, an energy that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The half-life is the time in which half of the atoms present will have decayed, and there is also a biological half-life, the time the body takes to clear half of the compound, with the physical and biological values combining into an effective half-life always shorter than either alone, all of which must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Production begins far from the imaging room, in a nuclear reactor or a cyclotron. A reactor makes nuclides by bombarding a stable target with neutrons, which is how iodine-131 and several therapeutic agents are produced, while a cyclotron accelerates charged particles into a target and is the usual source of fluorine-18 and other positron emitters. Most hospitals never see either machine, because the commonest imaging nuclide, technetium-99m, arrives through a generator on a bench in the hot laboratory, where molybdenum-99 with a half-life of about 66 hours decays into technetium-99m with a half-life of about 6 hours. The eluate is washed out with sterile saline once or twice a day, and the first elution carries the most activity. Every generator has a limit on the molybdenum that may break through into the eluate, and that breakthrough must be tested before the eluate reaches a patient, because molybdenum adds dose without adding image, with the elution efficiency and breakthrough limit confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The dose calibrator turns a prescription in megabecquerels into a measured volume in a syringe, and it is the first quality control device of the department. It is a gas-filled ionization chamber with a well in the top, and when a radioactive source sits in the well the radiation ionizes the gas and the resulting current is displayed as activity. The reading depends on the geometry of the source, so a syringe lying flat, a syringe upright in a holder, and a vial of different shape give different readings for the same radioactivity. The commonest operator error in nuclear medicine is to measure a dose in one geometry and inject it in another, or to leave the cap and needle shield in the well and count their attenuation as part of the dose. A daily constancy check with a sealed long-lived source proves the instrument has not drifted, and the characteristic alarm is a high background or an over-range indication that must stop the work rather than be silenced, with the calibration factors and constancy limits confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The patient's journey has three parts, and each has its own equipment and safety question. Before anything is injected the patient is identified against the request and asked about pregnancy and breastfeeding, because a radiopharmaceutical given to a pregnant woman irradiates the fetus and a tracer excreted in milk reaches the infant. A thyroid blocking agent may be given before certain examinations to protect the thyroid from free iodine. The injection is usually intravenous, and the activity is drawn behind a lead shield, checked against the prescription, and recorded with the time so decay between measurement and injection is accounted for. Then comes the uptake period, minutes or hours depending on the tracer, during which the patient waits in a designated area and is asked to drink water and empty the bladder, because a full bladder obscures the pelvis and receives unnecessary dose. The scan comes last and demands that the patient lie still for many minutes in a machine that may feel close and sound noisy, with the blocking dose, injected activity, and scan duration confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The gamma camera is the workhorse of single photon imaging, and it converts the position and energy of each arriving photon into a signal a computer accumulates into a picture. Its first element is the collimator, a thick plate of lead pierced by thousands of parallel holes, which admits only photons traveling roughly perpendicular to the crystal and absorbs the rest. The collimator gives the camera its sense of direction and makes it insensitive, because most photons emitted by the patient strike lead between the holes and are lost. Behind it sits a large crystal of sodium iodide activated with thallium, which absorbs a photon and converts its energy into a brief flash of visible light that an array of photomultiplier tubes detects, the relative brightness of neighboring tubes being used by position logic to calculate where the event occurred. A pulse height analyzer compares the total energy of the event with the expected energy of the radionuclide and rejects events outside a chosen window, so scattered radiation and background are separated from true gamma rays, with the energy window and collimator choice confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Collimators are exchanged for different examinations, and the choice follows the energy of the radionuclide and the resolution the question requires. A low energy collimator has thin septa and many small holes and gives the best resolution for technetium-99m, whose principal photon energy is about 140 kiloelectron volts, a value that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A medium or high energy collimator has thicker septa and fewer, wider holes, because the higher energy photons of iodine-131 or indium-111 would penetrate thin septa and blur the image in a fog of septal penetration, while a pinhole collimator magnifies a small organ such as the thyroid. The most damaging operator error is to scan with the wrong collimator for the radionuclide in use, because the image still looks like an image, and the loss of contrast and the false counts are easy to blame on the patient rather than the mistake. A dropped collimator may have bent septa invisible to the eye, so it is inspected rather than merely dusted, with the septal thickness and usable energy range confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Quality control of a gamma camera is a daily and monthly ritual, because a camera can drift silently while still producing plausible pictures. The daily uniform flood image, made with a uniform source, reveals a photomultiplier tube that has failed, a crystal damaged by moisture, or an energy correction out of date. The energy spectrum of each radionuclide is checked and the peak position adjusted, because a drifting peak throws the energy window off the photopeak and lets scattered events into the image. Uniformity, spatial resolution, spatial linearity, sensitivity, and energy resolution are measured on a written schedule, and any result outside the tolerance set by the manufacturer must be corrected before patients are scanned. The characteristic alarm on a modern camera is a detector or energy warning at the console, and the correct response is to stop imaging and repeat the quality control rather than accept the study, with those tolerances confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Single photon emission computed tomography adds depth to the planar image by rotating the camera around the patient and reconstructing the data mathematically. The camera head orbits the body and collects a series of projections, each a planar image from a different angle, and the computer combines them into slices through the organ of interest. The reconstruction is usually iterative, because the algorithm models photon attenuation and scatter and adjusts its estimate until it matches the measured projections, and modern systems also correct for attenuation by the patient's own tissue. The first dominant error is patient motion, because a few centimeters of movement between projections produce streaks and false defects that resemble disease, and the second is a miscalibrated center of rotation, which produces artifacts that can mimic a lesion. The patient is therefore positioned securely and watched throughout the orbit, the center of rotation is measured and corrected on a written schedule, and the number of projections and the attenuation correction method must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Positron emission tomography, called PET, detects the annihilation event rather than the emitted positron, and this indirectness is the source of both its power and its complexity. A positron travels a few millimeters through tissue until it meets an electron, and the two annihilate and convert their combined mass into two photons of 511 kiloelectron volts traveling in nearly opposite directions, an energy that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A ring of detectors surrounds the patient, and when two detectors on opposite sides record a photon within a few nanoseconds of each other the system accepts the event as a coincidence and draws a line between them. Coincidence detection replaces the physical collimator of the gamma camera and explains why a PET scanner is far more sensitive than a single photon camera, since no lead is needed to establish the direction of the photons. Random coincidences arise when two unrelated photons arrive inside the timing window and scattered coincidences when one photon is deflected before detection, and both are corrected during reconstruction, while attenuation is measured with a computed tomography scan, called CT, on the same bed. The coincidence timing window, the energy window, and the attenuation correction method must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The detectors of a PET scanner are dense scintillator crystals arranged in blocks and coupled to photomultiplier tubes or solid state light sensors, and the crystal must stop 511 kiloelectron volts radiation efficiently as well as emit light quickly. The crystals are packed into rings around the bore, and the patient table moves through the ring in bed positions so that a whole body image can be assembled, while time of flight detection measures the tiny difference in arrival time between the two photons and localizes the annihilation along the line of response. A modern scanner is usually combined with a CT scanner in the same gantry, so a short CT scan is performed first for attenuation correction and anatomical localization and the two image sets are fused for reading. The characteristic alarm of a PET scanner is a detector block warning or a coincidence timing failure, and it must be investigated before the patient is injected, because an injected dose cannot be recovered. The scan time per bed, the reconstruction parameters, and the detector performance limits must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The most widely used PET tracer is fluorine-18 fluorodeoxyglucose, a glucose analog in which one oxygen atom has been replaced by a positron emitting fluorine atom. It is made in a cyclotron, and its half-life of about 110 minutes governs the logistics of a PET service, because a dose must be produced, tested, transported, injected, and imaged within a few hours. Fluorodeoxyglucose enters cells through the same transporters as glucose, is phosphorylated by the same enzyme, and then cannot proceed along the metabolic pathway, so it is trapped inside the cell in proportion to that cell's glucose metabolism, which is why tumors, infected tissue, and active brain regions appear bright. Because the tracer competes with the patient's own glucose, a high blood glucose level depresses uptake into tumors and produces a misleading scan, so patients fast and a blood glucose measurement is taken before the injection. Insulin moves the tracer into muscle and fat, so a patient who has recently taken insulin or eaten may show intense muscle uptake that obscures the findings, with the injected activity, fasting interval, blood glucose limit, and uptake time confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The hot laboratory is where radiopharmaceuticals are received, prepared, measured, and drawn into syringes, and it is designed to contain contamination rather than merely store material. Its walls, floor, and bench are smooth and non-absorbent so a spill can be wiped up, and its surfaces are covered with disposable absorbent paper changed whenever it is soiled. Radiopharmaceuticals are kept in lead pots behind a lead brick wall or in a shielded safe, syringes are carried in a lead or tungsten shield, and vials are handled with tongs rather than fingers. The fume hood protects the product from the operator and the operator from the product, and it runs at a negative pressure so that airborne radioactivity is drawn away from the breathing zone. Everyone who enters wears gloves, a laboratory coat, and a dosimeter, and the room is surveyed with a contamination monitor at the end of each working day, with the shielding thickness, hood airflow, acceptable contamination level, and wipe test frequency confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The consumables of nuclear medicine are cheap individually and indispensable collectively, and a single missing item can stop a patient list. There are sterile syringes and needles of several sizes, sterile vials, sterile saline for elution, and alcohol swabs handled on an absorbent pad that catches drips, together with lead or tungsten syringe shields, lead pots, transport containers, forceps, and vial holders. There are disposable gloves, laboratory coats, eye protection, absorbent bench covering, and a spill kit containing absorbent pads, tongs, bags, and a contamination monitor. There are the patient care items, including cannulae, adhesive dressings, warm blankets, pillows, positioning aids, and the urinary collection bags needed when a patient cannot control the bladder. There are the dose labels, activity record forms, the instruction sheets given to a patient after a therapy dose, and the sharps containers and radioactive waste bags that receive everything at the end, with the sterility, expiry date, and labeling of every syringe confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Radioactive waste is not removed but held until it is no longer radioactive, and the storage room is therefore part of the equipment. Solid waste goes into lined bins, liquid waste into shielded tanks or decay sinks, and sharps into a rigid container that is itself labeled as radioactive. The rule of thumb is to store the waste for at least 10 half-lives of the radionuclide present and then survey it before release, and the exact holding time and release level for each nuclide must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Iodine-131 is the difficult one, because its 8 day half-life means contaminated items may be held for weeks, and patients treated with it excrete radioactivity in urine, saliva, and sweat for several days. Cleaning a camera or a table after a patient with incontinence begins with gloves and an absorbent pad, continues with a detergent that lifts the material rather than spreading it, and ends with a survey of the area. Blood, urine, and vomit are all potentially radioactive after a dose, so the cleaning materials themselves become radioactive waste.

Radiation protection for staff rests on three variables, and every habit in the department manages one of them. Time is the first, because the dose received is proportional to how long a person stays close to the source, so injections are quick, rehearsed, and never performed with a colleague standing nearby. Distance is the second, because the dose rate falls steeply with separation, so a syringe is carried at arm's length and a patient is spoken to from the doorway. Shielding is the third, and it is why the syringe shield, the lead pot, and the lead-lined wall exist. Each worker wears a whole body dosimeter and, when handling large activities, a ring badge on the hand that receives most exposure, and the badges are read regularly because a dose never measured is a dose that can never be reduced. A declared pregnancy changes the working pattern, and any dose limit approached or exceeded triggers an investigation, with the dose limits and investigation levels confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The people who live with a patient are exposed after the patient goes home, and the equipment of their protection is a written instruction and a nurse who explains it. After a diagnostic scan the activity is small and the risk negligible, but after treatment with iodine-131 the patient remains a moving source for days, and the instructions are specific. The patient may be asked to sleep in a separate bed, keep a certain distance from children and pregnant women, use a separate toilet and flush it twice, and wash soiled linen separately, and the numbers attached to each instruction must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The commonest failure is a patient who cannot read the written sheet, or who is too polite to ask what it means, so the instruction is spoken and then checked by asking the patient to explain it back. The real hazard is not the trained worker, who is monitored and rotates away from the source, but the child held on a lap the evening after a therapy dose.

Two smaller instruments support nuclear medicine by measuring radioactivity outside the imaging room, and both need their own care. The well counter contains a sodium iodide crystal with a hole through it, and a small sample of blood, urine, or tissue placed in the well is surrounded by the crystal, which detects a large fraction of the emitted photons. It is used for blood volume, glomerular filtration, and other measurements where activity in a sample is counted against a standard, while the uptake probe is a collimated detector placed against the neck to measure the fraction of an administered iodine dose taken up by the thyroid. Both are energy sensitive, so both are checked with a source of known activity and their energy window and background verified before a patient is counted. The characteristic error with either device is a change in geometry, because moving a sample tube or the probe changes the count rate without any change in the patient, with the counting time, background subtraction method, and calibration factor of each instrument confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Nuclear medicine equipment needs scheduled maintenance, and every operator should be able to name its failure modes. The camera crystal is hygroscopic, so a crack or a failed seal lets moisture in and produces a cold spot or a spreading discoloration that no software can repair, which is one reason a camera room is kept at a stable temperature and humidity. A photomultiplier tube can fail or become noisy, which appears as a hole or a bright spot on the flood image, and the tube is replaced by an engineer rather than adjusted by the operator. The collimator is heavy and easily damaged, the gantry and table carry motors and position sensors that drift and fail, and the detectors of a PET scanner contain thousands of crystals and light sensors whose gains must be checked and corrected. The dose calibrator is the instrument most often neglected because it always appears to work, yet a drifting calibrator changes every dose in the department at once, with service intervals, calibration frequencies, and the acceptance tolerances that follow any repair confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A short start of shift check catches most of the faults that would otherwise spoil a day of imaging. The operator confirms that the dose calibrator is switched on, that its background reading is at the expected level, and that the daily constancy check with the sealed source agrees with the previous day's value. The camera is checked for the correct collimator, the energy peak is verified for the radionuclide in use, and the morning flood image is inspected before the first patient rather than after the last. The survey meter is switched on, tested against its check source, and its battery confirmed, because a survey meter with a dead battery gives false reassurance, and the generator is eluted according to schedule with the breakthrough test recorded. The patient list is checked for the correct tracer, activity, and timing, and the pregnancy and breastfeeding questions are asked and documented for every patient of childbearing age, with the energy peak, constancy limit, breakthrough limit, and background level confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The first field variant is not a frontier clinic at all but a large vehicle, because the smallest practical version of this imaging is a scanner that travels between hospitals. A mobile PET and CT unit is built into a trailer or container and carries the scanner, a small hot laboratory with a shielded bench, a dose calibrator, and a waiting area for patients. It gives up the cyclotron and the radiochemistry laboratory, so it depends entirely on delivered doses of fluorine-18 fluorodeoxyglucose, whose short half-life and tight delivery schedule dictate the whole day, and it gives up the hospital's stable power and cooling, so it carries its own electrical supply and air conditioning. It demands that the receiving site provide a parking place with power, water, and a route wide enough for a stretcher, and the dose rate at the outer wall is surveyed whenever the unit is parked near another building or a public path. Because the equipment is moved, its alignment, energy calibration, and image quality are rechecked at every new site, and those checks and their tolerances must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A district hospital may support a much smaller service, and the compromises there are a lesson in what the physics actually requires. Such a service usually has a technetium-99m generator, a single-head gamma camera, a dose calibrator, a well counter or an uptake probe, and one lead-lined room that serves as hot laboratory and imaging room together. It gives up the cyclotron, the PET scanner, and the radiochemistry, so it performs planar and single photon emission computed tomography studies of thyroid, bone, kidney, and heart rather than metabolic whole body imaging, and it depends on a trained technologist who can recognize a failing photomultiplier tube rather than on a service contract that arrives within hours. The generator must be delivered on a schedule that respects its own decay, which makes a reliable supply chain as essential as the camera itself, and the activity available on any given day must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

In a mobile or frontier clinic nuclear medicine does not travel, and honesty about that fact is more useful than a modified machine. There is no generator, no hot laboratory, no shielded room, and no way to manage radioactive waste, and a radiopharmaceutical that cannot be delivered within its useful half-life cannot be used at all. Ultrasound answers many of the questions a thyroid scan or a renal scan would have answered, because it shows the size, structure, and blood flow of an organ without any radiation at all, and it is the single most valuable imaging device in a remote setting. Plain radiography answers questions about bone, lung, and bowel, and a CT scanner at a referral center answers questions about the brain, chest, and abdomen, while laboratory tests substitute for some functional studies, because a thyroid stimulating hormone level or a renal function panel may answer the clinical question an uptake measurement would have answered. The decision to refer is itself a piece of equipment and should be made early rather than after several days of tests that cannot settle the diagnosis.

Sending a patient for nuclear medicine from a remote clinic is a logistical exercise, and the equipment that makes it work is mostly paper and communication. The referral note must state the clinical question, the relevant history, the tests already done, and whether the patient can lie still and cooperate, because a scan without that information may answer the wrong question. The appointment must be timed with the tracer's half-life in mind, since a dose prepared for a morning appointment is useless to a patient who arrives in the afternoon, and the travel plan must include the return journey, because a patient who has received a therapy dose should not sit for hours on public transport beside other passengers. Images travel better than patients in some cases, and where a picture archiving and communication system, called PACS, is available the images are sent in the Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine standard, called DICOM, rather than photographed, because a photograph of a screen loses the quantitative information on which interpretation depends. Where no such link exists, films or a disk may be carried by hand, and the appointment timing, the transport restrictions after a therapy dose, and the data format accepted by the receiving center must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

To summarize, nuclear medicine images radiation that starts inside the patient, using a radiopharmaceutical whose radionuclide decays with a known half-life and emits photons a detector can locate. The gamma camera collimates those photons with a lead plate, converts them to light in a sodium iodide crystal, and positions each event with photomultiplier tubes, while single photon emission computed tomography rotates the camera and reconstructs slices and PET detects the two opposed photons of an annihilation event in coincidence and merges that metabolic image with CT. The supporting instruments matter as much as the camera, because the dose calibrator measures what is injected, the well counter and the uptake probe measure activity in a sample and in an organ, and the hot laboratory keeps the radioactivity where it belongs. The hazards are contamination, unnecessary dose to staff and families, and the silent drift of an instrument that still produces a plausible picture, and the defenses are time, distance, shielding, monitoring, and a written daily check routine that is actually followed. Among the commonest operator errors are the wrong collimator, the wrong geometry in the dose calibrator, an unasked pregnancy question, and a patient who moves during a long acquisition, while in the field none of this travels, so the clinic substitutes ultrasound, radiography, CT at a referral center, and laboratory tests and relies on an early and honest referral. Every setting, activity, energy, and threshold must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.
