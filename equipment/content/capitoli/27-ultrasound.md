# Chapter 27 — Ultrasound

An ultrasound image begins with a crystal that changes shape when an electric field is applied to it and produces a voltage when squeezed, and that reversible property is the piezoelectric effect. A short electrical pulse makes the crystal ring at a chosen frequency, and the ringing leaves the surface as a pressure wave that travels into the body. Every boundary between materials of different acoustic impedance reflects part of that energy back, and because sound crosses soft tissue at roughly 1540 meters per second, the time until each echo returns becomes depth while its strength becomes brightness. Every pixel is therefore an inference from timing rather than a photograph, and the assumed speed of sound and the depth scale must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Frequency decides both what can be seen and how deep it can be seen, because a higher frequency has a shorter wavelength and resolves smaller detail yet is absorbed and scattered faster. A lower frequency penetrates farther but blurs small detail, because its longer wavelength cannot separate structures that lie close together. The usual compromise is 2 to 5 megahertz for the abdomen, 7 to 12 megahertz for superficial structures, and higher still for the eye, and each range must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Attenuation rises with frequency and with depth, which is why the machine amplifies late echoes more than early ones and why a higher frequency aimed at a deep structure shows less rather than more.

A modern probe is an array of many small elements, each pulsed slightly later than its neighbor, and by delaying pulses across the face of the array the machine shapes a beam, focuses it at a chosen depth, and steers it without any moving part. The same delays applied in reverse during reception make the array listen preferentially to echoes from one direction, which is how a stationary probe sweeps a sector through the body. Behind the elements a backing block shortens the pulse for finer depth resolution, while in front a matching layer eases sound into skin and a plastic lens protects the layers beneath. A damaged element, a cracked lens, or a broken wire removes part or all of one line of the image, so the probe is the most fragile part of the machine, and the element count, the array geometry, and the electrical drive must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Probe shapes follow the anatomy they must serve, and the frequency, the insertion depth, and the disinfection level for any of them must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A linear array fires its elements in a straight line for a rectangular image and serves superficial structures such as vessels, tendons, the thyroid gland, and the breast, while a curvilinear, or convex, array curves its face for a fan shaped field and is the general abdominal and obstetric probe for the liver, the kidneys, and the uterus. A phased array has a small footprint and steers electronically through a sector, letting it look between the ribs at the heart, and it is the standard cardiac probe. A transvaginal probe is a long narrow high frequency device shaped for insertion into the vagina to view the uterus and ovaries from a few centimeters away, a transesophageal probe on a flexible gastroscope lies in the esophagus behind the heart during surgery, and intracavitary and endorectal probes apply the same idea to the rectum and prostate.

The console converts echoes into a readable image, and its controls are few but decisive. Gain is overall amplification and sets the brightness of the picture rather than the truth of it, so too much fills the screen with noise and too little hides real structures. Time gain compensation is a row of sliders that amplifies late echoes from deep tissue more than early ones, depth sets how far the machine listens, and focus places the narrowest part of the beam where detail matters. Output power is the strength of the transmitted pulse and changes what the patient receives rather than what the screen shows, so it is kept as low as the image allows, and the gain, the time gain compensation, the depth, the focus, and the output power must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The preset menu holds starting points, one for each probe and examination type, so the operator does not rebuild the image from scratch. The cardiac preset selects the cardiac probe, a low frequency, a fast frame rate, a small sector, and a Doppler mode for fast flow, while the abdominal preset selects a convex probe, a slower frame rate, and a deeper field. A preset is only a starting point, because body size, bowel gas, and organ depth still demand adjustment, and a preset copied from another machine will not transfer its numbers. The commonest misuse of the console is to correct a poor image with gain alone, which brightens noise without recovering information, when the true fault is poor coupling, the wrong probe, or the wrong preset, and every preset, its probe pairing, and the values it loads must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The machine displays the same echoes in several ways, and each mode answers a different question. Brightness mode draws each echo as a bright dot whose position shows depth and produces the familiar gray cross sectional image, while motion mode records one line of that image over time so a vertical strip shows how a structure moves, which is still used to measure the rate of a fetal heartbeat or the excursion of a valve leaflet. Doppler modes use the change in frequency, or phase shift, of echoes from moving red blood cells to measure the speed and direction of flow, and color Doppler paints those velocities as a colored map while spectral Doppler plots them over time as a waveform. Power Doppler shows the presence of flow rather than its direction and is more sensitive to slow flow in small vessels, and the mode, the sample volume, the angle of insonation, and the velocity scale must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Doppler deserves its own caution because it concentrates energy in time and can reach the highest acoustic outputs the machine produces. Pulsed wave Doppler sends bursts from one element and listens with the same one, so it measures flow at a chosen depth but only up to a limited velocity before the reading wraps around, an error called aliasing. Continuous wave Doppler transmits and receives with separate elements at the same time, so it measures very fast flow without aliasing but reports everything in the beam without depth resolution. The angle between the beam and the direction of flow changes the measured velocity by a cosine term, and at angles greater than about 60 degrees the number becomes too unreliable to report, so the angle correction, the sample volume, the velocity scale, and the exposure limits must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The safety hazard of ultrasound is easy to forget because nothing is felt at the time, yet the beam deposits energy as heat and can exert mechanical forces at microscopic gas bubbles. The thermal index estimates the temperature rise the beam could produce, and the mechanical index estimates the likelihood of cavitation, the formation and collapse of small bubbles that can damage tissue. Both rise when output power and dwell time increase, and both matter most where the beam is held still, as in fetal scanning, ocular scanning, and long Doppler studies, so the habit is to use the lowest output that answers the question and to keep the dwell time short. The mechanical index, the thermal index, and the exposure time limits must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Electrical safety is a second hazard, because a cracked lens or damaged cable can leak current, and probe overheating is a third whose characteristic alarm is a probe temperature warning that cuts the transmission off.

The commonest operator error in ultrasound is not a wrong button but a wrong assumption, and it usually begins with coupling. A thin, uneven, or dried film of gel lets air sit between probe and skin, and air reflects almost all the sound, so the operator turns up the gain and produces a bright noisy image from almost no signal. The second error is reading a single view as the whole truth, because a structure can look normal in one plane and be abnormal in the next, so every important finding should be seen in at least two planes. The third is accepting an image that was never saved, because an interpretation that is not documented cannot be reviewed later, and the characteristic alarm is a disconnected probe message or a probe identification failure when the connector is not fully seated. The thresholds and the correct response to each alarm must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Cleaning a probe is a clinical procedure because the same device touches every patient, and the method depends on what it touched. A probe that has touched only intact skin needs a wipe with a compatible disinfectant between patients, while one that has entered a body cavity or contacted mucous membrane is a semicritical device needing a high level of disinfection, and one used in a sterile field needs a sterile sheath and sterile gel. The lens and housing are fragile, because many disinfectants craze the plastic, cloud the lens, and eventually crack the seal that keeps fluid out, and the maker states which chemicals are compatible, so the list must be checked before any new disinfectant is introduced. Transesophageal and intracavitary probes need particular care, because their long flexible shafts can be kinked, their controls can be flooded, and their connectors must never be immersed, and the contact time, the concentration, the temperature, and the compatibility of the disinfectant must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The consumables are cheap and indispensable, and running out of one stops the examination as surely as a power failure. Coupling gel excludes air from the space between probe and skin, and it must be water based, acoustically matched, and free of bubbles, while oily or scented products damage some lenses and dry out quickly. Where gel is unavailable, clean water, a water soluble lubricant, or a thin film of vegetable oil will carry sound, though water runs off, lubricant may contain substances the maker forbids, and oil can stain and degrade the lens. A standoff pad lifts very superficial structures into the focal zone, and probe sheaths, sterile gel, sterile covers, and a needle guide are needed in a sterile field, with the expiry dates, the concentrations, and the compatibility of all of these confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A complete examination follows a fixed order, and the order is what makes it repeatable. The patient is positioned so the target organ lies within reach, the area is exposed, gel is applied, and the probe is placed with its orientation marker matched to the marker on the screen. The operator scans systematically rather than hunting for the expected finding, adjusting depth, focus, and gain as the anatomy changes, and comparing right with left. Measurements are made on a frozen image with electronic calipers, and each should be taken in the plane that truly contains the structure, because an oblique cut exaggerates a length. Images and clips are labeled, dated, and stored with the patient's identity, and the report separates what was seen from what it might mean, with the depth, the focus, the gain, the storage format, the required image set, and the labeling rules confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Artifacts are not rare faults but predictable consequences of how sound behaves, and a beginner who does not recognize them will diagnose diseases that are not there. Acoustic shadowing appears when a dense structure such as a stone, a bone, or a calcified plaque absorbs or reflects the beam so completely that everything behind it is dark, and the beginner reads that dark band as a mass or as fluid. Posterior acoustic enhancement is the opposite, a bright band behind a fluid filled structure because fluid weakens the beam less than the tissue around it, and a beginner can mistake it for a solid lesion. Reverberation appears as evenly spaced parallel lines at a fixed depth, produced when the pulse bounces between two strong reflectors such as the probe face and a fascial layer, and comet tail and ring down artifacts are short bright streaks from metal or air that hide the structure being examined. Every one of these must be recognized before it is reported, and the display settings that create or reduce them must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Mirror image artifact most often deceives a beginner, because it looks exactly like real anatomy. When a strong flat reflector such as the diaphragm lies between the probe and a structure, returning echoes can bounce a second time and appear on the far side of the reflector, so a liver lesion or a pleural collection seems to exist on the wrong side of a boundary. Refraction and speed error artifacts bend the apparent position of structures when the beam crosses an interface at an angle or passes through tissue whose true speed of sound differs from the value the machine assumes. Slice thickness artifact comes from the finite width of the beam, which averages echoes from tissue beside the plane of interest and can fill a simple cyst with fake internal echoes, while aliasing turns a fast jet into a reversed waveform that looks like a different disease. The honest defense is to change the approach, the probe angle, or the window, because an artifact usually moves when the operator moves, and the settings that affect these artifacts must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Ultrasound is used in almost every specialty, and in several it has replaced procedures that once required radiation or surgery. In obstetrics it dates a pregnancy from the length of the embryo, checks the number of fetuses, follows growth, locates the placenta, measures the fluid around the fetus, and guides a needle for amniocentesis, while Doppler of the umbilical artery gives information about the placental circulation. In gynecology a transvaginal probe examines the uterus and ovaries, characterizes a cyst, follows a follicle, and guides the retrieval of eggs for assisted reproduction, and in cardiology the phased array probe measures chamber size, wall thickness, and valve motion while Doppler quantifies the velocity of flow across a narrowed or leaking valve. In emergency and general medicine a rapid bedside scan looks for free fluid in the abdomen, blood around the heart, fluid in the chest, and an abdominal aortic aneurysm, and in vascular practice it maps the veins for a clot and follows a graft or a dialysis fistula, with the specific protocols, the required views, and the measurement thresholds confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Other specialties use the same physics for narrower purposes. In urology a probe measures the prostate, estimates residual urine in the bladder, and guides a biopsy, while in musculoskeletal practice a high frequency linear probe examines tendons, ligaments, joints, and nerves and can guide an injection or a nerve block. In anesthesia and intensive care it helps place a central line under direct vision, confirms the position of a tube in the airway, and assesses the lungs for fluid or collapse. In pediatrics it examines the infant hip, the neonatal brain through the fontanelle, and the urinary tract, and its lack of ionizing radiation is a particular advantage in a growing child. In ophthalmology a very high frequency probe measures the length of the eye and examines the retina when the cornea or lens is opaque, and in breast care it characterizes a lump and guides a core needle biopsy, with the probe frequency, the needle guide, and the sterility requirements confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The hospital grade machine is a cart with a large display, a keyboard, a trackball, several probe ports, and a computer inside. It needs a stable mains supply, and in a busy department it is usually plugged into a circuit backed by an uninterruptible power supply, called a UPS, because a machine that loses power during an examination may lose the study. It needs a dedicated examination table with a cleanable surface, a screen for privacy, dim lighting so the display can be read, and enough space for the operator to stand on both sides of the patient. Its images travel over the hospital network in the standard format of Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine, called DICOM, and are filed in the picture archiving and communication system, called PACS, where the report and the images meet the patient's record. It needs a gel warmer, a supply of probe covers and disinfectant, a waste bin, a rack to store probes, and hand hygiene materials and personal protective equipment, called PPE, within reach, and the voltage, the power rating, the network configuration, and the storage pathway must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Between the cart and the pocket device sit two more classes of machine, and the difference is mostly the display and the software. A point of care device is a compact unit, often with a sealed keyboard and a touch screen, designed for the bedside, the emergency department, or the intensive care unit, and its presets are simplified so a non specialist can answer one narrow question quickly. A handheld unit is smaller still, sometimes no larger than the probe itself, and it either has a tiny built in screen or sends the image by a cable or wireless link to a mobile telephone or tablet, where an application performs the processing. Both classes accept a limited number of probes, both usually lack the full measurement package of a cart machine, and both depend on a battery and on software updates that the user must actually install. The advantage is that the machine reaches the patient rather than the patient reaching the machine, and the battery capacity, the charging cycle, the wireless configuration, and the software version must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Maintenance is mostly inspection and restraint, because the electronics are sealed and the vulnerable parts are the probe and the cable. Every probe is examined in good light before each shift for cracks in the lens, a lifted seal, a kink, a worn strain relief, or a bent connector pin, and any probe that fails is withdrawn until tested, because a damaged probe is both an infection risk and an electrical risk. A tissue equivalent phantom or small test object is scanned at intervals to check that the same structure produces the same brightness and the same measured distance, and the record of that test is what proves the machine has not drifted. Dead elements show as dark vertical bands in a uniform phantom image, and a probe that has lost a group of elements may still produce a convincing picture of the wrong anatomy, while cable and connector faults often appear only when the cable is flexed, so the electrical safety test, the calibration interval, the phantom test, and the inspection schedule must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The failure modes of ultrasound are few and each has a signature. A probe with dead elements produces fixed vertical dark lines that stay in the same place whatever the patient and whatever the depth. A break in the cable produces a sudden loss of signal when the cable is bent, and the characteristic alarm is a disconnected probe message or a blank image with a fault code. Fluid that has entered a cracked lens or flooded connector produces an image that fades, flickers, or fills with noise, and a probe in that condition must never be used on a patient, because the same defect that lets fluid in can let current out. A console that overheats may shut down or lose its image storage, a battery that no longer holds charge turns a portable machine into one that must be carried with its power supply, and a software fault can freeze the image while the machine continues to transmit. The fault codes, the temperature limits, the shutdown thresholds, and the battery replacement intervals must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The operator's start of shift check takes a few minutes and prevents most of the day's failures. The machine is switched on and allowed to complete its self test, the date and time are verified, and the patient identification fields are cleared of the previous patient. Each probe that will be used is connected, inspected, and briefly tested on a known structure, and its preset is confirmed correct, while the display is checked for uniformity and for any dark band that suggests a dead element. The trackball, keyboard, freeze button, and measurement calipers are exercised, the supply of gel, probe covers, disinfectant, and cleaning wipes is confirmed, and the battery of any portable unit is confirmed charged with its charger present. The previous day's studies are confirmed to have reached the picture archiving and communication system, because an unsent study may have to be repeated, and the self test, the probe check, the cleaning materials, and the storage confirmation must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

In a mobile or frontier clinic the ultrasound variant is usually a single handheld probe paired with a small screen or a telephone, powered by a battery bank that may itself be charged from a solar panel. The device gives up the cart, the large display, the several probe ports, the full measurement package, the image archive, and the reporting software, and in exchange it fits into a bag, runs for hours without mains power, and can be carried to a patient who cannot be moved. The most common configuration is a convex probe for the abdomen and obstetrics and a linear probe for superficial work, and the choice of which to carry is a decision about which questions the clinic must answer. The image is smaller and dimmer than a cart image, and the operator often works with daylight falling on the screen, which reduces the apparent contrast and makes a marginal finding harder to see. A gel substitute is often necessary, and the battery capacity, the charging arrangement, the probe selection, and the gel substitute must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

What the field unit demands instead of infrastructure is discipline, and the discipline is mostly about power, cleanliness, and interpretation. Power must be managed as a clinical supply, which means charging whenever a source is available, carrying a charged spare, avoiding deep discharge, and protecting the battery from heat, because a battery left in a hot vehicle loses capacity permanently. Cleanliness must be improvised without cutting corners, so a probe that has touched skin is wiped between patients, and a probe used inside a body cavity is covered with a sheath and then disinfected to the highest level the setting allows. Protection of the probe is a daily habit, because a handheld unit is dropped, sat upon, and packed against hard objects, and a cracked lens in a remote clinic may end the ultrasound service for months. Interpretation is the hardest demand, because a handheld image is easy to obtain and hard to read, and the operator must know the limit of his or her own skill, with the charging routine, the disinfection level, the sheath, and the referral criteria confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The field clinic commonly sends images or short clips to a distant reviewer, and that practice has real value and real limits. A compressed image sent over a weak mobile link loses resolution and may lose the measurement scale, so the reviewer must be told the probe used, the depth, the patient's age and sex, and the exact question being asked. A telephone photograph of a screen is a triage aid rather than a diagnostic study, and it should decide whether a patient must travel, not exclude a serious condition at home. Where a data link exists, a short clip is more informative than a still image for anything that moves, such as a fetal heart or a valve, and the file should be sent in a standard format where the software allows it. Documentation in the field is often the weakest point, because a study that is not written down cannot be compared at the next visit, so the operator should record the date, the probe, the findings, the images saved, and the name of anyone who reviewed them, and the referral pathway and the transmission format must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

In the field the handheld probe earns its place through a small number of high value questions. It can confirm a pregnancy and estimate its age, locate a fetal heartbeat, check the position of the placenta, and measure the fluid around the fetus, which guides a decision about referral before labor begins. It can look for free fluid in the abdomen after trauma, for fluid around the heart, and for an abdominal aortic aneurysm, and it can guide a needle into a joint, an abscess, or a vein when blind puncture would be dangerous. It can examine the gallbladder, the kidneys, and the bladder, and it can show a deep vein clot in a leg, which is otherwise a clinical guess. What it cannot do reliably in inexperienced hands is exclude disease, because a negative handheld scan is not the same as a negative full examination, so the operator must treat the device as a question answerer and not as a verdict and must confirm the specific protocols and measurement thresholds against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The last component of an ultrasound service is the trained person, and ultrasound is unusual in that the same machine produces a useful examination or a dangerous one depending entirely on the operator. Training must cover the physics well enough that the operator understands depth, attenuation, and artifacts, the anatomy of the regions being scanned, the standard views and how to obtain them, the controls and why each is adjusted, and the safety limits of acoustic output. Competence is best proved by a period of supervised scanning with a logbook of examinations and images reviewed by an experienced teacher, because a certificate without practice does not produce a safe operator. Periodic refresher training matters more in ultrasound than with many devices, because the operator's skill decays when the case mix is narrow and because new presets and new probes change the technique. Every service should have a written scope of practice stating which examinations each operator may perform and which must be referred, and the training requirements, the supervision period, the logbook, and the scope of practice must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

To summarize, ultrasound is a pulse of sound reflected from boundaries of differing acoustic impedance, and the machine turns the delay into depth and the strength into brightness. The frequency of that pulse fixes the trade between resolution and penetration, the array of crystals focuses and steers it, and the console controls gain, time gain compensation, depth, focus, and output power to make the returning echoes readable. The probe's shape follows the anatomy, coupling gel excludes the air that would reflect the beam, and the modes of brightness, motion, and Doppler answer different questions while the mechanical index and the thermal index warn of the energy being delivered. Artifacts such as shadowing, enhancement, reverberation, and mirror image can imitate disease, so every finding must be confirmed in more than one plane, and the same probe that touches every patient must be cleaned and disinfected according to what it touched and what its maker permits. In the hospital the machine is a cart with a network, an archive, and an uninterruptible power supply, while in the field it is a handheld probe with a battery bank, a gel substitute, and a distant reviewer. In every setting the rule is the same: use the lowest output that answers the question, keep the dwell time short, treat the probe as the fragile instrument it is, and confirm every setting, threshold, and disinfection cycle against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before a patient is scanned.
