# Chapter 59 — Personal Protective Equipment

Personal protective equipment (PPE) is the last barrier between an infectious patient and the skin, airway, and mucous membranes of the person caring for that patient. The physical principle behind every item in this chapter is selective passage, a material that lets air and water vapor through while holding back particles, droplets, and liquid. Screening stops a particle larger than the gaps between fibers, interception and impaction catch a particle that cannot follow the bending airstream around a fiber, and electrostatic attraction pulls a charged particle out of the air, so a crushed, wetted, or overheated mask loses much of its performance. Liquid resistance is a separate property from air filtration, since a material can pass vapor freely and still hold back a film of blood under pressure. A barrier is therefore only as strong as its weakest point and only while it stays dry, and equipment sits at the bottom of a hierarchy of controls, below ventilation and distancing, because it depends on human behavior at the moment of contact, when a glove that is not worn or a mask pushed below the nose turns a protective system into a decorative one.

A glove is an elastomer film stretched over the hand, and its protection depends on that film remaining continuous under tension. Natural rubber latex is the most elastic of the common materials, so it conforms to the fingers and seals at the wrist, but its proteins cause the most serious glove allergy. Nitrile is a synthetic rubber that resists oils, punctures, and many disinfectants better than latex and contains no natural rubber protein, which makes it the usual choice for the sensitive wearer, though it is stiffer and tears sooner at a sharp edge. Vinyl is the least expensive and the weakest barrier, and thickness at the fingertip is often near 0.2 millimeter for an examination glove, a figure that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Size is a safety feature rather than a comfort feature, since a glove that is too tight restricts the blood flow of the hand and splits at the cuff while one that is too loose wrinkles at the fingertips and slides off during a procedure. Gloves are supplied sterile and non-sterile, powdered and non-powdered, and in single-use form, with a sterile pair for an aseptic procedure, a non-sterile pair for routine contact, and double gloving when a second film may need to be removed.

Two distinct allergies follow the glove, and confusing them delays the right response. Immediate hypersensitivity to natural rubber latex proteins begins within minutes of contact and can progress from itching and hives to wheezing, a fall in blood pressure, and shock, which makes it a medical emergency rather than a skin complaint. Delayed contact dermatitis is a reaction to the chemicals that accelerate the curing of the rubber, and it appears hours later as redness, swelling, and itching at the back of the hands. Powder increases the risk of the immediate form, because it binds latex protein and carries it into the air as an aerosol that a sensitive worker can inhale, so powdered gloves should be removed where an alternative exists and a known allergy calls for a latex-free room. The common operator error is to pull a glove on with wet hands, and the characteristic warning is a sudden looseness, a damp feeling at a fingertip, a visible tear at the web of the thumb, or the rash and wheeze that follow contact.

A surgical mask is a loose-fitting filter that covers the nose and the mouth, and its physics are those of a fiber mat carrying an electrostatic charge. The fibers lie in a random mat so that air follows a winding path, and particles are removed by interception, impaction, and diffusion, with the smallest captured mainly by random motion that brings them into contact with a fiber. A middle layer of charged polymer adds electrostatic attraction, so a mask must not be crushed, folded, wetted, or stored in a hot place, because those actions destroy the charge without leaving a visible sign. A surgical mask is tested on particles of about 1 micrometer, while a filtering respirator is tested on particles of about 0.3 micrometer, and every efficiency figure and test condition must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The mask protects in two directions at once, since it blocks the wearer's respiratory droplets from reaching the patient and blocks a splash of blood from reaching the wearer's mouth and nose. It does not seal to the face, so air and particles bypass it around the cheeks and the nose, and a mask worn below the nose, left hanging around the neck, or reused for a second patient does neither job, while a valved mask releases unfiltered air and cannot serve as source control in an operating room or an isolation room.

A filtering respirator is a tight-fitting device that seals against the face, and its performance depends on how well the filter removes particles and how well the mask seals to the skin. Filters are grouped by filtration efficiency and by resistance to oil, so a class may be rated at 95 per cent, 99 per cent, or nearly 100 per cent capture of the hardest particle size, and those figures must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A respirator with an excellent filter and a poor seal leaks at the bridge of the nose, along the jaw, or through a gap created by facial hair, because leakage always follows the path of least resistance. Breathing resistance rises as the filter loads with particles, so the device becomes harder to wear as it becomes more protective, and an exhalation valve eases breathing but releases unfiltered air. A filtering respirator can never be used where the oxygen content is low or where the contaminant is a gas for which the filter was not designed, because a filter adds no oxygen and removes only what it was built to capture. The characteristic warning of a failure is not a sound but a sensation, the collapse of the mask against the face on inhalation, the puff of air at the eye on exhalation, or the slow fogging of spectacles. The common operator error is to trust a mask that was never fitted, and every selection, class, and resistance limit must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The powered air-purifying respirator answers the two worst complaints about a tight-fitting filter, which are the effort of breathing and the heat trapped against the face. A battery-driven blower draws air through a filter and pushes it into a loose-fitting hood, helmet, or half mask, so the wearer breathes filtered air delivered under slight positive pressure. Because the pressure inside the head cover is higher than the pressure outside, any leak tends to flow outward, which is the physical reason this device tolerates a looser fit and a beard that would ruin the seal of a filtering facepiece. The price is a system with more parts and more ways to fail, including a battery with a finite charge, a blower that can stall, a filter that can clog, a hose that can kink or disconnect, and a head cover that can tear. Airflow is usually stated in liters per minute and battery duration in hours, and both depend on the filter load and the temperature, so every airflow rate, battery duration, and alarm threshold must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The characteristic alarms are a low-battery alarm, a low-airflow alarm, and a filter-blockage alarm, and each one means that protection is ending, so the common error of silencing an alarm and finishing the task leaves the wearer unprotected in a contaminated space.

Eye and face protection answers three different threats, and a single piece of equipment rarely answers all three. Impact carries kinetic energy that must be absorbed or deflected, which is why a lens is made of a tough transparent polymer rather than of glass. A splash of blood, vomit, or chemical flows along the face and seeks the eye, the corner of the mouth, and the skin of the cheek, which is why a shield must cover more than the eye alone. Radiation demands the absorption of a specific wavelength, as with ultraviolet light from a welding arc or a germicidal lamp, or with the scattered beam of a laser, and every wavelength and impact rating must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Goggles seal around the orbit and protect from every direction, spectacles protect only from the front and leave gaps at the brow and the cheek, and a face shield stops a splash but is not a substitute for goggles against impact or dust. A sealed goggle fogs and an open one admits droplets, so an anti-fog coating or an indirect vent manages that balance, and the common errors are to hang reusable goggles on a hook beside the bed and to treat ordinary prescription spectacles as eye protection.

A gown or an apron is a barrier between the patient's fluid and the worker's clothing and skin, and its performance is graded by how much liquid it resists, how much pressure that liquid carries, and how long the contact lasts. The grading systems used in different countries are not identical, but they share the same logic, with a light level for a small splash, a middle level for a moderate volume, and a top level for a procedure that produces a heavy flow of blood or a jet of fluid. A plastic apron protects the front of the body while leaving the sleeves, the shoulders, and the back uncovered, and a reinforced gown adds panels at the forearms and the chest because those are the surfaces that press against a wet field. The selection of a level is a risk assessment rather than a habit, and every level designation, test method, and resistance figure must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The back of a gown is never considered sterile and is often not a barrier at all, so a wearer who turns away from the patient has already exposed the surface that will touch the next patient.

A shoe cover keeps a splash off the footwear and carries contamination out of the room on its own surface, so it must be removed before the wearer leaves, and a cover with a slippery sole is a fall hazard on a wet floor. A rubber boot is a heavier barrier that can be washed and disinfected for reuse, and its height determines how deep a puddle the wearer can cross without soaking the trouser leg. A hood or a head cover closes the gap between the respirator and the gown, and a taped seam closes the gap between the glove and the sleeve, because every opening in an ensemble is a channel. The outer surface of one piece always lies over the outer surface of the next so that a splash runs off rather than in, and the common operator error is to tuck the trouser leg inside the boot or the glove inside the sleeve, which creates a cup that catches fluid and holds it against the skin.

Strike-through is the moment liquid reaches the far side of a fabric, and it turns the barrier into a bridge that connects the contaminated surface to the skin. A woven material wicks fluid along its threads from a wet area to a dry one, so a cotton gown soaks upward from the cuff even while its front still looks clean, while a non-woven material resists wicking but can still be pierced, torn, or abraded. Pressure and time move the fluid as well, so a wet sleeve pressed against a wet field passes liquid through a fabric that would hold it under no pressure, and a soaked glove is no longer a barrier after a few minutes. The characteristic warning is a cold, heavy feeling at the forearm, a dark patch that spreads upward from a cuff, or dampness felt inside a glove, and the common error is to keep working in a wet gown because the case is nearly over.

The first step of donning is hand hygiene, because the hands are the tool that performs the rest of the sequence, and the gown goes on first, tied at the back by an assistant or by the wearer with the ties passed around without touching the front panel. The respirator goes on next and must be fitted and seal checked before the eye protection goes on, because the straps of the goggles pass over the mask and cannot be adjusted afterwards without breaking the seal. The eye protection follows, then the gloves, which are pulled over the gown cuffs so that the cuff is covered and the gap between the sleeve and the glove is closed, and a second pair is added when a high-risk procedure requires it. The donning area must be separate from the doffing area, and the wearer must check with a mirror or a partner that the mask covers the nose and the mouth, that the eye protection seals, and that no skin is exposed.

Doffing is the most dangerous moment of the sequence, because the outer surface of every item is contaminated and the hands must remove it without touching the skin or the clothing. The order reverses the donning, with the gloves off first, then the gown or apron, then the eye protection, then the respirator, and hand hygiene performed between the steps. Each glove is removed by grasping the outside of one glove with the other gloved hand and peeling it away so that it turns inside out, then sliding a bare finger under the cuff of the second glove. The gown is unfastened and pulled away from the shoulders by the inside, rolled into a bundle with the contaminated surface inward, and discarded without being shaken, and the respirator is removed by the straps from behind, never by the front. The common errors are touching the face, pulling a gown over the head so that the contaminated front brushes the hair, snapping a glove off so that it flicks droplets into the air, and reaching for a door handle with a gloved hand. The characteristic warning of a contaminated doffing is a wet sensation on the skin, a smear of visible soil on the forearm, or the sudden awareness that a bare hand has touched the outer face of the mask, and every step and hand hygiene point must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A filtering respirator protects only if it seals, and the seal must be proved twice, once by a formal fit test and once by a check before every use. The user seal check is a quick physical test performed by the wearer, and it works by pressure, since the wearer covers the filter or the exhalation valve and breathes in gently so that the mask should collapse against the face, then breathes out gently so that air should not escape at the edges. A positive pressure check that lets air puff at the eye, or a negative pressure check that lets air whistle in while the mask stays loose, indicates a leak at the nose bridge, the cheek, or the jaw. A formal fit test is a measurement rather than a feeling, and it compares the concentration of a test agent or of ambient particles outside the mask with the concentration inside it while the wearer breathes deeply, turns the head, and talks. The result is a fit factor, commonly required to exceed 100 for a tight-fitting half mask, and that figure and those exercises must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Anything that breaks the seal must be resolved before the mask is worn, including facial hair, a scar, a missing tooth, and a strap that is too loose or too tight, and the test is repeated when the model or the face changes and at the interval the local program sets.

Protective equipment is a consumable and an inventory, and the program that manages it is as important as the items themselves. Gloves, masks, and single-use gowns have a shelf life, and latex, elastic, and the electrostatic layer of a mask all age faster in heat, sunlight, and humidity, so stock must be rotated and stored cool and dry rather than in a hot cupboard or a vehicle. Reusable goggles, face shields, rubber boots, and reusable gowns must be cleaned of organic soil and then disinfected with a product compatible with their material, and the number of laundering or disinfection cycles each item tolerates must be recorded so that it is withdrawn before its barrier fails. A powered air-purifying respirator needs a charging routine, a battery rotation, a filter change schedule, and a spare blower or hose, and the pre-use check for any item is a short inspection for a tear, a pinhole, a cracked lens, a lifted seal, a damp wrap, an expired date, and a battery indicator that shows a low charge. Every storage temperature, shelf life, disinfectant concentration, contact time, and filter change interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

When supplies fail, the pressure to reuse protective equipment becomes intense, and the honest answer is that some items can be managed through a shortage and others cannot. A single-use glove cannot be safely reprocessed, because the film absorbs detergent, loses its elasticity, and develops pinholes that are invisible to the wearer, so a washed glove is a false barrier. A filtering respirator can be worn for an extended period during a defined work shift under a written policy, but its filter media, straps, and nose clip degrade with moisture and repeated donning, and its electrostatic layer can be destroyed by alcohol, by steam, or by heat that leaves no visible mark. Some health systems have authorized decontamination of filtering respirators by hydrogen peroxide vapor or by ultraviolet irradiation under controlled conditions, and every method, cycle count, and reuse limit must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Extended use means wearing the same item for several patient contacts without removing it, which avoids the riskiest moment of removal at the cost of heat, moisture, and a false sense of duration. Reuse means taking an item off and putting it on again, which adds the risk of self-contamination at the face and of storage in a contaminated pocket, and the common error is to wear a soiled mask for a second shift because it still looks whole.

The person inside the equipment is the least specified component of the system, and heat is the limit that most often ends a task. The body loses heat mainly by evaporation of sweat, and evaporation slows as the humidity inside the garment rises, so an impermeable gown traps a layer of saturated air against the skin and removes the main cooling route. Core temperature rises with the workload, and the first effects are a fast pulse, heavy sweating, thirst, and headache, together with a decline in fine judgment that appears before any of those signs is noticed, while heat exhaustion brings weakness, dizziness, and nausea and heat stroke brings a hot dry skin, confusion, and loss of consciousness. Work and rest cycles, prehydration, electrolyte replacement, a cooling area, and a buddy who watches for the change in a colleague are the controls, and every work duration, rest period, and fluid volume must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Beyond heat, the equipment narrows the senses and the voice, since a respirator muffles speech and filters the high frequencies that carry consonants, goggles fog and reduce peripheral vision, and gloves reduce the sense of touch. Claustrophobia affects a proportion of wearers, and its signs are a rising breath rate, a feeling of suffocation while the air supply is intact, and a powerful urge to tear the mask away.

In a mobile clinic or a frontier hospital, the supply of certified protective equipment may be absent for weeks, and improvisation begins, so the honest task is to separate what still protects from what only looks like protection. A cloth mask of tightly woven cotton gives source control, because it holds back a proportion of the wearer's own droplets and shortens the distance they travel, and that is a real benefit when everyone in a room wears one. It is not respiratory protection, because it does not seal, it carries no electrostatic layer, and its fibers are far too coarse to capture the smallest particles, so a cloth mask must never be counted as a filtering respirator. A plastic sheet, a raincoat, or a heavy-duty refuse bag can serve as an apron for a splash, and a transparent sheet can serve as a face shield when it is held on a frame and cleaned between uses, while a plastic bag over the head is never a hood because it can suffocate the wearer. What improvisation gives up first is measured performance, since no improvised item has a filtration efficiency, a liquid resistance level, or a fit factor, and what it demands instead is a stricter discipline of distance, ventilation, hand hygiene, and task design. Improvisation is acceptable for source control and for splash protection, and it is not protection at all against an airborne pathogen in a closed room without ventilation, so every improvised material, thickness, and cleaning method must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The field version of doffing and cleaning has no anteroom, no running water, and often no waste system, so the sequence has to be built from what is present. A safe doffing point is established at the edge of the patient area, with a container for the contaminated items, a hand hygiene station, and a clean zone that the wearer steps into only after the removal is complete. Reusable cloth masks, gowns, aprons, and goggles are washed with soap and clean water, rinsed, and then treated by a method the local protocol accepts, which may be a dilute chlorine solution with a measured concentration and a measured contact time, or a boil, or drying in direct sunlight on a clean surface. A chlorine solution is a chemical hazard as well as a disinfectant, since it irritates the eyes and the airway and loses strength with heat and light, so it must be mixed to the stated concentration and used within the stated period, and every concentration, contact time, and boiling time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Items that cannot be cleaned, including the single-use glove, a used mask with a broken strap, and a soaked gown, are burned or buried by the method the local protocol defines, and the common error in the field is to wash and reuse a glove because gloves are scarce.

The field operator has no laboratory and no fit testing chamber, so the checks must be visual, functional, and repeated before every patient. A rough seal check can be done at the bedside with a hand over the front of a filtering facepiece, since a wearer who breathes in should feel the mask pull against the face, and a glove can be held to the light for a pinhole, a goggle held up for a scratch in the line of sight, and a gown turned to the light for a thin patch or a broken seam. Where a powered air-purifying respirator exists in a field setting, the battery must be charged on a fixed schedule, the filter must be changed on the schedule of hours the maker states, and the spare hose and head cover must travel with the unit. The honest limit of the field version is that it trades a measured barrier for a disciplined practice, and the operator must be willing to postpone a procedure when the barrier is not there. The common error is to assume that a device works because it worked last week, and the characteristic warning in the field is an item that has quietly changed, a mask that has gone soft, a strap that has stretched, a lens that has clouded, or a battery that no longer holds its charge, and every field threshold and replacement interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

To summarize, personal protective equipment is the barrier between the patient's infection and the staff's skin, and it works by screening, interception, diffusion, and electrostatic capture, with liquid resistance as a separate property from air filtration. Gloves are elastomer films whose protection depends on continuity, material, and fit, and natural rubber latex carries an allergy that can be immediate and dangerous while nitrile avoids the protein. The surgical mask is a loose filter that protects in both directions but does not seal, while a filtering respirator is classified by filtration efficiency and oil resistance and depends on a fit factor that only a fit test and a seal check can establish. A powered air-purifying respirator trades the effort of breathing for a battery, a blower, and a filter, and its low-battery and low-airflow alarms must be obeyed because they mark the end of protection. Eye and face protection answers impact, splash, and radiation with different designs, gowns and aprons are graded by liquid volume, pressure, and time, and boots, hoods, and sealed seams close the remaining gaps. In the field, cloth gives source control and plastic gives splash cover, but neither gives a measured barrier, and the honest rule is that improvisation is acceptable for droplets and for soil and is not protection against an airborne pathogen in a closed room without ventilation.
