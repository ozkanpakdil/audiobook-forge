# Chapter 38 — Anesthesia Machines and Monitoring

Anesthesia is a controlled and reversible unconsciousness, and the machine that produces it mixes gases and moves them. A liquid agent evaporates into a carrier gas, and the concentration of that vapor produces unconsciousness, so the machine must make a mixture of known composition and move it into the lungs. Gases flow only when a pressure difference pushes them from a higher pressure to a lower one, so the compressed supply, the regulator, the needle valve, and the flowmeter create and measure that difference. The circuit turns a steady fresh gas flow into the rhythm of breathing, the ventilator replaces the patient's muscles, and the monitor reveals what is happening inside.

Anesthetic gases reach the machine from a pipeline or a cylinder, and the physics of each decides how it behaves as it runs low. A hospital pipeline delivers oxygen, medical air, and nitrous oxide at a working pressure of about 4 bar, a value that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A full oxygen cylinder at room temperature holds gas at about 137 bar, a value that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, so a regulator must lower the pressure first. The gauge reads pressure and not contents, so a cylinder reads full until the charge is nearly spent, and each cylinder connects through a pin index arrangement and a color code that admits only the correct gas. A crossed pipeline can deliver the wrong gas, and the oxygen failure alarm is the last defense, with the characteristic alarm a rising whistle followed by a shut-off of nitrous oxide, a threshold that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Flowmeters turn a pressure difference into a readable number, because a float in a tapered tube rises until the gap around it passes the chosen flow. The reading is taken at the top of a ball or the widest edge of a bobbin, a convention that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A needle valve controls each gas, and the anesthetist sets the total flow and the oxygen proportion by eye, which is why the flowmeter is among the most misread instruments on the machine. A hypoxic guard links the valves so that the oxygen concentration cannot fall below about 25 per cent, a value that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the common error is to reuse the flow settings chosen for one patient on a small child.

A vaporizer converts a liquid agent into a precise concentration of vapor, and its physics is evaporation from a saturated surface. A liquid anesthetic has a vapor pressure at room temperature, and gas passed over it becomes saturated at a concentration set by temperature and by the agent rather than by the flow. To deliver a concentration far below saturation, the vaporizer splits the fresh gas between a vaporizing chamber and a bypass, and a bimetallic strip opens the bypass as evaporation cools the liquid, which is temperature compensation. Each vaporizer is calibrated for one agent only, so its dial means nothing with a different liquid inside, and the wrong agent, an overfilled chamber, or a tipped vaporizer produces a concentration far above the setting. An interlock prevents two agents from being used at once, and a typical dial setting is 1 to 2 per cent, a value that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, while the common error is to fill while the vaporizer is in use.

The circle system lets the patient breathe the same gas more than once, and it exists to save agent, heat, and moisture. Fresh gas enters near the patient, and the exhaled gas passes through an expiratory valve, a canister of carbon dioxide absorbent, a reservoir bag that stores gas between breaths, and an inspiratory valve back to the patient. An adjustable pressure limiting valve, also called the pop-off valve, releases the excess and sets the circuit pressure, and the unidirectional valves are the heart of the design, because a valve that sticks open or is fitted backwards lets exhaled carbon dioxide flow back to the patient. The absorbent, usually soda lime, removes carbon dioxide by a reaction of carbon dioxide, water, and calcium hydroxide that releases heat and leaves calcium carbonate, so the canister grows warm and the granules crumble as they are spent. Moisture is essential to that reaction, so a dried canister passes carbon dioxide, and the characteristic warning is a rising inspired carbon dioxide on the capnograph, a threshold that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, while the common error is to trust the color of the granules alone.

When the circuit has no absorber it is called a Mapleson arrangement, and its physics is the physics of flow, because fresh gas must sweep the exhaled gas out before the patient breathes it again. The arrangements are lettered from A to F, and the Bain arrangement is a coaxial version in which fresh gas runs down a narrow inner tube to the patient while exhaled gas returns through the outer tube to a reservoir bag and an expiratory valve at the machine end. For spontaneous breathing the fresh gas flow must exceed the peak inspiratory flow, which may reach 20 to 30 liters per minute in an adult, a range that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and for controlled ventilation a flow of about 70 milliliters per kilogram per minute is often quoted, a figure that must also be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The Bain circuit is light, warm, and easy to position, but it consumes large volumes of fresh gas, and a reversed inner tube turns it into a large dead space whose sign is a rising carbon dioxide and a falling saturation. The common error is to use a low flow suitable for a circle system in a Mapleson arrangement, because the rules of one circuit do not transfer to another.

Waste anesthetic gas leaving the expiratory valve is a hazard to everyone in the room, and scavenging collects it and carries it away. A scavenging interface sits between the circuit and the disposal system and contains a reservoir and relief valves, because the system may pull with active suction or the patient may push gas out faster than the duct can carry it. If the scavenging hose kinks or the vacuum fails, the reservoir bag fills and the circuit pressure rises, and the negative pressure relief valve exists so that the patient does not breathe against a suction that is too strong. Chronic exposure of theater staff to volatile agents and to nitrous oxide is associated with headache, fatigue, and reproductive harm, and the common error is to clamp a scavenging hose that keeps falling off.

The integrated ventilator is a machine inside the machine, and it takes over the work of breathing when the patient is paralyzed or deeply anesthetized. Most anesthesia ventilators are driven by compressed gas or by an electric motor and deliver a set volume or a set pressure at a set rate through the same circuit the patient has been breathing from. The direction of the bellows matters, because an ascending bellows rises when it fills while a descending bellows falls by its own weight, so a disconnected circuit can let a descending bellows cycle and look normal. The controls set the tidal volume, the respiratory rate, the ratio of inspiratory to expiratory time, the positive end-expiratory pressure, and the pressure limit, and typical adult values are a tidal volume of 6 to 8 milliliters per kilogram and a rate of 10 to 12 breaths per minute, values that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The high airway pressure alarm usually means a kink, a cough, secretions, or a stiff lung, while a low pressure alarm means a leak or a disconnection, and the common error is to silence the alarm and continue.

The machine is checked before every case, and the check is a physical examination rather than a ritual. The operator confirms that the cylinders are full and the pipeline hoses are connected, that the flowmeters move, that each vaporizer is filled, seated, and locked, and that the interlock prevents two agents at once. The breathing system is then tested for leaks by occluding the patient end and closing the adjustable pressure limiting valve, and a pressure of about 30 centimeters of water must hold for about 10 seconds, values that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The test finds the cracked gasket, the worn seal, the loose canister, the split reservoir bag, and the badly seated valve, and it must be repeated after any part of the circuit has been taken apart. The unidirectional valves are inspected, the absorber is checked for fresh granules, the scavenging hose is checked for kinks, the ventilator is cycled against a test lung, and the airway trolley, the suction, the drugs, and the resuscitation equipment are checked as one package.

Capnography is the measurement of carbon dioxide in the exhaled breath, and it reports that the tube is in the trachea, that the circuit is connected, and that the patient is producing carbon dioxide. The sensor works because carbon dioxide absorbs infrared light at a particular wavelength, and the amount absorbed is proportional to the number of molecules in the light path, so a chamber in the gas stream and a detector produce a continuous reading. The waveform has four parts, an inspiratory baseline near zero, a steep expiratory upstroke, an alveolar plateau that ends at the end-tidal value, and an inspiratory downstroke, and the number alone is almost useless without the shape. A tube in the esophagus produces no sustained waveform, a disconnection produces a flat line, and an exhausted absorber produces a baseline that never returns to zero. The end-tidal value is normally a few millimeters of mercury below the arterial value, a difference that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the common error is to read a low number as hyperventilation when the sampling line is blocked by water.

Pulse oximetry measures the oxygen carried by hemoglobin, and its physics is the different absorption of light by hemoglobin with and without oxygen bound to it. Oxygenated hemoglobin absorbs more infrared light and deoxygenated hemoglobin absorbs more red light, so a probe that shines both wavelengths through a finger or an ear can calculate a ratio that corresponds to the saturation. The calculation uses only the pulsatile part of the signal, assumed to come from arterial blood, which is why motion, poor perfusion, cold, ambient light, and nail polish corrupt the reading. A saturation below about 90 per cent is a warning that the oxygen content of the blood has fallen sharply, a threshold that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, yet the device cannot distinguish a patient who is not breathing from one who is breathing well, and the common error is to trust the saturation as a measure of ventilation when it measures oxygenation alone.

The gas analyzer measures the concentration of oxygen and of the volatile agent in inspired and expired gas, and it closes the loop that the flowmeter and the vaporizer dial leave open. A paramagnetic analyzer uses the fact that oxygen is drawn into a magnetic field while most other gases are not, and a fuel cell produces a current proportional to the oxygen it consumes, so each type ages and must be calibrated. The agent channel uses infrared absorption at wavelengths chosen for each agent, and some instruments identify the agent itself, which is how a vaporizer filled with the wrong liquid is detected before the patient is harmed. The characteristic alarms are the low inspired oxygen alarm and the agent concentration alarms, with thresholds that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the common error is to treat the analyzer as decoration and rely on the dials alone.

Depth of anesthesia monitoring tries to measure the effect of the drug on the brain rather than the amount of drug in the machine. A processed electroencephalography (EEG) monitor places a few adhesive electrodes on the forehead and reduces the cortical signal into a single index number between about zero and one hundred, with values lower than about 40 suggesting deep anesthesia and values higher than about 60 suggesting awareness, thresholds that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The raw EEG and the trend are more informative than the index, because muscle activity, electrical interference, and the surgical diathermy contaminate the signal, and some drugs including ketamine and nitrous oxide produce unconsciousness with a high index. The monitor is most useful during total intravenous anesthesia and in patients at high risk of awareness, and the common error is to deepen anesthesia to chase a number while the blood pressure falls.

The anesthesia monitor also watches circulation, temperature, and the return of muscle power, and each channel has its own physical principle. The electrocardiography (ECG) electrodes measure the small voltage generated by heart muscle as it depolarizes, so a change in the surgical field or in the depth of anesthesia is seen at once. Blood pressure is measured non-invasively by an oscillating cuff that detects the pulsation of the artery, or invasively by a catheter connected to a fluid column and a transducer that converts pressure into an electrical signal. A neuromuscular monitor stimulates a peripheral nerve and measures the muscle response, and a ratio of the fourth response to the first greater than about 0.9 is needed before safe extubation, a value that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Temperature is measured by a probe in the esophagus, the nasopharynx, or the bladder, and a fall below about 36 degrees Celsius is worth treating, a threshold that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Anesthetized patients lose heat quickly, and the warming devices that prevent it are as much a part of the machine as the ventilator. Heat leaves the body by radiation, convection, conduction, and evaporation from the wound and from the lungs, and anesthesia removes the normal defenses by dilating the blood vessels and by reducing the shivering response. A forced air warmer blows warm air through a disposable blanket over the patient, a resistive blanket uses electrical current in a carbon-loaded fabric, and a circulating water mattress conducts heat from below, while fluid warmers pass intravenous fluid or blood through a coil in a warm water bath or against a heated plate. A typical target is a fluid temperature near 37 degrees Celsius, a value that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the hazards are a burn, an air embolism from a warmer that was not primed, and a damaged blood product from a heater that is too hot. The characteristic alarm is an over-temperature cutout, and a patient whose temperature rises instead of falls may have malignant hyperthermia, a rare reaction to a volatile agent or to succinylcholine that must be recognized early and treated with the specific drug and with active cooling.

Maintenance is a schedule of small tasks that together prevent most serious failures, and it begins with the daily check and the leak test already described. After each case the surfaces are wiped with the agent the maker approves, the water traps are emptied, the condensate is removed, the soda lime is changed when it is spent, and the breathing circuit, the mask, the filter, and the sampling line are reprocessed or discarded according to their labeling. Filters, circuits, reservoir bags, valve discs, gaskets, seals, and temperature probes are consumables with a stated life, and their replacement intervals must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, because a reused filter that has been wetted or autoclaved no longer filters. At intervals set by the maker, the vaporizers are serviced, the oxygen analyzer cell is replaced, the gas analyzer is calibrated with a known gas, the relief valves are tested, the battery is replaced, and the electrical safety of the machine is verified. The service record is part of the equipment, and the common error is to clean a machine with a dripping cloth so that fluid enters the electronics or the flow sensors.

The serious failures of the anesthesia machine fall into a small number of families, and naming them makes them recognizable. A failure of oxygen supply, from an empty cylinder, a closed valve, a crossed pipeline, or a plant shutdown, leads to a hypoxic mixture and a falling saturation, and the oxygen failure alarm gives the operator seconds to act. A failure of the circuit, whether a disconnection, a kink, a stuck valve, or an exhausted absorber, leads to a rising carbon dioxide or to no ventilation at all, and capnography shows it first. A failure of the vaporizer, from overfilling, tipping, wrong agent, or a defective temperature compensator, leads to a concentration far from the dial, and a failure of the ventilator requires immediate hand ventilation with a self-inflating bag. Fires and burns occur in an oxygen-rich atmosphere when the diathermy, a laser, or a spark meets alcohol-based skin preparation that has not dried, so the anesthetist must announce every change and keep the record in real time.

The field variant begins with the draw-over vaporizer, which works on the opposite principle to the hospital machine because the patient generates the flow. The patient breathes in through a low-resistance vaporizer and a one-way valve, drawing room air across the liquid agent, so no compressed gas and no electricity are required and the whole apparatus fits in a case. A self-inflating bag can be added to assist ventilation, and oxygen from a concentrator or a small cylinder can be enriched into the inhaled gas, but the concentration depends on the patient's minute volume, the temperature, the agent, and the altitude. The output is therefore less predictable than that of a plumbed machine, so the dial setting must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the hazards are hypoxemia, overdose when a high minute volume pulls more vapor than expected, and hypothermia from cold dry gas. None of these is announced by an alarm because there is no gas analyzer and usually no capnography, and the common error is to leave the patient unattended while the surgeon works.

Oxygen in the field comes from a concentrator, from cylinders, or from a small plant, and the concentrator manufactures the gas from room air. It works by pressure swing adsorption, in which air is compressed and passed through a bed of zeolite that adsorbs nitrogen in preference to oxygen, so the gas leaving the bed is enriched to about 90 to 95 per cent oxygen, a range that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Two beds alternate so that the flow is continuous, and a compressor, a set of valves, intake filters, and a sieve bed are the parts that fail, usually because of dust, heat, and humidity. A concentrator delivers a limited flow, often 5 to 10 liters per minute, a range that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and it cannot drive a ventilator or fill a cylinder because it produces low pressure, which is a dangerous misunderstanding.

Where no machine exists at all, anesthesia is produced by drugs rather than by gas, and ketamine has carried most of the surgery performed in frontier clinics. Ketamine produces unconsciousness and profound analgesia while preserving the patient's own breathing and airway reflexes more than most agents, and it is given intravenously or intramuscularly at a dose that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Its limitations include a rise in blood pressure and heart rate, excessive secretions, emergence delirium, and the possibility of laryngospasm or vomiting, so a drying drug is often given first and suction and an airway kit must be within reach. Spinal anesthesia, regional blocks, and local infiltration are the other pillars of the field technique, and in all of them the operator must be trained and must stay with the patient, because there is no ventilator, no gas analyzer, and no alarm to call for help.

Monitoring in the field is reduced to the observations a trained person can make with the senses and with a few battery-powered devices. A precordial stethoscope taped over the upper chest or the sternal notch carries the breath sounds, the heart sounds, and the movement of air, and it is the cheapest continuous monitor in existence. A colorimetric carbon dioxide detector placed between the tube and the breathing bag changes color when carbon dioxide is present, because a pH-sensitive indicator reacts with the gas, so it confirms that the tube is in the trachea, though gastric contents can confuse it. A battery-powered pulse oximeter with a reusable probe gives the oxygen saturation and the pulse, and a manual blood pressure cuff, a watch, a thermometer, and a urine collection bag complete the set. The operator must record the observations at a fixed interval, usually every 5 minutes during a general anesthetic, a figure that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, because the trend is more informative than any single value.

The field equipment survives by simple maintenance and by planning for the part that will fail next. The draw-over vaporizer is drained, cleaned, dried, and stored upright with its filler capped, and the concentrator filters are cleaned after every dusty day and replaced at the maker's interval. Oxygen cylinders are checked for the hydrostatic requalification date fixed by national rules, which must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and a cylinder is stored upright, secured, away from grease and flame, and never left with a regulator attached in transit. Batteries are charged whenever power is available, and the spare parts that matter most are the seals, the breathing tubes, the valve discs, the oxygen tubing, the fuses, and a spare pulse oximeter probe.

The comparison between the two versions is the heart of the matter, and the field version gives up a great deal and demands a great deal in return. The hospital machine gives continuous measurement of oxygen, carbon dioxide, and agent, a ventilator of known performance, a scavenging system, and a maintenance organization behind it, while the field kit gives up all of that for portability and independence from pipelines and mains power. What it demands instead is a trained provider who never leaves the patient, a disciplined oxygen supply, a pre-use check performed from memory with the same care as a printed list, and a plan for the three emergencies of apnea, hypoxemia, and cardiac arrest. The measure of safety is therefore not the amount of equipment present but the presence of a trained person who understands the physics, watches the patient, and expects the failure before it happens.

To summarize, the anesthesia machine is a gas mixer, a vaporizer, a breathing circuit, and a ventilator that together produce a controlled concentration of anesthetic vapor and move it in and out of the lungs. The gas arrives from a pipeline or a cylinder, the flowmeter proportions it, the vaporizer adds the agent, the circuit conserves it, the absorber removes the carbon dioxide, and the scavenging carries the waste away. The monitors are the other half of the machine, because capnography confirms the tube and the circuit, pulse oximetry follows the oxygen, the gas analyzer confirms the mixture, and the processed electroencephalogram estimates the depth. Every number named in this chapter, whether a pressure, a flow, a concentration, a temperature, or an alarm threshold, must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The machine must be checked before every case, the circuit must hold pressure, and the consumables that filter, seal, and sample must be replaced on schedule rather than when they visibly fail. In the field, the draw-over vaporizer, the oxygen concentrator, the colorimetric detector, the pulse oximeter, and the drugs replace the plumbed machine, and what they demand instead is a trained provider who stays at the head of the patient and expects the failure before it happens.
