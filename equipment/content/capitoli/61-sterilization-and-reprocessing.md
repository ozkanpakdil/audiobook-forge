# Chapter 61 — Sterilization and Reprocessing

Every instrument that touches a patient carries a history, and this chapter is about making that history safe to repeat. Sterilization kills what remains, including the spores that survive boiling and alcohol. The central sterile services department, shortened to CSSD, is a one-way flow from dirty to clean. Every setting, temperature, cycle time, and alarm threshold named here must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

The vocabulary of reprocessing is a ladder of decreasing certainty. Cleaning removes soil with water, detergent, and friction, and it kills nothing. Disinfection destroys most organisms but not necessarily spores, while sterilization destroys all microbial life, including spores. A validated cycle leaves less than one organism in a million, and the acceptance criteria must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

The workflow runs in one direction only, from collection back to the patient. Its order is collection, cleaning, rinsing, inspection, packaging, sterilization, drying, storage, and distribution. The first and last links fail most often, because a tray that dries on a trolley and a pack kept damp both defeat a perfect cycle. The common operator error is to carry a clean pack back through the dirty zone.

Collection decides how hard every later step will be, because blood sets into a cannulated instrument within minutes. The safety hazard is the puncture, because loose scalpels and needles cause most needlestick injuries. The common operator error is to soak instruments in saline, which corrodes steel. The delay before cleaning must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

Cleaning is the decisive step, and its chemistry rewards understanding. An enzymatic detergent contains proteases that break protein, lipases that break fat, and amylases that break starch. Every joint is opened and every lumen is brushed until the bristles emerge at the far end. The safety hazard is the sharp instrument and the aerosol, so brushing happens below the water. The detergent concentration, the water temperature, and the contact time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

The ultrasonic cleaner reaches where a brush cannot, and its principle is the collapse of small bubbles rather than the sound itself. A transducer drives the water at a high frequency, often near 40 kilohertz. This collapse is called cavitation, and the tank must be degassed and the load immersed for it to work. The foil test marks a dead spot as a smooth region on a foil strip, and the frequency and the cycle duration must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

The ultrasonic cleaner heats its solution, so the operator wears gloves, and the common error is to rest the load in a solid tray that blocks the sound path. The washer-disinfector combines washing, rinsing, and thermal disinfection in one chamber, where a rotating spray arm throws jets at every angle. A cycle runs through a pre-wash, a wash, a rinse, a thermal disinfection phase, and a hot air drying phase. The disinfection phase holds the load near 90 degrees Celsius for several minutes, and it is measured by the combination of temperature and time. A low temperature alarm or a phase timeout means the load is reprocessed rather than released.

The safety hazard of the washer is a chamber that opens with hot water inside, so the door interlock is essential. The common operator error is to overload the basket to save a cycle, which shadows the jets. Hard water dries as a scale that traps organisms, so the final rinse uses treated water. The load weight limit, the rinse water quality, and the residue limit must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

Inspection under a light is the human quality gate, and it happens after cleaning and before packaging. The surface is examined for cracks and rust, and a lumen is checked by looking through it toward a light. The safety hazard is an instrument returned to service with a defect, because a cracked jaw can break inside a wound. The magnification must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

Packaging exists because of a physical compromise, since a package must pass steam and air while holding organisms out. Sterilization wrap, a crepe paper, a paper and plastic peel pouch, and a rigid container with a filter all achieve this compromise. Air must leave and steam must enter, so a wrap applied too tightly leaves an air pocket at the center. The safety hazard is a package closed with staples or pins, and the package weight limit and shelf life must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

The common hospital pattern is the envelope fold, in which one corner forms a flap that opens without contaminating the contents. Pouches are sealed with a heat sealer along a straight line that leaves a margin. The closure tape carries a chemical process indicator, and the package is labeled with the contents, the cycle number, the date, and the preparer's name. The common operator error is to apply so much tape that steam cannot pass.

Moist heat works because heat plus water destroys the protein and the nucleic acid of an organism. Dry heat at 160 degrees Celsius takes 2 hours, while saturated steam at 134 degrees Celsius achieves the same result in a few minutes. Steam that condenses on a cool surface releases latent heat directly onto the organisms, and water is what makes moist heat fast. The temperature, the pressure, and the air removal method must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

The gravity displacement sterilizer is the older design, and its physics is a race between steam entering at the top and air falling out below. A typical gravity cycle holds the chamber near 121 degrees Celsius for 20 to 30 minutes at a gauge pressure near 1 bar. The safety hazard is a load that retained an air pocket, because the record looks normal while the center of the pack is unsterile. The cycle temperature, the holding time, and the pressure must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

The pre-vacuum sterilizer removes air with a pump rather than with gravity. A vacuum pump draws air out of the chamber and the load in one or more pulses before steam is admitted. A typical pre-vacuum cycle holds the chamber near 134 degrees Celsius for 3 to 4 minutes at a gauge pressure near 2 bar, so it can process a dense set and a long lumen. The safety hazard is a vacuum leak, which admits air and leaves a cold pocket in the middle of a load. The vacuum depth and the holding time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

The air removal test is the daily proof that a pre-vacuum sterilizer does what the cycle assumes. A standard test pack printed with chemical indicator ink is placed near the front bottom of an empty chamber, and the ink changes color uniformly when steam reaches it. A cold spot in the middle shows that air was trapped. A leak rate test measures whether the chamber holds a vacuum, so a rapid pressure rise means air is entering through a gasket or a valve. The common operator error is to adjust the door without replacing the worn gasket, and the test pack specification and the permitted pressure rise must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

Steam quality decides whether condensation actually happens, and it is invisible in the daily rush. Saturated steam is steam just at the point of condensing, so a dryness fraction near nine tenths is the minimum often quoted. Wet steam carries droplets that contribute no latent heat, while non-condensable gases form insulating films on the load. The dryness fraction and the non-condensable gas limit must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

Loading is the operator's contribution to steam penetration, and a correctly loaded chamber is the difference between a validated cycle and a decoration. Trays are placed flat so that condensate can run off, and pouches stand on edge with the paper facing one way. Nothing touches the chamber wall, because the wall is at the steam temperature while the item beside it is still heating. The maximum load weight and the spacing between packs must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

The instrument panel records the cycle, and reading that record is a required operator task. The printout shows the time, the temperature, the pressure, and the phases, and the operator compares the trace with the specified cycle before releasing the load. The characteristic alarms are a door that is not sealed, a chamber that fails to reach its temperature, a low water supply, and an interruption by the emergency stop. The safety hazard is a load released on the strength of a green light alone, since the light says only that the controller finished its program. The acceptance range for each phase must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

Drying is the phase operators most often cut short, and a wet package is a contaminated package. Water that remains in a pack wicks along the paper and carries organisms inward, a phenomenon called strike-through that means the barrier has failed. The safety hazard is a hot load handled immediately, and the common operator error is to open the door the instant the cycle finishes. The drying time and the cool-down period must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

The daily and periodic care of a sterilizer keeps the machine inside its validated state. The drain strainer is cleaned of lint, because a partly blocked drain traps air and produces wet loads. The door gasket is inspected for cuts, and the water supply and steam filters are checked. The safety hazard is stored heat and pressure, because a vessel at 134 degrees Celsius can scald, and the common operator error is to defer a small fault to the end of the month. The cleaning interval and the safety valve test pressure must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

Not every instrument can survive steam, and the low-temperature methods exist for the plastics, electronics, and fine optics that heat would destroy. Ethylene oxide sterilizes by alkylating the nucleic acid and the proteins of an organism, so it kills spores and viruses without heat damage. The gas requires a precise concentration, a temperature near 55 degrees Celsius, a humidity, and a long exposure, and it is followed by an aeration phase that draws residual gas out of porous materials. The safety hazard is severe, since the gas is a carcinogen that irritates the airway and forms an explosive mixture with air, so the sterilizer stands in a ventilated room and staff wear a monitoring badge. The gas concentration, the exposure time, and the aeration time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

Hydrogen peroxide gas plasma is the low-temperature method most often found beside an operating theatre. The hydrogen peroxide is vaporized into a chamber, and a radio frequency field turns the vapor into a plasma that completes the killing. The process leaves no toxic residue, because the by-products are water and oxygen, but the vapor cannot reach the inside of a long narrow lumen or the layers of a cellulose material. Water left inside a lumen blocks the process, so the load must be dry, and the chamber temperature and the material compatibility list must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

Peracetic acid is a liquid sterilant used mainly for endoscopes that tolerate a short immersion. The process is automated in a processor that pumps the solution through the channels of the device and then rinses with treated water, so no residue remains inside a channel. The usual working concentration is near 0.2 per cent, the temperature is often held near 50 degrees Celsius, and a cycle of about 12 minutes is typical. The solution has a limited use life, and the concentration, the temperature, and the contact time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

Chemical high-level disinfection is a separate tier, and confusing it with sterilization is one of the most consequential errors in reprocessing. A glutaraldehyde solution near 2 per cent disinfects at a contact time of about 20 minutes, while many hours of immersion are needed before any claim of sterilization. The safety hazard is the corrosive effect on skin, so gloves and eye protection are worn. The common operator error is to top up a used batch instead of mixing a fresh one, and the contact time and the ventilation requirement must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

The indicators of a cycle are the paper trail that connects a machine's behavior to a patient, and three families answer three different questions. An external process indicator, such as the tape on the outside of a pack, confirms only that the package was exposed to a process, and it changes color in a dry chamber as readily as in a wet one. An internal chemical indicator sits inside the pack at the hardest point for the agent to reach, but it still measures the environment rather than the death of organisms. A physical indicator is the independent record of temperature and pressure, and the common operator error is to treat a changed tape as proof of sterility, when the tape proves neither condition nor killing.

The biological indicator is the only routine test that measures killing rather than conditions, and it does so with living spores. A carrier holds a known population of highly resistant spores, and the carrier is placed inside a test pack in the least favorable position in the load. After the cycle it is incubated at a controlled temperature for a set period, usually between several hours and 2 days, and a color change indicates that spores survived. The characteristic alarm is a failed indicator, and it triggers a recall of every load processed since the last acceptable test. The common operator error is to incubate the test without a positive control, and the incubation temperature and the incubation time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

Validation is the formal proof that a process does what it claims, and it happens before a machine is trusted and after every significant change. Installation qualification confirms that the machine was installed to specification with the correct utilities. Operational qualification proves that the chamber can reach and hold its temperature and vacuum across its volume. Performance qualification then uses a full load of the most difficult items and a large number of biological indicators, and the machine is requalified on a fixed interval. The requalification interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

Sterile storage is where a perfectly sterilized load becomes a contaminated one. A package remains sterile as long as the barrier is intact, dry, and undisturbed. The storage area is a clean, dry, dust-free room with controlled temperature and humidity, and shelves keep packs above the floor and away from the wall. A package that is crushed, wet, torn, or punctured has lost its barrier, so the department sets the shelf life and rotates stock so that the oldest pack is used first. The safety hazard is a pack stored on a wet floor, and the shelf life of each material must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

Distribution carries packs to the theatre in a closed trolley, and a case cart holds the instruments, the drapes, and the consumables together. The safety hazard is a sterile pack transported in an open basket through a corridor where waste and soiled linen also travel. Traceability is the memory of the department, so every load carries a record of the sterilizer, the cycle number, the date, the operator, and the contents. Every package carries a label that links it to that record, and when a biological indicator fails the record identifies every pack from the affected loads. The common operator error is to write the load contents from memory after the fact, and the recall procedure must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

The consumables of a sterile processing department are cheap in isolation and decisive in aggregate. The list includes enzymatic and neutral detergents, rinse aids, instrument lubricant, brushes, sterilization wrap and pouches, sealing tape, indicator strips, biological indicators and their incubators, rigid containers and filters, treated water and softener salt, gloves and personal protective equipment, labels, and log books. The maintenance of the department is a schedule of small tasks shared between the operator and the biomedical technician, and the safety hazard is a substitute product bought because it is cheaper. The stock level and the service interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

The inspection of a pack before it is opened is the last chance to catch a barrier that has already failed. The operator looks for a torn wrap, a broken seal, a punctured pouch, water stains, a crushed package, and a label that matches the patient. The safety hazard is the pack that looks intact but has been stored damp, since the strike-through is invisible from the outside. The people in the department are the last instrument in the chain, and a technician who understands why air must be removed will load a chamber correctly.

The field variant of this entire department is a pressure cooker, a source of heat, a timer, a thermometer, and a log book. The physics is the same as a gravity displacement sterilizer in miniature, because a sealed pot raises the pressure inside and water then boils above 100 degrees Celsius. The lid carries a weight or a valve that holds the pressure at a set value, and the load stands on a trivet above a measured depth of water. The timing begins when the weight first rocks steadily, not when the flame is lit. The target pressure, the water volume, and the timing point must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

A correctly run pressure cooker cycle is short and strictly defined, and most failures come from rushing one of its steps. The usual field parameters are a temperature near 121 degrees Celsius held for 15 to 20 minutes at a gauge pressure near 1 bar. The heat is raised until the weight rocks and steam is vented, at which point the interval is counted. After the interval the pot is allowed to cool until the weight can be lifted without resistance, and only then is the lid opened, because forcing a lid under pressure throws boiling steam onto the operator. The cooling time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

Cleaning in the field is the same three-step process carried out in three marked containers instead of a machine. The first holds plain water for the gross removal of blood, the second holds warm water with an enzymatic detergent, and the third holds clean water for the rinse. Instruments are opened, disassembled, brushed below the surface of the detergent, and their lumens are flushed with a syringe. The safety hazard is the sharp instrument in a bucket of cloudy water, where the hand cannot see the point. The common operator error is to rinse in the same water used for the wash, and the detergent dilution must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

Boiling is high-level disinfection and not sterilization, and the distinction decides whether a spore survives. Water boils at 100 degrees Celsius at sea level and at a lower temperature at altitude. That temperature kills most bacteria and viruses but does not reliably kill spores, so a boiled instrument is acceptable for many contacts and unacceptable for a sterile body site. The pot has a lid, the load sits on a trivet, and the timing starts when the water boils. The safety hazard is the pot topped up with cold water during the cycle, which lowers the temperature and stops the clock, and the boiling duration and the altitude correction must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

Chemical high-level disinfection is the field alternative where heat is unavailable, and it is the most error-prone procedure in the chapter. A chlorine solution at a working strength near 0.5 per cent is used with a contact time in the range of 10 to 30 minutes for a heavily contaminated instrument. A weaker solution near 0.05 per cent is used for clean surfaces, and a glutaraldehyde solution near 2 per cent is used for heat-sensitive devices. The solution is prepared by adding concentrate to water, never the reverse, and its strength is checked with a test strip. The device is rinsed with boiled and cooled water afterwards, and the safety hazard is the chemical in the eye or the airway.

Drying and packaging in the field are the steps where a well-sterilized instrument is most often lost, because a wet wrap is no wrap at all. The dry instrument is then wrapped in two layers of cloth or heavy paper, sealed with tape that carries a process indicator, and labeled with the contents and the date. The package is stored in a covered box, off the floor and away from the cooking fire. The safety hazard is a pack dried on a dusty surface, and the typical field shelf life is short, often measured in days for a cloth wrap, so the shelf life must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

The field log is the only quality record the clinic has, and it turns a habit into a system that a new person can follow. The book records the date, the pot, the number of items, the time the weight began to rock, the temperature reached, and the duration. A chemical indicator strip inside the pack and an external tape on the outside are the field substitutes for a printout, and a maximum-reading thermometer shows whether the target was ever reached. The safety hazard of a log completed from memory is a false record, and the common operator error is to fill it in before the cycle is finished. The log content and the supervision interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

The fuel and the heat source are part of the field sterilizer, and their behavior explains many of the cycles that fail. A kerosene stove or a gas burner delivers a high, steady flame and is preferred. Electricity is the cleanest source when it is available, so a field clinic keeps a backup fuel. The safety hazard is the open flame beside a fuel container, and the flame size and the fuel storage distance must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

Dry heat is the field method of last resort for items that cannot take moisture. An oven or a closed metal box at 160 degrees Celsius for 2 hours sterilizes glass, metal, and powders. Dry heat penetrates by conduction and radiation rather than by condensation, so it does not reach the inside of a wrapped pack. The load must be spaced so that hot air circulates, and the timing begins when the oven has actually reached its temperature. The safety hazard is a load removed while it is still hot, and the temperature and the holding time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

The turnaround time of the field cycle shapes the whole clinic schedule, because a gravity pot with no vacuum needs a long cycle plus cooling plus time for cleaning and drying. The clinic keeps three or four complete sets per operating table in rotation, and the instruments needed most often are duplicated first. The safety hazard is a decision to use an instrument that has only been boiled and not sterilized, and the common operator error is to shorten the cooling time to keep up with the list. The number of sets and the planned cycle time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

What the field variant gives up is the vacuum, the automatic control, the drying, and the record, and what it demands instead is time, fuel, water, and discipline. It gives up the pre-vacuum cycle, so it cannot reliably process a long lumen or a dense wrapped set. It gives up the independent printout, so the only evidence is a thermometer reading, an indicator strip, and a written log, and it gives up the validated shelf life. The field version is not a worse sterilizer but a slower and more demanding one, and it fails for the same reasons as the hospital machine.

The two halves of this chapter meet at one point, which is that sterilization is a physical process that cannot be certified by a light or a color alone. A hospital buys penetration, repeatability, monitoring, and traceability with its vacuum pumps and its laboratory. A field clinic buys independence and portability with its fuel, its water, and its discipline. Neither can skip the removal of air, the removal of soil, the removal of water, and the confirmation that the load actually reached its conditions.

To summarize, reprocessing is a chain of physical steps that begins with cleaning and ends with a traceable sterile pack. Enzymatic detergent and friction remove the soil that would shelter organisms and consume chemical agents, and only then can steam, ethylene oxide, hydrogen peroxide plasma, or peracetic acid kill what remains. Indicators measure exposure, conditions, and death in three different ways, so the external tape proves only that a process occurred, the internal indicator proves the environment, and the biological indicator proves the kill. Every setting, pressure, temperature, cycle time, concentration, and alarm threshold in this chapter must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.
