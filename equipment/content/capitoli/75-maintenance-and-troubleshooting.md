# Chapter 75 — Maintenance and Troubleshooting

Maintenance is the management of wear, and troubleshooting is the disciplined reading of its symptoms. Every physical device changes as it is used, because bearings lose their lubricant, seals harden, solder joints crack from thermal cycling, filters load with dust, batteries lose capacity, and sensors drift as their materials age. These changes do not announce themselves, and they accumulate quietly until a device crosses from working to not working, often during the busiest hour of the ward. Maintenance intervenes before that crossing, and troubleshooting finds the cause rather than the symptom after it has happened. This chapter describes its subject twice, first the hospital-grade program of schedules, work orders, calibration, and a biomedical engineering department, then the same discipline rebuilt in a frontier clinic from a toolkit, a manual, and a telephone.

Wear is a physical process with identifiable mechanisms, each with its own signature. Abrasion removes material where two surfaces slide, which is why cables fail where they bend and pump tubing wears thin where rollers squeeze it. Fatigue cracks metal that flexes repeatedly, so a bracket breaks after many small movements. Corrosion eats contacts and connectors where moisture, salt, or cleaning fluid collects, and its result is an intermittent fault that appears and disappears. Thermal cycling opens solder joints as a device warms and cools, while battery chemistry ages with every charge and every hour spent hot.

The pattern of failure over the life of a device is usually drawn as a curve with 3 regions. Early failure comes from manufacturing defects and shipping damage, the middle region is a long plateau of low failure, and the final region is wear-out, when failures rise steeply and each repair buys less time than the last. Preventive maintenance keeps a device on the plateau by replacing parts with a known life before they reach wear-out. A hardened seal changed during planned downtime costs a predictable part and a few hours of labor, while the same seal failing during a dialysis session costs a contaminated machine and a disrupted treatment. The schedule exists rather than the repair because prevention is cheaper, safer, and more controllable, and every interval and replacement life named here must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A preventive maintenance visit is a defined set of actions rather than a vague inspection. It begins with the history, because the technician reads the log before touching the machine, and then inspects housing, cables, connectors, seals, filters, and labels. Moving parts are cleaned and lubricated, fasteners are checked for torque, worn items with a known life are replaced, and the interior is cleared of dust that traps heat. The device is then powered and tested for its measurement, its output, and every alarm and safety interlock. The work is recorded with the parts used, the measurements taken, and the next service date, because the characteristic failure of a program is not the missed repair but the maintenance performed without testing the alarms.

The schedule is generated from the recommendations of the manufacturer, the intensity of use, the environment, and the clinical criticality of the device. A ventilator running continuously in a humid intensive care unit ages far faster than the same model used intermittently in a recovery room, and a device in a dusty or coastal clinic ages faster than one in a climate-controlled store. Criticality is the other axis, because a device whose failure would immediately endanger a patient is served on the shortest interval the maker allows, and the schedule is written into the equipment register so that the next service date travels with the machine. A schedule that is never reviewed becomes fiction, so it should be audited against real failure data at least once a year, a period that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The work order is the unit of maintenance work and the record that it happened. It identifies the device, the reporter, the symptom in the reporter's own words, the priority, the technician, the parts used, and the measurements obtained, and priority is assigned by clinical risk. The log becomes valuable only when it is read as a series of events, because a device repaired repeatedly for the same complaint is not unlucky but failing, and the pattern itself is the diagnosis. The history reveals causes a single visit cannot see, such as a pump that fails whenever a particular nurse sets it up.

The user-level check is the most repeated maintenance action in the hospital and the one with the largest effect. Before a device touches a patient, the operator confirms that it is the correct device, and that it has not been tagged out of service. The physical state is inspected for cracks, exposed conductors, crushed tubing, moisture, and cleaning residue, and power, battery charge, the self-test result, and the presence, fit, and expiry of consumables are confirmed. Settings are checked against the patient in front of the operator rather than the previous patient, and alarm limits are set deliberately and provoked once so that the operator knows the alarm can sound. Any failure means the device is removed, tagged, and reported, and every check interval, battery threshold, and alarm limit must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

When a device misbehaves, a fixed sequence prevents the random swapping of parts that wastes time and hides the true cause. The sequence is power, connection, consumable, setting, calibration, alarm, and each step is cheaper and more likely than the next. Power comes first, so the operator confirms that the outlet is live, the plug is seated, the cable is undamaged, and the device has charge. In a hospital this includes checking whether the socket is fed from the normal supply or the emergency circuit, and whether an uninterruptible power supply (UPS) is in bypass or has a discharged battery. A blown fuse, a tripped breaker, or a cord strained against a bed frame accounts for many apparent failures, so the tester used for this check must itself be verified.

Connection comes second and means every junction between device and patient or supply. The operator checks that tubing is seated and uncrimped, that connectors are locked, that gas fittings sit on sound washers, and that nothing is compressed by a bed rail or a door. A loose connector can look like an internal failure, because the machine reads an open circuit or a low pressure and reports it as a sensor or supply problem. The consumable comes third and is the most frequent single cause of apparent failure, whether it is an empty reagent pack, an expired test strip, a blocked filter, a worn sensor, or a canister whose float has sealed. The operator checks identity, expiry, fit, and remaining capacity before concluding that the device has failed, and all consumable specifications, expiry dates, and replacement intervals must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Setting comes fourth, and it is where the assumptions of the operator are most often the fault. A healthy device can still produce a wrong result because it is in the wrong mode, set for the wrong patient size, using the wrong units, or carrying limits left from the previous patient. A ventilator in the wrong mode or a monitor using an adult algorithm on a newborn behaves within specification while delivering the wrong therapy. The operator therefore compares every displayed setting against the written order and the patient rather than against memory, and checks the units with particular care. When two people use a device, the setting should be read back aloud, and all modes, limits, units, and patient-size selections must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Calibration and verification are different acts, and confusing them is a serious error. Calibration establishes the relationship between what a device displays and the true value it measures, by comparing it with a traceable reference and recording the difference, after which adjustment changes the device so that the two agree. Verification is the simpler check that the device still reads within its stated tolerance, using a reference whose accuracy is known and adequate, so a device can pass verification and still be uncalibrated in the formal sense. Drift is the slow change in the link between a physical quantity and the electrical signal that represents it, and it is why calibration is repeated, because a strain gauge loses its bond and an optical source dims. The devices that most need calibration are those whose numbers become decisions, such as defibrillators, infusion pumps, ventilators, gas analyzers, and laboratory analyzers, and every tolerance and calibration interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Verification and calibration require instruments that are themselves trustworthy, and this is where a hospital program meets its limits. Test equipment must be calibrated against a reference that is traceable, step by step, to a national or international standard, and each step must be documented with a date and an uncertainty. Where no traceable reference exists, a clinic can cross-check one device against another, keep a dedicated reference instrument, and write the limitation down, because a cross-check between two equally drifted devices proves only that they agree. An uncalibrated device used knowingly is a named risk while the same device used unknowingly is a trap, and all reference accuracies and recalibration intervals must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Spare parts determine whether maintenance is a plan or a hope, and their lead times decide the real downtime of every device. A critical spare is a part whose absence stops the device and for which no substitute exists, such as a proprietary sensor, a circuit board, a door gasket, or a pump head, and a consumable ordered today may arrive in 3 months, after the season in which it was needed has passed. The store therefore holds a minimum stock of critical spares set by lead time and failure rate. Consumables deserve the same discipline because they are the true constraint on care, since a hospital can own 20 ventilators and treat only 6 patients if it holds circuits and filters for 6. The dangerous design is the device that accepts only one proprietary consumable from one supplier, and a cannibalized part must be recorded with its donor and condition, so all stock levels and shelf lives must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Repair against replacement is an economic and clinical decision. The questions are whether the repair restores the specified performance, whether parts arrive in useful time, whether the cost is a sensible fraction of an equivalent replacement, and whether the maker or the regulator has withdrawn the device for safety. The cost of an old machine includes not only parts but downtime, power, obsolete consumables, and staff time, so a device that is cheap to buy can be expensive to keep. A device becomes unsafe to keep when its failure could harm a patient and cannot be reliably repaired, such as cracked insulation, an alarm that no longer sounds, a radiation output beyond tolerance, or a swelling battery. Such a device must be removed at once and stored where it cannot quietly return to the ward, and every tolerance, energy limit, and test date must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Planned downtime is the arrangement that keeps care running while a device is out of service, and it must be made before the device fails. The tools are redundancy, substitution, scheduling, and a written clinical fallback: a second device of the same type, a manual method the staff have practiced, a loan from another department, or a temporary referral of the patient. The schedule should place maintenance in the quietest hours the ward has, and the clinical team must know in advance what to do when the machine is unavailable, because improvisation during a crisis is not a plan. No maintenance may begin on a device that has no fallback unless a clinician has agreed the risk, and all downtime limits, backup capacities, and substitution rules must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The handover between shifts is where a fault that is not yet a failure is either caught or lost. A device that makes a new noise, runs warmer, takes longer to charge, or reads differently from yesterday is not yet broken, and the person who noticed it holds the most valuable information in the building. The handover must carry device status as deliberately as patient status, naming what is in use, what is out for repair, what is borrowed, and what is merely suspect. The report should name the device, the exact symptom, when it started, and what makes it worse, because a note that the pump is acting strange cannot be acted upon. Reporting early is a clinical skill built on knowing what normal looks like, and the common operator error is to assume that someone else has already reported it, which is how a warning becomes an incident.

The biomedical engineering department is the memory and skill of the institution for maintenance, and it is a partner rather than a repair shop. It receives new equipment, performs acceptance testing, keeps the register, schedules preventive maintenance, calibrates what it can and contracts what it cannot, repairs what is repairable, investigates incidents, trains users, manages spare parts, and recommends decommissioning. It works best when it is included at the moment of purchase and told about near misses. Its most valuable product is the trend, which is the model that fails repeatedly, the cleaning method that damages a surface, and the training gap that keeps producing the same user error. A hospital rarely performs all of its own maintenance, so the boundary between in-house work and vendor work must be drawn deliberately, and every service contract, response time, and warranty condition must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Electrical safety testing is a distinct branch of maintenance because it protects the patient from a hazard with no smell and no sound. The physical principle is leakage current, the small current that escapes from a device through insulation, capacitance, and protective components to earth or to the patient. A device with intact insulation and a sound earth leaks an amount too small to matter, but a cracked housing, a pinched cable, or a broken earth can route current through a patient who is connected to the machine. The test measures the resistance of the protective earth and the leakage current under normal and single-fault conditions and compares them with the limits the standards specify, and the general standard for medical electrical equipment is maintained by the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC). Every limit and test interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and a device that fails is removed from service immediately.

The room is part of the machine, and much of what looks like device failure begins in the environment around it. Heat shortens the life of every electronic component and battery, so cooling fans, filters, and air paths must be kept clear and the room kept within the temperature range the maker specifies. Dust blocks filters, raises the internal temperature, and can conduct electricity across a circuit board, while humidity corrodes contacts and static discharge damages sensitive electronics. Clean water is part of the specification for sterilizers, analyzers, humidifiers, and dialysis machines, because dissolved minerals scale internal surfaces and alter each process. Cleaning is itself maintenance, and its principle is that soil, salt, and protein must be removed before any disinfectant can work, because organic material shields the organisms beneath it and consumes the chemical meant to kill them.

Several hazards cross every device in this chapter and must be named where maintenance meets them. Electrical shock from a faulty lead or a broken earth is the oldest hazard. Fire is the second, and it hides in dust, in blocked cooling, in a lithium battery that has swollen, and in a device left running under a pile of linen. A swelling battery is an imminent fire and must be removed from the building rather than stored on a shelf, and it must never be punctured, crushed, or charged again. Radiation sources, lasers, and high-energy devices bring hazards that no untrained person may approach, and every safety limit and test criterion must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Alarms are the account a device gives of its own condition, and their classification tells the operator how fast to move. A high-priority alarm signals an immediate threat to the patient and demands an immediate response; a medium-priority alarm signals a condition that needs prompt attention, while a low-priority alarm can wait. A technical alarm, sometimes called a device alarm, is the machine reporting its own fault rather than the patient, such as a sensor failure, a calibration due notice, an occlusion, or a low battery. Alarm fatigue is a maintenance problem as much as a human one, because badly set limits produce so many alarms that the staff stop hearing them, so every threshold setting must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The common operator error is silencing an alarm and continuing, which converts a warning into an unnoticed event, and a device whose alarm cannot be provoked must be removed from service, because an untested alarm is a safety system of unknown state.

In a mobile or frontier clinic the entire hospital maintenance structure disappears, and the same clinical needs must be met from a toolkit, a manual, and a telephone. The field variant gives up the biomedical engineering department, the maintenance management system, the calibration laboratory, and the spare parts store, and in exchange it demands that the clinician become the technician, the record keeper, and the supply chain. The physical principles do not change, because wear, drift, corrosion, and battery aging happen just as fast in a hot, dusty room and often faster. What changes is who notices and who acts, which is why the field program is built from simple tools, short written routines, and a strict distinction between what may be repaired locally and what must be sent away.

The field toolkit is deliberately small, and its contents are chosen by the faults that actually occur. A typical kit holds screwdrivers, hex keys, pliers, a small wrench set, a multimeter, a soldering iron, spare fuses, cable ties, a knife, a headlamp, and self-amalgamating tape, in a case that can be carried on a motorcycle. It also holds the small consumables that decide whether a device runs, including fuses of the correct rating, O-rings and washers, tubing, batteries, and the filters the manual names. The multimeter is the most important instrument in it, because it turns a guess about power into a measurement, and the person who uses it must know how to check a supply, a fuse, and a cable. The kit must be inspected after every use and restocked before the next trip, and fuse ratings, battery types, and part numbers must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The service manual is the second half of the field toolkit, and it must be obtained at the time of purchase rather than sought after the first failure. The operator manual explains the daily checks, the alarms, and the cleaning that is allowed, while the service manual gives the fault codes, test points, adjustment procedures, and parts lists that turn troubleshooting into a method. Where the manual is missing, the fault codes and the wiring diagram should be copied, photographed, and kept with the device, because the original will not survive a clinic without electricity or files. The clinic should also keep a bound logbook that records every check, fault, repair, and part replaced, and any specification or fault code must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before it is applied to a patient.

The technician reached by telephone is the substitute of the field clinic for the engineering department, and the relationship must be built before it is needed. A named regional technician, a service line of the maker, or an experienced colleague at a district hospital can often diagnose a fault from a description and a photograph, and the value of that call depends on the quality of the report. The caller should have the device name and number, the fault code, the exact symptom, what was checked, and what the display shows, and should be standing at the machine with the manual open. The clinic should also agree in advance who may authorize a repair and who pays for a part, and every adjustment advised by telephone must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The field preventive maintenance routine is performed by the clinical team on a fixed cycle, built from the tasks the manual allows. Each day the operator checks power and battery, cleans what may be cleaned, inspects cables, tubing, and connectors, and confirms that the alarms can sound. Each week the filters are cleaned, the moving parts inspected, and the battery cycled and charged. Each month the device is opened only as far as the manual permits, dust is cleared from vents and boards, fasteners are tightened, and battery capacity is estimated from the run time it actually delivers. The routine demands discipline because no engineer will arrive to enforce it, and honesty because a skipped filter is invisible until a device overheats or a patient is harmed, and every cleaning method, filter interval, and battery cycle must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Improvisation is sometimes the only repair available, and it is legitimate when it stays inside the manual and restores a specified function. It becomes a hazard when a fuse is replaced with wire, an alarm is disconnected, or a part is substituted without knowing its rating, because a device that appears to work is not the same as a device that works. What the field variant demands instead of infrastructure is training, judgment, and a tiered repair system that keeps decisions at the lowest competent level. The first tier is the user, who cleans, checks, changes consumables, and reports; the second is a trained clinic technician, who replaces modular parts and performs the tests in the manual; the third is the district workshop, which handles board-level repair and calibration; and the fourth is the maker or its agent, which handles warranty and sealed systems. Each tier needs a written description of what it may do, because a clinician who opens a sealed radiation device out of necessity can create a far worse hazard than the original fault, and the clinic needs a stock of the modular parts that the first two tiers consume.

Training is a component of the device, and maintenance training is what makes the rest of the program possible. Its purpose is to build a working mental model in the operator, so that the person understands what the device is doing, what normal looks and sounds like, and what early failure looks like. A good handover covers the start-up sequence, the daily and weekly checks, the meaning of the settings, the alarms and their correct responses, the cleaning that is allowed, and the faults that require a technician rather than a nurse. It should be practical and repeated, with a competency check rather than a signature on an attendance sheet. At least one person on every shift should be a designated superuser who can teach others and who knows whom to call, and any device whose training needs are unclear must be clarified against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.

Documentation is what turns a collection of machines into a managed system, and it is also what protects patients when something goes wrong. The equipment register, maintenance log, calibration certificate, and training record form the legal and clinical history of the device, and they are the first things requested after an incident. That history also makes a recall possible, because a safety notice from a manufacturer can be acted upon only if the affected serial numbers and their locations are known. Underneath every schedule and every log is a human arrangement, and the failures that repeat are usually failures of ownership, because the machine that drifts between rooms, loses its accessories, and is discovered with a fault nobody reported is not badly designed but unowned. A culture of maintenance is built by making the report easy and never punishing the person who names a fault that turns out to be minor, and every record retention period and recall procedure must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

To summarize, maintenance is the management of wear and troubleshooting is the disciplined reading of its symptoms, and together they keep a device trustworthy when a patient needs it. Wear arrives through abrasion, fatigue, corrosion, thermal cycling, and battery aging, and it follows a curve whose far end is reached by every machine used without attention. The hospital-grade program answers with a schedule built from risk, a written work order, a log read as history, calibration against traceable references, spare parts held against lead time, planned downtime, and a biomedical engineering department included early and told about near misses. The field variant gives up that structure and demands a toolkit, a service manual, a telephone relationship with a technician, a short written routine, and the judgment to know which repairs restore a device and which only hide its fault. In both settings the same sequence governs the first response to a fault, which is power, connection, consumable, setting, calibration, alarm, and the most valuable instrument is the operator who knows what normal looks like. Every setting, interval, pressure, temperature, energy level, and alarm threshold mentioned in these pages must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.
