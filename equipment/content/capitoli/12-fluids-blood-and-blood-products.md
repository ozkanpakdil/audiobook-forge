# Chapter 12 — Fluids, Blood, and Blood Products

Every fluid that reaches a patient arrives inside something, and here the container matters as much as its contents. A bag, a bottle, or a blood collection bag holds a column of liquid whose weight creates pressure at its outlet, and that pressure drives the fluid through tubing, a filter, and into a vein. Raise the container and the pressure rises, lower it and the pressure falls, and squeeze it and the pressure rises without any change in height. The solutions divide into crystalloids, water with small dissolved particles that cross capillary membranes, and colloids, which carry large molecules that stay in the circulation and hold water by osmotic attraction. Blood is a third case, a living tissue that must be collected, tested, cooled, warmed, and given within strict limits, and every storage temperature, flow rate, pressure, and alarm threshold named in this chapter must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The flexible crystalloid bag is the most familiar container in the hospital. It is usually made of polyvinyl chloride, a soft plastic that can be heat sealed and squeezed without cracking, with a large spike port for the giving set and a smaller medication port with a rubber septum under a clear plastic overwrap. The bag carries graduations and a label with the solution name, volume, concentration, additive, lot number, and expiry, and before use the operator confirms that the fluid is clear, the overwrap is intact, the ports are capped, and the expiry is in the future, all of which must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The rigid bottle behaves differently because it does not collapse, so a vacuum forms above the falling fluid and stops the flow unless air enters, which is why a bottle is hung with a vented set whose air inlet carries a filter. The common errors are to use a bag that has been frozen or dropped, whose hairline port crack leaks only later, to choose a fluid by its shelf position rather than by reading the label, and to hang an unvented bottle and then wonder why the drip chamber has stopped.

The solutions themselves are devices with labels, and the labels are not interchangeable. Sodium chloride at 0.9 per cent is the reference isotonic fluid, balanced electrolyte solutions disturb the acid and base balance less when large volumes are given, and dextrose solutions expand the circulation poorly because the sugar is metabolized and the water spreads through the body. Bags compounded by a pharmacy carry a second label that can fall off, and potassium-containing fluids deserve particular care, because a rapid infusion can stop the heart. Every concentration and rate limit for these solutions must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The common error is to choose a bag by its shelf position or packaging color rather than by reading it.

Colloid solutions work by oncotic pressure rather than by volume alone. The capillary wall lets water and small ions pass but holds back large molecules, so albumin, gelatin, dextran, or a synthetic starch stays in the vessel and draws water toward itself, and a smaller volume expands the circulation more than the same volume of crystalloid. Each colloid carries its own dangers, since gelatin and dextran can cause anaphylaxis, dextran can interfere with crossmatching, and starches have been associated with kidney injury and impaired clotting. The operator checks the label, concentration, expiry, and clarity, and every volume limit and infusion rate must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The common error is to treat a colloid as a stronger crystalloid and give it freely, when its place is narrow and its cost is high.

Blood is not a solution but a living tissue, and the bag that collects it is a small system rather than a container. A collection bag is a set of two to four plastic bags joined by integral tubing, with the primary bag holding a citrate anticoagulant before the donation begins. The citrate binds calcium and stops clotting, and its volume is matched to the volume of blood to be collected, while the bag is rocked during collection so that every drop meets the anticoagulant and no clot forms. Short lengths of tubing, called segments, are sealed off from the main bag and remain attached, and each segment holds a representative sample so the blood bank can test the unit without opening it, with every collection volume and anticoagulant ratio confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The common operator error at the donor's arm is to let the tubing kink or the bag lie still, which wastes a donation.

Everything that happens to a unit afterward depends on a number. At collection the bag receives a unique donation number, printed on the bag, repeated on every segment, on the paperwork, and on the crossmatch sample tubes, and the label records the blood group, the Rhesus factor written Rh, the component, the volume, the anticoagulant, the collection and expiry dates, and the storage temperature range. The number is the thread that ties the unit to the patient, and the most serious error in transfusion medicine is not a laboratory mistake but an identity mistake, in which a correct unit reaches the wrong patient because a number was copied by hand without a second check. The characteristic alarm of this system is a mismatch at the bedside, a bag whose number, group, or expiry does not agree with the paperwork or the wristband. That mismatch must stop the transfusion before the line is connected, and the identity check itself, along with every date and storage temperature printed on the label, must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

One donation becomes several components because blood separates by weight. Spun in a centrifuge, the dense red cells move to the bottom, a thin layer of white cells and platelets gathers above, and the lighter plasma stays at the top, and the blood bank draws these layers off in sequence to produce packed red cells, platelet concentrate, fresh frozen plasma, and cryoprecipitate. Red cells are stored between 2 and 6 degrees Celsius, platelets between 20 and 24 degrees Celsius with gentle agitation for a shelf life of only several days, and plasma frozen at minus 18 degrees Celsius or colder, and every one of those ranges and times must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. This is why a power failure threatens the inventory before it threatens the patient.

The blood bank refrigerator is a precision instrument that looks like a domestic appliance. It holds red cells near 4 degrees Celsius by circulating cold air through the cabinet, and the circulation matters as much as the cooling, because a still refrigerator develops warm and cold pockets and cells in the warm corner age faster. Its safety features are what make it a medical device: an independent thermometer whose probe sits among the bags, a continuous chart recorder or an electronic data logger, and an alarm that sounds on high temperature, low temperature, and power failure, with a battery that keeps it alive when the mains is gone. Every alarm threshold, recording interval, and battery test schedule must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The operator records the temperature at fixed intervals, confirms that the independent thermometer agrees with the display, and the common error is to trust the display alone and silence a sounding alarm.

Two more machines complete the storage chain. The plasma freezer pulls its contents down quickly and holds them far below freezing, because rapid freezing protects the clotting factors that plasma is given to replace, and it carries the same independent thermometer, recorder, and battery-backed alarm as the refrigerator. The platelet agitator rocks a flat tray gently back and forth, usually a few cycles per minute, to keep platelets suspended and let them exchange gas through the permeable bag, and a stalled agitator is an alarm condition rather than a convenience. Because platelets are stored warm and their permitted storage time is short, they are the component most vulnerable to bacterial growth. Every freezer temperature and agitator setting must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the common errors are a stalled agitator nobody notices and a freezer inventory that no one has checked.

Between the blood bank and the ward there is a journey, and the journey is part of the device. A unit leaves the refrigerator in an insulated box with a sealed lid, cooled by ice packs wrapped so that they do not touch the bag directly, because frozen red cells hemolyze and a bag pressed against bare ice can freeze at the point of contact. At the receiving end the nurse reads the temperature, inspects the bag for leaks, clots, discoloration, and a broken seal, confirms the number and the expiry, and starts the transfusion promptly. The usual rule is that a unit out of controlled storage for more than 30 minutes may not be returned, a limit that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The common operator error is to carry a unit in a pocket or on a car seat while finishing another task, and the result is a unit that is either discarded or, worse, given.

The transfusion set is the consumable that turns a bag into an infusion, and its central component is a filter. A filter is a screen with holes of a chosen size, and a standard blood administration set carries a screen with pores between 170 and 260 micrometers, large enough to pass red cells and small enough to catch clots and debris, while a finer screen of about 20 to 40 micrometers is used where microaggregates are a concern and a leukoreduction filter removes white cells by adhesion. Both of those pore sizes must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Pediatric sets have a smaller priming volume and often a graduated burette so that a small volume can be measured without wasting a whole unit, and pump sets for blood use tubing whose diameter and elasticity match the pump that squeezes it. The common operator error is to use an ordinary crystalloid set for blood, where the filter is too coarse to protect the patient.

Flow through a transfusion line obeys the same physics as any other fluid, and the numbers surprise people. Resistance rises steeply as a tube narrows, so a cannula of 14 gauge carries far more flow than one of 22 gauge, and the difference grows with the length of tubing, which is why a long extension set can halve the rate of a gravity transfusion. That is the purpose of the pressure infuser, a cuff that wraps around the bag and inflates to a chosen pressure, commonly around 300 millimeters of mercury, a target that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The device causes two classic accidents, air embolism when the bag empties and the pressure pushes air down the line, and over-infusion when the cuff stays inflated. The operator must watch the gauge, never let the bag empty completely, and never pressurize without a gauge, because a hand can generate pressures far above any safe target.

The rapid infuser is the hospital's answer to hemorrhage, and it is a system rather than a single device. It draws fluid or blood from bags and from a reservoir through wide tubing, passes it over a high-capacity heater, removes air in a chamber with a level sensor, and pumps it into the patient through a large-bore catheter at several hundred milliliters per minute, far beyond any gravity set. The characteristic alarms are air in line, over-temperature, occlusion, and reservoir empty, and an air detector, a temperature control, and an occlusion detector stand behind them, with every threshold confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The common operator error is to connect a rapid infuser to a small peripheral cannula, which defeats the flow and can rupture the vein.

A blood warmer is a heat exchanger whose only job is to stop a cold fluid from cooling the patient. Two fluids at different temperatures, separated by a conducting wall, move toward a common temperature, and the warmer arranges that contact in one of three ways: a water bath that pumps warmed water through a jacket, a dry heat plate that presses against the bag or tubing, or an in-line cassette inside a warmed block immediately before the fluid enters the patient. The target is body temperature, and the danger is the upper limit, because red cells are damaged by heat and warming blood above roughly 41 degrees Celsius causes hemolysis, a ceiling that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The characteristic alarm of a powered warmer is an over-temperature alarm, sometimes with an independent cut-out whose setting must also be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The common operator error is to warm a bag on a radiator, in a sink, or in a microwave, where the temperature is unknown and uneven.

Cell salvage recovers the patient's own blood and returns it, which removes the compatibility problem and reduces the demand on the blood bank. Its physical principle is density separation in a spinning bowl, where shed blood is sucked from the surgical field with a drip of anticoagulant and collects in a reservoir with a filter that holds back clots and tissue. From the reservoir the fluid is pumped into a centrifuge bowl, where the dense red cells pack against the wall while the lighter plasma, wash solution, and debris are displaced to a waste bag, and the concentrated cells are reinfused through a filter. Every pressure, speed, and anticoagulant ratio must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the alarms include air in the line and a full waste bag. The common operator error is to salvage from a field contaminated with bowel contents or from a patient with a malignancy or an active infection, where the recovered blood should not return.

The blood bank laboratory is a room full of devices that make antibodies visible. When an antibody meets its matching antigen on a red cell surface, it links several cells into a lattice, and that lattice is large enough to be seen as clumping, or agglutination, against a light or under a microscope. The simplest method reads that clumping by eye on a white tile, and modern laboratories use a column of gel or glass beads where agglutinated cells are trapped at the top and free cells travel to the bottom. The equipment behind these methods includes a refrigerated centrifuge, a water bath or incubator held at 37 degrees Celsius, a cell washer, calibrated pipettes, a reagent refrigerator, and a well-lit reading station. Grouping uses anti-A, anti-B, and anti-D reagents with the patient's own cells, a reverse grouping with known cells, an antibody screen, and finally the crossmatch, and every reagent's storage temperature, the 37 degrees Celsius incubation, and every expiry must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

All of this equipment is worthless if the sample in the tube is not the patient's. A blood sample for grouping and crossmatching must be labeled at the bedside, at the moment it is drawn, with the patient's full name, a unique identifier, the date and time, and the name of the person who drew it, and a tube without that label must be rejected rather than used. Many services require two separate samples from two separate venipunctures for a first crossmatch, precisely because a single mislabeled tube is the classic route to a fatal reaction. The common operator error is to prepare labels in advance or write them from memory in a busy room, and every sample acceptance rule and repeat-testing interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The last step in the chain is the bedside check, and it is performed by people rather than by a machine. Two trained people confirm the patient's identity from the wristband and by asking the patient, compare it with the compatibility label and the unit number, check the blood group and the expiry, inspect the bag for leaks, clots, or discoloration, and record baseline vital signs before the line is opened. The transfusion then begins slowly, at a rate that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the first 15 minutes are the most dangerous because a severe hemolytic reaction usually declares itself early. The nurse stays with the patient through that period, watching for fever, chills, rash, back pain, breathlessness, falling blood pressure, and dark urine. Any of those signs stops the transfusion at once, keeps the vein open, and sends the unit, the set, and the paperwork back to the blood bank with a reaction report, because the characteristic alarm of transfusion is the patient and the common operator error is to leave the bedside during those first minutes.

When a reaction is reported, the blood bank investigates with its own devices. The clerical check comes first, because a surprising number of apparent reactions are identity errors, and the laboratory then repeats the patient's group and the unit's group, performs a direct antiglobulin test to look for antibody coating the red cells, inspects the plasma for the pink discoloration that indicates hemolysis, and cultures the unit if bacterial contamination is suspected. The results decide the diagnosis and the patient's future treatment, which is why the bag is never discarded and every reaction is entered in a register. The World Health Organization recommends that every country operate a national blood system with a reporting scheme for adverse events, so that the same error is not repeated in the next hospital, and local reporting duties must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A transfusion service rests on infrastructure that the ward never sees, and its reliability is built from ordinary maintenance and an inventory of consumables. The blood bank needs power that survives an outage, usually a circuit supported by an uninterruptible power supply and a generator, because a freezer that warms over a weekend destroys an inventory that took weeks to collect. Refrigerators and freezers are temperature mapped so that every shelf position is known, alarm systems are tested at intervals that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and data loggers are checked against a reference thermometer. Cleaning is part of maintenance, since spilled blood is removed with the approved disinfectant, door gaskets are wiped so that they seal, condenser coils and air filters are cleaned so that the compressor can reject heat, and the inside of a refrigerator is never scrubbed with a harsh agent. The consumables run from blood administration sets and filter sets to collection bags with anticoagulant, crossmatch tubes, antisera that must stay cold, insulated boxes with ice packs, and the plastic overwrap that keeps plasma ports out of a thawing bath. The centrifuge needs special care, because an unbalanced rotor damages its bearings, and the common errors include recording a temperature from memory, silencing an alarm, and defrosting a freezer without moving the inventory to a monitored alternative.

The failure modes of this system can be sorted by which link has broken. The cold chain fails when power is lost, a door is left open, a freezer is overfilled, or a box sits in the sun, and the result is blood that is warm, blood that is frozen, or blood that has grown bacteria. The machine fails when a compressor, a thermostat, an alarm battery, or an agitator motor gives way, often without warning, which is why the independent thermometer and the written record are the real safety devices. The laboratory fails when a reagent is expired, a centrifuge is unbalanced, or a result is misread, and the people fail when the wrong sample is drawn, the wrong unit is issued, the first minutes of a transfusion are unobserved, or a reaction is dismissed as a fever from something else. The clinical outcomes have physical names: hemolysis with free hemoglobin and potassium, a hemolytic or febrile reaction, bacterial sepsis, circulatory overload, citrate toxicity, and air embolism.

The field clinic usually has no blood bank, and that single absence reorganizes everything. There is no inventory, no crossmatched unit waiting in a refrigerator, no component therapy, and often no laboratory that can type blood and screen for antibodies on demand. What the field has instead is a donor, often a relative of the patient, a cool box or a small refrigerator, a rapid test or a set of grouping reagents, and a clinician who must decide whether the risk of transfusing is greater or smaller than the risk of waiting. The field gives up the inventory, the screening, and the second pair of experienced eyes, and it demands donor selection, testing, labeling, and close observation in their place. Even the simplest field transfusion involves numbers, and every hemoglobin threshold, volume, and storage temperature in this half of the chapter must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The walking donor is a person well enough to give blood and close enough to give it now, and selecting that person is the field substitute for a blood bank's testing. The donor is questioned and examined for age within an accepted range, a weight above a stated minimum, a hemoglobin measured from a finger-prick sample against a threshold the facility has set, a normal pulse and blood pressure, no fever, no recent illness, no recent donation, and no medication that would harm a recipient. Every one of those thresholds must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, because a donor who is anemic, infected, or too small is a danger to the recipient and to themselves. The donation uses a sterile collection bag with anticoagulant that is kept moving so that blood and citrate mix, and the bag is labeled at the bedside with the donor's identity, the time, the volume, and a unique number that is also written in the register beside the patient's name. The common operator error is to accept a donor on the strength of appearance alone, and the second error is to fail to record who gave the blood and when.

Some testing must precede any field transfusion, and its purpose is to avoid giving a unit that will destroy the patient's red cells. The patient and the donor are each grouped for the A, B, and O antigens and for the Rhesus factor using antisera on a tile or a card, and the patient's plasma is tested against the donor's cells in a crossmatch, on a tile, in a tube, or on a rapid test strip, to look for the clumping that predicts a reaction. Reading agglutination requires good light and a magnifying lens or a microscope, and the result is recorded before the unit leaves the donor's side, because minutes can matter in a hemorrhage. Expired, warm, or contaminated reagents give misleading answers, and every reagent's storage condition, reading time, and interpretation must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Where no grouping or crossmatch is possible at all, a group O Rh negative unit is the emergency choice for the widest range of recipients, but this is a calculated risk, and the common operator error is to accept a single weak or ambiguous reading under pressure rather than repeating the test.

When the field has a tested donor and a patient who needs blood, the administration must still obey the physics of the hospital. Ideally the blood is collected into a bag with anticoagulant and given through a blood administration set with a filter, because a filter protects the patient from clots and a bag lets the volume and the rate be seen and controlled. Passing blood directly from donor to recipient through a short set without a collecting bag is the oldest method and the most dangerous, because the volume is unmeasured, the anticoagulant is absent or improvised, air can enter, and a crystalloid set has no blood filter. Storage is the field's hardest constraint, so a cool box with wrapped ice packs and a thermometer, or a solar refrigerator designed for vaccines, must be monitored with an independent thermometer and the reading written down at intervals that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Whole blood in a citrate solution has a shelf life measured in weeks, a limit that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, while warming is done in a basin of warm water or against a warm pack with a thermometer to keep the fluid below its safe limit, a limit that must also be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A pressure infuser is improvised from a blood pressure cuff with its own gauge rather than a hand, and the field's final safeguard is the record, because the donor register and the patient's chart are the only crossmatch that survives the night.

To summarize, this chapter has been about containers, tubing, filters, refrigerators, and freezers rather than about the drugs inside them, because those objects determine what the patient actually receives. A crystalloid bag or bottle delivers fluid by height and pressure, a colloid adds the physics of oncotic attraction, and a blood collection bag adds anticoagulant, integral segments, and a number that must follow the unit to the bedside. The transfusion set's filter, the pressure infuser's gauge, the rapid infuser's air detector, the blood warmer's temperature limit, and the cell salvage circuit's centrifuge each exist to make an old physical process safer, while the blood bank's refrigerators, freezers, and agitators are medical devices whose alarms, records, and independent thermometers are the real inventory. The safety hazard is the wrong unit or the wrong temperature, the common operator error is an unchecked identity or an unobserved first 15 minutes, and the characteristic alarm of a transfusion is the patient rather than a machine. The field variant gives up the inventory, the component therapy, and the laboratory, and demands donor selection, testing before transfusion, and a written record instead, with every threshold there confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.
