# Appendix A — Visual Atlas of Equipment by Department

This appendix exists because a photograph cannot be heard, and a spoken atlas must do what a picture does. It fixes in the listener's mind where each machine stands, what it looks like from the doorway, and which sound identifies the room from the corridor. The walk follows a patient's route through the emergency department and its trauma bay, the ward, the operating theatre, the intensive care unit, the delivery room, the laboratory, the imaging suite, the central sterile services department, the pharmacy, the laundry, and the morgue. In every room the fixed equipment comes first, because it belongs to the building, and the movable equipment comes second, because it can be borrowed or lost. Every setting, pressure, temperature, and alarm threshold named here must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.

The emergency department announces itself from the corridor by layered sounds, the beep of a monitor, the chime of a call bell, the hiss of oxygen, and the gurgle of a vacuum canister. Its fixed equipment sits within reach of a standing team, so the resuscitation bay carries oxygen and vacuum at the head of the trolley, a ceiling light, a socket strip, and a leaded screen. On wheels it holds the crash cart with sealed drawers, the monitor, the defibrillator, the airway trolley, the suction unit, and the trauma bay's collars, boards, rapid infusion device, and chest drain set. A serious hazard is the unsecured oxygen cylinder, which becomes a missile if it falls and shears its valve, and the common error is silencing an alarm without looking at the patient first. The characteristic alarms are the defibrillator self-test beep and the monitor low oxygen saturation tone, and every defibrillator energy setting must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before a shock is delivered.

The general ward is the quietest department that still holds a machine at every bed, identified by call bells, chattering infusion pumps, and a monitor alarm whose lead has loosened. The fixed equipment of a bed space is the bed frame with its motor and brake, the mattress, the overhead light, the oxygen, air, and vacuum outlets, and the call bell on its coiled cord. The movable equipment is the monitor, the infusion pumps on their pole, the suction canister, the walking frame, and the portable oxygen cylinder on its stand. The principle of the bed is mechanical advantage, because a small motor turns a screw that lifts a heavy patient with a force no nurse should provide by hand. A serious hazard is the gap between the side rail and the mattress, where a frail patient can be trapped by the neck, and the common error is hanging a pump on the side rail; every pump rate and alarm limit must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the infusion begins.

The atlas of a bed space is incomplete without the drawer beside it, because consumables are what the ward consumes fastest. That drawer holds gloves, alcohol swabs, dressings, tape, a tourniquet, specimen tubes, a glucose meter with its strips, and the paper for the observation chart. The clean utility room stores sterile packs and intravenous fluids, while the dirty utility room holds the sluice and the bin for contaminated waste, and the two must never be treated as one. Cleaning follows a rule of direction, because the cloth moves from the cleanest surface to the dirtiest and never back, and the nurse's start-of-shift check runs the bed through its range, tests the brake, checks the outlets for leaks, and draws the suction against a thumb. A serious hazard is the reusable device that is wiped but not disinfected, and the common error is trusting an outlet nobody has tested; every vacuum setting must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before it is applied.

The operating theatre lies behind a changing corridor, and its approach is a sequence of rooms rather than one door, with street clothes outside, scrub suits in the middle zone, and the sterile inner zone where the masked team stands. The room announces itself by the hum of ventilation, which is reassuring because filtered air is pushed down from the ceiling and swept out near the floor. Its fixed equipment is the table that tilts and breaks under power, the large lights on articulated arms that fill the shadow of a hand, and the pendant carrying oxygen, air, vacuum, and anesthetic gas scavenging. Its movable equipment is the anesthesia machine, the monitor, the electrosurgery generator with its return electrode, the instrument tables, and the counts board. A serious hazard is fire in an oxygen-enriched field when electrosurgery is used near the face, and the common error is beginning the case before the counts are recorded; every electrosurgery power setting must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the first cut.

The intensive care unit is a ward in which every bed is a life-support station, recognized by a chorus of alarms punctuated by the rush of a ventilator and the click of an infusion pump. Its fixed equipment is the bed space itself, with oxygen, air, vacuum, and sockets, some on an uninterruptible power supply, a monitor arm, a ventilator bracket, and lighting that can be dimmed. Its movable equipment is the mechanical ventilator, the monitor, the stack of infusion pumps, the continuous renal replacement machine, and the resuscitation trolley. The principle of the unit is distribution, because every machine at the bedside is fed by a pipe, a wire, or a battery that begins far away in a plant room, and consumables are used once and discarded. A serious hazard is alarm fatigue, the gradual deafening that leads a team to silence the single alarm that matters, and the common error is changing a ventilator setting without a second reader; every ventilator setting and alarm limit must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before it is applied and again before transport.

The delivery room sits close to the operating theatre, because a normal birth can become a surgical one in minutes, and it is identified by the beat of a fetal heart and the thin cry of a newborn. Its fixed equipment is the delivery bed with its leg supports, the procedure light on an arm, the radiant warmer with its heater and skin probe, the oxygen, air, and vacuum outlets, and a resuscitation table with a firm surface. Its movable equipment is the fetal monitor, the portable ultrasound, the vacuum extractor, the forceps, the cord clamp, the sterile pack, and the neonatal transport incubator. The principle of the radiant warmer is that heat reaches the baby by radiation without touching it, and the skin probe closes a control loop so the heater responds to the baby rather than to the room. A serious hazard is the newborn left uncovered under a cooler that has not been switched on, and the common error is failing to dry and stimulate the baby; every warmer temperature and heater output must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the first delivery of the shift.

The laboratory is a room of benches, announcing itself by the whine of centrifuges, the clatter of an analyzer, and the alarm of a refrigerator that has drifted warm. Its fixed equipment is the bench with its sink, the fume hood, the biological safety cabinet that draws air away from the worker, the gas and vacuum taps, the analyzer, the refrigerator and freezer, and the eyewash. Its movable equipment is the centrifuge, the microscope, the pipettes, the tube racks, and the specimen bags. The principle of the centrifuge is that spinning a tube makes the dense cells settle outward while the lighter plasma stays behind, and the safety cabinet keeps the worker out of the aerosol a tube produces when it is opened. A serious hazard is blood splashed onto a bench or into the eye, and another is the needle recapped by hand, while the common error is accepting an unlabeled specimen, which must be discarded; every temperature limit must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before a specimen is stored. Behind its own locked door, the blood bank is built around cold and identity, with refrigerators for red cells, a freezer for plasma, a platelet agitator, and a tag that ties every unit to one patient.

The imaging suite is a corridor of similar rooms that behave very differently, so each is identified by its fixed hardware rather than by a label. The radiography room holds a floating table, a tube on a ceiling or floor mount, a collimator that shapes the beam, and a console behind a leaded screen. The computed tomography room, called a CT room, holds a large ring-shaped gantry with its rotating tube and detector array and a sliding table, while the magnetic resonance imaging room, called an MRI room, holds a magnet that is always on and a control room behind a shielded door. The ultrasound room holds a console, a display, and a rack of probes, and the principles differ, because radiography depends on differential absorption while computed tomography reconstructs slices and ultrasound times returning echoes. A serious hazard in the magnetic room is the field itself, because a forgotten cylinder can be pulled across the room with lethal force, and the common error is skipping the implant questionnaire; every field strength, exposure setting, and probe output must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before a patient is examined.

The central sterile services department, called CSSD, is the one place where the flow runs in a single direction, and its atlas is a corridor rather than a room. Soiled instruments arrive at the dirty end and move through a receiving bench, a washer-disinfector or ultrasonic bath, an inspection bench, a packaging table, a sterilizer, and finally the clean store. Its fixed equipment is the sink with its long handle and air break, the washer-disinfector, the ultrasonic bath, the drying cabinet, the autoclave with its chamber and door, and the hand wash basin at the boundary. Its movable equipment is the basket, the tray, the perforated container, the instrument rack, the transport trolley, and the biological indicator that proves a cycle worked. The principle of the autoclave is that saturated steam under pressure gives up its latent heat as it condenses on a cooler instrument, and that heat coagulates the proteins of every microorganism, but the principle fails when air remains trapped and insulates the load. A serious hazard is the wet pack, whose wrapper can carry organisms through itself, and the common error is overloading the chamber; a common cycle is 134 degrees Celsius for 3 to 4 minutes, and nothing leaves the clean end without a lot number, an expiry, a chemical indicator, and a signature, so that temperature and time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before any load is released.

The pharmacy and the general store are rooms about preservation and control, recognized by the hum of a refrigerator and the beep of an automated dispensing cabinet. Their fixed equipment is the shelving, the refrigerator that holds drugs between 2 and 8 degrees Celsius, a range to be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before anything is stored, the freezer, the safe for controlled substances, and the dispensing cabinet whose drawers open only for an authorized user. Their movable equipment is the medication trolley, the delivery box, the cold box with its ice packs, the thermometer, and the logbook that records every temperature. A serious hazard is the look-alike label, where 2 ampoules of nearly identical appearance hold very different drugs, and the common error is placing a vaccine against the freezer compartment, where it freezes and is silently destroyed. The characteristic alarm is the refrigerator temperature alarm, commonly set to sound near 8 degrees Celsius, and that threshold must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the unit is trusted.

The laundry is entered at the soiled end and left at the clean end, and it is identified from the corridor by the thud of a washer, the hiss of steam, and the smell of hot linen. Its fixed equipment is the soiled chute, the industrial washer loaded at one end and unloaded at the other, the dryer, the ironer, the boiler that supplies steam, the ventilation that carries moisture away, and the hand wash basin at the barrier. Its movable equipment is the bag, the trolley, and the cart that carries clean linen to the ward. The principle of thermal disinfection is that heat kills microorganisms when the temperature is high enough and held long enough, so a common wash cycle reaches 71 degrees Celsius for at least 3 minutes, a temperature and holding time that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before a load is released. A serious hazard is the needle left in a sheet, which injures the worker who shakes it out, and the common error is mixing soiled and clean linen on one trolley.

The morgue is the last room on the walk and the quietest, recognized by cold, stillness, and the low hum of refrigeration. Its fixed equipment is the bank of refrigerated cabinets with their sliding trays, the ventilation that keeps air moving out, the floor drain, the washable walls, the identification table with its light, and the locked cabinet for the property of the dead. Its movable equipment is the trolley with its cover, the body bag, the identification tag, and the register in which every entry is signed. The principle of the cabinet is the same as any refrigerator, because cold slows decomposition and the body is stored near 4 degrees Celsius, a value that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the cabinet is loaded. A serious hazard is misidentification, which is a legal and emotional catastrophe rather than a clinical one, and the common error is accepting a body without a tag checked twice against the register; the characteristic alarm is the cabinet high temperature tone, and the silence of a door left open matters more, because memory is not a record.

Now the atlas must be compressed, because the same walk in a mobile or frontier clinic passes through 1 or 2 rooms instead of 12 departments. The clinic announces itself differently, by the click of a solar charge controller, the whir of a generator, the flutter of a tent wall, and the voices of a queue, and its rooms are defined by time rather than by walls. The consultation corner holds a table, 2 chairs, a blood pressure cuff, a thermometer, and a pulse oximeter, while the treatment corner holds the same table in the afternoon with a tray of sterile instruments, a pressure cooker, a sharps box, and a bucket of chlorine solution. The ward corner is a mattress with a mosquito net, a bedpan, a suction foot pump, an oxygen cylinder with its regulator, and a hand-held monitor if the clinic owns one, and the delivery corner adds a sterile pack, a cord clamp, and a radiant lamp. The laboratory corner is a bench with a microscope, a centrifuge, a rapid diagnostic test for malaria, and a cold box, and the pharmacy corner is a locked cabinet with a thermometer and a logbook, so the discipline that replaces the walls is cleaning between patients, a single direction for soiled and clean items, and a written morning check.

The field clinic gives up redundancy first, and what it demands instead is a plan for every single point of failure. It has 1 oxygen source, 1 suction device, 1 sterilizer, 1 light, and 1 vehicle, so a broken part stops a service rather than slowing it. The power system is its own small plant, with a solar panel, a charge controller, a battery, and an inverter, and the battery is the weakest link because it is cycled every day, so its state of charge must be read each morning. A serious hazard is the petrol generator run indoors or beside a window, because its exhaust contains carbon monoxide, a gas that is invisible, odorless, and lethal, and another is the extension lead that feeds a sterilizer and a heater from 1 circuit. The common error is leaving a device switched on overnight and draining the battery the next day depends on, and the characteristic alarms are the inverter low voltage warning and the charge controller fault light; every charging voltage and battery threshold must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the system is trusted with a patient.

On the field the most dangerous substitution is the one that looks like sterilization and is not, so the atlas must name it plainly. A pressure cooker can genuinely sterilize a wrapped load when the air is driven out and the timing is respected, while a pot of boiling water kills most bacteria and leaves the spores that cause tetanus and gangrene alive. A chemical soak in a chlorine solution or a glutaraldehyde substitute is high-level disinfection rather than sterilization, and it fails when the solution is too weak, too old, or in contact for too short a time. A serious hazard is the instrument rinsed in a bucket of water after disinfection and reused at once, and the common error is topping up the disinfectant instead of replacing it, while the morning check covers the power, the oxygen pressure, the suction, the sterilizer, and the cold box temperature. The clinic also carries a second atlas inside its vehicle, where a stretcher locks into a floor track and an oxygen cylinder, a suction unit, a monitor, a defibrillator, and a spine board are each strapped so a sudden stop does not turn them into projectiles; every sterilization temperature, disinfectant concentration, and cold box limit must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the clinic opens.

To summarize, a visual atlas of a hospital is a walk in which each room is recognized by what is bolted to it, what stands on wheels, and what it sounds like from the corridor. The emergency department keeps oxygen, suction, monitor, and defibrillator within a single step, the ward repeats one bed space many times and lives on consumables and cleaning, the theatre arranges lights, table, gas scavenging, and a sterile field around one wound, and the intensive care unit is a distributed life-support system whose machines depend on pipes and wires that begin elsewhere. The delivery room holds a warmer beside the bed, the laboratory and blood bank turn a specimen into a number while guarding identity and temperature, the imaging suite makes radiography, computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging, and ultrasound each demand a different habit, and the sterile services department is a one-way flow in which cleaning decides whether sterilization can work. The pharmacy, the laundry, and the morgue are rooms of preservation, identity, and record, while the field version is not a smaller hospital but a different arrangement in 1 or 2 rooms, where the same need is met by a pressure cooker, a foot pump, a solar battery, and a cold box; in every room the rule is the same, namely understand the principle, name the hazard and the common error, learn the alarm, and confirm every setting against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device touches a patient.
