# Chapter 72 — HVAC and Water Systems

Air and water are the two utilities a hospital consumes without ever counting them as equipment, and yet almost every device described in this book depends on one or both of them. Ventilation carries heat, moisture, odor, anesthetic gas, and infectious particles out of a room, while water carries heat, dissolves chemicals, feeds sterilizers and kitchens, and can also carry organisms into a patient. This chapter treats heating, ventilation, and air conditioning, usually shortened to HVAC, and the water system as two clinical systems with their own principles, their own consumables, and their own ways of failing. The hospital-grade version is a designed and monitored system with sensors, alarms, records, and a maintenance schedule, while the field version is a tank, a filter, a solar heater, and a bottle of chlorine, and the difference between them is not comfort but safety.

Start with the physics, because ventilation is nothing more than moving air on purpose. Air expands when heated, becomes less dense, and therefore rises, while cool air is denser and sinks, and that difference in density is the engine of natural ventilation. Heat always travels from a warmer body to a cooler one by conduction through solids, by convection through fluids, and by radiation across a gap, and a building must manage all three at once. Moisture behaves in a parallel way, because warm air holds more water vapor than cool air, so when air is cooled its excess vapor condenses on the nearest cold surface.

A mechanical HVAC system replaces natural leakage with a controlled path for air. Outdoor air is drawn through a louver and a damper into an air handling unit, where it passes a coarse prefilter and a finer filter and then a heating or cooling coil, and a fan pushes it through ductwork to diffusers in each room. Exhaust air is removed from the rooms that produce contamination, such as toilets, soiled utility rooms, laboratories, and isolation rooms, so that polluted air never travels toward patients. Ventilation is measured in air changes per hour, which counts how many times the entire volume of a room is replaced in 1 hour, so a room of 60 cubic meters receiving 720 cubic meters each hour has 12 air changes per hour. The air change rate, supply and exhaust positions, and measurement method must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the ventilation of a clinical room is accepted.

Pressure relationship is the second measure, and it decides which way air moves through every gap. Air always flows from a region of higher pressure to a region of lower pressure, so a room supplied with more air than it exhausts becomes slightly positive and leaks outward, while a room exhausted faster than it is supplied becomes slightly negative and pulls air inward. Operating theatres, cleanrooms, and protective environments are held positive so that corridor air cannot enter, while isolation rooms, soiled utility rooms, and laboratories handling infectious material are held negative so that their air cannot leave. A pressure difference near 2.5 pascals in the intended direction is a common design target, and that figure, the direction of flow, and the alarm threshold must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the room is used. The common operator error is to prop a door open with a chair, a wedge, or a linen bag, which destroys the differential within seconds and quietly turns a contained room into a source.

Filtration is the third measure, and it is graded by the size of particle it is expected to capture. A coarse prefilter removes lint, dust, and insects and protects the equipment behind it, a medium-grade filter removes most pollen and larger spores, and a high-efficiency particulate air filter removes at least 99.97 per cent of particles near 0.3 micrometers in diameter. A filter is only as good as its frame and gasket, because air takes the easier path around a torn seal rather than through the medium, and a differential pressure gauge across the filter reports how hard the fan must work as the medium loads. The filtration grade, the pressure drop at which the filter must be changed, and the test method must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the filter is accepted.

The operating theatre is the most demanding air space in the hospital, because a surgical wound is open for hours and every particle that lands in it is a potential infection. A modern theatre is held positive to its corridor, usually near 15 to 25 air changes per hour, with supply air delivered through a ceiling array that sweeps downward across the operating table. Temperature is held cool enough for the gowned team, often near 18 to 21 degrees Celsius, while humidity is controlled to reduce both static electricity and the survival of organisms in the air. The air change rate, temperature, humidity, pressure relationship, and filtration grade must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before a case begins.

Isolation rooms, laboratories, and pharmacies each impose a different requirement on the same system. A room for a patient with an airborne infection, such as tuberculosis or measles, is held negative and its exhaust is filtered or discharged high above the roof, so the organism never reaches the corridor. A clinical laboratory uses fume hoods and biological safety cabinets, which are local exhaust devices that pull air away from the worker and, in the case of a cabinet, filter it before returning it to the room, and the sash height and face velocity are their critical settings. A pharmacy that compounds sterile preparations needs a clean area held positive and a separate negative area for hazardous drugs, so that powders are contained rather than spread through the building. The pressure direction, air change rate, face velocity, filtration grade, and alarm threshold for each of these spaces must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before work begins.

Humidity and temperature are not matters of comfort alone, because both change the behavior of materials and organisms. High humidity corrodes metal instruments, swells paper records, encourages mold on walls and inside ducts, and shortens the life of electronic equipment, while low humidity dries mucous membranes, increases static electricity, and makes dust lighter and more easily airborne. The characteristic alarms are a high or low temperature alarm, a humidity alarm, a filter pressure alarm, a fan failure alarm, and a room pressure alarm, and each exists because the condition it reports is invisible to the eye. The common operator error is to silence an alarm that keeps repeating, or to move a set point to stop the noise rather than find the fault, and both habits convert a warning into a hidden hazard. The temperature range, humidity range, alarm limits, calibration interval, and the response expected of staff must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the readings are trusted.

Maintenance of an HVAC system is unglamorous, scheduled, and decisive, because almost every component degrades slowly and silently. Fans are checked for belt tension, bearing noise, vibration, and correct rotation, and a slipping belt reduces airflow long before it fails completely. Coils are cleaned of dust and biological film, condensate drain pans and traps are cleared so that water does not overflow into a duct or a ceiling, and dampers are exercised to confirm that they still move when commanded. The inspection interval, belt tension, filter pressure limit, and coil cleaning method must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the work is signed off.

The consumables of an HVAC system are few but they are the items that stop the system when they run out, and the same short list explains most failures. It includes prefilters and final filters in each required grade, fan belts, gaskets and sealant, damper actuators, bearing lubricant, drain pan treatment, and calibrated replacement sensors for temperature, humidity, and pressure. The recurring failures are a stuck outdoor air damper that makes the building recirculate its own air while everyone feels merely sleepy, a fan that trips on a thermal overload and reverses the pressure relationship without any change in the sound of the room, and a blocked condensate drain that overflows into a ceiling or an electrical panel. The stock level, storage condition, replacement interval, and the inspection that would have caught each fault must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the system is relied upon.

Water is the second utility, and it arrives from a municipal main, a borehole, a river, or a delivered tanker, and it is then stored, treated, and distributed. A hospital usually holds water in a storage tank or cistern so that an interruption does not stop surgery, dialysis, sterilization, or the kitchen. Different departments need different water, because wards need water safe to drink and clear, dialysis needs water stripped of minerals, chlorine, and bacteria so that nothing crosses the dialyzer membrane into the blood, and the central sterile services department, usually shortened to CSSD, needs soft water low in dissolved solids to avoid spots, corrosion, and biofilm on instruments, while the laboratory needs water free of dissolved ions for its reagents and the pharmacy needs purified water for compounding. The tank capacity, pump pressure, pipe material, quality standard, and testing schedule must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the system is commissioned.

Water treatment is a train of processes, each aimed at a different kind of contaminant, and the first of them is filtration. Sediment filters of wound thread or pleated material remove sand, rust, and suspended particles that would clog everything downstream, and a carbon filter removes chlorine, organic compounds, and some tastes and odors by adsorption onto its very large internal surface. Softening removes the calcium and magnesium that make water hard by passing it through a resin that exchanges those ions for sodium, and it also protects boilers, sterilizers, and hot water tanks, because hard water deposits a mineral crust when it is heated. The filter rating, carbon capacity, resin capacity, regeneration schedule, and hardness limit must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the treatment train is relied upon.

Reverse osmosis is the treatment that removes the widest range of dissolved material, and its principle is pressure against a natural tendency. In ordinary osmosis, water crosses a semipermeable membrane from the dilute side toward the concentrated side until the difference in level creates a pressure called osmotic pressure that stops the flow. Reverse osmosis applies a pressure greater than that osmotic pressure to the concentrated side, forcing water molecules through the membrane while rejecting most dissolved salts, bacteria, and larger organic molecules. Membranes are damaged by chlorine and by sediment, so carbon filtration and prefiltration precede them, and the operating pressure, recovery fraction, rejection rate, and membrane replacement interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the water is accepted for clinical use.

Chlorination is the disinfection step, and it works by oxidizing the cell walls and enzymes of microorganisms. The amount that remains after the organisms and other impurities have consumed their share is called the residual, and it is the residual that protects the water as it travels through the pipes, so it is the quantity that a test kit measures. A color-matching test kit compares a treated sample with a reference scale, and the reading is recorded daily at each supply point, because a tank can be dosed once and then lose its residual within hours. The dose, contact time, residual target, and testing frequency must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the water is distributed.

Hot water is a clinical tool and a hazard at the same time, and its dangers are set by its temperature. Water hot enough for laundry, kitchen sanitation, and instrument washing can cause a deep burn in seconds, so taps in patient areas are fitted with thermostatic mixing valves that blend hot and cold water to a safe delivery temperature. The other hazard lives in the lukewarm zone, because the bacterium Legionella grows best in water held between roughly 20 and 45 degrees Celsius, and a storage tank kept only warm becomes a breeding vessel. The organism is spread as an aerosol from showers, taps, and cooling towers, and cooling towers deserve particular care because they evaporate water to reject heat and throw out a fine mist called drift that can travel a considerable distance. Control is by keeping stored water hot enough to kill the organism, by flushing or thermally disinfecting outlets on a schedule, and by cleaning and biocidal treatment of towers, and the storage temperature, delivery temperature, flushing interval, biocide dose, and testing method must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the system is changed.

Steam is the hospital's other hot utility, and it is generated in a boiler that heats water in a closed vessel until it vaporizes. Steam carries a large amount of energy in the latent heat it releases when it condenses, which is why it is used for sterilization in the autoclave, for cooking in the kitchen, for laundry, and for heating large buildings. The boiler is a pressure vessel with a fuel supply, a burner, a water level control, a safety valve, and a treatment program for the feedwater, because dissolved solids concentrate as water boils away and then form scale and carryover. Steam used directly on instruments or in sterile processing must be clean, because boiler additives, rust, and dissolved solids can contaminate a load, and some departments therefore use a separate clean steam generator. The boiler pressure, safety valve setting, feedwater treatment, and steam quality test must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the boiler is operated.

The materials chosen for pipes and fittings decide how long the system lasts and what it releases into the water, and the drainage side needs the same care. Copper is durable and resists heat but corrodes in acidic or soft water, stainless steel is used where cleanliness and corrosion resistance matter most, and chlorinated polyvinyl chloride, usually shortened to PVC, is common for drains and for cold water where its joints are acceptable. Every drain connected to a sewer carries a trap, a U-shaped section that holds a water seal and blocks sewer gas, and that seal fails when the trap dries out in an unused sink or is pulled empty by a pressure surge. Backflow prevention keeps dirty water from being drawn back into the clean supply when pressure drops, and it is achieved by an air gap, a vacuum breaker, a check valve, or a reduced pressure zone assembly. The pipe material, joint method, trap seal depth, flushing frequency, backflow device type, and annual test must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the plumbing is approved.

Water quality is proved by testing, and testing is worthless without records and without a person who reads them. Free residual chlorine is measured at the tank, at the farthest tap, and at each clinical point of use, because the residual falls as water travels and as it sits. A log book or an electronic record is kept for every result, together with the date, the sampler, the method, and any corrective action, and the log reveals a slow drift that a single reading cannot. The operator is the last and most important sensor in the system, and a short round at the start of each shift covers the temperature and humidity displays, the pressure gauges for theatres, isolation rooms, and laboratories, the filter status, the alarm panel, the chlorine reading, the clarity and smell of the water, and any visible leak or overflow. The sampling points, test methods, acceptable limits, recording interval, check list, and escalation path must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the results are used to certify the supply.

The consumables of the water system are the items that keep treatment working between repairs, and they include a few genuine hazards. They include filter cartridges and membranes, softening resin and the salt used to regenerate it, chlorine solution or tablets, test kit reagents and reference color charts, thermostatic mixing valve cartridges, gaskets, O-rings, pipe fittings, thread sealing tape, sampling bottles, calibration buffers, and descaler for coils and boilers. The hazards are worth naming together, because each has killed patients, and they include scalding from a failed or bypassed mixing valve, chlorine gas released when hypochlorite is mixed with acid or ammonia, Legionella carried from a lukewarm tank or a shower head, sewer gas and aerosol from a dry floor drain, a cross connection between a dirty drain and a clean supply during a pressure loss, and carbon monoxide from a badly vented boiler or heater, which is odorless and fatal. Electrical equipment operated with wet hands or beside a leaking pipe is an electrocution hazard, and the stock level, storage conditions, expiry check, and the engineering control that prevents each hazard must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the supply is used.

The field clinic answers the same two needs with a tank, a filter, a solar heater, and chlorination done by hand, and it gives up central control in exchange for independence. Rainwater is soft and relatively clean but carries dust and bird droppings from the roof, so a first-flush device discards the opening minutes of each rain and a screen keeps out leaves and insects. A protected well, a spring box, or a borehole with an apron and a drainage channel is safer than an open river, and a hand pump, a bucket and rope, or a small solar pump is the extraction device. Storage is in a tank, a drum, or a collapsible bladder, and the vessel must be covered, vented through a screen, and emptied and cleaned on a schedule, because a clean source poured into a dirty tank becomes dirty water. The tank capacity, cleaning interval, cover condition, and pump power supply must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the supply is used for patients.

Treatment in the field is a sequence of simple steps, and the order matters more than the elegance of any single device. Coarse cloth or a sand filter removes the visible sediment first, because turbidity shields organisms from chlorine and clogs a finer filter. A ceramic candle filter or a biological sand filter then removes most bacteria and larger organisms by physical straining and by the living layer that grows on the sand, and the candle is scrubbed and backwashed when its flow slows. Boiling is the most reliable single method where fuel is available, because a rolling boil kills bacteria, viruses, and parasites, though it concentrates any dissolved chemicals and consumes fuel. The filter rating, boil duration, and the method chosen for each source must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the water is given to a patient.

Chlorination by hand is the step that most often goes wrong, because it depends on a person measuring a volume and a concentration with no laboratory behind them. The usual approach is a stock solution made to a known strength and then a measured number of drops or milliliters added to a known volume of water, mixed, and left to stand for the required contact time. A small color-matching test kit, using a tablet or a liquid reagent, measures the free residual chlorine, and the result decides whether to add more or to wait for the level to fall. The common errors are dosing by eye, using an expired reagent, testing immediately instead of after the contact time, and trusting a single reading at the tank while the farthest tap has lost its residual. The dose, contact time, residual range, and test frequency must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the water is distributed, and the field worker must be able to read and record the result.

Hot water in the field is made by the sun, and the same two hazards of temperature apply at a smaller scale. A simple solar heater is a black tank or a coil of black pipe inside a glazed box, and water circulates by gravity as it warms, so the tank sits above the collector and the system needs no pump. The output temperature depends on the sun, the insulation, and the flow, and it can be scalding at midday and lukewarm by evening, which is exactly the range in which Legionella grows in a stored tank. The field answer is to draw water fresh rather than store it warm, to keep the tank covered, and to mix at the point of use, and the storage temperature, delivery temperature, and cleaning interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the water is used.

Air in the field is moved by wind, by heat, and by a fan rather than by a designed duct system. Cross ventilation uses openings on two opposite sides so that wind pushes air through, and stack ventilation uses a high outlet and a low inlet so that warm air rises and leaves while cool air enters below. A ceiling fan or a standing fan driven by a solar panel and a battery increases air movement and makes a hot room survivable, but it does not remove infectious particles unless it moves air all the way out of the space. Where a generator or a fuel heater is used, the exhaust must be led outside, because carbon monoxide is colorless, odorless, and lethal in a closed room, and a charcoal brazier indoors is the same hazard with a familiar smell that people wrongly trust. The airflow direction, fan placement, opening area, and venting of any fuel appliance must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the space is occupied.

Steam for sterilization in the field comes from a pressure cooker or a small autoclave heated over a fire or a burner, and the same physics applies as in a hospital boiler. The device must reach a defined temperature and pressure for a defined time, and the air must be removed from the chamber, because trapped air prevents steam from contacting the load. A pressure cooker with a weighted gauge reaches a temperature near 121 degrees Celsius at about 1 bar above atmospheric pressure, and that figure, the cycle time, and the cooling method must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before a load is released. Monitoring is by a chemical indicator strip, a temperature record, or a biological test where one is available, because a gauge can stick and a burner can run out of fuel in the middle of a cycle. The field operator must therefore load the chamber loosely, allow a free path for steam, and treat any interruption of the cycle as a failed load.

Sanitation in the field is a separate structure from the clinic, and the separation is deliberate. Latrines are sited downhill and downwind of the water source, with a minimum separation distance that the local protocol sets, and graywater from washing and from the clinic is led to a soak pit rather than to the ground beside the door. Treated water is kept in a covered container with a narrow opening and a tap or a ladle used only for that container, the container is cleaned and dried on a schedule, and drinking cups are never dipped into the store. What the field version gives up is central control, continuous power, calibrated instrumentation, and the ability to hold a room at a fixed temperature and humidity, and it replaces them with simpler but far more labor-intensive controls. What it demands instead is that one named person owns each routine, that the consumables are on the shelf before they are needed, and that every reading is written down rather than remembered, and the spare part list, cleaning schedule, and inspection interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the clinic depends on the system.

Put the two halves side by side and the contrast is a lesson in engineering rather than in wealth. The hospital-grade system holds a room at a chosen temperature, humidity, and pressure with sensors, alarms, and records, and its weakness is that people stop noticing when the numbers are right and stop reading them when an alarm repeats. The field system has almost no automatic control, so it works exactly as well as the person who fills the tank, doses the chlorine, and tests the result, and its weakness is that one skipped morning destroys a fortnight of good practice. The set points, alarm thresholds, treatment doses, and testing intervals named in this chapter are examples only and must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before any system is used on a patient.

To summarize, air and water are clinical equipment even though they are rarely listed as such, and their physics is simple enough to guide every decision. Air moves from higher pressure to lower pressure, carries heat and moisture with it, dilutes and filters what it cannot remove, and must be directed so that contaminated air leaves and clean air arrives. Water follows gravity and pressure, dissolves whatever it touches, carries organisms and minerals, and must be filtered, softened, disinfected, and held at the right temperature for each department it serves. Hot water scalds and lukewarm water grows Legionella, steam sterilizes and also burns, drains protect and also backflow, and cooling towers condition air and also aerosolize. The hospital version replaces human attention with sensors, alarms, and a scheduled maintenance program, while the field version replaces them with a tank, a filter, a solar heater, and a test kit that a named person uses every day. In both settings the same principle holds, because what is not measured, cleaned, and recorded will eventually fail, and it will fail quietly.
