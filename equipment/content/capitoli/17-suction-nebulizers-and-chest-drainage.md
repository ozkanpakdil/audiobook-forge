# Chapter 17 — Suction, Nebulizers, and Chest Drainage

Three families of equipment share this chapter because they share one physical idea, which is that gas and liquid move from a place of higher pressure to a place of lower pressure. Suction lowers the pressure at the tip of a tube below the pressure of the room, so the atmosphere pushes air, blood, vomit, or secretions into the tube. A nebulizer drives a gas through a narrow opening and uses that fast stream to tear a liquid drug into a mist that the patient's own breath carries into the airway. Chest drainage restores a one-way path for air or fluid to leave the pleural space, where the pressure is normally a little below atmospheric pressure. Each system is described twice, first in its hospital form with pipelines and single-use consumables, and then in its field form, where the work is done with a hand pump, a bottle, and sterile water.

Suction is not a force that pulls material toward a machine. It is the removal of gas from a closed space so that the pressure inside falls, after which the higher pressure outside pushes whatever lies at the opening into the tube. A pump creates the difference, a regulator decides how large it will be, a canister receives what is removed, and a catheter carries the opening to the place that must be cleared. The flow depends on the pressure difference, the length and internal diameter of the tubing, and the resistance of the catheter, so a long narrow tube joined to a weak pump moves little material no matter what the gauge says.

In a well-resourced hospital the suction arrives at the bedside through a central vacuum pipeline. Two or more pumps in a plant room draw air from a receiver tank, and filters and traps protect them from liquid and dust. The pipework runs to terminal units at the wall, each a gas-specific socket keyed by a diameter index safety system so that oxygen or air cannot be plugged into it by mistake. The pipeline carries an area alarm panel, usually near the ward entrance, and a low-vacuum alarm sounds when the level falls below the set threshold, with the threshold confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The response is to establish whether patients are receiving suction and, if not, to move to a portable unit, because a closed isolation valve or a leaking terminal can produce the same reading.

The regulator tames the pipeline into a usable level, and it hangs below the wall outlet or sits on the lid of the canister. Inside it a diaphragm and a spring oppose the vacuum, so turning the dial changes the point at which the two forces balance and therefore the suction that reaches the patient. The gauge is commonly marked from 0 to 300 millimeters of mercury, a range that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Continuous suction holds the set level, while intermittent suction switches it on and off on a timed cycle, and the mode must be chosen deliberately rather than left wherever the previous user placed it.

The canister is the collection vessel, designed so that a handling mistake does not reach the pump. It is a rigid jar of about 1 to 3 liters, a range that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, with an inlet for the patient tubing and an outlet for the vacuum line, and most modern systems line it with a disposable bag. A float valve rises with the fluid and closes the outlet when the jar is nearly full, and a hydrophobic filter stops liquid and aerosol from entering the pipework. Overfilling can spill into the vacuum line and contaminate the whole pipeline, and a canister struck by a trolley can pour its contents back through the patient tubing, so the jar must hang on the wall or stand below the level of the chest.

The connecting tubing is thick-walled and non-collapsible, usually about 6 to 8 millimeters in internal diameter and 1.5 to 2 meters long, values that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. It must be transparent enough that the operator can see fluid moving through it. Longer tubing adds resistance and reduces the flow that reaches the patient, which is why a tube coiled on the floor is a common cause of ineffective suction. A kinked tube is the classic silent failure, because the pump runs, the gauge reads correctly, and nothing arrives at the tip. The tubing is a single-patient item and is replaced when soiled or cracked.

Suction catheters are soft tubes with a rounded tip, side openings, and a connector at the machine end, and they are sized in French units, which describe the external circumference. Sizes commonly run from 5 to 18 French, a range that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the rule for airway suction is that the catheter should be no larger than one half the internal diameter of the artificial airway. The catheter must reach the carina, which the operator estimates by measuring from the corner of the mouth to the earlobe and then to the sternal notch. Vacuum is applied only while the catheter is withdrawn, never while it is advanced, and each pass should be brief and followed by rest and oxygen. For the mouth a rigid tip with a single large opening is used instead, and it is never passed into the nose, where it can tear the mucosa.

For the ventilated patient, disconnecting the breathing circuit to pass a catheter is dangerous, and the closed suction system avoids it. The catheter is enclosed in a transparent sleeve and stays attached to the airway between uses, so the circuit is never opened and the positive end-expiratory pressure is never lost. A thumb port lets the operator advance the catheter and apply suction, and an irrigation port allows sterile saline to clear the tube. Closed systems are replaced at a defined interval that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. When sputum is needed, a small trap is inserted between the catheter and the canister so the specimen is collected sterile, sealed, labeled, and sent to the laboratory without delay.

Where no pipeline exists, an electric suction unit is a self-contained machine with its own pump, motor, canister, and regulator. A rotary vane pump may be oil-lubricated or run dry, and the motor is cooled by a fan and protected by a thermal cutout that opens the circuit when it becomes too hot. A battery version is built for transport, and its runtime falls as the battery ages and as the canister and filter add resistance. The characteristic alarm is the thermal cutout, which stops the machine rather than warning, and the response is to switch to a backup unit. The oldest device, and still the most reliable without electricity, is the manual pump, worked by a foot bellows or a hand piston, and its level is lower and less steady but it needs no power. The other powerless option is a Venturi device, in which compressed oxygen or air is forced through a narrow jet and drags a vacuum with it, with a driving flow typically 10 to 15 liters per minute, a value that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Suction of an airway is never a neutral act, and its hazards must be named at the machine rather than deferred. The first is a fall in blood oxygen, because the catheter occupies the airway and the vacuum removes the oxygen in it, so the patient should be pre-oxygenated and each pass kept short, commonly under 15 seconds, a limit that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The second is a vagal response, in which suction slows the heart and drops the blood pressure. The third is direct injury to the mucosa, which bleeds and then swells and narrows the airway. The fourth is infection carried on the catheter, the canister, or the hands. The commonest operator error is to believe that stronger suction is better suction, when a vacuum at its maximum tears tissue and removes more oxygen than secretion, and the correct habit is to use the lowest level that clears the material.

Cleaning is what keeps the next patient safe. After each use the liner and tubing are discarded, and the canister is washed with warm water and detergent, rinsed, disinfected with a compatible product, and dried, with the contact time and concentration confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The lid, float valve, and hydrophobic filter are inspected, and a wet or clogged filter is replaced because it blocks the vacuum while a missing one sends fluid to the pump. In a mobile or frontier clinic the suction system is assembled from what can be carried, usually a foot or hand pump driving a single bottle, with a rigid tip for the mouth and a few soft catheters for the airway. A 12 volt pump run from a vehicle or solar-charged battery gives a steadier vacuum but adds a battery to cycle and a pump to keep dry, while a Venturi device run from an oxygen cylinder is strong enough for resuscitation but empties the cylinder quickly. The field demands that every part be washed and dried, every filter inspected, every battery logged, and a manual pump kept as the fallback that cannot fail.

A nebulizer turns a liquid medicine into a cloud of droplets small enough to be carried into the lower airway, and the size of those droplets decides where the drug lands. Droplets larger than about 5 micrometers, a figure that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, tend to strike the walls of the mouth and throat and never reach the lung. Droplets of about 1 to 5 micrometers travel with the airstream into the bronchi and the alveoli, and droplets smaller than about 1 micrometer may be exhaled before they deposit. Three mechanisms make this cloud, namely a fast jet of gas, a vibrating crystal, and a vibrating mesh, and each produces a different droplet spectrum and a different set of maintenance problems.

The jet nebulizer is the classic and the cheapest. A compressor or a cylinder pushes gas through a very narrow opening, and as the gas accelerates its pressure falls, so liquid is drawn up a capillary tube into the stream and torn apart into droplets. A baffle in the path removes the largest droplets and returns them to the reservoir, so the same liquid is recycled until it is used up. The compressor usually delivers about 6 to 8 liters per minute, a value that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, the chamber is filled with about 2 to 5 milliliters of solution, and a treatment commonly lasts 5 to 10 minutes. Jet nebulizers are robust and inexpensive, but they waste drug, because much of it remains in the chamber.

The ultrasonic nebulizer makes its mist with sound rather than with gas. A piezoelectric crystal is driven by an electrical oscillator at a frequency of roughly 1 to 3 megahertz, a value that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the crystal vibrates against a thin layer of liquid. The vibration sets up standing waves, and at their crests the liquid breaks into fine droplets that rise as a visible mist. The output is higher and quieter than that of a jet device and the treatment is faster, but the vibration warms the solution and can degrade some drugs, especially proteins, and the crystal and its coupling surface need careful cleaning. The device stops producing mist when the liquid level falls too low.

The mesh nebulizer is the newest of the three and the one that has travelled furthest into the field. A plate pierced with thousands of tiny tapered holes is vibrated, either by a piezoelectric ring around it or by a small pump that pushes the liquid through the holes, and each hole produces a droplet of very uniform size. The device is small, quiet, battery powered, and delivers a large fraction of the drug with very little left in the reservoir, which matters when the medicine is expensive or scarce. Its weakness is the mesh itself, which clogs with dried drug, with salt, and with sticky solutions, and which must be rinsed and dried after every use. The characteristic fault indicator is a blocked mesh warning or a low battery signal, and both must be treated as an instruction to stop and clean or recharge.

The nebulizer chamber is connected to the patient by a mask, a mouthpiece, or a T-piece with tubing, and the choice changes how much drug is delivered. A mouthpiece with a good seal gives the most reliable dose in a cooperative adult, while a mask is used for children, for the breathless, and for anyone who cannot hold a mouthpiece. Between the chamber and the patient there is often a short length of wide tubing and sometimes a one-way valve, and exhaled gas and condensation must escape so the patient does not re-breathe carbon dioxide. After every use the chamber, the mask or mouthpiece, and the tubing are washed in warm water and detergent, rinsed, disinfected if the maker allows it, and dried completely, because a damp nebulizer grows organisms that are then inhaled into the lung.

For a drug supplied in a pressurized canister, the spacer is often a better delivery device than a nebulizer. When the canister is pressed, a measured dose of drug and propellant leaves the valve at high speed, and without a spacer much of it strikes the back of the throat. A spacer is a plain tube or chamber that holds the cloud while the propellant evaporates, so the particles become smaller and slower and the patient can inhale the whole dose in one or two quiet breaths. A valved holding chamber adds a one-way valve at the patient end, which keeps the drug in the chamber during exhalation and makes the device usable by a young child. Where no spacer is available, a clean plastic bottle of about 500 milliliters can be made into a chamber, with a hole cut to accept the inhaler and the joint sealed with tape. The improvised chamber is not valved, and it must be clean, dry, and replaced when cracked.

The common operator errors in nebulization are simple and repeated. The first is to fill the chamber beyond the marked line or to mix drugs without checking compatibility, so the output falls and the mixture becomes unpredictable. The second is to run a compressor with a clogged intake filter, which reduces flow and droplet production and overheats the motor, and the third is to leave the tubing full of condensation so the patient inhales water rather than mist. The fourth is to dry the parts with a towel, which leaves lint and organisms, or to store them damp in a closed bag. The safety hazard that follows is infection of the lower airway with organisms that grew in the device. Some compressors carry a thermal cutout as their only alarm, and a mesh device may signal a blocked mesh or a low battery, and every such threshold must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

In a mobile or frontier clinic the nebulizer is usually a small compressor run from a vehicle battery through an inverter, from a solar-charged battery, or from a foot pump in an emergency. A mesh device powered by a rechargeable battery is increasingly common, because it is light, quiet, and parsimonious with the drug, but it depends on a charged battery and a clean mesh. An oxygen-driven jet nebulizer needs no electricity at all, but it consumes oxygen at 6 to 8 liters per minute, a value that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and it will empty a small cylinder during a single treatment. The field demands a cleaning routine that a busy ward can forget and a lone clinician cannot, namely wash, rinse, disinfect where possible, and air dry, with each part stored dry and protected from dust.

Chest drainage begins with a small space that is almost invisible when it is healthy. The pleural space is a thin layer of fluid between the lung and the chest wall, and the pressure within it is normally a little below atmospheric pressure, which is what holds the lung expanded against its own elastic recoil. If air enters the space, as after a puncture or a burst bleb, or if blood, pus, or lymph collects there, the lung is pushed away from the chest wall and collapses, and breathing becomes difficult and inefficient. A chest drain reopens a path from that space to the outside and then controls the direction of flow, so air and fluid can leave but nothing can be drawn back in. The system therefore has three parts to think about, namely the pleural space, the atmosphere, and the one-way valve between them.

The chest tube itself is a soft, flexible tube of silicone or polyvinyl chloride, and it is sized in French units, with common adult sizes running from about 20 to 40 French and smaller sizes used in children, values that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A straight tube has several side openings near its tip, called drainage eyes, and a stripe of radiopaque material along its length so its position can be seen on a radiograph. A pigtail catheter is thinner, coiled at the tip, and designed for air or for a small collection, and it is usually placed over a guide wire rather than by cutting. Some tubes carry a sharp inner trocar that helps guide the tube through the chest wall, and that trocar is also the source of the most serious injuries, because it can be pushed into the lung or the heart.

Once the tube is in place it must be held there, because a tube that slips out is both a treatment failure and a new open wound. The tube is fixed with a heavy suture through the skin and around the tube, and the site is covered with an occlusive dressing that keeps air from entering around the tube while allowing the tube to leave it. The dressing is changed with a sterile technique, the skin is inspected for infection, and the suture is checked for security at every shift. The tube is connected to the drainage system with a connector of the correct size, and the connection must be locked and taped so it cannot separate when the patient moves. A loose connection, a stretched suture, and a dressing that has come away are the three commonest mechanical failures of chest drainage.

The simplest and most important part of the drainage system is the underwater seal, and it works by the same principle as a valve in a drainpipe. The end of the tube from the patient is held below the surface of a small volume of sterile water, so that when the patient exhales or coughs, air and fluid push out through the water, and when the patient inhales, the water is drawn back up the tube and seals it before air can enter. The depth of the tube below the surface is usually about 2 centimeters, a value that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, because a deeper seal makes the patient work harder to push air out and a shallower one can be lost with a tilt. The water column also moves slightly with each breath, and that gentle rise and fall, called tidaling or swinging, is the sign that the tube is still open.

Modern hospital systems combine three functions in a single molded unit. The first chamber collects the fluid and is marked with a scale so the output can be measured. The second chamber is the underwater seal, with its 2 centimeters of water that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and it shows both the swing of breathing and any continuous bubbling that means air is still leaking. The third chamber controls suction, either by a column of water set to about 10 to 20 centimeters of water, a value that must also be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, or by a dry regulator and a dial, called a dry suction system. Before molded units existed, the same three functions were performed by three separate glass bottles. The first bottle collects the drainage, the second holds the underwater seal, and the third is the suction control bottle, and the three-bottle system works when assembled correctly, but it is fragile, it tips easily, and every joint is a place where air can enter or fluid can spill.

For transport and for walking patients, the water seal is replaced by a one-way valve, usually a flattened tube with a flexible membrane inside that opens in one direction only. The valve allows air and fluid to leave but not to return, it cannot spill, and it cannot be lifted above the chest and then drain back into the patient, which makes it the safest choice when a patient must be moved between buildings or in a vehicle. It gives up the information that the water seal provides, because there is no column to swing and no bubbles to watch, so the clinician can judge the drain only by the amount of fluid in the bag and by the patient's condition. The valve must be kept free of kinks and blood clot, and its direction of flow must be confirmed before it is connected, because a valve fitted the wrong way blocks the drain completely.

The chest drain is monitored by watching and measuring rather than by listening for a tone, though some electronic drainage systems do provide alarms for an occluded tube or a persistent air leak, with thresholds that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The first sign to check is the swing of the water column, because a column that has stopped moving usually means the tube is blocked or kinked, or that the lung has re-expanded. The second is bubbling, which is normal for a short time after insertion and afterwards means a continuing air leak. A blocked drain can allow air to collect under pressure in the pleural space, and that tension pushes the heart aside, a pattern that requires immediate decompression. Draining a long-standing collection too quickly can pull fluid into the lung, a condition called re-expansion pulmonary edema, so the rate is often limited, with the limit confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The common operator errors in chest drainage are almost all positional. The first is to lift the drainage unit above the level of the patient's chest, for example to hang it on a stand or carry it up a stair, which lets fluid run back into the pleural space. The second is to clamp the tube while the patient is moved or the dressing is changed, because a clamped drain cannot relieve a tension pneumothorax. The third is to strip or milk the tube to clear it, a practice that generates very high negative pressure and can injure the lung. At every shift the operator confirms that the unit is upright and below the chest, that the water seal is at its marked level, that the connections are locked, that the dressing is intact, and that the tube is not kinked, and the output and any swing or leak are recorded. The drainage unit is replaced when it is full, broken, or at the interval the maker and the protocol specify.

In a mobile or frontier clinic the drainage system is built from a bottle, a tube, and sterile water, and it must be built correctly or not at all. A clean bottle is filled with sterile water to a marked depth, the tube from the patient is passed through the cap and its tip held 2 centimeters below the surface, a value that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the bottle is placed below the level of the chest. The cap must be sealed around the tube, because an open bottle is an open pneumothorax, and the bottle must be marked with the time and the starting level so the output can be measured. Where a commercial one-way valve is available it is preferred for transport, because it cannot spill or drain back. Where no sterile water and no clean bottle exist, a chest drain cannot be improvised, and the patient must be moved to a place where one can be placed.

To summarize, suction, nebulization, and chest drainage are three ways of managing pressure and flow around the airway and the pleural space. Suction works by lowering pressure at the tip of a tube so that the atmosphere pushes material out, and it demands the lowest effective vacuum, the shortest pass, and a clean canister and filter. Nebulization works by breaking a liquid into droplets of a size that will reach the lung, and it demands the correct fill volume, an unobstructed jet, crystal, or mesh, and a device that is washed and dried after every use. Chest drainage provides a one-way path for air and fluid to leave the pleural space, and it demands a system that stays upright, below the chest, unkinked, and sealed. In every setting the operator who understands the pressure gradient recognizes failure before the patient does, and every setting, pressure, and interval named in this chapter must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.
