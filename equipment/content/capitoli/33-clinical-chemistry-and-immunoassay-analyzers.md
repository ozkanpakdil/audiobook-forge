# Chapter 33 — Clinical Chemistry and Immunoassay Analyzers

Clinical chemistry and immunoassay analyzers are the instruments that measure molecules rather than cells, and that distinction explains how they are built. A hematology analyzer counts and sorts formed elements, while a chemistry analyzer asks how much of a dissolved substance is present in serum, plasma, urine, or another body fluid, and an immunoassay analyzer asks the same question about substances so dilute that ordinary chemistry cannot see them. The physical problem is that a molecule is invisible, so the instrument must persuade it to produce a signal. There are 3 great routes to that signal: a chemical reaction that creates color and therefore absorbs light, an electrical potential that appears across a membrane admitting one ion and refusing others, and a light-emitting label attached to an antibody that has captured its target. Each route converts an invisible quantity into a number that a photodetector or an amplifier can measure, and knowing which route an instrument uses tells the operator what will disturb it, what will drift, and what must be cleaned. This chapter describes the hospital-grade laboratory first, with its plumbing, its reagents, and its service needs, and then the field variant that must work where there is dust, heat, and no reliable refrigerator.

The workhorse of the chemistry laboratory is spectrophotometry, and its logic is easy to hold in mind. A lamp, either a tungsten halogen bulb or a light emitting diode, called LED, produces a broad beam; a monochromator or an interference filter narrows it to a band of wavelengths; the beam crosses a transparent cuvette holding the reaction mixture; and a photodetector measures how much light survives the journey. The instrument first measures a blank and then the sample, and compares the 2. What it calculates is absorbance, the logarithm of the ratio between the light that entered and the light that left, and the reason this quantity is used is that absorbance rises in direct proportion to the amount of colored substance in the light path. In plain words, twice the concentration absorbs twice the light, and a longer path through the liquid absorbs more. That is the Beer-Lambert relationship, and it is what allows a color change to be translated into a concentration. The wavelength chosen for a test, the incubation temperature of 37 degrees Celsius, and the path length of the cuvette must all be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.

Most molecules of clinical interest are colorless, so the reagent mixture is designed to turn the analyte into a colored product, or to consume a colored product at a rate that depends on the analyte. In an endpoint reaction the color develops fully and is read once, which is simple and robust; in a rate reaction the instrument reads the changing absorbance several times over a fixed interval and calculates the slope, which is how enzymes are measured, because an enzyme present in tiny amounts acts on its substrate again and again and its catalytic speed reveals its concentration. Many such reactions are coupled to the oxidation of reduced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide, which absorbs light strongly at 340 nanometers, so the disappearance of that absorption tracks the reaction. The common operator errors are physical rather than chemical: a cuvette with a fingerprint or a scratch, a bubble in the light path, a sample cloudy from fat, pink from released hemoglobin, or deeply yellow from bilirubin, and a reaction read before it has reached its proper point. A cloudy or discolored sample scatters and absorbs light on its own, so the result may be a plausible number that is simply wrong, and severe hemolysis can add substances that the reagent mistakes for the analyte. The characteristic alarm is the absorbance range or reaction error flag, which warns that the reading fell outside the range in which the mathematics still holds, and the exact limits and the correct response must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.

The second physical route is the ion-selective electrode, which measures an electrical potential rather than light and is used above all for sodium, potassium, and chloride. The heart of the device is a membrane permeable to one ion and nearly impermeable to all others, so a charge separation builds across it whose size depends on how much of that ion is present; a stable reference electrode completes the circuit, and a very high impedance amplifier reads the voltage between the 2. The relationship between voltage and concentration is logarithmic, which lets the electrode respond over a wide range but also means it must be calibrated often. Most instruments calibrate with 2 solutions of known concentration, one near the low end and one near the high end of the clinical range, and the resulting slope and offset convert voltage into a number. Some analyzers measure the sample directly while others dilute it first, and this difference matters enormously when a patient has very high protein or very high lipid levels, because the diluted measurement can report a falsely low sodium that is not present in the body. The membrane is also easily fouled by protein, by clot, and by air bubbles, and an electrode near the end of its life loses its slope and drifts, which appears as a calibration that will not hold. The electrode slope, the calibration voltage, and the operating temperature of 37 degrees Celsius must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.

The third architecture abandons liquid reagents and places the whole reaction in a thin film, and this is the dry chemistry slide. The slide is a small rectangle of transparent plastic carrying several layers: a spreading layer that distributes a drop evenly over a defined area, sometimes a scavenger layer that removes interfering substances, a reagent layer holding the enzymes and dyes, and an indicator layer where the color finally appears. The instrument applies a measured drop, usually about 10 microliters, incubates the slide, and shines light through the transparent support from below, reading the color by reflectance rather than transmission. Because the slide is the reaction vessel, there are no reagent bottles, no tubing to rinse, and no wash water, so the instrument can be small, quiet, and battery powered. The price is a narrower menu and a higher cost for each test, since every measurement consumes a manufactured slide. The characteristic operator errors are a drop too small or too large, an air bubble under the spreading layer, a finger touching the reagent surface, a slide taken from a pouch left open in humid air, and a slide used after its expiry date, and the analyzer usually answers these with an insufficient sample or slide error message rather than a number. The storage conditions, the sample volume, the incubation time, and the reflectance limits of each slide type must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.

Between the tiny dry slide and the enormous automated line sits the discrete cuvette analyzer, the instrument most hospital laboratories actually run. A robotic arm carries a sample probe that descends into the sample cup, senses the liquid surface, and aspirates a precise volume measured in microliters; a second probe adds one or more reagents; the mixture moves into a plastic cuvette, is warmed to 37 degrees Celsius, and is read by a photometer at one or more wavelengths. After the reading the cuvette is rinsed by a wash station and dried with a jet of air, or discarded if it is a single-use design. Behind the visible probes are peristaltic pumps, syringe pumps, valves, and stepper motors, and the precision of the whole instrument rests on those hidden parts. The characteristic hazards are a probe that strikes a cuvette because the mechanical position has drifted, a probe clogged by a fibrin strand from an incompletely clotted sample, and reagent carryover from one test to the next when the wash is incomplete. Because the sample is handled in open air, aerosols and spills are a real biohazard, and the operator should treat the sample area as contaminated and wear gloves and eye protection. The pipetting volumes, the incubation temperature, the wash volumes, and the level-sensing sensitivity must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.

The largest chemistry instruments are not single analyzers but automated lines, in which a track carries tubes between modules. The line begins with a sample handler that reads the barcode on each tube and records its position, then a decapper that removes the cap, a centrifuge module that separates serum, and an aliquoter that splits the sample into smaller cups for different destinations. Along the track stand chemistry modules, immunoassay modules, and sometimes a module for coagulation or for glycated hemoglobin, and at the end a refrigerated archive holds the finished tubes for later add-on testing. Throughput is measured in tests per hour and can reach several thousand on a large line, and the software decides which module receives a test, dilutes a result above the measuring range, repeats a result that failed a check, and adds a reflex test when an initial result crosses a rule. The great safety hazard of the line is misidentification, because a barcode read in the wrong orientation, a tube loaded into the wrong position, or a label smeared by condensation can attach one patient's result to another patient's name. The common operator errors are loading tubes the wrong way round, providing too little blood for the requested menu, using a tube type the line cannot process, and leaving a clot that blocks a probe downstream. The characteristic alarms are a track jam, a barcode unreadable message, and a sample not detected warning, and every threshold, speed, and alarm response must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.

An immunoassay does not rely on color chemistry or on an electrode but on the selectivity of an antibody, and that selectivity is the whole basis of the method. An antibody is a protein produced by the immune system that binds one particular molecular shape, called an epitope, and ignores almost everything else. In the laboratory such antibodies are raised deliberately, either by immunizing an animal and harvesting its antibodies or by growing cells engineered to secrete one single antibody, and the result is a reagent that finds its target in a mixture of thousands of other molecules. This is essential because hormones, cardiac markers, and therapeutic drugs circulate at concentrations of nanograms or picograms per milliliter, far below the reach of ordinary spectrophotometry. The common formats are the sandwich assay, in which one antibody captures the analyte and a second labeled antibody binds a different part of it, and the competitive assay, in which a labeled copy of the analyte competes with the patient's own analyte for a limited number of antibody binding sites. In every format the bound material must be separated from the unbound before the signal is read, and an incomplete separation is a classic source of a falsely high result. The operator must also respect cross-reactivity, in which a similar molecule is mistaken for the target, interference from the patient's own antibodies, and the hook effect, in which an extremely high concentration overwhelms the capture and produces a falsely low reading. The interference limits and the dilution protocol must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.

The signal at the end of an immunoassay can be produced in 3 principal ways, and each carries its own failure modes. In the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, the detection antibody carries an enzyme, and after the unbound material is washed away a substrate is added that the enzyme converts into a colored product whose absorbance is read in a plate reader; the plate has many small wells, so dozens of samples can be run at once. In chemiluminescence the label itself emits light when triggered by a chemical reaction, and a photomultiplier tube counts the photons, which gives enormous sensitivity and a wide working range and needs no lamp or monochromator, but also makes the measurement sensitive to stray light and to contaminated reagent. In the lateral flow format the reagents are dried onto a strip of nitrocellulose, the liquid sample wicks along it by capillary action, a labeled antibody travels with it, and the captured complex forms a visible line at a fixed position, with a second control line proving that the strip worked. The lateral flow test is cheap, needs no instrument, and is ideal in the field, but it is only as good as its storage and its timing, because a strip read too early shows nothing and a strip read too late can develop a faint line that means nothing at all. The characteristic alarms of a laboratory immunoassay module are a calibration failure and a signal below the detection limit. The cutoffs, incubation times, wavelengths, and alarm responses for every one of these formats must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.

The analytes a chemistry analyzer reports are chosen because each answers a physiological question, and each method has its own quirks. Glucose is measured by an enzyme that oxidizes it and produces hydrogen peroxide, which then drives a second reaction that creates color, or by an electrode that measures the same reaction as an electric current; because red cells continue to consume glucose after the blood is drawn, the tube should contain a substance that stops that consumption, and otherwise the sample must be separated quickly. Creatinine, the marker of kidney filtration, is measured either by its reaction with alkaline picrate, the older method, or by an enzyme, and the older method is cheaper but disturbed by bilirubin, by ketones, and by several common drugs. Urea is measured by the enzyme urease, which splits it into ammonia and carbon dioxide, after which the ammonia is quantified, and because urea production depends on diet and hydration as well as on the kidneys the result is read alongside creatinine. Sodium, potassium, and chloride come from the ion-selective electrodes described earlier, while calcium and magnesium are measured by related electrode or dye methods. A single chemistry panel may therefore rest on 3 or 4 completely different physical principles at once, and the operator must know which method produced a result before interpreting an odd value. The reaction temperatures, incubation intervals, and method-specific interference notes must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.

The liver tests show how enzymes reveal organ injury. Alanine aminotransferase and aspartate aminotransferase are measured by rate reactions in which the enzyme's own activity generates a color change, and their concentration in blood rises when liver cells leak; alkaline phosphatase and gamma-glutamyl transferase are measured in the same fashion and point more toward bile ducts and bone. Bilirubin is measured by coupling it to a diazonium salt to form a colored compound, albumin by its binding to a dye that changes color when bound, and total protein by the biuret reaction, in which copper in alkaline solution forms a colored complex with peptide bonds. Cardiac markers are immunoassays, because troponin and the myocardial band of creatine kinase circulate in vanishingly small amounts, and a rising troponin over several hours is more meaningful than one value, which is why the timing of the second sample is written into every chest pain protocol. Hormones such as thyroid stimulating hormone, free thyroxine, cortisol, and human chorionic gonadotropin, and therapeutic drugs such as digoxin, gentamicin, vancomycin, and phenytoin, are also immunoassays, and for a drug the moment of sampling matters as much as the number, because a level drawn at the wrong time relative to the dose cannot be interpreted. The reference intervals, dilution rules, and interpretive cutoffs for all of these tests must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.

Calibration is the bridge between a signal and a concentration, and quality control is the proof that the bridge is still standing. A calibrator is a material with a known concentration, usually traceable to a national or international reference preparation, and the instrument measures it to build a relationship between signal and concentration; some tests need a single calibrator, some need a full curve of several points, and a new reagent lot or a replaced electrode almost always demands a fresh calibration. Quality control material is different from a calibrator: it is a sample with an assigned value that the instrument treats as if it were a patient, and it comes at more than one level, typically a normal level and an abnormal level. The laboratory runs controls at the start of every shift, after maintenance, after a reagent lot change, and after any calibration, and plots the results on a chart; a result that drifts or jumps triggers a defined rule, and by long convention a control more than 2 standard deviations from its mean is a warning while a larger excursion is a rejection. If a control fails, no patient result from that run may be reported until the cause is found and corrected, and the most damaging common error is the temptation to recalibrate until the control looks acceptable instead of finding the fault. Internal control proves only that the instrument is consistent with itself, so laboratories also join an external quality assessment scheme, in which unknown samples arrive from outside and the returned results are compared with those of other laboratories. The control limits, the accepted rules, and the corrective actions must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.

The quiet requirements of a chemistry laboratory are reagents, water, and waste, and each can ruin results long before any electronic fault appears. Many reagents must be kept cold, typically between 2 and 8 degrees Celsius, some must be frozen, and some arrive as a concentrate that is reconstituted and then remains stable for only a few days; most degrade with heat and light, and a bottle left on the bench overnight may give a result that is wrong in a direction nobody can predict. Reagents must never be topped up into a partly used bottle, because that mixes lots and invites contamination, and a reagent past its expiry date or changed in color should be discarded rather than used. Water is a reagent in its own right, since it dilutes samples, rinses cuvettes, and carries reagents, so it must be purified by distillation, by deionization, or usually both, and its purity is judged by electrical resistivity, commonly expressed in megohm centimeters, with bacteria and dissolved carbon dioxide removed as well. Waste is the other half of the equation, because the instrument produces a continuous stream of liquid containing patient sample, reagent, and cleaning solution, and this stream is both a biological and a chemical hazard that may carry heavy metals, azide, strong acid, or strong base. Waste must be collected in labeled containers, never poured into an ordinary sink without checking local rules, and the fill level watched because a full container will eventually shut the instrument down. The storage temperatures, the reconstituted stability limits, the water resistivity specification, and the waste handling rules must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.

A chemistry or immunoassay analyzer rewards routine care and punishes neglect in ways that are easy to predict once the design is understood. Every day the operator should confirm that the water supply is flowing, the waste container has room, the probes are clean and undamaged, the reagent compartments are loaded correctly, and the controls have been run and passed before any patient sample is handled. Every week the instrument usually needs a cleaning or wash cycle, a check of the cuvettes or reaction cells, and a look at the lamp or detector readings; every month the syringes, seals, tubing, and filters deserve inspection; and once a year, or at the interval the maker states, the photometer is checked, the lamp and the peristaltic pump tubing are replaced, and the probe alignment is verified. The typical failure modes leave characteristic traces: a lamp near the end of its life produces rising blanks and noisy results; flattened pump tubing delivers a smaller volume than it should and shifts every result in the same direction; an aging ion-selective electrode loses its slope and reads low; a drifting incubation temperature disturbs every enzyme reaction measured by rate; and a partly blocked sample probe gives intermittent short samples that appear as random errors. The characteristic alarms are no water, waste full, low lamp intensity, temperature out of range, probe collision, and control out of range, and the operator must never silence an alarm without identifying what caused it. Each maintenance interval, lamp hour limit, temperature tolerance, and alarm threshold must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.

Long before the analyzer is involved, the fate of a chemistry result is decided by how the blood was taken and handled, and this pre-analytic stage causes more errors than any instrument fault. The patient should be fasting when the test requires it, the tourniquet should be released within about 1 minute, and the patient should not clench and relax the fist repeatedly, because that alone can raise the potassium measurably; the tube must be filled to its marked volume so the additive is correctly proportioned, and the order of draw matters because a trace of the chelating agent from a lavender-topped tube will lower calcium and raise potassium in the next tube. Hemolysis is the most common visible problem, since red cells broken by a narrow needle, by pulling too hard on a syringe, by shaking a tube, or by forcing blood through a needle release their contents, which raises potassium, raises lactate dehydrogenase, and interferes with many colorimetric reactions. Serum or plasma must be separated from the cells within the time the local protocol allows, because potassium leaks out of cells on standing and glucose is consumed by them, and some analytes, notably bilirubin, must be protected from light. The sample must then be centrifuged at the specified speed for the specified time, transported at the required temperature, and delivered with a label that the analyzer and the technologist can both read; sharps are never recapped by hand, and a blood spill is cleaned with gloves and a fresh disinfectant under local rules. Every time limit, centrifuge speed, storage temperature, and tube requirement must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.

Once a result exists it must travel to the person who will act on it, and that path is part of the device's design. Most analyzers connect to a laboratory information system, which in turn feeds the electronic health record, called EHR, and the common language for that conversation is Health Level Seven, called HL7; results may also carry interpretive flags for hemolysis, lipemia, or icterus detected by the instrument itself. Modern software performs a delta check, comparing each new result with the patient's previous value and holding back any change larger than a defined limit for human review, and it may autoverify results that pass every rule so that the technologist examines only the exceptions. Critical values, those results so far outside the reference interval that they demand immediate action, must be communicated directly to a clinician and documented, and the thresholds defining them are set by the laboratory in agreement with the clinical services. The operator's final checks before release are therefore identity, plausibility, units, reference interval, and any flag attached to the result, because a perfectly accurate measurement attached to the wrong patient is more dangerous than no measurement at all. The delta check limits, the critical value thresholds, and the notification procedure must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.

A field clinic at the end of a long road cannot support the laboratory just described, so the field variant is built around simplification and self-containment. There is often no reliable mains electricity, no purified water system, no refrigerator that holds a stable temperature, no continuous supply of liquid reagents, and no service engineer within a day's travel, and the person running the test may be the same person who took the sample while other patients wait. The instrument that answers this is a small reflectance or electrochemical analyzer using sealed dry slides or single-use cartridges, in which every reagent needed for the reaction is already inside the package, so there is nothing to reconstitute, nothing to refrigerate after opening, and no liquid waste to manage. It runs on a rechargeable battery that can be charged from a solar panel, and it calibrates itself by reading a code printed on the cartridge or the slide packet, which removes the burden of a manual calibration curve. Whole blood from a finger prick or a small tube can often be applied directly, which saves the centrifuge and the pipette, and the menu usually covers glucose, creatinine, urea, a few electrolytes, and perhaps 1 or 2 enzymes. What it gives up is throughput, since samples are run a sample at a time, and also the wide menu, the automatic dilution of high results, the delta check against previous values, and the full program of daily quality control with several levels of material. What it demands in exchange is discipline about the operating temperature range, which for some devices is roughly 15 to 30 degrees Celsius, about shade and protection from dust and wind, about the exact sample volume, about the timing of the reading, and about keeping a written log of every result, control, battery charge, and error message. Each of those figures, and any control schedule the field site can sustain, must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.

The device that does most of the field's chemistry work is the handheld glucose meter, and its physical principle is small enough to fit in a pocket. The strip contains an enzyme, usually glucose oxidase or a glucose dehydrogenase, plus a mediator molecule and 2 electrodes; when a drop of blood enters the strip, the enzyme oxidizes glucose and the mediator carries the electrons to the electrode, producing a current proportional to the amount of glucose present. Some meters use reflectance instead, reading a color change on the strip, but the electrochemical design dominates because it needs no optics and very little power. The strip is the fragile part: the reagents are dried onto it and are destroyed by moisture and heat, so the vial must be capped immediately, stored out of the sun, and never used past its expiry date, and the meter must be set to match the strip lot, either by a code chip, a keypad entry, or an automatic read of a code on the vial. The classical interferences are hematocrit and other blood constituents, because anemia can push the reading up and a very high red cell count can push it down, while ascorbic acid and severe dehydration distort it further, and extreme altitude and temperature change the chemistry as well. The common operator errors are a finger still wet with fruit juice, a drop too small to fill the strip, squeezing the fingertip so that tissue fluid dilutes the blood, an expired or heat-damaged strip, and a meter left uncoded for the strip in use. The characteristic alarms are a strip error, a low battery warning, a temperature out of range message, and a result flagged as below or above the measuring range, and the meter's error codes, the sample volume, the storage temperature, and the difference between whole blood and plasma readings, which is roughly 10 to 15 per cent, must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.

Immunoassay in the field is usually reduced to the lateral flow strip, and that reduction is both its strength and its limit. A pregnancy test, a malaria test, a human immunodeficiency virus test, and several others are the same physical object: a plastic housing containing a nitrocellulose strip, a pad holding a dried labeled antibody, a test line, and a control line, driven only by capillary action. The strip must be stored within its stated temperature range, usually below 30 degrees Celsius and never frozen, and it must be kept dry, because heat and moisture destroy the dried antibodies; the result must be read within the exact window printed by the maker, since a line that appears later is meaningless. The limit is sensitivity, because a lateral flow test cannot detect the tiny concentrations a laboratory immunoassay measures, and it may be negative early in an infection when the laboratory test is already positive, which is why the timing of the sample relative to the illness matters as much as the test itself. For sodium and potassium an older field method survives in the flame photometer, in which a diluted sample is sprayed into a flame, the sodium and potassium atoms emit light at their own characteristic wavelengths, and a filter and detector measure that light; the method is fast but needs a combustible gas, a very stable flame, and careful dilution, so it carries a fire hazard and a hot-surface hazard. A battery-operated immunoassay reader for troponin and similar markers exists for clinics without a laboratory, but its reagents still need a cold chain, and that chain is the real constraint in the field. Without a daily program of quality control at several levels, a field site cannot claim the accuracy of a hospital laboratory, so the most it can do is run a control solution when one is available, send a split sample to a reference laboratory from time to time, and compare the 2 results in writing; a device that has been dropped, soaked, or overheated should be withdrawn until it can be checked, and a result near a decision threshold should be repeated or confirmed by a laboratory method. The storage temperatures, reading windows, detection limits, gas and flame settings, cleaning solutions, and transport conditions for referral samples must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.

To summarize, clinical chemistry and immunoassay analyzers turn invisible molecules into measurable signals by 3 physical routes: the absorption of light by a colored reaction, the electrical potential across an ion-selective membrane, and the light or color produced by an antibody label. Hospital-grade instruments add plumbing, robotics, tracks, and software to gain throughput, menu breadth, and automatic checking, and in return they demand purified water, refrigerated reagents, waste handling, calibration, and a daily program of quality control before any patient result may be reported. Immunoassays extend the reach of the laboratory to hormones, cardiac markers, and drugs by exploiting the specificity of antibodies, whether in a plate, a chemiluminescent module, or a lateral flow strip. The field variant strips the instrument down to sealed dry slides, cartridges, and a handheld glucose meter, which gives up throughput, menu, and formal quality control while demanding discipline about temperature, sample volume, timing, dust, and battery charge. In both settings the safety hazards begin with the contaminated sample itself and with the misidentification that attaches a correct result to the wrong patient, and the characteristic alarms are the instrument's way of saying that a number cannot be trusted. The operator's real work is therefore not pressing the button but controlling the pre-analytic phase, checking the controls, watching the maintenance intervals, and knowing when to doubt a convenient result. Every setting, temperature, threshold, and interval mentioned in this chapter must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.
