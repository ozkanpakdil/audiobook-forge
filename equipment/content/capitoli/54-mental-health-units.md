# Chapter 54 — Mental Health Units

Every other chapter here describes a machine, while this chapter describes a room that acts on a patient. Its most important component is the absence of something, because a person in acute distress looks for an anchor above the body and a line to reach it. A ligature concentrates the whole weight of the body onto a band a few centimeters wide, which compresses the vessels to the brain and the airway. Nothing in that mechanism needs a special instrument, so this equipment is mostly a set of decisions to deny the mechanism its parts. A room without an anchor, a cord, or a breakable edge changes what a crisis can become before any clinician arrives. Every fixture here exists because a patient in crisis is an engineer of the nearest available material.

The ligature point is the first object to understand. A suspension point must hold a falling body, resist cutting the line, and stand high enough that the feet leave the floor, and hooks, door tops, rails, and grab bars all qualify while remaining ordinary furniture. The load is not the resting weight of the patient but a dynamic load several times greater, because a sudden drop turns motion into a sharp pull. Ligature-resistant hardware removes the hook shape, replaces it with a flat plate or a steep slope, and releases at a defined load, and that rating must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before installation. The common operator error is to see an odd piece of design rather than a safety device and to add a familiar hook beside it. The characteristic alarm here is not electronic, because it is a patient's unannounced absence from where the observation schedule expects them.

Door hardware is where most ligature points are created by accident. A lever handle, a coat hook on the back of a door, a closer arm, and the gap between door and frame all offer purchase, and the top edge of a door is a classic anchor because it is strong and above head height. Ligature-resistant doors use a continuous hinge line, a handle sloping inward at 45 degrees or more with no gap behind it, and a closer set in the floor or inside the frame. A door must also open outward or be openable against a patient who is pushing it closed, and the anti-barricade leaf resolves that conflict with a second set of hinges. Observation panels are the other half of the design, because a door that protects privacy also blocks the view, so a vision panel is set at a height that lets a standing adult see the whole room, and its obstruction from the inside by a poster or tape is an emergency rather than a housekeeping problem. The characteristic alarm is furniture dragged across a floor behind a closing door, and the team must open the way in without entering the narrow space between a pushing patient and a moving leaf.

The surfaces carry the same logic, because every projection, shelf bracket, and exposed pipe is a potential anchor. Ligature-resistant design flattens the room into recessed shelving, flush light fittings, ceiling-mounted fixtures, and sanitary ware set into the wall. The glazing must survive an impact without producing fragments that can cut or be swallowed, and here the physics of glass matters. Ordinary annealed glass breaks into large sharp shards, laminated glass holds its pieces on a plastic interlayer and stays in its frame, and polycarbonate does not shatter but deforms and scratches. The unbreakable pane is always a compromise, because the toughness that resists a fist also resists a rescue tool, and every glazed panel must be assessed for what it protects and what it prevents. The characteristic failure is a cracked pane reported and not replaced, because a crack becomes a cutting edge as it propagates, and the replacement criteria must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Furniture is designed against two threats at once, because a chair can be thrown and a bed can be barricaded. A chair that can become a weapon has a heavy frame, a removable leg, or a hard edge, and a chair that can barricade can be wedged under a handle or stacked against a doorway. The safe answer is furniture heavy, formed in one piece, and rounded, or furniture light enough to be harmless and unable to become a projectile. Beds follow the same rule, with a solid platform, no cavity for hidden objects, no exposed bolt to unscrew, and no rail for a line, while a mattress has no removable cover and no spring that can be pulled out. The common operator error is to bring an ordinary visitor chair in for a conversation and leave it there. Every item must therefore be checked for loose fasteners, torn covers, and new sharp edges on each maintenance round.

Everything described so far assumes that a human being is looking, and here observation is the practice that turns the room into a working device. Observation places a patient where a member of staff can see them at a defined interval, and its equipment is a sight line. The corridor is angled so a nurse at the desk can see into every room, and the panel, the window, and the light are arranged so a patient standing, sitting, or lying on the floor is visible from one point. The common operator error is to trust the plan on the drawing rather than the view at eye level, because a new notice board or a parked trolley can block a sight line an architect once verified. The characteristic failing is an observation sheet signed without the patient having been seen, and the observation interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Camera systems are the electronic form of observation, and they obey the optics of any lens. A camera has a field of view, a resolution, and a light sensitivity, and a wide field that covers a corridor leaves each face small, while a narrow field identifies a face and leaves blind spots behind a door and under a bed. Infrared illumination lets a camera see in darkness without a visible lamp, which is exactly why it can watch a sleeping patient without disturbing them. The safety hazard is the belief that the camera replaces the human round, because a lens cannot see a ligature being prepared in a space it does not cover. The common operator error is to reaim a camera to avoid a privacy complaint and to create a blind spot over the bed, and the retention period and the resolution setting must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the system is relied upon.

Alarms here are mostly about movement rather than physiology. A nurse call button is placed where it can be reached from the bed and the floor, is wired so a break in the circuit is itself an alarm, and is shaped so its cord cannot be removed and used as a ligature. Wander and exit alarms detect a patient crossing a doorway by a pressure mat, an infrared beam, or a radio tag, because an unsecured door is both a flight risk and a traffic risk. Bed and chair sensors detect a patient leaving a surface, and they serve those at risk of falling as well as those at risk of self-harm. The characteristic alarm is a repeating tone at the staff station, and the common operator error is to silence it at the panel instead of answering it at the room. Every alarm threshold, sensitivity setting, and delay must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, because a sensor tuned to ignore small movements will also ignore a patient who has stopped moving.

The seclusion room is the most heavily engineered space in the unit, and its purpose is to hold one person safely while a crisis passes. The room is stripped to the essential, with a bed fixed or set at floor level, a mattress that cannot be torn, a ligature-resistant toilet and washbasin, and a light behind a sealed diffuser. An observation panel and often a camera give a continuous view, and the door is a heavy anti-barricade leaf with a lock releasable from outside. The physical principle is that the fewer objects and edges a room contains, the smaller the number of ways a person can be hurt, and the clinical use is to reduce stimulation until the patient can return to the open ward. The physical dangers are not only self-harm, because a patient may fall from the bed, swallow a piece of the mattress, become dehydrated, or deteriorate from a medical cause mistaken for agitation, so the observation interval, the vital sign interval, and the maximum duration of seclusion must all be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The characteristic alarm is a silence that is too complete, and staff must learn to find that silence more alarming than noise.

Restraint equipment is used only when a person is at immediate risk, and every item in it is a compromise between control and harm. A soft limb holder is a cuff of padded fabric that spreads the pull of a limb over a wide area so the skin and nerves beneath it are not cut, while a rigid cuff concentrates the same force and injures. The mechanism of restraint injury is pressure and position, because a wrist restraint that is too tight compresses the median nerve and the artery beside it, and a patient left face down has the abdomen pressed into the mattress and the diaphragm unable to descend. Restraint asphyxia is the name of the death that follows, and it can happen quickly and quietly without a struggle, which is why position matters more than the tightness of any single strap. The common operator error is to secure a patient prone because it is the easiest way to control them, and the second error is to leave the room because the patient has gone quiet. Every restraint must be inspected for worn stitching, frayed webbing, a bent buckle, and a lock that does not turn, and the maximum continuous time in restraint must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Because the danger is positional, the check is positional before it is numerical. The patient must be turned so the chest and abdomen are free, the head is raised, and the airway is clear, and this is repeated often enough that any change is noticed. Vital signs are then recorded, including the pulse, the respiratory rate, the blood pressure, the oxygen saturation, and the color and warmth of each restrained limb. The circulation of a restrained wrist or ankle is judged by comparing color, warmth, and capillary refill with the other side, and a limb that is cool, pale, or numb means the restraint must be released and the finding reported. The characteristic alarm is a falling oxygen saturation or a rising respiratory rate in a patient who looks settled, and those numbers are a warning about position rather than a psychiatric change. Records must show the time, the position, and the findings at every check, and the check interval and the trigger values must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The safety hazard is documentation that describes a check that never happened, because it conceals the only evidence that a patient was deteriorating.

Everything that can be swallowed, burned, or used as a cord must be removed from a room where a patient is at risk. The list is longer than a glance suggests, and it includes matches and lighters, plastic bags, batteries, small magnets, coins, glass, razors, scissors, needles, medications, alcohol-based hand rub, aerosol cans, and cleaning fluids. Shoelaces, belts, drawstrings, scarves, and the cords of headphones and chargers are ligature material, and the physical principle is that an ingestible object becomes a weapon once it is swallowed while a cord becomes a noose once one end is tied above the body. The common operator error is to search a room thoroughly at admission and never again, although patients accumulate objects over the days that follow. Searching is itself an equipment task, because a hand search depends on a written list and on consistency, while a walk-through metal detector finds only a metallic object in a pocket. The safety hazard is the false sense of completeness a detector creates, because glass, plastic, ceramic, and bone are invisible to it, and the sensitivity setting and the test procedure must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Lighting and power are part of the safety equipment, not merely the comfort of the room. Light fittings are sealed behind diffusers so no lamp or cable can be reached, and the emergency circuit keeps a corridor lit when the mains supply fails, which is the moment a dark ward becomes most dangerous. A corridor at night needs enough light for a nurse to see a patient from the doorway while remaining dim enough for sleep, so the lighting level must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. An uninterruptible power supply protects the nurse call system, the door alarms, and the records, and the batteries of every portable alarm and torch must be tested on a schedule. Water and sanitation carry their own mechanisms, because an anti-ligature tap has a smooth body, a short lever, and no exposed pipe, and a thermostatic mixing valve holds a fixed outlet temperature so a patient cannot be scalded, with that temperature confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the valve is set. The safety hazard is a socket outlet inside a patient room, and the characteristic alarms of these services are a door alarm that does not sound when the door opens and a bathroom that has gone quiet.

Not all equipment here is defensive, because therapeutic activity equipment gives the patient a way to discharge tension and rebuild the routine of a day. Its design must satisfy the therapeutic purpose and the safety rules at once, and that double requirement decides every purchase. Art materials must be washable, non-toxic, and free of solvent, and the shared scissors and needles of a sewing group must be counted out, counted back, and locked away like any other sharp. Sensory items, including weighted blankets, textured fabrics, soft balls, and rocking seats, work through the physiology of the nervous system, because deep pressure and rhythmic movement stimulate the large sensory fibers and the balance organs, which is thought to calm the arousal that drives agitation. Weighted blankets must be light enough that a patient cannot be trapped beneath one, and the weight and the supervision required must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The common operator error is to leave a well-intentioned craft material or a weighted item in a room overnight, where it becomes the material of the next crisis.

Outdoor space is therapeutic equipment made of ground. A garden, a courtyard, or a secure terrace provides daylight, fresh air, and the change of scene that lowers agitation, and access to it is one of the most effective measures in the unit that involves no medication at all. Designing it safely means applying the rules of the room to the open air, so there is no cord, no high anchor point, no climbable wall, and no tool left in a shed. The characteristic alarm of an outdoor area is a gate signal or a missing patient report, and the gate release and the supervision ratio must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The safety hazard is garden equipment such as a hose, a rope, a spade, or a watering can left outside because it seems harmless in daylight. The operator check before any group goes out is a slow walk of the ground that removes everything a patient could tie, climb, or swallow.

De-escalation is the practice that makes most of the equipment unnecessary, and it is worth describing as a technique rather than a virtue. The physical principle is that agitation rises when a person feels trapped, unheard, or overwhelmed by noise and light, and it falls when the environment becomes predictable and the person regains a measure of control. A clinician who speaks slowly, stands at an angle rather than face to face, keeps the hands visible, offers a choice, and moves to a quieter space is adjusting the same variables the room was designed to control, only faster. The common operator error is to begin with the alarm and the restraint team and to treat talking as the delay before the real intervention, when talking is the intervention and the restraint team is the failure. The characteristic alarm is a raised voice or a sudden change in the pace of speech, and it should trigger an environmental change and a second staff member rather than a stronger physical response.

Documentation is equipment in this field, because the observation chart, the risk assessment, and the restraint record carry a patient's safety from one shift to the next. The chart must be simple enough to complete at the bedside and complete enough to show the time, the position, the behavior, and the vital signs at each check. The risk assessment must name the specific hazard for the specific patient, because a form that says only that the patient is at risk of self-harm tells the next nurse nothing that can be acted upon. The common operator error is to copy the previous entry, which turns the record into fiction and hides a change a fresh observation would have caught, and the characteristic alarm of a failed system is a discrepancy between what the chart claims and what the camera shows.

Maintenance in this environment is a safety activity that looks like housekeeping. The routine round checks every ligature point in every room, which means the door top, the handle, the hinge, the hook, the rail, the tap, the shower fitting, the light, the bed frame, the mattress seam, and the edge of every panel. A fixture is tested by loading it and by looking at it, because a fixing loosened by repeated dynamic load may still look intact, and the inspection interval and the test load must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Glazing is examined for cracks and failed seals, and a cracked pane is replaced rather than patched, while door closers are adjusted so a leaf does not slam and a lock that sticks during an emergency is a hazard of its own. A maintenance record matters as much as the maintenance, and the common failure is a log signed as complete while a new coat hook has been installed by a contractor. The operator must also watch for the quiet change, whether it is a new notice board or a new bedside table, because each of those can reintroduce the anchor point the room was built to remove.

Cleaning and disinfection here serve infection control and searching at the same time. The cleaning chemical must be effective against the organisms of the ward while remaining safe for the surfaces in use, and the concentration and the contact time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. An alcohol-based product left in a room is a flammable hazard as well as an ingestible one. Consumables are deliberately few, and that is a design decision rather than a shortage, because paper towels, soap, laundry, and incontinence pads are each a potential plug, a potential cord, or a potential fire load. A bin liner is a suffocation hazard, so a bin is small, fixed to the wall, and emptied at every round instead of being lined with a loose bag. Bed linen is checked for tears because a torn sheet can be twisted into a cord, and restraint padding, replacement webbing, spare buckles, and breakaway rings of the hook-and-loop type are stocked, while the common operator error is to leave a cleaning trolley unattended in a corridor, where it offers both a chemical and a cord.

Failure modes in this field often take the form of a system that works exactly as designed and is still wrong. A camera clean and focused can be aimed at the wrong part of the room, an alarm that sounds correctly can be silenced at the panel, a correctly fitted restraint can still leave a patient prone, and a correctly locked door can keep help outside when it is needed inside. The deeper failure is organizational, because a ligature-resistant room can be defeated by one new object, a careful policy by one rushed shift, and a written risk assessment by a patient whose presentation has changed since it was made. The characteristic alarm of an organizational failure is a near miss that nobody reports, and a near miss is the cheapest warning a unit will ever receive. The physical failure that must never be missed is a cracked panel, a loosened fixing, or a missing screw, because those are the quiet beginnings of the next serious event, and the reporting threshold and the review interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The field variant of this chapter is not a portable machine, because it is an ordinary room in a general ward, a health center, or a tent in which an extraordinary risk has arrived. There is no anti-ligature hardware, no observation panel, no camera, and no seclusion room, so the nurse must build the unit out of what the building already holds. The work begins with a walk through the room at the height of a patient, taking down every projection that can be taken down, because an ordinary ward is full of cords, poles, and lockers that nobody has had reason to see as a risk. The patient is then moved near the staff station where a nurse can see the whole room, and every cord, belt, shoelace, scarf, and charger is removed and handed to the family or locked away. The bed is lowered or the mattress is placed on the floor, the frame is inspected for a projecting bolt, hook, or rail, and a family member or trained attendant stays in the room, with the shorter check interval for that patient confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

What the field version gives up is the certainty of the engineered room, because it loses the tested fixing, the sealed light, the locked door, the camera, and the alarm, and it replaces all of them with attention from a workforce that is present and a plan shared with the family. A low-resource unit can improvise the hardware with cheap material, using a smooth board over a projecting bracket, a shortened curtain with breakaway rings of the hook-and-loop type, a plastic chair instead of a metal one, and a mattress without a removable cover. The field unit must also hold the medical equipment a behavioral emergency can demand, because a crisis can end in a seizure, a head injury, an overdose, or the shallow breathing that follows sedation. Oxygen, a self-inflating bag with a mask, suction, a glucose meter, and a means of measuring the pulse and blood pressure must be within reach of the room, and if a sedative is given the dose and observation interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The common operator error is to give a sedative and then leave the patient alone because the ward has gone quiet, and the characteristic alarm is snoring respiration in a patient who was shouting a moment before.

The physical space of a field unit has to be chosen before it can be adapted. A tent offers no ceiling anchor and no lock, so the risk shifts to the frame, the guy ropes, the mosquito net, and the tent poles, all of which must be examined with the same eye that would examine a door, while a general ward offers lockers, bed rails, window frames, and curtain tracks that can be removed, lowered, or covered. Lighting matters in the field as much as in the hospital, because a lamp, a torch, and a candle are all sources of fire and of cord, and a battery lantern hung beyond reach is safer than a kerosene lamp on the floor. The family presence that substitutes for an observation panel must be explained, because a relative who does not understand the task will sleep through the event they are there to prevent. Training and rehearsal belong in the same inventory, because a team that has rehearsed a restraint, a seclusion, and a medical emergency will use less force and recognize deterioration sooner, with the frequency of the rehearsal confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

To summarize, a mental health unit is a building engineered against the physics of the cord, and its equipment is a set of deliberate absences: no anchor above the body, no cord within reach, no edge that cuts, no door that can be barricaded, and no blind spot that hides a patient from the people responsible for them. The hospital version invests in ligature-resistant fixtures, laminated glazing, anti-barricade doors with vision panels, cameras and movement alarms, a stripped seclusion room, restraint equipment with its positional checks, and the therapeutic materials that give a patient another way to spend a crisis. The field version gives up the tested hardware and buys back safety with presence, a room stripped by hand, a mattress on the floor, a family member at the bedside, and a schedule that is actually kept. In both versions the decisive instrument is the same, because the equipment can only remove the means and the staff must remove the opportunity, and every interval, load, and threshold mentioned in this chapter must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.
