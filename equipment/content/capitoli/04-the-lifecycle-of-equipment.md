# Chapter 4 — The Lifecycle of Equipment

A machine in a hospital is not an event but a biography. It begins as a need, becomes a budget line, arrives in a crate, is bolted to a floor, is used by hands that did not choose it, and ends as scrap or as a spare part inside another machine. Most equipment failures are not electronic failures at all but failures of supply, training, maintenance, and documentation. Wear itself is physical: surfaces rub, seals harden, solder joints fatigue, batteries lose capacity, and sensors drift as their materials age. Corrosion eats contacts, dust blocks filters, heat cooks the electrolyte, and moisture finds every crack. The discipline that follows a device from first idea to final disposal is called the lifecycle.

The first stage is not procurement but an honest recognition of a need. Someone must ask what problem the machine solves, how often that problem occurs, who will operate it, who will repair it, and what happens on the day it breaks. The real question is never only whether a clinic can afford to buy a machine, but whether it can sustain one. A hospital-grade device assumes a building that can feed it: stable electricity at the correct voltage and frequency, clean water, drainage, compressed air, vacuum, and a supply chain that delivers filters and reagents on time. Before writing a specification, the buyer should walk the route the machine will take and check each of those requirements. A need that cannot be sustained is a future piece of furniture, and the graveyards behind clinics are full of machines that answered a question nobody had asked carefully.

For every device there is a second device hidden inside it, and that second device is the consumable. An analyzer is really a pump, a lamp, and a set of reagents; an ultrasound machine is a console, a probe, and a bottle of coupling gel; a ventilator is a turbine, a circuit, a filter, and a humidifier chamber. The purchase price is only the entrance fee, and the true cost of ownership is paid over the whole life of the machine in consumables, service, calibration, electricity, water, staff time, and downtime. That cost is estimated by listing every expense the device will generate in a year and dividing it by the number of patients it will actually serve. A cheap device with expensive consumables can cost more per patient than an expensive device with cheap ones. A machine that is too expensive to run is a machine that is not available, however modern it looks on arrival.

Procurement begins with a specification written around the clinical need rather than around a catalog. It should state what the device must measure or do, with what accuracy and over what range, in what environment, and by whom it will be operated. It should name the consumables, the service, the training, the documentation, and the warranty, including the response time for a breakdown. It should describe the electrical supply, whether single phase or 3 phase, at what voltage and frequency, because a machine built for 60 hertz will not behave correctly on a 50 hertz supply, a requirement that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before purchase and again before the first patient. It should state the gas connections and cylinder fittings, so that they match what the hospital actually has. A specification written in the language of the clinic rather than the salesman is the buyer's only real protection.

The tender is the formal contest in which suppliers offer to meet that specification, and it is where good intentions are most often lost. Evaluation must compare not only the offered price but the offered life: the consumable pack, the service visit, the spare parts, the distance to the nearest engineer, and the years the maker promises to keep supplying. A showroom demonstration proves very little, because a device can perform beautifully on a stable bench and fail in a dusty room on an unstable supply. The buyer should ask for the service history of the model and the name of a reference site where it has worked for several years. The lowest price is not the lowest cost, and the machine nobody can service is the most expensive machine in the building.

The field variant of procurement inverts almost every one of those assumptions. A mobile or frontier clinic usually cannot host a tender, cannot demand a reference site, and cannot wait for a service visit. It must therefore choose devices that are simple, robust, repairable with common tools, and tolerant of dust, heat, humidity, and unstable power. What it needs is not maximum capability but minimum complexity: fewer moving parts, fewer proprietary connectors, and fewer sealed modules that cannot be opened. It should prefer devices that run on a widely available battery format, that charge from a solar panel, and that can be repaired locally rather than returned to a capital city. The field buyer is really buying a relationship with a supply chain, and the machine is only the visible part of that relationship.

Acceptance testing begins the moment the crate is opened, and its purpose is to prove that the device that arrived is the device that was ordered and that it works as promised. A machine travels by ship, truck, and hand, so it may have been dropped, stored in the rain, built for a different voltage, or configured in a way nobody intended. The test verifies the model and accessories against the purchase order, inspects the housing, cables, connectors, and seals, and confirms that the manual, the certificate, and the service contact are present. The device is then powered up and made to perform a known measurement against a known reference, so that a reading can be trusted rather than merely believed. Electrical safety belongs here too, because the protective earth must be intact and the leakage current within the limits the manufacturer states and the local standard requires.

Installation places the device in the environment it will live in, and commissioning proves that device and environment work together. The room must supply what the machine demands: the correct circuit and grounding, the correct gas outlet or cylinder connection, adequate ventilation, a stable temperature, acceptable humidity, and enough space for the operator to work and reach the patient. A large imaging device may also need floor reinforcement, radiation shielding, cooling, and a dedicated power feed, and each requirement must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the room is prepared. Heavy equipment should be anchored, and cables routed so that nobody trips and no bed crushes them. Commissioning includes the first full function test in place, the setting of language and units, and entry into the equipment register. The moment to discover that the doorway is too narrow is before the machine is inside the room.

Inventory is the memory of the hospital, and without it no maintenance system can exist. Every device should carry a unique identifier that survives cleaning, linked in a register to its description, maker, serial number, location, custodian, purchase date, warranty, and full service history. A device without a number cannot be found, cannot be scheduled, and cannot be proven to have been maintained. The register also reveals the pattern of loss: pumps that migrate to another department, monitors that are borrowed and never returned, and accessories thrown away with the packaging. A hospital-grade register is usually electronic, searchable, and reconciled by physical count at intervals, with every discrepancy investigated. A field clinic can keep the same discipline with a paper ledger, a painted number, and a monthly walk through the rooms. What cannot be found cannot be maintained.

Training is not an accessory to the device but one of its components, and a machine handed over without it is only half delivered. Its purpose is to build a working mental model in the operator, so the person understands what the device is doing, what normal looks and sounds like, and what early failure looks like. A good handover covers the start-up sequence, the daily checks, the meaning of the settings, the alarms and their correct responses, the cleaning that is allowed, and the faults that require a technician rather than a nurse. It should be practical, repeated, and recorded, with a competency check rather than a signature on an attendance sheet. At least one person on each shift should be a designated superuser who can teach others and who knows whom to call. A device is only as safe as the least trained person who will be left alone with it.

Calibration is the process that keeps a measurement honest. Its physical basis is comparison: an instrument is made to measure a known reference, and the difference between what it reads and what the reference states is recorded, so that its error becomes known and can be corrected. The reference must itself be traceable, meaning it has been compared, step by step, with a national or international standard, and each comparison is documented. Drift is why calibration is repeated, because sensors age, amplifiers wander, mechanical parts wear, and the link between a physical quantity and an electrical signal slowly changes. Calibration is not verification, which only checks that a device still reads within tolerance, and it is not adjustment, which changes the device to restore agreement. Intervals are set by the manufacturer and by the risk of the device, and each interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before it is relied upon.

The devices that most need calibration are those whose numbers become decisions. Sphygmomanometers, thermometers, infusion pumps, defibrillators, gas analyzers, laboratory analyzers, and dialysis machines all belong to this group, and each has its own reference and tolerance. A defibrillator is checked by delivering a stored charge into a test load and measuring the energy in joules, and the acceptable error must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is cleared for use. An infusion pump is checked by measuring the volume it actually delivers over a set time, because a pump that under-delivers silently changes every dose in the ward. The general rule is that the more directly a number drives a drug dose or an energy delivery, the more often it must be questioned.

The field variant of calibration is a compromise openly made rather than a failure hidden. Where no traceable reference exists, a clinic can still cross-check one device against another, keep a dedicated reference instrument that is never used on patients, and send that instrument to a regional laboratory for periodic comparison. Simple physical checks remain valuable: a thermometer against the freezing and boiling behavior of water at the local altitude, a scale against a known mass, and a pressure cuff against a second cuff and a careful manual reading. The limits of these substitutes must be written down, because a cross-check between two equally drifted instruments proves only that they agree. An uncalibrated device used knowingly is a named risk, while the same device used unknowingly is a trap.

Preventive maintenance is the work done before a fault becomes a failure, and its logic comes from the way machines wear. Many components fail on a curve that begins with early failure, settles into a long plateau, and then rises sharply as wear accumulates, and replacing the wearing parts on schedule keeps the device on that plateau. Preventive maintenance includes inspection, cleaning, lubrication, tightening, testing, and replacement of parts with a known life, such as filters, seals, tubing, batteries, lamps, and probe covers. It includes the functional test of every alarm, because an alarm that has never sounded is an untested safety system and tends to be found silent on the worst possible day. The interval is set by the manufacturer and the intensity of use, and it must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol, since a device used continuously needs attention far sooner than one used occasionally. The common operator error is postponing maintenance while the machine is busy, which is precisely when wear accumulates fastest.

Failure modes cluster in the places the designer could not protect. Cables break where they bend, connectors corrode where liquid collects, seals leak where they are compressed, filters block where dust is drawn in, and circuit boards fail where heat has nowhere to go. A battery is a consumable that looks like a permanent part: its capacity falls with every cycle, it ages even when unused, and it fails fastest at high temperature and at full charge. A device always left plugged in may carry a battery that has never been exercised, and the first time it is needed on transport it may fail within minutes. The operator's contribution to reliability is not repair but early reporting, because a new noise, a new smell, or a slightly different reading is the cheapest warning a device will ever give. The safety hazard here is the gradual and invisible decline that leaves the machine looking exactly as it always did.

Every device has a short list of things the operator must check before it touches a patient, and that list belongs on the machine or in the log rather than in memory. The check begins with the physical state: plug, cable, housing, connectors, probe, cuff, tubing, and seals, looking for cracks, exposed conductors, discoloration, moisture, or residue from the last cleaning. It continues with power and self-test, battery charge, the presence and expiry date of consumables, and the correct selection of patient size or operating mode. Alarm limits should be set deliberately for this patient and provoked once to prove they sound, because a threshold that is not confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol is only a hope. Settings left from the previous patient are a frequent and dangerous error, especially on ventilators, pumps, and monitors, where the wrong mode can be delivered with complete confidence. If anything fails the check, the device must be removed, tagged, and reported, and the temptation to use it just this once must be treated as the hazard it is.

Characteristic alarms teach an operator what a device is worried about, and each one has a correct response. A low battery alarm means the machine is warning before it stops, and the answer is to connect it rather than to silence it. An occlusion alarm on an infusion pump means the pressure in the line has risen, and the answer is to examine the line, the cannula, and the clamp rather than press start repeatedly. An air-in-line alarm means the sensor has seen a bubble, so the line must be stopped and the air removed before restarting. A sensor fault or a calibration due alarm means the measurement itself is suspect, and the machine is telling the truth about its own limits. The common operator error with every alarm is silencing it and continuing, which converts a warning into an unnoticed event.

Cleaning and reprocessing belong to the lifecycle because they are the most frequent form of wear a device will ever experience. The physical principle is that soil, salt, and protein must be removed before any disinfectant can work, because organic material shields the organisms beneath it and consumes the chemical meant to kill them. Cleaning therefore precedes disinfection, and disinfection precedes sterilization where sterilization is required, and each step must reach every surface that will contact the patient. An instrument that will enter sterile tissue must be cleaned, inspected, and sterilized, often in a steam cycle at 134 degrees Celsius for several minutes, a cycle that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The materials constrain what may be applied, because alcohol cracks some plastics, chlorine corrodes metal and ruins some seals, and water enters anything not sealed against it. Every device has an ingress rating and a list of approved agents, and these must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before any liquid is applied.

The biomedical engineering department is the institution's memory and skill for all of this work. It receives new equipment, performs acceptance testing, maintains the register, schedules preventive maintenance, calibrates what it can and contracts what it cannot, repairs what is repairable, investigates incidents, trains users, manages spare parts, and recommends decommissioning. It usually keeps a small equipment library of pumps, monitors, and beds that can be lent during a repair, which is one of the cheapest ways to reduce the clinical cost of downtime. Its most valuable product is not the repair but the trend: the model that fails repeatedly, the cleaning method that damages a surface, and the training gap that keeps producing the same user error. A hospital without this function does not avoid the work, because it distributes the work invisibly among nurses who have no time for it.

A clinic without a biomedical engineering department must build a substitute, and the substitute can be surprisingly effective if it is deliberate. The usual arrangement is a named person with a written role, a basic toolkit, a multimeter, a maintenance schedule pinned to the wall, and a telephone relationship with a regional workshop or the maker's service line. Consumable parts and the most common spares should be held locally, because a journey of 200 kilometers for a fuse is a day of lost care. Simple tasks can be delegated to trained users: cleaning, the alarm test, the battery check, cable inspection, and recording faults in a log. More complex work should be sent out early rather than attempted late, because an amateur repair on a device that delivers energy or measures a dose can turn a fault into an injury.

Repair against replacement is a decision made on evidence rather than sentiment. The questions are whether the repair restores the device to its specified performance, whether parts are available in useful time, whether the cost is a sensible fraction of an equivalent replacement, whether the device is still clinically needed, and whether the maker or regulator has withdrawn it for safety. A device becomes unsafe to keep when its failure could harm a patient and cannot be reliably repaired: cracked insulation, an alarm that no longer sounds, a radiation output beyond tolerance, a pressure vessel past its test date, a swelling battery, or a fault the maker will never correct. Such a device must be removed immediately, tagged with the reason and date, and stored where it cannot quietly return to the ward. The temptation in a low-resource setting is to keep a marginal device alive, and that temptation must be named as a hazard, because the machine that is almost working is often more dangerous than the one that is clearly broken. A repaired device must be tested against its specification before returning to use.

Decommissioning is the orderly end of the biography, and it begins with a decision that is written down. The device is removed from the active register, its location and final state recorded, and any patient data it holds erased according to the rules that govern the electronic health record and the local privacy law. Its special contents must be handled before the metal: batteries, mercury switches and thermometers, refrigerants, compressed gas cylinders, oils, and any sealed radioactive source, each with its own route and record. A radiation source cannot simply be sold or dumped, and the responsible authority must be involved before anything moves. Useful parts may be harvested, manuals and accessories kept as spares for surviving units, and the remainder sent to electronic waste recycling or another licensed route. The removal must be complete, because a decommissioned device left in a corridor will eventually be plugged in by someone who does not know why it was retired.

Cannibalization is the practice of taking parts from one device to keep another alive, and it is neither shameful nor safe by itself. It is rational when a model is out of production, the part cannot be bought, and the donor device is beyond repair, because the alternative is two dead machines instead of one working one. It becomes dangerous when the donor is merely inconvenient, when worn parts are moved into a working machine, when the swap changes calibration without verification, and when the change is not written down. A cannibalized device may pass every function test and still be unreliable, because the transplanted part carries the wear of its previous life and the machine has lost the traceability that proves it was assembled as designed. The safe version keeps a controlled store of harvested parts, pairs each with its donor and condition, and verifies the function and calibration of the recipient after the swap. In a field clinic cannibalization is often the only repair available, which makes discipline around it more important and not less.

Disposal is the last physical stage, and it is a clinical and environmental act rather than a housekeeping one. Devices that have contacted blood, body fluids, or infectious material must be decontaminated before they leave the building, and the waste they generate must be separated into the streams local rules define: general, infectious, sharps, chemical, pharmaceutical, and radiological. Electronic waste must not be burned in the open, because the plastics release toxic fumes and the batteries can ignite, and a lithium battery crushed in a landfill is a fire waiting for a reason. Metals can be recovered, and in some settings that recovery is a livelihood, which makes removing hazardous components before sale a matter of public safety. A device that disappears without a record has not been disposed of, because it has only been lost.

Donation is one of the largest sources of equipment in low-resource settings and one of the largest sources of unsustainability. The failures are consistent and well known: the device arrives without consumables, without a manual, without a service history, and without anyone trained to use it. It may be built for a different electrical supply, gas fitting, cylinder valve, language, or clinical practice, and it may already be at the end of its supported life when shipped. The receiving clinic often pays transport, customs duty, and storage, so a gift can cost more than a purchase and deliver less. A clinic that accepts a donation should inspect it before it is sent, require the documents and a service record, identify the consumables and their supplier, and confirm that someone can maintain it. It is entirely legitimate to refuse a donation that cannot be sustained, and to ask instead for the consumable budget that would make an existing device work.

The field variant compresses every stage of the lifecycle and places it in the hands of a few people. Acceptance testing becomes a careful inspection on a table, because there is no engineering department to call, and the person who unpacks the device must check the voltage setting, the battery, the accessories, and the basic function before the crate is discarded. The register is a notebook or a card taped inside the carrying case, listing the device, its number, its faults, and the name of whoever fixed them. Preventive maintenance is a daily and weekly ritual performed by the user, and the person who cleans the device is the one most likely to notice the crack, the noise, and the failing battery. Repair is tiered: the user changes what the manual allows, a district technician handles the next level, and the device travels to the capital only when nothing else can be done. In this setting the lifecycle is not a department but a habit.

Across every stage, 3 failures repeat themselves, and naming them is the purpose of this chapter. The first is the machine used without being understood, which happens when training is treated as optional and the manual is written in a language the operator does not read. The second is the machine maintained only after it fails, which converts a cheap scheduled part into an expensive repair and a period of unavailable care. The third is the machine nobody owns, which drifts between rooms, loses its accessories, and is discovered with a fault no one reported. The characteristic alarm of a neglected device is rarely dramatic; it is the low battery, the calibration due, and the sensor fault that everyone has learned to dismiss. The operator error joining all three is the assumption that a machine which turns on is a machine that can be trusted.

Documentation is what turns a collection of machines into a managed system, and it is also what protects patients when something goes wrong. The equipment register, maintenance log, calibration certificate, acceptance test record, training record, and disposal record together form the legal and clinical history of the device, and they are the first things requested after an incident. That history also makes recalls possible, because a manufacturer's safety notice can be acted on only if the affected serial numbers and their locations are known. In a hospital these records live in a maintenance management system and increasingly connect to the electronic health record. In a field clinic the same records live on paper, in a bound book that stays with the equipment. The deepest point of the lifecycle is that maintenance is clinical care performed in advance, because the nurse who checks the suction before the airway is needed is practicing medicine.

To summarize, the life of a medical device runs from recognition of a need through specification, procurement, acceptance, installation, training, inventory, calibration, preventive maintenance, repair, and finally decommissioning and disposal, and every stage determines whether the machine will be safe when a patient needs it. The hospital-grade device depends on infrastructure, consumables, documentation, and a biomedical engineering department, while the field variant depends on simplicity, repairability, local skill, and a supply chain that can actually reach it. The physical realities are wear, drift, corrosion, battery aging, and contamination, and the human realities are operator error, alarm fatigue, skipped maintenance, and the machine nobody owns. The operator's own responsibility is the pre-use check: cables, housing, battery, consumables, correct settings, and an alarm provoked and heard. Every setting, interval, temperature, and threshold named in this chapter must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, because no number in a book can replace the document that belongs to the machine.
