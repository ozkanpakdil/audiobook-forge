# Chapter 51 — Neurology and Neurosurgery

The brain and spinal cord are electrical organs wrapped in bone, and almost every device in this chapter exists because that electricity can be reached from outside. A nerve cell holds about 70 thousandths of a volt across its membrane at rest. When it fires, ions cross the membrane and that voltage collapses for about 1 thousandth of a second. Those currents reach the scalp as fields of a few millionths of a volt, far weaker than a heartbeat on a chest electrocardiogram. Neurodiagnostics is therefore a craft of amplification and noise rejection, while neurosurgery is a craft of opening the skull safely and managing the pressure inside.

Electroencephalography, or EEG, records the summed electrical activity of millions of cortical neurons through small silver discs held on the scalp with paste or gel. The trace is a murmur of a population rather than a picture of one cell, and its value lies in its rhythm, its symmetry between the hemispheres, and its response to provocation. Placement follows the ten to twenty system, measured from the bridge of the nose to the bony bump at the back of the skull, with 20 per cent steps along the midline and 10 per cent across the side. The contact quality, called impedance, the abrasion technique, and the maximum time an electrode may stay in place must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Good impedance is low and, just as importantly, similar from electrode to electrode, because a single high-resistance electrode makes a channel that looks like slow activity or a flat line. The amplifier is a differential amplifier, measuring the difference between two nearby electrodes and rejecting the voltage common to both, which is how noise from mains wiring and fluorescent lights is largely cancelled. A low-frequency filter removes slow drift from sweating, a high-frequency filter removes muscle activity, and a narrow notch filter removes the 50 hertz hum of mains power. The gain, the notch width, and the impedance balance tolerance must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A montage is the pattern of connections that decides which electrodes are compared with which. Referential montages compare each electrode with a common reference, while bipolar montages compare adjacent electrodes in a chain, and the same discharge can look focal in one and diffuse in the other. Filters set too aggressively will remove a genuine slow wave along with the artifact they were meant to remove. The filter corners and the montage selection must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Long studies turn a recording into a monitoring system. For a video EEG the electrodes are fixed with an adhesive so they survive days of movement, and a camera watches the patient continuously while the record is synchronized with the image. The patient is usually admitted to a monitored bed with padded rails, because the purpose of the study is to capture seizures and the seizure is the hazard. Nursing checks include electrode impedances, skin condition under the adhesive, the camera position, and the working state of the event button. The characteristic error is an interface error, since paste squeezed between two electrodes bridges them and creates a phantom rhythm, so the impedance limit and the electrode-fail alarm threshold must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Evoked potential equipment measures the brain's answer to a deliberate stimulus that is far too small to see in a single trial. The amplifier records the same brief window after each of hundreds of identical stimuli, and a computer adds the windows together so random noise cancels while the time-locked response grows, a technique called averaging. The averaged waveform has named peaks whose latency in thousandths of a second and amplitude carry the information, and visual, auditory, and somatosensory stimuli test different pathways. During spinal and skull base surgery the same equipment warns that a maneuver is stretching a neural pathway before permanent injury occurs, which demands a baseline before incision and a defined response when the signal changes. The stimulus intensity, the averaging count, and the alarm criteria must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Electromyography, or EMG, listens to muscle rather than to brain, and a needle electrode records the activity of the fibers it touches while the amplifier drives a display and a loudspeaker. The ear is good at telling the crisp crackle of a healthy contracting muscle from the sparse ticking of a diseased one. Nerve conduction studies pass a controlled current through the skin over a nerve and record the response further along, and the time from stimulus to response together with the distance gives a conduction velocity in meters per second. The stimulus is raised until the response stops growing and a cold limb is warmed first, so the needle size, the warming target, and the stimulus duration must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Transcranial Doppler uses sound to measure the speed of blood in the arteries at the base of the brain, a small probe emitting ultrasound at about 2 megahertz and listening for the echo from moving red cells. The difference between the emitted and returning frequency is called the Doppler shift, and it is proportional to the speed of the blood. The probe sits over the thin temporal bone window above the cheekbone, and the examiner searches at increasing depth for the middle cerebral artery, usually found between 45 and 60 millimeters deep. Because the technique is blind, vessels are identified by depth, direction of flow, and response to compression of the carotid artery in the neck. The probe frequency, the depth range, and the velocity threshold that triggers concern must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Transcranial Doppler is used most often after bleeding around the brain, when arteries can narrow in spasm and the narrowing appears as a rising velocity before the patient deteriorates. The study is entirely operator dependent, so two examiners can report two different numbers unless the technique is standardized. The characteristic operator error is over-interpretation, mistaking a normal variant or a high flow state such as anemia for vasospasm. The velocity criteria and the repeat interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The skull is a rigid box containing brain, blood, and cerebrospinal fluid, and because it cannot expand, an increase in one must be balanced by a decrease in another. This explains why a slowly growing mass can cause no pressure rise for a long time while a small hemorrhage can be rapid and fatal. The compensating reserve is used up first, and once it is exhausted the pressure rises steeply with each additional milliliter. Normal intracranial pressure is often quoted as 5 to 15 millimeters of mercury in an adult lying flat, and the values that require treatment must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The classic way to measure that pressure is a catheter passed through a burr hole into the fluid-filled ventricle, connected to tubing that transmits pressure to an external transducer through a column of sterile fluid that must have an unobstructed path and no bubbles. The transducer is leveled to a fixed anatomical reference, usually the opening of the ear canal, and zeroed to the atmosphere through a stopcock before the reading is trusted. A parenchymal monitor instead places a strain gauge or fiberoptic diaphragm in the brain tissue and is zeroed before insertion, because it cannot be re-zeroed once inside the skull. That design removes the leveling and bubble problems and is less likely to become infected than an open drain, but it cannot drain fluid and its readings drift over several days. The probe is often fixed through a hollow bolt, so the zeroing procedure and the drift limit must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The external ventricular drain is the same ventricular catheter used both to measure pressure and to remove cerebrospinal fluid, and its regulation is pure hydrostatics. Fluid flows from the ventricle to a collecting chamber held at a chosen height above the patient's reference point, so raising the chamber raises the pressure at which drainage begins and lowering it lowers that pressure. A column of water about 1.4 centimeters high produces a pressure of 1 millimeter of mercury, and the chamber is usually set between 5 and 20 centimeters above the reference point. The exact height, the reference landmark, and the drainage volume target must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The dangers are infection, over-drainage, under-drainage, blockage, and disconnection, and every day the catheter lies in the brain adds to the risk of ventriculitis. Over-drainage pulls the brain away from the skull and can tear the bridging veins, while under-drainage lets pressure climb while the patient becomes drowsy.

The quiet, lethal error is a change in the reference level that nobody notices, as when the head of the bed is raised and the chamber is not moved with it. At each shift the nurse confirms that the stopcocks are in the correct position, that the tubing is unkinked and unclamped, that the chamber sits at the marked height, and that the insertion site shows no leak or redness. Pressure is measured with the drain temporarily clamped and the drain is reopened after the measurement, because a drain left clamped can raise pressure dangerously. During transport the drain is usually clamped so the chamber position cannot drive fluid in or out. The period of clamping and the person responsible for reopening it must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Neuronavigation links the images of the patient to the patient on the table, with a computer holding the scans and a camera or magnetic field locator tracking a pointer and the patient's head. Optical systems use infrared light and reflective spheres and demand a clear line of sight, while electromagnetic systems use a field generator near the head and are disturbed by metal. Registration is usually done by touching the pointer to markers stuck on the skin when the scan was taken, and the reported accuracy of a couple of millimeters is only as good as that registration. The limitation the display does not advertise is that the scan was taken before the skull was opened, and once air and fluid are lost the brain moves inside the skull, so the pointer can be accurate at the skin and misleading deep in the field. The registration method and the acceptable accuracy limit must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A stereotactic frame establishes a coordinate system in which any point in the head is described by three numbers measured from the frame itself, which is fixed rigidly to the skull with pins. The target is chosen on a computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging study performed with the frame in place. The arc is then set so that a probe entering at the chosen point reaches the target along a line the surgeon has reviewed. Verification is done with a phantom or against known points before the patient is touched, so the pin torque, the scanner compatibility, and the verification method must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The operating microscope puts a bright, magnified, three-dimensional view exactly where the hands are working, through two separate light paths that converge at the field. Magnification changes by swapping the objective lens, which also changes working distance, or by a motorized zoom, while illumination travels along the same axis as the viewing path so the field is lit without shadows at the bottom of a narrow corridor. The microscope floats on a balanced articulated arm and is moved by handles, a foot pedal, or a mouth switch when the hands are busy. The concentrated beam can scorch a drape or burn tissue if the instrument is left focused with nothing in the field, so the light is shuttered whenever the surgeon is not looking. The lenses are cleaned only with the solvent the maker specifies, so the magnification range and the illumination level must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The cranial drill is a high-speed motor that turns a small cutting tool at tens of thousands of revolutions per minute, running on compressed air or nitrogen through a hose, or on an electric motor. A perforator burr has a clutch that disengages when it breaks through the inner table, a cutting burr enlarges the hole and shapes bone, and a craniotome has a footplate that slips under the bone and lifts the dura away so a flap can be cut. Diamond burrs cut slowly and are used near delicate structures because they do not grab and tear soft tissue. The rotational speed, the air pressure or electric current, and the irrigation flow must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A drill is the most dangerous hand tool in the set, so the burr is tested spinning in the air before it touches the patient and no glove, cloth, or finger may approach it.

Moisture in the air line, a worn bearing, or over-tightened jaws make the tool wobble or slip, a dull burr generates heat instead of cutting, and a hose that releases under pressure whips and can strike the face. The sterilization of the handpiece, usually by steam at 134 degrees Celsius for 3 minutes or by the maker's alternative, the lubrication intervals, and the maximum running time between services must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. An aneurysm clip is a small spring-loaded clamp placed across the neck of a ballooned artery, and its closing force comes from the elasticity of the metal, usually a cobalt-chromium alloy or titanium. The clip is designed never to open on its own, so a clip that has been opened and released has been stressed and must not be reused. Clips come in straight, curved, and fenestrated shapes so the surgeon can close a vessel without trapping the small perforating arteries beside it, and each needs an applier that matches its design.

Old clips made of strongly magnetic steel can move or heat in a magnetic resonance scanner, so the clip type and the scanner compatibility must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Before the dura is closed the clip is verified rather than assumed, because it may be open, misaligned, or placed across a perforating vessel. The scrub nurse keeps a strict count on a board, because a dropped clip is a retained foreign body, and a temporary clip placed to stop flow must be timed because the brain tolerates only a short period without blood. The tolerated minutes must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The common operator error is to force an applier that does not match the clip, which bends the spring and leaves a clip no longer guaranteed to hold.

A shunt diverts cerebrospinal fluid from the fluid spaces of the brain to another part of the body, most often the abdominal cavity, where it is absorbed. The fluid is produced continuously, so the shunt runs continuously, with a valve that opens at a chosen pressure difference so fluid is not drained faster than it is made. The classic valve is a differential pressure valve built from a tiny spring and a ball or a slit membrane, its opening pressure described in centimeters of water, and an antisiphon device prevents the siphon effect of gravity when the patient stands. Programmable valves can be adjusted through the skin with a magnetic tool, which is convenient and also dangerous, because a strong external magnet can change the valve without anyone intending it. The valve opening pressures and the adjustable range must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A shunt that fails usually fails at its upper end, where the catheter lies in the ventricle and can be blocked by tissue, blood, or a cluster of the choroid plexus. The signs are the return of the symptoms the shunt was placed for, often headache, vomiting, drowsiness, and swelling around the valve, so the clinician presses the reservoir to see whether it refills, reads the valve with the maker's indicator, and traces the course for a kink or a pool of fluid. Infection is the other great failure, presenting with fever, wound redness, and abdominal pain, and it almost always means the hardware must be removed. Deep brain stimulation treats tremor and other movement disorders with continuous electrical pulses delivered to a precisely chosen point deep in the brain. The implanted system has a lead with several contacts, an extension cable tunneled behind the ear and down the neck, and a pulse generator under the skin of the chest.

The stimulation is described by three numbers, the amplitude in volts, the pulse width in millionths of a second, and the frequency in hertz, and each contact can serve as the negative pole. Programming is a search rather than a prescription, because the clinician changes one parameter at a time and watches for the intended benefit and for unwanted effects such as tingling, muscle pulling, or altered speech. The impedance of the circuit is checked to detect a broken or shorted lead, and an impedance that is suddenly very high or very low suggests a fracture or a short. The battery has a finite life measured in years, and the patient carries a programmer and often a magnet to switch the system on and off. The parameters, the contact selection, and the impedance limits must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The consumables of this specialty are mostly small, expensive, and single use. Electrodes, conductive paste, abrasive skin preparation, subdermal needle electrodes, and adhesive tape are consumed in every EEG and EMG study, and needle electrodes must never be reused because they blunt and carry infection between patients. Pressure monitoring kits, drainage chambers, transducer sets, and their tubing are sterile single-use items, while burrs, craniotome blades, drill covers, and microscope drapes are discarded or reprocessed strictly as the maker allows. The reuse policy and the substitution rules for these items must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Reprocessing in neurosurgery is dominated by delicate geometry, because micro-instruments, dissectors, and drills have narrow channels, hinges, and fine tips that trap blood and bone dust. Cleaning begins immediately after use, before the debris dries, and instruments are opened, disassembled where possible, and cleaned in an ultrasonic bath before inspection under magnification. Steam sterilization is the standard, commonly 134 degrees Celsius for 3 minutes in a vacuum cycle or 121 degrees Celsius for 15 minutes, and the operating microscope cannot be steamed, so it is covered with a sterile drape. The cycle, the load configuration, and the drying step must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and instruments whose tips no longer meet are sharpened or rejected.

Preventive maintenance is mostly electrical safety, calibration, and consumable life tracking. The biomedical technician verifies the leakage current and the protective earth of every line-powered amplifier, checks pressure transducers and nerve stimulators against a reference, and tests every alarm function. The air compressor or gas supply that drives the drills needs its filters changed and its dryer working, since water in the line destroys bearings, and each handpiece has an hours-of-use limit after which it is serviced or retired. The maintenance intervals, the calibration tolerances, and the acceptance criteria after repair must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

What the operator checks before every use is a short list that catches most disasters. The patient is identified, and the procedure and side are confirmed aloud with the team. The machine passes its self-test, the electrode impedances are acceptable, and the pressure transducer is leveled and zeroed. The drain stopcocks and chamber height are correct, the drill runs true in the air with a secure hose, and the microscope is balanced with its light shuttered. The clip board and the instrument count are started before the incision, so the specific checks and the documentation they require must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The field variant of this chapter is defined by what is missing. There is no computed tomography scanner in many district hospitals, no magnetic resonance imaging, no neuronavigation, no operating microscope, no air-driven drill with a clutch, and no sterile processing department with validated cycles. What remains is the burr hole, the catheter, the fluid column, a hand drill, a good light, and a trained hand. The equipment that survives is the equipment that works without mains power, without compressed gas, and without a supply chain, so the field clinician must be honest about which problems can be treated where.

The field EEG is a reduced instrument that is still useful for the questions it can answer. A battery-powered amplifier with 8 or 16 channels can be carried in a case and set up on a table, with reusable disc electrodes held on with saline-soaked pads or paste. A clear seizure pattern, a marked asymmetry, or the electrical silence of a severe injury can still be identified. The instrument gives up the full montage, the video, the prolonged recording, the exact electrode placement, and the artifact rejection of a hospital system, so its findings are suggestive rather than definitive. The channel count, the electrode type, and the interpretation limits must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Field EMG and nerve conduction use a handheld stimulator and a simple amplifier and are most valuable for locating a divided nerve in a limb injury. The field external ventricular drain is improvised from a ventricular catheter, sterile tubing, and a graduated container held at a measured height. The zero point is marked on the bed frame or the wall beside the patient's ear with tape and a pen, and the height above it is measured with a ruler or tape. Without a transducer there is no continuous waveform and no alarm, so the pressure is inferred from the height and the patient is watched for drowsiness, vomiting, and a falling level of consciousness. The reference mark, the chamber height, and the volume drained must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The field burr hole is the central procedure of frontier neurosurgery. Without a clutch perforator, the surgeon uses a hand-turned brace and bit or an electric drill with a sterile bit, applies light and intermittent pressure, and stops the moment the resistance changes. Bone is cleared with a curette rather than driven through, because the dura and the brain lie a few millimeters beyond the inner table. The site is prepared with an antiseptic solution and draped, local anesthetic is infiltrated, and the hole is enlarged only as much as the task requires. The preparation, the anesthesia, and the draping routine must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, because sterile technique is more demanding in a tent than in a theater.

Some patients must be transferred, and saying so early is part of good equipment practice. A falling level of consciousness, a new focal weakness, a suspected aneurysm, or an open fracture of the skull with brain visible needs imaging and a neurosurgeon, and no amount of improvisation replaces either. The transfer is planned with the airway secured and the drain clamped so it cannot siphon. Images travel badly, so a copy on a storage device or a photograph of the scan is sent with the patient, and the receiving center is spoken to before departure. The transfer criteria, the clamping rules, and the observation interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Field sterilization is the weakest link in the whole chain, and the honest clinician treats it as such. Where there is no steam autoclave, instruments may be boiled, which kills most organisms but does not reliably kill spores and is not sterilization. A chemical soak may be used instead, but its concentration cannot be verified and it irritates the eyes and the airways. A burr that cannot be sterilized should be single use if possible, and a catheter that cannot be guaranteed sterile should not be placed in the brain. Where a real autoclave is available, a common cycle is 134 degrees Celsius for 3 minutes or 121 degrees Celsius for 15 minutes, and the cycle and its monitoring must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Power and environment shape the field equipment more than the clinical task does. A battery-powered amplifier, a rechargeable drill, and a headlamp remove the dependence on mains electricity, but their batteries must be charged from a generator that may deliver unstable voltage. Heat, dust, humidity, and altitude change the behavior of motors, compressed gas supplies, and electronics, and a device rated for a clean room may fail in a tent. Instruments intended for these conditions should be simple, mechanical where possible, repairable with ordinary tools, and supplied with spare batteries and seals rather than a service contract. The storage temperature, the charging routine, and the cleaning limits must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The characteristic alarms of this chapter are few, and each one means something specific. An electrode impedance alarm means a contact has failed or dried, and the response is to fix the electrode rather than silence the channel. A pressure alarm or a suddenly flat waveform on a drain means a blocked catheter, a bubble, a disconnection, or a patient who has moved, and it is answered by checking the patient first and the tubing second. A drill that slows, heats, or changes pitch has a supply, bearing, or burr problem and must be stopped, so the threshold of every one of these alarms must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

To summarize, neurology and neurosurgery ask machines to do two difficult things at once. They must detect signals smaller than the noise around them, and they must work inside a closed space where a millimeter of error or a drop of contamination can be permanent. The hospital-grade versions use differential amplifiers and averaging, transducers leveled to a bony landmark and zeroed to the atmosphere, and navigation and frames that translate an image into a patient. They add microscopes that light the bottom of a narrow corridor, drills that cut bone and spare the dura beneath, and implants such as clips, shunts, and stimulators that must be chosen, counted, programmed, and verified. The field versions keep the burr hole, the drain, the measured fluid column, and the careful observation, and they give up the imaging and the validation that make the complex operations safe. The clinician who understands why the fluid column measures pressure, why averaging reveals the evoked response, and why a clip must never be reused will use the simplest version of this equipment correctly and will know when the honest answer is to transfer the patient.
