# Chapter 60 — Hand Hygiene and Cleaning

Hand hygiene prevents more hospital-acquired infection than any drug or device in this book, yet it depends on equipment that is cheap and constantly broken. The physical principle is contact transmission, because organisms travel between patients on staff hands, on touched surfaces, and on objects carried from bed to bed. A hand is warm, moist, and nutrient-rich, so it carries transient organisms from patients and resident organisms living in the skin. Soap and water lift both into a lather and rinse them away, while an alcohol-based rub kills transient organisms in place without removing soil. This chapter covers the hospital-grade equipment first, then the field variant of a bucket, a bar of soap, and a correctly mixed chlorine solution, and each figure must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

Soap works because of the shape of its molecule, which carries two ends that behave in opposite ways. One end is attracted to water and the other to oil and grease, so it buries itself in the film holding organisms against the skin. Many molecules cluster into a sphere called a micelle, trapping dirt, oil, and organisms inside while the outside stays dissolved in water. Rubbing supplies the energy that lifts the film and running water carries the load away, so friction and rinsing matter as much as the soap, and the lather must reach the thumbs, the fingertips, the spaces between the fingers, and the wrists. A bar kept in a wet dish grows a film of its own, so the hospital form is liquid soap in a sealed cartridge, and its volume and any dilution must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

An alcohol-based rub works by a different principle, and that difference explains both its speed and its limits. Alcohol denatures protein and disrupts the lipid membrane around many organisms, doing so within seconds and drying without a towel, so it can be used while moving between beds. Water content is critical rather than incidental, because water is needed for denaturation, so a preparation of 60 to 80 per cent alcohol by volume kills far better than one near 100 per cent. The rub must cover every surface and stay wet for the whole contact time, because a preparation that evaporates in 10 seconds has not achieved that time. The characteristic failure is technique rather than chemistry, since a small volume rubbed only into the palms leaves the thumbs and fingertips untreated, and its concentration, volume, and contact time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

The choice between washing and rubbing is a choice about what is on the hands, not about which product is better. Soap and water are required when the hands are visibly soiled or stained with blood, after using the toilet, and whenever the patient carries a spore-forming organism such as Clostridioides difficile, whose spores survive alcohol. The rub is faster and gentler for the many clean contacts of a shift, and repeated use of a product with an emollient causes less skin damage than repeated washing. Damaged skin is itself a hazard, because cracked skin sheds and harbors more organisms and makes the wearer less willing to perform hygiene. The gel is also flammable, burning with an almost invisible flame, so it is kept from open flames and arcing equipment, and its shelf life and storage temperature must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

The dispenser turns an intention into an action, and its design decides whether hygiene is convenient or annoying. A wall unit holds a sealed cartridge replaced as a whole, while a bulk-fill reservoir is refilled from a large container and is therefore the type most easily contaminated. The mechanism is a manual pump, a lever worked by the wrist or forearm, or an electronic unit whose infrared sensor detects the hand and releases a fixed dose. The electronic form removes all contact and delivers a reproducible volume, but it depends on batteries, a clean sensor window, and a pump that does not clog with dried gel. The safety hazard is a dispenser mounted where it drips onto a clean field or above a child's head, and its dose volume, sensor range, and battery type must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

Placement is a clinical decision disguised as a maintenance decision, because a dispenser that is not at the point of care will not be used. The standard is a unit at every patient room entrance, one at each bedside within reach of the person giving care, and pocket bottles for staff who move between beds. The geography matters because the moment of hygiene occurs at the threshold of contact, and a walk of 10 meters to a corridor dispenser means the moment has passed. The common operator error is to place dispensers only in corridors, where inspectors see them but staff at the bedside cannot use them. The operator must check that each unit delivers a full dose, that the nozzle is clear, that the cartridge is not empty, and that the bracket is firm, and each figure must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

Refilling is where a clean product becomes a contaminated one, because a reservoir that is topped up instead of replaced is a culture vessel. Diluting a partly used cartridge with fresh product introduces organisms from the container and from the hands of the person refilling, and the residual film on the inside wall feeds them. Liquid soap especially supports organisms that thrive in water, and an outbreak traced to a soap dispenser is a classic and entirely preventable event. The safe rule is to discard the remainder, clean and dry the reservoir, insert a new sealed cartridge, and record the replacement so that a ward's consumption can be compared with its bed days. The safety hazard is a dispenser filled with a solution mixed by hand at the wrong strength, and its cleaning interval and cartridge expiry date must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

The handwashing station is a small plumbing installation whose hydraulics determine whether it protects or contaminates. A clinical sink is deep enough to contain splashing, its bowl is smooth and seamless, and its tap discharges above the bowl rather than directly over the drain, because a jet aimed down the drain produces an aerosol of whatever sits in the trap. Running water is essential, since a basin of standing water concentrates contamination, and the tap must give a steady stream at a comfortable temperature rather than a scalding one. A sensor-operated or foot-operated tap removes hand contact with the handle, while a wrist-blade handle is the manual compromise where there is no power. The drain trap is a warm, wet reservoir that grows a biofilm, and its water temperature, flow rate, and the distance between tap and drain must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

The faucet and the waste arrangement fail first, and each failure has a characteristic signature. A sensor faucet with a failing battery may open for a fraction of a second, stay open continuously, or refuse to open, and its low battery indication is usually a repeating chirp or a blinking lamp. A foot pedal leaks at the valve stem, a wrist blade works loose, and an aerator screen clogs with scale until the stream becomes a weak spray that cannot rinse both hands. The common operator error is to wash in a sink also used for discarding body fluids, which turns a hygiene station into a disposal point and contaminates the bowl and the counter. The operator must check the stream, the temperature, the drain for slow emptying, and the counter for pooled water at the start of every shift, and its battery type and drainage capacity must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

The five moments of hand hygiene describe a workflow rather than a slogan, and each is tied to an object in the room. The first is before touching a patient, the second before a clean or aseptic procedure such as inserting a vascular access device or preparing an injection, the third after contact with blood or body fluid, the fourth after touching a patient, and the fifth after touching the patient's surroundings, including the bed rail, the chart, and the monitor. The moments follow the direction of risk, so the second protects the patient from the hands of the operator and the fourth protects the operator and the next patient. Gloves extend the barrier but never replace the moments, and the common operator error is to wear one pair of gloves through several contacts and wash the gloves instead of the hands. Direct observation, a point-of-care dispenser beside every bed, and a workflow in which gloves, swabs, and trays are arranged so that the operator need not choose between hygiene and reaching the materials are what make the moments possible, and each figure must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

Environmental cleaning rests on a principle that is often reversed in practice, because a surface must be cleaned before it can be disinfected. Cleaning is the physical removal of soil with detergent, water, and friction, and disinfection is the chemical killing of what remains, so a disinfectant applied over blood, pus, or dried organic film is consumed by the soil and never reaches the organisms beneath. Chlorine is especially vulnerable, because it reacts with organic matter and is spent within seconds, which is why the sequence begins with detergent and ends with a disinfectant. The product must keep the surface visibly wet for the full contact time, since a surface that dries in a few seconds has received a fraction of the intended dose. The safety hazard is mixing chlorine with an ammonia-based cleaner or an acid, which releases a toxic gas, and each dilution and contact time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

Chlorine is the workhorse disinfectant of the hospital and the one most often prepared at the wrong strength. Added to water it forms hypochlorous acid, the uncharged molecule that passes through the cell wall and oxidizes proteins and nucleic acids, and the amount available is what the term available chlorine measures. A solution is made from a liquid concentrate, a powder, or a tablet, and its strength falls with exposure to light, heat, and organic matter, so a bucket prepared in the morning may be nearly inactive by afternoon. The usual working strengths are a weak solution near 0.05 per cent for routine surfaces, a stronger solution near 0.5 per cent for a spill of blood or body fluid, and a stronger still solution near 1 per cent for heavy contamination of a porous surface. Each of those figures must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol, and a fresh batch is stored covered away from sunlight and checked with a test strip before it is relied upon.

Every surface tolerates some chemicals and is destroyed by others, and compatibility decides what is used where. Chlorine corrodes stainless steel and aluminum, pits the metal fittings of beds and monitors, and bleaches fabric, so it is rinsed from metal after the contact time, while alcohol is safe on many hard surfaces but degrades the rubber seals of some devices and dulls plastic screens. Quaternary ammonium compounds are gentle enough for mattresses, walls, and floors and leave a residual film, but soap and organic soil inactivate them and some organisms survive them. Hydrogen peroxide and peracetic acid are stronger oxidizing agents used for specialized equipment and some spills, and they demand their own protective measures. The safety hazard is a chemical left where a patient will touch it or a food tray will rest, so each compatibility, dilution, and rinsing requirement must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

The wipe is the most convenient cleaning tool in the hospital and the one most often used incorrectly, because its performance depends on saturation and on changing the cloth. A pre-wetted wipe carries a fixed volume of disinfectant and is meant for one surface or one small defined area, and it must leave that area visibly wet for the contact time rather than merely passing over it. A wipe reused from a clean surface to a dirty one carries organisms along with it, which is the same error as a mop that is never rinsed. The common operator error is to wipe a large area with a single wipe until it dries, because the last part of that area receives water and soil rather than disinfectant. The safety hazard is a tub left open so that the top of the stack dries out, and each wipe size, number per surface, and contact time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

A mop is a vector as well as a tool, because a wet mop head carries the contamination of one room into the next unless water and head are separated by function. The two-bucket method uses one bucket for the solution and a second for the rinse, with a third for wringing a heavily soiled floor, and the clean and dirty sides are never mixed. The floor is worked from the area farthest from the door toward the door and from the cleanest zone toward the dirtiest, with a fresh solution for each room or defined area. The mop head is laundered or discarded on a set interval, because a head washed in dirty water and dried in a closet becomes a durable reservoir. The safety hazard is a wet floor under a walking patient, so a sign and a dry path are part of the equipment, and each bucket volume, detergent dilution, and mop head change interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

Terminal cleaning is the fuller procedure performed after a patient is discharged, transferred, or has died, and it is defined by the removal of the patient rather than by a schedule. Linen and waste leave first, the mattress and pillow are checked for damage and either cleaned or replaced, and the bed frame, locker, table, and bathroom are cleaned from top to bottom with fresh solution, moving from high places to low and from the cleanest area to the dirtiest. Curtains are changed on the facility's interval or immediately after a patient with a transmissible infection. The high-touch surfaces carry the most traffic and include the bed rails, the call button, the overbed table, the door handle, the light switch, the infusion pump, the monitor buttons, and the bedside locker. The common operator error is to clean around the patient's belongings without lifting them, and the room must then rest until dry, because a wet surface carries organisms from the cloth. The safety hazard is a room returned to service while still damp or while the previous patient's equipment remains, and each figure must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

Ultraviolet germicidal systems use a physical effect rather than a chemical one, and their geometry is both their strength and their weakness. Light in the ultraviolet C band, at a wavelength near 254 nanometers, is absorbed by the nucleic acid of an organism and cross-links it so that it can no longer replicate, and the dose is the product of intensity and time. A lamp far from a surface or shadowed by a bed rail delivers very little, and because the light travels in straight lines the room must be cleared of soil and clutter first. The light does not penetrate dust, fabric, or shadow, does not remove soil, and does not reach inside a drawer or under a mattress, so it supplements manual cleaning and never replaces it. Some spores need a larger dose than vegetative bacteria, and the dose falls as the lamp ages and its sleeve coats with dust. The safety hazard is severe, because the same wavelength damages skin and cornea, so the room must be empty, the door locked, and an interlock must shut the lamp off if anyone enters. The characteristic alarm is a lamp-hour counter or a lamp-failure indication, the common operator error is to trust a dusty lamp that still glows visibly, and each figure must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

Monitoring turns a cleaning program from an intention into a measurement, and each method observes something different. A fluorescent marker is a gel or powder applied to defined high-touch points before cleaning and checked with an ultraviolet lamp afterwards, so it measures whether the surface was touched rather than whether it was clean. A swab recovers organisms from a measured area and cultures them, which measures surviving bioburden but takes days and depends on the recovery efficiency of the swab. A bioluminescence swab detects adenosine triphosphate, the energy molecule in all living cells and in organic soil, and returns a number in seconds, which suits immediate feedback although it cannot distinguish a harmless organism from a dangerous one. Direct observation with a documented tool measures the behavior of staff and is the only method that captures the moments of hygiene at the bedside, and each marker acceptance level, swab area, and observation sample size must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

Maintenance of hand hygiene equipment is a schedule rather than a repair, and it is almost entirely the work of the person who uses it. Dispensers are wiped, checked for a full dose, and refilled before they run dry, while sensor units receive a battery change on a fixed interval rather than when they fail. Sinks are checked for a clear drain, a firm tap, and a clean aerator, and the trap is flushed on schedule to limit biofilm. Ultraviolet lamps are cleaned and their hours logged, chlorine dosing pumps are checked against a measured sample, and every action is written in a log naming the person and the time. The common operator error is to leave a broken dispenser empty for weeks because the fault belongs to no assigned task, and the battery type, cleaning interval, trap flush interval, and calibration tolerance must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

Failure in this system is quiet rather than loud, and the characteristic alarms are small and easily ignored. A soap dispenser shows a low-level window or a colored indicator, an electronic unit may chirp when its cartridge is empty, and a sensor faucet may blink or chirp when its battery falls, while a chlorine dosing pump may alarm when the reservoir is low or the flow leaves its set range. A clogged dispenser nozzle delivers a thin stream that the user does not notice, and a slow drain announces itself only when the sink overflows. The safety hazard is the silent failure, because a ward believes it is performing hygiene while the equipment delivers half a dose or no water at all. The operator must check the indicators, listen for the chirps, and test the dose by hand at the start of every shift, and the alarm thresholds and pump calibration must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

The consumables of hand hygiene are cheap individually and decisive in aggregate, and managing them is a supply problem before it is a clinical one. The list includes liquid soap, alcohol-based rub, paper towels, surface wipes, detergent, chlorine concentrate or tablets, mop heads, cleaning cloths, examination gloves, waste bags, and the brackets and cartridges that hold them. Consumption is measured against bed days so that a sudden fall is recognized as a supply failure and a sudden rise as waste or a spill. The safety hazard is a shortage that pushes staff to dilute a product or reuse a wipe, and the common operator error is to order a year of stock that expires before it is used. The operator must check the stock level, the expiry date, and the seal of every container at handover, and the storage temperature and dilution ratio must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

The field variant begins with three objects that together do most of the work of a hospital installation: a container of water, a bar of soap, and a vessel that catches the waste. A tippy tap is a jerrycan suspended on a frame with a string tied to its cap, so that a foot presses a stick and the can tips forward to release a small stream, and the design keeps both hands off the container. Water is poured over the hands, the soap is worked into a lather, and the run-off falls into a soak pit or a drainage channel rather than pooling at the feet of the next user. A bar of soap on a string dries in the air instead of sitting in a wet dish, and a container with a tap at its base is better than a bowl because it gives running water without a pump. The safety hazard is a container a child can pull over, and each container volume, tap height, and soak pit distance from any water source must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

The field station must be arranged around a queue rather than a single user, because a mobile clinic may have 100 people waiting. A tap stand with several outlets, a raised platform, and a soak pit sized to the expected run-off make the difference between a line that moves and a mud pit that stops it. Water is the heaviest supply in the field, so it is carried to the point of use in the smallest container that will serve the queue, and the container is cleaned and dried between fills because a jerrycan left full in the sun grows a biofilm. The safety hazard is a station placed downhill from a latrine or waste pit, because one heavy rain connects the two, and the operator must check the water level, the soap, the drainage, and the firmness of the frame before the clinic opens. Each water treatment dose and soak pit dimension must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

Chlorine in the field is prepared by hand, and this single operation is the most error-prone task in the chapter. A stock solution is a liquid concentrate or a powder of stated strength, and the working solution is made by adding a measured volume of stock to a measured volume of water, never the reverse, because adding water to a concentrated base can react violently. The dilution is calculated from the label and checked with a test strip, because a solution that smells strongly of chlorine may still be far too weak or far too strong. The usual working strengths are a weak solution near 0.05 per cent for routine surfaces, a stronger solution near 0.5 per cent for blood and body fluid spills, and a stronger still solution near 1 per cent for heavy contamination, and each figure must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol. The safety hazard is a splash into the eye or inhalation in a confined space, so gloves, eye protection, and open air are required, and chlorine is never mixed with ammonia or an acid. A fresh batch is labeled with its strength and time and discarded when its strength falls.

Environmental cleaning in the field follows the same physics with less chemistry available, and the principles do not change. Soil is removed first with detergent and friction, the surface is rinsed, and a disinfectant is applied and left wet for its contact time, even when the only agent is the chlorine solution prepared that morning. Cloths are boiled or soaked and dried in the sun, mop heads are washed and hung to dry rather than left in a bucket, and clean and dirty water are kept apart in two marked containers. The surfaces that must be cleaned are the same high-touch points, and in a tent or shelter they include the frame poles, the cot rails, the latrine door, and the water tap handle. The safety hazard is a disinfectant stored in a drinking water container, because a child cannot tell the two apart, and monitoring depends on a visual check, a log, and a supervisor who spot-checks the actual surfaces rather than the paperwork, so each log format and marker acceptance level must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

What the field variant gives up is precision, volume, and the automatic delivery that makes hygiene effortless, and what it demands instead is discipline and water. It gives up the measured dose, because a bar of soap in the hand delivers an unknown amount, and it gives up the electronic sensor that removes contact with the dispenser. It gives up a continuous supply of clean running water, so every liter must be carried, treated, and accounted for, and the station must sit where the queue can use it without exhausting the day's supply. It gives up laboratory monitoring, so the program depends on the eyes of a supervisor and the honesty of a log. In exchange it demands that the soap stays dry, that the chlorine is mixed by a trained person with a measure, and that the same five moments are observed at the bedside of a shelter, and each container volume and chlorine dilution must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

To summarize, hand hygiene is a mechanical and chemical process rather than a moral one, and the equipment exists to deliver the mechanics reliably at the moment of contact. Soap lifts organisms into a lather that running water carries away, and an alcohol-based rub denatures them in place, so the two are chosen by what is on the hands and not by preference. The dispenser, the sink, the drain, the wipe, the mop, and the ultraviolet lamp are each a device with a placement rule, a consumable, a maintenance interval, and a characteristic quiet failure. The five moments define where hygiene must occur, and gloves extend but never replace it. Cleaning removes soil before disinfection kills what remains, and chlorine is mixed fresh, measured, and applied for its full contact time. The operator who checks the dose, the drain, the stock, and the log at every shift is the last barrier in the chain. Every setting, concentration, contact time, and alarm threshold must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.
