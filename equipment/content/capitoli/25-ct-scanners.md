# Chapter 25 — CT Scanners

A computed tomography scanner, or CT scanner, does not take a picture the way a camera does; it measures how much of an X-ray beam survives a journey through the body and then computes what must have been in the way. As a narrow beam of X-rays crosses tissue, some photons are absorbed and some are scattered, and the fraction that passes depends on the density and the atomic composition of everything along that line. Bone removes far more than fat does, fat removes more than air, and a small difference in tissue density produces only a small difference in the surviving beam. One measurement along one line is almost meaningless by itself, because many different arrangements of dense and thin material could produce the same reading, so the scanner makes thousands of measurements along thousands of lines and lets a computer find the single arrangement of tissue that explains all of them. The image is a calculation rather than a photograph, so a fault in the calculation or in the geometry can produce an image that looks plausible and is wrong.

The idea is older than the machine that made it practical. In the early twentieth century, radiologists blurred out unwanted anatomy by moving the X-ray source and the film in opposite directions, leaving one plane in focus, and they called the technique tomography. The insight that transformed it was mathematical, because if transmission measurements are collected from enough angles around an object, the interior can be reconstructed exactly, a result proved in 1917 and then ignored for half a century until computing power caught up with the arithmetic. The first clinical scanners, built in the 1970s, imaged only the head, took minutes for a single slice, and needed a water-filled bag around the patient to steady the geometry. They were slow, but they showed hemorrhage, tumor, and swelling inside the skull without opening it.

The raw material of the image is called projection data. Each measurement records the total attenuation along one straight line at one angle, and the complete set, arranged by angle, forms a mathematical object that engineers sometimes call a sinogram. Reconstruction turns that set back into a picture, most often by filtered back projection, which smears every measurement back across the image along the line it came from and then corrects the resulting blur with a filter. Older scanners and modern low-dose protocols may instead use an iterative method, which proposes an image, simulates the measurements it would produce, compares them with the real ones, and adjusts until the two agree, which tolerates noisy data and allows a usable image to be made from fewer X-ray photons. The practical point is that reconstruction is a choice, because a sharp filter that flatters bone will also manufacture noise that hides a subtle bleed, and every reconstruction setting and filter must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Reconstructed values are expressed on a scale of attenuation numbers conventionally called Hounsfield units. Water is defined as zero, air as roughly minus one thousand, and dense bone as several thousand, and the numbers are stored far more precisely than any screen can display. Windowing makes them visible: the operator chooses a narrow range of numbers and stretches that range across the gray levels of the display, so that soft tissue becomes distinguishable while fat and bone are clipped to flat black or flat white. A lung window, a soft tissue window, a bone window, and a brain window are simply different choices of that range, and each one must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. No window, however well chosen, can repair a slice that was never reconstructed thinly enough to contain the answer.

The mechanical history of the scanner is usually told as a series of generations. The first generation moved a pencil-thin beam and a single detector across the patient, then rotated a fraction of a degree and repeated the stroke, an arrangement so slow that breathing and bowel movement ruined the data. The second generation used a small fan of detectors to shorten each stroke, and the third generation abandoned translation altogether and rotated one wide fan of detectors around the patient with the tube, which is the geometry of nearly every scanner in service today. The decisive change was not the geometry but the cable, because a continuously rotating gantry cannot be tethered by wires, so engineers introduced the slip ring, a set of sliding contacts that carries power and data across a rotating joint.

Once the gantry could spin without stopping, the table could move while it spun, and the scan path became a helix rather than a stack of separate circles. Helical scanning removed the pause between slices, shortened examinations, and allowed a volume of anatomy to be captured in a single breath hold, which is what made CT practical for the chest and the abdomen. The next step was multislice detection: instead of one row of detectors, the scanner carries many rows, so a single rotation produces many slices at once. Modern machines acquire 64, 128, or more slices per rotation, with rotation times of a fraction of a second, and each of those figures must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The reward is speed, because a scan short enough to finish in one breath is a scan free of motion blur, while the price is a wider cone of beam, more scattered radiation, and a reconstruction problem that grows with every added row.

The gantry is the ring-shaped housing that the patient passes through, and it contains the entire rotating assembly. Inside it sit the X-ray tube, the high-voltage generator that feeds it, the collimator that shapes the beam into a fan or a cone, the bowtie filter that evens out its intensity, and the arc of detectors opposite the tube. The gantry tilts, usually through a range of about 30 degrees, so that the scan plane can be angled to avoid the eyes or to follow a disc space, and the tilt must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Low-power lasers project thin lines onto the patient to mark the scan plane and the center of the field, and these lasers are the most common source of a wrongly positioned scan, while the bore, the tunnel the patient enters, is usually 70 to 80 centimeters across. The characteristic gantry alarms are the tube heat alarm, the rotation fault alarm, and the table travel limit alarm, and every one must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The patient table is a precision mechanism rather than a bench. It must advance the patient through the gantry at an exactly controlled speed, hold a heavy load without sagging, and return to the same position reproducibly when a patient is rescanned. Every table carries a maximum patient weight, often between 200 and 300 kilograms, and that limit must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, because exceeding it damages the drive and can drop the table. The table also has a lowest position and a maximum travel, so a patient who cannot lie flat may simply not fit the geometry. The common operator error at this stage is starting the scan before checking that the arms are out of the beam, which produces a streak artifact and often forces a repeat examination.

Four numbers dominate the dose a patient receives: the tube voltage, usually between 80 and 140 kilovolts, the tube current in milliamperes, the time the beam is on, and the pitch, which is the distance the table moves per rotation divided by the width of the beam. Slice thickness, usually between 0.5 and 5 millimeters, sets the detail available, and a thin slice through a large volume costs more dose than a thick slice through the same volume. A pitch greater than one shortens the scan and lowers dose but may leave gaps in the data that reconstruction must interpolate. Every one of these settings must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, because the difference between a diagnostic scan and an unnecessarily high dose is a set of numbers chosen by a human being.

Dose is reported in milligray for the radiation delivered to a specific tissue, and in millisievert for the biological effect when tissue sensitivities are taken into account. A chest radiograph delivers on the order of 0.1 millisievert, while a CT scan of the chest delivers roughly 7 millisieverts, and a multiphase abdominal study can exceed 20 millisieverts, and each of these figures must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The safety hazard is not a burn or a shock; it is a small, cumulative, stochastic risk of cancer that no patient can feel and no operator can see, which is why it is so easy to ignore. The scanner should display and archive a dose report for every examination, and the operator error that matters most is repeating a scan without a new clinical reason, because a repeat doubles the dose to the same patient for no new information. Children, pregnant patients, and patients having repeated follow-up are the groups in which the justification must be argued most carefully, and their protocols must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Intravenous contrast is what turns a CT scan from an anatomical map into a physiological one, and a powered injector is what makes contrast delivery reproducible. The injector is a programmable pump that holds a syringe of contrast and a syringe of saline, and it delivers a chosen volume at a chosen flow rate, typically between 2 and 5 milliliters per second, followed by a saline flush, and that rate must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Timing is everything, because an arterial phase, a portal venous phase, and a delayed phase each require the scan to begin at a defined interval after the injection, and many scanners use bolus tracking, in which a low-dose monitoring sequence watches the contrast arrive in a chosen vessel and starts the scan when the density crosses a threshold. The characteristic alarm of this part of the examination is the injector pressure limit alarm, which sounds when the line is occluded or the cannula has moved out of the vein. The hazard the operator must fear is extravasation, the leakage of contrast into the tissue around the vein, which causes pain and swelling and demands that the injection stop at once, that the arm be elevated, and that the event be documented.

The control console is where the operator stands, behind a lead-glass window or in an adjoining room, and it carries the scan prescription, the contrast timing, the image display, and the emergency controls. A clearly marked emergency stop button halts the gantry and the table instantly, and every operator must know its location before the first patient of the day rather than during the first emergency. Scanner alarms are named for the fault they detect, and the ones an operator will meet are the tube heat alarm, the rotation fault alarm, the table limit alarm, the injector pressure alarm, and the motion or gating alarm. The most dangerous alarm is the one that never sounds, because a scanner can produce a complete and well-formed image from a failing detector without any warning, which is why daily quality control on a phantom matters more than any single reading on the console. The common operator error is quiet rather than dramatic: accepting an image because it looks familiar, choosing a protocol from memory, or scanning a region that another examination has already covered, and every alarm threshold and time limit must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Image quality in CT is a balance among noise, spatial resolution, and dose, and every artifact is the visible signature of that compromise. Motion artifact appears as blurring or as ghosted bands, and it comes from breathing, swallowing, cardiac motion, or a patient who is cold and shivering on the table. Beam hardening appears as dark bands between two dense objects, typically between the bones at the base of the skull or across a shoulder, because the low-energy part of the beam is absorbed first and the remaining beam is harder than the reconstruction assumes. A ring artifact, a perfect circle centered on the isocenter, is the classic sign of a single miscalibrated detector channel, while a streak artifact radiating from metal or from a surgical clip can be reduced by angling the gantry, increasing the tube output, or applying a metal artifact reduction algorithm. Photon starvation, a mottled noise through a very thick body part, is a sign that the protocol was written for a smaller patient.

The X-ray tube is a consumable with a finite life, and its end is usually announced by a rising heat load, a falling output, and a gradual loss of contrast in the images. The anode and its bearing wear, the vacuum slowly degrades, and the focal spot spreads until the resolution the protocol expects is no longer produced. Detector maintenance is quieter but equally decisive, because the detector array must be calibrated against known attenuation, and a channel that drifts produces the ring artifact already described. An air calibration, performed with the gantry empty, and a phantom calibration, performed with the manufacturer's test object, are the routines that keep the numbers honest, and the interval and method for each must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The reconstruction computer needs maintenance as well, because software versions, filter files, and geometry tables can become corrupted, and a mismatch between the calibration table and the current configuration produces images that look real and measure wrongly. A quality assurance program should record tube output, noise, uniformity, spatial resolution, and slice thickness at fixed intervals, so that a slow drift appears as a trend rather than as a failure.

The consumables of CT scanning are ordinary items with serious consequences. They include the intravenous cannula, the extension tubing and the injector syringe set, the contrast medium itself, saline, the pressure-resistant connectors, and the adhesive dressing that holds the cannula in place. Add the electrocardiogram electrodes when the scanner gates to the heartbeat, the respiratory belt, the positioning foam, the table covers, and the disinfectant wipes used between patients. Contrast medium must be stored within its temperature range, protected from light, and checked for the expiry date before every single use, because a degraded agent provides poor opacification and wastes a dose of radiation. The injector tubing is single use and must be discarded even when the examination is abandoned, the syringe in the injector must never be refilled for a second patient, and sharps from cannulation go into a puncture-resistant container at the point of use.

Cleaning the scanner is a patient safety task rather than housekeeping. The table top, the pads, the positioning aids, the injector head, and the gantry surfaces are touched by every patient, and they must be cleaned and disinfected between examinations with an agent compatible with the manufacturer's materials, because some disinfectants cloud the detector covers or crack the table pad. The bore, the control knobs, the touch screen, and the keyboard deserve the same treatment, and the disinfectant contact time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Personal protective equipment, or PPE, includes gloves for handling contrast and body fluids and a gown or apron wherever splashing is likely, and the operator at the console is not exempt from hand hygiene between patients. Patients with a known transmissible infection do not need a special machine, but they do need a defined cleaning sequence and, where the procedure generates droplets, a mask and eye protection, and the cleaning record belongs in the log.

A CT scanner is not simply bought and installed; a room is built around it. The gantry and the table are heavy, the gantry alone often between 1,500 and 2,500 kilograms, and the floor must be rated for that concentrated load, which may require a reinforced slab beneath the finished surface. Power is the second demand, because the tube generator draws a large current for short periods, so the supply must be sized for peak load rather than average load, and a voltage drop during a rotation degrades image quality without an obvious alarm. Cooling is the third, because a rotating anode converts most of its energy into heat, and the room needs either a chilled water circuit or a substantial heating, ventilation, and air conditioning, or HVAC, capacity, with temperature and humidity held in a range that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Humidity control prevents condensation and static discharge, an uninterruptible power supply, or UPS, protects only the console and the computer rather than the tube, and the radiation shielding in the walls, floor, ceiling, and lead-glass window must be designed by a qualified medical physicist for the scanner's output and the occupancy of the neighboring rooms.

The reconstruction workstation is the other half of the scanner, and it is where an examination becomes a diagnosis. From the raw projection data it produces the reconstructed slices, and from those slices it produces the coronal, sagittal, curved, and three-dimensional views that a clinician actually reads. The images travel as Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine, or DICOM, objects, and the picture archiving and communication system, or PACS, stores, distributes, and retrieves them, so a failure of that link can make a perfectly good scan invisible to the person who needs it. The workstation also generates the dose report, the structured record of the radiation the patient received and the protocol that delivered it. Post-processing tools, including vessel analysis and artificial intelligence, or AI, assisted detection, are aids rather than replacements, and the operator should verify that the correct patient identity travels with every image, because a mislabeled examination can be read as another person's anatomy.

The field position must begin with an uncomfortable fact: a full CT scanner does not travel, and no amount of improvisation makes it portable. The gantry, the high-voltage generator, the cooling loop, the shielding, and the floor loading together add up to a fixed installation that costs more than many district hospitals spend on all their other equipment. A remote or mobile clinic therefore does not own a CT scanner, it owns a relationship with one. What the field service can own is a compact head scanner, a small machine built specifically to image the brain, which weighs a few hundred kilograms rather than a few tonnes and can be moved in a reinforced vehicle or installed inside a shipping container. Even that machine demands a stable power supply, a cool and dry space, a trained operator, and a maintenance contract that reaches the site, and the distinction that matters is between the scanner designed for the field and the full scanner that is merely hoped to be.

The compact head scanner gives up a great deal in order to gain its mobility. Its bore is small, often just large enough for a head and a pair of shoulders, so it cannot examine the chest, the abdomen, or the pelvis, and it cannot comfortably admit a patient on a ventilator with a full monitoring array. Its detector coverage is limited, its rotation is slower, and its tube operates at a lower output, so the images are noisier and the fine detail that a full scanner delivers through the base of the skull is simply absent. It usually reconstructs a narrow set of standard brain windows rather than a full library, it may not support the contrast phases that a body scan requires, and its table limit may exclude an obese or unstable patient on mechanical grounds alone. Its dose for a head examination is broadly comparable to a full scanner's head protocol, and every setting on it must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

What the field variant demands instead is discipline in everything that surrounds the machine. It demands a power supply that is either genuinely stable or backed by a properly sized generator and an uninterruptible supply for the computer, because a brownout during a rotation ruins the study and abuses the tube. It demands a room that can be kept cool and dry, because a container in the sun becomes an oven and a humid climate corrodes the electronics. It demands a trained radiographer or clinical officer who can position a head, recognize motion artifact, and decide when an image is not good enough, since there is no physicist in the next room, and it demands a supply chain for the disposable head holders and the cleaning agents. It demands a service plan with spare parts held locally, because a scanner that must be shipped abroad for repair is a scanner that stops working for a year, and above all it demands a clear referral rule, so that cases beyond its ability are identified early and sent onward rather than scanned badly.

A remote clinic with no scanner at all does something more useful than pretending: it triages. The clinician in the field examines the patient, records the history and the vital signs, and decides whether the presentation is one that CT would change. A suspected stroke, a head injury with a falling level of consciousness, a suspected tuberculosis cavity, or an unexplained abdominal mass may justify a journey, while a simple fracture, a pneumonia, or a migraine usually does not. Plain radiography, ultrasound, and careful clinical examination answer many of the questions that a scanner would be asked, and a portable ultrasound probe is the diagnostic device that genuinely travels. Where a scan is required, the clinic stabilizes the patient first, arranges transport with a trained escort who carries the referral question in writing, and checks kidney function before any intravenous contrast. Teleradiology, in which images are transmitted for reading at a distance, turns a scanner in one city into a service for a whole province, provided the images carry the right identifiers and the report returns to the right clinician.

The operator's daily check is the cheapest safety device in the room. Before the first patient, the operator confirms that the gantry rotates smoothly, that the lasers align with the isocenter, that the table travels its full range and returns to zero, that the emergency stop functions, and that the intercom and the patient observation camera work. The warm-up routine must be completed exactly as written, because a cold tube asked for a high-output protocol may fail or produce unstable output, and the warm-up interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The air calibration status, the last phantom quality control result, and the dose display should be checked and logged, and any deviation reported rather than repeated. Contrast stock, injector syringes, cannulas, and the emergency drugs and airway equipment must be present and in date, the patient identity, the pregnancy question, the allergy history, the kidney function, and the presence of any metal implant must be confirmed before the table moves, and the operator should look at the patient and not only at the screen.

The failure modes of a CT scanner can be read from the outside. A tube nearing its end shows a longer warm-up, more frequent heat alarms, and images that look softer than the previous week's, and then one morning it simply stops. A detector that drifts draws its ring across every image, and the ring's position identifies the channel at fault, while a slipping or misindexed table makes every slice a little wrong in the same direction, which is easy to miss precisely because the error is uniform. A cooling failure raises the tube temperature, triggers an over-temperature alarm, and eventually forces the scanner to refuse a scan, while a power disturbance resets the console and can leave a reconstruction incomplete. A corrupted calibration file produces plausible images with wrong numbers, and that is the most dangerous failure of all, so the daily phantom check catches the quiet failures while the operator's attention catches the loud ones.

In the field, maintenance is a matter of survival and must be planned before arrival. The compact scanner needs a service schedule, a set of consumable parts such as the tube, the detector module, and the cooling filter, and at least one local technician trained to replace them. The most common field failures are power quality, heat, dust, and humidity, in that order, and each has a cheap defense in a voltage stabilizer, a shaded and ventilated enclosure, a filtered air intake, and a dehumidifier. Calibration must be repeated after any transport, because the geometry shifts, and the calibration phantom must travel with the machine. The operator keeps a log of every examination, every fault, and every calibration, since that log is the only maintenance history the machine will ever have, consumables must be ordered with enough lead time to survive a broken supply road, and when a remote scanner fails the entire referral pathway fails with it.

To summarize, the CT scanner is a mathematical instrument built around a rotating X-ray tube and a ring of detectors, and it produces a computed slice rather than a photograph. Its speed comes from the slip ring and multislice detection, its contrast from a timed intravenous injection, and its images from a reconstruction that can be tuned, filtered, and reformatted long after the patient leaves the table. The safety hazard is the radiation dose, delivered silently and cumulatively, and the operator error that matters most is the unnecessary or repeated scan. The characteristic alarms are the tube heat alarm, the rotation fault, the table limit, and the injector pressure alarm, and behind them the daily phantom check is the routine that finds the quiet faults. The infrastructure is a room built around a heavy, hot, and power-hungry machine, and the consumables are cannulas, contrast, injector tubing, electrodes, and cleaning agents whose availability decides whether any of the rest of it works. The field variant is a compact head scanner, or more often a referral pathway that uses plain radiography, ultrasound, and teleradiology instead of a scanner that cannot travel, and no setting, dose, temperature, time, pressure, or threshold in this chapter may be used from memory.
