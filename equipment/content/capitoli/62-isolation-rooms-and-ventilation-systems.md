# Chapter 62 — Isolation Rooms and Ventilation Systems

This chapter is about rooms built around a diagnosis and about the moving air that carries an infection from one patient to the next. An infectious organism travels on something that can be moved, and that something is usually air, a water droplet, or a hand. Airborne organisms ride on particles small enough to stay suspended for hours, while droplet-borne and contact organisms travel on larger droplets or on hands and equipment. The hospital isolation room answers with an anteroom, a pressure differential, filtration, and monitoring, while the field version answers with a tent, a fan, and a separate entrance. Every pressure, air change rate, filtration efficiency, temperature, and alarm threshold named here must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before it is relied upon.

To understand the design, the listener must picture the particles involved. When a person breathes, speaks, coughs, or sings, the respiratory tract releases droplets ranging from less than 1 micrometer to more than 100 micrometers across. Large droplets carry more water and settle within about 1 meter, which is why droplet precautions rely on distance and on a mask worn near the patient. Small droplets evaporate before they fall and leave a dry residue called a droplet nucleus, which floats on room currents, passes around a curtain, and enters a ventilation duct. Dilution and filtration remove those nuclei, and the local protocol and the manufacturer's instructions define the precautions a specific organism requires.

The three precaution categories are distinguished by the route, and each demands different accommodation. Contact precautions address organisms spread by touch, such as those colonizing a wound or the intestine, and require gloves and a gown for every entry plus equipment that stays with the patient. Droplet precautions address organisms carried in large droplets, such as influenza or bacterial meningitis, and require a mask for anyone within about 2 meters plus a single room where possible. Airborne precautions address organisms carried in droplet nuclei, including tuberculosis, measles, and chickenpox, and require a room ventilated to remove those nuclei and held at a lower pressure than the space around it. The distance, the mask specification, and the pressure difference must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the precautions are applied.

A negative pressure room works because air always moves from higher pressure toward lower pressure, and the room is deliberately made the lower one. The exhaust fan draws more air out than the supply diffuser pushes in, so a small deficit develops and the missing volume is pulled in through every gap, including the gap around the door. The usual design target is a difference near 2.5 pascals, with 12 air changes per hour for a new facility and a lower minimum in some older buildings. Those figures and the direction of the differential must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the room is used for a patient. The anteroom is the small room between corridor and patient where the barrier goes on and comes off, holding the personal protective equipment, hand hygiene supplies, waste container, and laundry bag. Many anterooms have interlocked doors so one must close before the other opens, and the common error is to use the anteroom for storage, which blocks the doffing sequence.

Air changes per hour counts how many times the entire room volume is replaced with fresh or filtered air in 1 hour. The room volume is divided into the volume delivered each hour, so a room of 60 cubic meters receiving 720 cubic meters per hour has 12 air changes per hour. Dilution follows an exponential curve, so a room at 12 air changes per hour clears most of a released cloud within about 20 minutes of well-mixed flow. The phrase well-mixed flow carries the weakness, because real rooms have dead corners behind the door, above the curtain rail, and beneath the bed. The air change rate, room volume, and supply and exhaust positions must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the design is accepted.

The pressure differential is monitored by a gauge mounted outside the room, where it can be read before anyone enters and where a failure is visible to passing staff. The common instruments are a mechanical diaphragm gauge, an inclined liquid manometer, a ball in a tapered tube, and an electronic sensor with a display and an alarm. The gauge must reference the corridor rather than the room itself, because a sensor plumbed to the wrong port reads zero or backwards. A characteristic alarm is an audible and visual negative pressure alarm that triggers when the differential falls below its threshold or when the door is held open too long. The alarm threshold, gauge range, calibration interval, and recording frequency must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the room is occupied.

Testing turns the design into evidence, and it is done at commissioning, at a fixed interval, and after any change to the ventilation system. The simplest test holds a small source of visible smoke or a light tissue at the door edge and confirms that it is drawn into the room rather than pushed out. A flow hood at each grille measures airflow in liters per second, and a tracer test releases a harmless aerosol at the patient's position and samples the corridor. These tests are performed by a trained person with calibrated instruments. The interval, acceptance criteria, tracer quantity, and instrument calibration must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the certification is signed.

A positive pressure room reverses the direction of the same principle in order to protect a patient rather than contain one. The supply air is pushed in faster than the exhaust removes it, so the room sits slightly higher in pressure than the corridor and air flows outward through the door gap. These protective environments are built for patients whose defenses are absent, such as after a bone marrow transplant or with a very low white cell count. The supply air is filtered to high efficiency, and the room is usually held near 2.5 pascals positive and near 12 air changes per hour. The pressure difference, filtration efficiency, air change rate, and alarm threshold must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the room is used.

High-efficiency particulate air filtration makes both containment and protection possible, and its principle is depth rather than a sieve. The medium is a mat of fine fibers folded into a deep pleated pack so a large surface fits inside a small frame. Particles are captured by four mechanisms at once: large particles strike a fiber, medium particles are intercepted as they graze one, the smallest diffuse into the fibers by Brownian motion, and charged fibers attract particles electrostatically. Because these mechanisms overlap, the hardest particle to capture is one near 0.3 micrometers, and a filter rated to remove at least 99.97 per cent of particles at that size removes even more of larger and smaller ones. The efficiency figure, particle size, and filter resistance must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the filter is accepted for a patient care area.

The filter is only as good as its housing, because a perfect filter with a leaking gasket is a bypass rather than a barrier. The pleated pack sits in a frame clamped against a gasket or seated in a gel channel, and that seal forces every cubic meter of air through the medium instead of around it. A coarse prefilter upstream catches lint and dust, while a differential pressure sensor across the filter reports how hard the fan must work as the medium loads. The filter is tested in place by releasing a known aerosol upstream and scanning the downstream face and seal with a particle-counting photometer, so a torn pleat or pinched gasket shows as a localized leak. The pressure drop limit, test aerosol, acceptance limit, and replacement interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the change is scheduled.

Ultraviolet germicidal irradiation is a supplementary control that damages the genetic material of organisms, and its physics explains its narrow limits. A low-pressure mercury lamp emits most of its energy at a wavelength near 254 nanometers, which nucleic acids absorb, producing cross-links that prevent bacteria, viruses, and fungi from reproducing. The effect requires line of sight and time, because the radiation travels in straight lines and is absorbed by dust and water films, so a shadowed corner receives no dose. In an upper-room installation the lamp is mounted high behind louvers that direct the beam above people's heads, and in a duct installation the lamp treats air as it passes. The safety hazard is immediate, because unprotected eyes can develop painful corneal inflammation and skin can redden, and the common error is to treat the lamp as a substitute for ventilation while leaving a dusty lamp in place for years. The wavelength, exposure time, shielding design, lamp replacement interval, and cleaning frequency must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the installation is used.

Exhaust is the other half of containment, because air drawn out of a room must go where it cannot harm anyone. A room on airborne precautions has a dedicated exhaust whose fan sits at the end of the duct, so the whole duct run is under negative pressure and a leak pulls air in. The discharge point is normally a stack rising above the roof, away from air intakes, operable windows, and walkways, with enough velocity to throw the air clear. A backdraft damper closes when the fan stops, and the characteristic alarm is a fan failure or airflow alarm, because a stopped exhaust reverses the pressure relationship and turns the room into a source. The common operator error is to switch off an exhaust fan to save energy or silence a noise complaint, which quietly disables the whole control. The stack height, discharge velocity, separation distance, fan alarm threshold, and damper test interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the system is relied upon.

The supply side, the exhaust side, and the controls together form heating, ventilation, and air conditioning, usually shortened to HVAC. Supply air is filtered and delivered through diffusers chosen to mix room air without a strong draft, while the return path sweeps the room rather than short-circuiting from diffuser to grille. Ward pressures are cascaded, so a corridor is slightly positive to the outside, an isolation room slightly negative to the corridor, and a soiled utility room more negative still. A single adjustment changes the pressure in several other rooms, so renovation, a new door, a sealed window, or a blocked grille disturbs the balance invisibly. The balance figures, diffuser throw, cascade pressures, and retest interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before any adjustment is made.

When no single room exists, cohorting is the alternative, and it is a compromise with rules rather than a free solution. Cohorting places patients carrying the same organism in the same bay, cared for by the same staff, so the group is contained as a unit even though no patient has a private room. Beds are separated by at least 1 meter and preferably more, and a curtain offers privacy but almost no protection against airborne nuclei, because fabric does not seal. Improvised barriers are built from plastic sheeting, tape, and distance, and heavy plastic across a bay opening stops large droplets but creates no pressure differential and filters no air unless a fan and duct are added. With a window fan pulling air out, the bay becomes a weak negative pressure zone, so the safety hazard is a fan mounted backwards, which pushes the patient's air into the corridor. The separation distance, fan direction, sheet material, and cleaning frequency must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the arrangement is used.

People and objects carry infection as surely as air does, so the room has rules for both. Visitors are limited in number, instructed in hand hygiene and any required barrier, and asked to keep their distance or stay away when unwell. Staff are assigned to the room or cohort rather than moving freely, and every entry follows hand hygiene, gown, gloves, and, where required, a fitted respirator, with the reverse sequence on the way out. The dedicated equipment set is a stethoscope, a thermometer, a blood pressure cuff, and any monitor that stays with the patient, because a stethoscope left on the bed rail is a reliable vehicle for a resistant organism. The visitor policy, equipment list, and entry record must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the room is opened.

The consumables of the isolation room are the items that make written policy physically possible, and a room that runs out of them fails quietly. The list includes disposable gowns and aprons, gloves in the correct sizes, fitted particulate respirators or surgical masks, hand hygiene solution, paper towels, waste bags, labeled laundry bags, and tape for closing them. It also includes high-efficiency filters, prefilter pads, gaskets and sealant, ultraviolet lamps, door seals, curtains and hooks, and the reference fluid for the pressure gauge. A stock check is done every shift so the room is never found short when a patient arrives, and expiry dates matter on respirators, disinfectants, and ultraviolet lamps. The stock list, expiry check, and par level must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the room is used.

Cleaning an isolation room begins with the least contaminated surface and ends with the most, so soil is not carried back onto a clean area. Daily cleaning covers the high-touch surfaces, including bed rails, call button, bedside table, door handle, light switch, intravenous pump, and monitor, wiped with detergent first and disinfectant second. The floor is cleaned last with a fresh mop head, and the mop is not carried into another room without laundering or disinfection. Terminal cleaning follows the patient's departure: linen and waste are removed, all surfaces are cleaned again, the curtain is changed, and mattress and pillow covers are inspected for breaches. The contact time, disinfectant concentration, and any additional treatment cycle must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the room returns to service.

Maintenance of an isolation room is mostly patience and record keeping, because the components fail slowly and invisibly. The ventilation system is inspected on a schedule that covers belt tension and bearings on the fan, damper movement, grille cleanliness, and the alarm panel supply. The pressure gauge is checked against a calibrated reference and its tubing inspected for kinks, water traps, and disconnection, because a tube pulled off a port is the commonest cause of a false reading. Door hardware is adjusted so the door closes and latches on its own, and seals and sweeps are replaced when they harden, since a worn seal is a leak no fan can overcome. High-efficiency filters are replaced when the pressure drop reaches its limit, prefilters more often, and ultraviolet lamps on hours of use, so the inspection interval and replacement rules must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the work is signed off.

Containment failures are unglamorous and they repeat, which is exactly why they can be anticipated. The door is propped open with a chair, a wedge, or a linen bag, and the differential disappears within seconds. The gauge is unplugged, zeroed on the wrong port, or its tube is cracked, so the reading is stable and false. The exhaust fan trips on a thermal overload or a power failure, and nobody notices because the room still feels normal. The supply grille is covered by a curtain, a sign, or a stored box, so the air short-circuits and the far side of the room stops being ventilated. The anteroom fills with supplies, so doffing happens in the doorway, and the alarm is silenced because it sounds whenever the door opens, so the habit outlives the fault it was reporting.

The daily round holds all of this together, and it takes only minutes when done in a fixed order. The operator reads the pressure gauge and writes the value and time, then listens for the exhaust fan and feels for airflow at the grille with a strip of tissue. The door is released from a partly open position and watched to confirm that it closes and latches, and the seal is examined for tears. The anteroom is checked for gowns, gloves, respirators, hand hygiene solution, waste bags, and a working light, and the log is checked for the previous shift's entries. The characteristic alarm is the negative pressure alarm, usually a red light and an audible tone, and a brief alarm that ends when the door closes is normal while one that stays on requires checking the door, the fan, the gauge, and the grilles. The common error is to mute the alarm and continue working, so the alarm threshold, delay, and escalation path must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the room is occupied.

In a mobile clinic, a frontier hospital, or a ward with no single rooms, the containment room is approximated by a tent, a fan, and a plan. A framed tent of coated fabric or heavy plastic is erected with one opening for the patient and another for staff, and a fan draws air out through a duct leading outdoors. The fabric leaks at every seam and zipper, so a pressure difference that a solid room holds at 2.5 pascals may be a small fraction of that in a tent, and that comparison figure must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the tent is used for a patient. The tent reduces the escape of contaminated air rather than eliminating it. What the field variant gives up is control, because there is no interlocked anteroom, no pressure gauge to show magnitude, and usually no high-efficiency filtration. There is no automatic air change control and no alarm, so heat, cold, wind, and rain change performance from hour to hour. Every one of these absences must be named in the local protocol, because a team that believes it has a pressure room when it has a tent will take risks it would otherwise avoid.

What the field variant demands instead is behavior, and behavior is a real technology when applied consistently. Distance is the first tool, because placing the patient at the far end of a large, well-ventilated space and keeping others several meters away reduces exposure even without a wall. A separate entrance and a separate path of travel matter, because a tent sharing a doorway with the general waiting area defeats much of its purpose. Dedicated staff and equipment matter more in the field than in the hospital, because there is no anteroom to enforce the boundary and no spare monitor to leave at the bedside. Hand hygiene becomes the primary control, performed before and after every contact, the number of visitors is reduced, and the patient is taught to cover the cough and wear a mask. The staffing rule, distance, entry path, and hand hygiene method must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the arrangement is adopted.

Natural ventilation is the field's most valuable and least visible piece of equipment, and its principle is buoyancy and wind rather than a fan. Warm air rises and leaves through a high opening while cooler air enters through a low one, and wind across a building drives air through any opening it finds. A ward with openings on opposite walls, a high and a low vent, or a courtyard on one side and a street on the other achieves a substantial air change rate with no electricity. Monitoring in the field is by observation, and a stick of incense or a tissue held at the tent opening shows the direction of flow, so smoke drawn toward the exhaust confirms inward flow while smoke drifting into the waiting area means the fan is off, reversed, or overwhelmed. The test proves direction and not magnitude, and the achievable air change rate, opening areas, smoke source, and outlet separation must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the ward or tent is relied upon.

The improvised doffing zone is where the field version is most often lost, because it is where contaminated material meets clean behavior. A corner of the tent marked with tape or a second small tent holds a hand hygiene station, a waste container, a laundry bag, and a mirror where available, because people remove a barrier more reliably when they can see what they are doing. The safety hazard is the order of removal, because a gown or glove that touches the face undoes the entire entry sequence, and the common error is to remove the respirator first, in the open, and then walk through the tent. The field consumable list is short and improvised, since cloth masks reduce droplet release but are not fitted respirators, and disinfection is usually a chlorine solution prepared fresh because chlorine loses strength in sunlight and within hours in an open container. The field failure modes are environmental, because the wind shifts the exhaust plume toward the waiting area, the fan battery dies, the duct falls from the window, and rain saturates the fabric, while the common error of adding a second fan inside the tent destroys the inward flow. The concentration, reuse limit, fan direction, battery reserve, duct route, and discharge clearance must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the tent is occupied.

Transport is a containment room that moves, and it follows the same physics with far less margin. An ambulance carrying a patient with an airborne infection is a small enclosed space with a strong airflow, so the goal is to move air from the front toward the patient compartment and out, never from the patient toward the driver. Climate system exhaust must be directed outside the cabin, and a vehicle with recirculation only redistributes the same air to everyone inside. A high-efficiency filter on the cabin system and an open window on the far side of the patient are useful where the local protocol allows, and the driver wears a fitted respirator whenever the partition is open. The patient is masked if it can be tolerated, corridors and elevators are cleared, and the destination room is prepared before the vehicle arrives, so the safety hazard is the unplanned stop, because a delay in a confined cabin multiplies exposure time. The honest statement is that the tent and the fan are not an airborne infection isolation room, and saying so is part of using them well, because they reduce the concentration of organisms that escape without eliminating risk. The airflow direction, filter specification, driver protection, and receiving room criteria must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the transfer begins.

To summarize, an isolation room is a set of physical controls built around a route of transmission, and its purpose is to change where the air and the contact go. The negative pressure room holds the patient at a lower pressure than the corridor, moves air inward, filters and exhausts it safely, and monitors the difference continuously. The positive pressure room reverses that flow to protect a vulnerable patient, while high-efficiency filtration captures particles by depth, the anteroom protects those who enter, and ultraviolet irradiation damages the genetic material of organisms in its line of sight. Cohorting, plastic sheeting, distance, and open windows are the compromises used when no engineered room exists, and the field tent, the battery fan, the duct, and the smoke test are the frontier version of the same idea. Every pressure, air change rate, efficiency, wavelength, contact time, and alarm threshold in this chapter must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before it is used on a patient.
