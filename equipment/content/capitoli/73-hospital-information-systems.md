# Chapter 73 — Hospital Information Systems

The hospital information system is the software and hardware that carries the patient's information from arrival to discharge and long afterward. It is an enormous and very literal filing cabinet that answers questions quickly, and every element in it is a fact with 3 parts, an identity, a time, and an author. Information is a pattern, and a pattern can be copied without loss, carried as pulses of voltage along a copper pair, flashed as light down a glass fiber, or broadcast as a radio wave, and rebuilt exactly at the far end. Because the pattern survives copying, the same fact appears on a ward screen, in the pharmacy, and in the operating theatre at once, and each place believes it holds the original. Every refresh interval, capacity limit, and alarm threshold named in this chapter must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The system holds 2 kinds of thing, data at rest and data in motion. Data at rest sits in a store and stays there after the power is removed. Data in motion crosses a network, where the pattern becomes a train of pulses, is given an address, and is handed from device to device until it reaches the right destination. A clock makes the whole thing trustworthy, because every event is stamped with a time and the record is rebuilt in that order, so devices that disagree about the time produce a record that contradicts itself. The time source, the identity rules, and the address scheme must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The electronic health record, called EHR, is the patient's chart in this form, and its structure follows the way clinicians ask questions rather than the way computers store files. It is organized around the patient's identity, usually a number issued once and never reused, and around the encounters, the episodes of care in which the patient met the institution. Inside an encounter sit the problems, the notes, the orders, the medication list, the results, the vital signs, and the documents, each stamped with author and time. The problem list is the spine of the record, because it is the short statement of what is being treated and it is what a colleague reads first. The identity format, the encounter rules, and the required fields must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Order entry and results reporting are the 2 loops that turn a filing cabinet into a clinical instrument. An order is a message carrying the requested action, the patient, the requesting clinician, and the reason, and it is routed to the department that must act on it. The result returns as another message, is attached to the same order, and stays flagged as new until a human being has opened it, so the loop closes only when someone has looked. The safety hazard is the warning that fires so often that staff learn to dismiss it, and the commonest operator error is to place the order on the wrong patient after choosing a name from a list of similar names without reading the identifier beneath it. The routing rules, the alert thresholds, and the critical result list must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Barcode medication administration ties the record to the patient's body at the moment of the dose, and its physical principle is the one used at every shop counter. A barcode is a printed pattern of dark bars and light spaces of varying width, and a scanner shines light onto it and measures how much is reflected back. Dark ink absorbs the light and a space between bars reflects it, so the reflected signal becomes a train of pulses that the decoder turns into characters. At the bedside the scanner reads 2 objects in sequence, the wristband on the patient and the label on the medication, so the software compares what was prescribed, what was dispensed, and who is in the bed. The wristband printer, the label stock, and the printed identifier must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The characteristic alarm of this loop is a mismatch warning, and it can be raised by 4 different faults. The wristband may be damaged, faded, or curled too tightly to read; the label may be printed on the wrong stock or smeared by a wet hand; the scanner battery may be too weak to illuminate the pattern; and the patient may genuinely be the wrong one. Each demands a different response, but the fatal one is the same in all 4 cases, because once staff learn that a stubborn barcode can be defeated by typing the number by hand, that shortcut is used for the wrong reasons. The scanner battery threshold, the label stock specification, and the barcode verification routine must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A nurse call system is an electrical circuit in which a switch closes and a current flows, and everything else is decoration on that principle. Each call point, whether a button on a handset, a plate beside the bed, or a pull cord in a bathroom, is wired to a loop that carries an address. When the switch closes, the controller learns which address has called, lights the indicator above the door, sounds a tone at the desk, and shows the bed on the annunciator panel. The cord is long enough to reach the floor, because a patient who has fallen beside the bed must still be able to pull it. The circuit voltage, the tone pattern, and the escalation interval after which an unanswered call is repeated must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The failure of a nurse call system is almost always a failure of silence rather than of sound. The tone may have been turned down at the desk, the call point may be hidden behind a bedside locker, the indicator lamp may have burned out, or the controller may be running on a backup battery whose charge has quietly expired. It also fails at the level of habit, because an answered call is not cancelled at the panel and the desk learns to ignore a light left on for an hour. The safety hazard is a patient who cannot reach help, and the commonest operator error is a call point left obstructed by equipment moved during the previous shift. The battery test interval, the tone volume, and the escalation time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Patient tracking and bed management are the same record seen from the point of view of space rather than care. Every patient occupies a location, and every location changes state as the patient is admitted, moved, or discharged, so the system is a machine with a fixed set of states and a few permitted transitions. The board at the ward desk, whether a screen or a white board with magnets, tells the coordinator which beds are free, occupied, reserved, or closed for cleaning. In a large hospital the location may also come from a radio frequency identification tag, called RFID, worn by the patient and read by receivers in the corridor ceiling, so the record updates itself and staff must confirm it is right. The location codes, the bed states, and the update interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The characteristic alarm of this part of the system is a bed that appears occupied when it is empty, or free when a patient is in it. A patient moved for an examination and returned to a different bed without the transfer being recorded leaves the record wrong in 2 places, and a clinician who trusts the board searches the wrong room. The most serious version is a missing patient, and units that care for confused patients fit door alarms and wrist tags whose failure is silent. The commonest operator error is the deferred update, in which the nurse intends to change the board after a task and the task leads to another. The transfer discipline, the reconciliation routine, and the door alarm test must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The laboratory information system is the record of specimens and results, and its most delicate part is the interface with the analyzers. A specimen tube is a physical object carrying a barcode that identifies the patient and the test, and the analyzer reads that barcode before it aspirates the sample, so the result is born with an identity attached. The interface is usually bidirectional, so the list of tests travels to the analyzer and the results travel back, and the software must match them to the order that began the process. The physical link is a serial cable or a network connection, fragile in a way the analyzer is not, because a cable pulled from its socket stops the flow without stopping the machine. The message format, the barcode rules, and the retransmission interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A result can be attached to the wrong patient in several ways, and every one begins with a tube in a hand. The tube may be labeled after it leaves the bedside, so label and blood no longer belong together, or the analyzer may be fed a tube whose barcode cannot be read and then be told the identity by hand. The characteristic alarm is a result produced but not transmitted, and the queue grows silently until someone opens the interface monitor. The critical result, which must be telephoned to the clinician and documented as received, is the one that cannot wait for that queue to clear. The labeling routine, the delta check limits, and the critical result call-back time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The radiology information system is the record of examinations, and its link to the image archive is the seam where most imaging errors are born. It holds the request, the appointment, the technologist, the room, and the report, while the images live in the picture archiving and communication system, called PACS, joined by an identifier that must survive every step. The images are carried in the standard called Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine, called DICOM, and the messages that tie a request to its report use the standard called Health Level Seven, called HL7. The bridge is a worklist the technologist sees at the console, so details are selected rather than typed, and the characteristic alarm is a study acquired without a report or a report that cannot be matched to a study. The worklist refresh interval, the matching fields, and the unread study target must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Device connectivity extends the record to the bedside machines, and it rests on the fact that a monitor or a pump is already a computer with a port. The port may be a serial connection, a universal serial bus connection, called USB, or a network socket, and the data leaving it are measurements with times. A gateway collects those streams, converts them to the format the record understands, and files them under the correct patient and bed. The difficulty is the mapping, because someone must tell the gateway which device is in which bed, and that decision must be maintained whenever the furniture moves. The port settings, the device to bed mapping, and the sampling interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

When the mapping is wrong, the record fills with another patient's numbers, and the error is plausible enough to be believed. The clocks of the devices and the server drift apart, so a value appears to have been measured before the patient arrived or after the transfer, and a trend drawn from mismatched times is a fiction. The characteristic alarm of the gateway is a device no longer found, or a stream that stopped, so a monitor disconnected from the network keeps alarming at the bedside while the central station shows nothing. The commonest operator error is to silence a bedside alarm and rely on the central station, which is precisely where the data may be missing. The time synchronization interval, the device mapping audit, and the stale data alarm must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The servers are the physical heart of the system, and each is an ordinary computer in a rack with processors, memory, storage, fans, and 1 or 2 power supplies. Its storage is usually several disks arranged so that a single failure does not destroy the data, built to survive a disk dying rather than a fire, which is why a separate copy is still required. The server room is a clinical dependency in its own right, needing power through an uninterruptible power supply, called UPS, cooling from the heating, ventilation, and air conditioning system, called HVAC, and a locked door. The redundant power configuration, the failover test schedule, and the room temperature and humidity limits must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The network is the part of the information system most often out of sight and most often at fault, and it begins with cable. A cable of twisted copper pairs carries the pulses as a difference of voltage between 2 conductors, and the twist cancels interference picked up along the run. The cables end in a patch panel and then in a switch, which learns which address is reachable through which port and forwards each frame only to the port that needs it. The cable standard, the maximum run length, and the switch port configuration must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Wireless coverage removes the cable but replaces it with a shared and variable medium, and the physics explains the misbehavior. An access point broadcasts in a band, commonly the 2.4 gigahertz band or the 5 gigahertz band, and those bands are also used by microwave ovens, cordless telephones, and neighboring access points, so the channel is crowded and the rate falls. Higher frequencies carry more data but travel less far and pass through walls less willingly, so a lead lined wall or a thick concrete column can create a dead spot that no software will cure. The design rule is to survey the building by measuring signal strength where devices are used, to plan for roaming between access points, and to reserve cable for anything that does not move. The band plan, the minimum signal level, and the coverage survey interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A backup is not a copy that exists but a copy that has been restored, the single most useful idea in the chapter. A full backup copies everything, an incremental backup copies only what has changed, and a good schedule combines them. The copy must be held somewhere the original cannot be harmed, which means a separate machine, a separate building, and at least 1 copy that ordinary users cannot alter, because extortion software encrypts every file it can reach. The practice that proves the arrangement is the scheduled restore test, in which a sample of records is recovered onto a spare system and found complete and readable. The backup schedule, the retention period, and the restore test interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The day the system stops is not a hypothetical, and the equipment that keeps the hospital working then is paper. A written downtime procedure names who decides the system is unavailable, who is called, how long the switch to paper will last, and how each ward records its work. The downtime box, sealed in a known place on every ward, holds the forms, the order sheets, the result log, the wristbands, and the instructions, and its seal is checked so nobody discovers an empty box during an outage. During the outage the paper record is the legal record, carrying the patient's identifier, the time, the drug and dose, and the signature of the person who acted, and after recovery every paper entry is reconciled against its electronic counterpart. The declared downtime threshold, the box contents, and the reconciliation deadline must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Security and privacy rest on 3 ideas, that only the right people may see a record, that every access must be recorded, and that the data must remain unreadable to anyone who intercepts it. Access begins with a unique account and a password or personal identification number never shared, because a shared login makes the audit trail worthless. The International Organization for Standardization, called ISO, publishes the information security family on which a hospital policy is usually built, and the International Electrotechnical Commission, called IEC, publishes guidance on the risk of joining a medical device to a shared network. Encryption protects data in motion and data at rest, so a stolen computer or a removed disk does not become a disclosure. The password rules, the session timeout, the encryption standard, and the audit retention period must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Access that is granted must also be revoked, and this is the task hospitals forget, because a departure is a human event and an account is a technical one. Every account must belong to a named person, contractors and students must have an end date set when they arrive, and a review must run on a schedule to find accounts of people who have left and accounts never used. Emergency access, sometimes called breaking the glass, must exist for the clinician who needs a record in a crisis, but it must be logged and reviewed the following day. The server room is part of the security boundary, with a lock, an entry log, and no stored cleaning materials, because a mop bucket beside a rack is a flood waiting to happen. The account review interval, the emergency access review, and the server room access list must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Maintenance of an information system is mostly preventive, mostly invisible, and mostly neglected, because a system that works gives no reason to look at it. The schedule covers the servers and their disks, the switches and their power supplies, the wireless access points, the workstations, the bedside scanners, and the printers, each with an interval, a task, and a written record. The tasks that matter most are dull, and they are reading the backup report rather than trusting it, checking free space, replacing every uninterruptible power supply battery on time, cleaning dust from every fan, and applying the software updates that close known faults. Updates are themselves a hazard, so a change is tested away from the clinical system, applied when the wards are quiet, and announced in advance. The service intervals, the free space threshold of 10 per cent, and the update window must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The operator's check at the start of a shift takes a few minutes and catches most of the faults that would otherwise waste a day. The clinician logs in with a personal account, confirms that the patient list shows the correct ward, opens 1 record and verifies that the results are today's rather than yesterday's. The nurse tests 1 call point from a bed, scans a wristband and a label with the bedside scanner, and prints a test label to confirm that the print is dark enough to read. The technician reads the overnight backup result, the free space on the main store, the temperature of the server room, and the list of unresolved alerts, and writes anything unusual in the log. The test record used, the label darkness standard, and the server room temperature range must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The consumables of an information system are few and cheap, yet one of them running out can stop a large service. They include wristbands and printed inserts, barcode labels in several sizes, thermal printer paper and ribbon, and cleaning swabs for the scanner glass. There are the batteries and charging docks for the scanners and call handsets, the spare patch cables that disappear one at a time, and the universal serial bus drives used to carry data between systems that are not joined. There is also the paper of the downtime box, stored for years and used in an hour, which must be replaced after every rehearsal and every real outage. The label adhesive, the wristband material, the printer paper specification, and the downtime form stock must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Cleaning is a clinical task here, because the shared keyboard and the bedside scanner are touched by many hands and washed by none. Keyboards, mouse, touch screens, scanners, workstations on wheels, and label printers are wiped between users with a compatible disinfectant, chosen after checking the maker's list, because alcohol attacks some plastics, bleach corrodes contacts, and a wet cloth drives liquid into a vent. The scanner glass is cleaned with a soft dry cloth first, because a scratched window causes failed reads that staff then defeat by typing the number by hand. Cables are inspected rather than scrubbed, and a frayed connector is replaced rather than taped, since a damaged cable is both a fault and a fire risk. The approved cleaning agents, the dwell time, and the cleaning frequency must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

In a mobile or frontier clinic that information system does not exist, and a telephone, a spreadsheet, and a paper register replace it. There is no server, no switch, no wireless coverage worth the name, and often no electric supply stable enough to keep a computer running through a clinic day. A mobile telephone is itself a radio with a subscriber identity module, called SIM, and a battery, and its coverage depends on a tower that may be distant, crowded, or powered by a generator that fails at night. A spreadsheet on a portable computer is a real database with rows, columns, and formulas, but it lives on 1 disk that can be dropped or corrupted, and it has no audit trail unless one is built by hand. The local power voltage, the charging routine, and the telephone coverage pattern must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The field system can be made trustworthy by 4 habits that require no electricity. The first is a single unique identifier for every patient, written on the register, the referral note, and any specimen, so a result can always be traced to a person even when names are spelled differently. The second is a double check at every entry, in which a second person reads the identifier and value aloud before it is written, because the error that matters is made quietly by a tired hand. The third is the parallel record, in which the register is photographed or copied each day and the copy carried to a place with power, so losing the original does not lose the day. The fourth is a written rule that a specimen is never labeled away from the bedside and a verbal order is always written down and read back. The identifier format, the double check routine, and the copying interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

What the field version gives up is the automatic check, the instant retrieval, and the shared view, and what it demands instead is discipline, redundancy, and a person who owns the register. Nobody is warned automatically about an interaction, so the clinician carries that knowledge in the head and in a printed formulary, and nothing can be looked up in seconds, so the register must be indexed well enough to find yesterday's entry. The register must be kept as carefully as a controlled drug book, with numbered pages, corrections signed rather than erased, and a storage place that is dry, locked, and known to the team. A radio or a telephone cannot replace it, because the battery dies and the listener writes on a scrap of paper, so the register remains the legal record and the memory of the clinic. The register format, the correction rules, and the retention period must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

To summarize, the hospital information system is a filing cabinet of facts, each carrying an identity, a time, and an author, held on servers and shown on screens at the bedside and the desk. The electronic health record gives it clinical shape, order entry and results reporting keep it moving, and the nurse call system, patient tracking, and bed management connect it to the ward. The laboratory information system, the radiology information system, and the connected monitors and pumps feed it from every department, and beneath them sit the server rack, the switch, the cable, the access point, the uninterruptible power supply, and the cooled room. Its safety depends on backups that have been restored rather than merely made, on downtime forms that have been rehearsed, and on access granted and revoked by name, while its hazards are the wrong patient selected, the alarm dismissed, the silent nurse call, the unsent result, and the shared password. In the field the telephone, the spreadsheet, and the paper register replace all of it, and they work only when a unique identifier, a double check, a daily copy, and a disciplined hand keep the record honest. Every refresh interval, capacity limit, alarm threshold, battery test interval, temperature limit, and retention period must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.
