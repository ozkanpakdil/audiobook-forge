# Chapter 66 — Collection and Drainage Systems

Every tube in a hospital ends somewhere, and the collection and drainage system is the apparatus that decides where. Its physical job is to move fluid away from the patient along a path that is downhill, one way, and closed, so that the fluid reaches a reservoir instead of the bed, the wound, or the worker. The clinical use follows the physics, since a reservoir below the source drains by gravity, a reservoir above the source can run backward into the patient, and a circuit open to the air invites organisms inward. This chapter describes the hospital-grade systems that collect urine, wound fluid, stool, bile, and respiratory secretions, and then the field variants assembled from bottles, bags, and buckets. Every setting, pressure, volume, temperature, and replacement interval named here must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before it is used on a patient.

Three physical ideas govern almost every collection system. The first is that a liquid finds its own level, so a reservoir hung below the patient drains by gravity alone and needs no pump. The second is that flow follows the pressure difference between two points, so a suction source simply makes the downstream pressure lower than the atmosphere. The third is that a one-way valve turns a tube into a one-way street, because a flexible membrane closes against reverse flow and opens with forward flow. When these ideas are respected the system is passive and reliable, and when one is violated the reservoir becomes a source of infection or the suction becomes a source of injury.

A closed system is one in which the fluid never meets the air of the room between the patient and the reservoir. Every opening in the circuit is a doorway through which organisms can travel against the intended direction of flow, and an open system has such a doorway at the catheter connection, at the bag mouth, and at the emptying spout. A closed system keeps a continuous path from the patient to a sealed reservoir and is emptied through a port that can be disinfected before and after use. The difference to infection has been measured, and it is the reason that modern urine drainage, wound drainage, and suction collection are all designed as closed circuits.

Hospital urine drainage begins at the indwelling urinary catheter and ends in a collection bag, and every part between them belongs to the same closed circuit. The catheter sits in the bladder, a length of wide-bore tubing carries the urine away, and the bag hangs on the frame of the bed below the level of the bladder. The tubing is wide because urine contains mucus, clots, and sediment, and the bag is transparent so that the color and volume can be seen without opening the circuit. A common adult night bag holds 2,000 milliliters, and that capacity must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the bag is hung.

A well-designed bag carries features the operator must recognize by sight and by touch. The inlet has a drip chamber, which lets the nurse see that urine is flowing rather than pooling, and the non-return valve below it closes when the bag is lifted. The sampling port is a needle-free access point where a specimen may be drawn after the port has been disinfected. The outlet tap at the bottom is the emptying spout that must never touch the floor or the measuring jug, and a vent when present lets air leave the bag as it fills.

The leg bag is a smaller reservoir strapped to the calf or the thigh so that a mobile patient is not tied to the bed. Its capacity is typically 500 milliliters, and that volume must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the bag is fitted, because a leg bag fills faster than its bearer expects. The physics does not change with the size, so the bag must still hang below the bladder level, and a leg bag strapped high on the thigh can push urine back into the bladder. The common operator error is to leave it in place for days without emptying or inspecting it, so that the straps dig into the skin and warm urine sits against the leg.

The night bag is the larger reservoir used while the patient sleeps, and it is usually connected to the leg bag through a link or joined directly to the catheter for the night. The correct routine is to empty the leg bag first, then disconnect at the catheter with a disinfected connection, then attach the night bag, and then leave the circuit undisturbed until morning. The safety hazard is the night bag hung on the bed rail above the bladder, which turns the bag into a reservoir that can siphon backward. The bag must be positioned below the bladder and above the floor, and that position must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the patient settles for the night.

Urine left in a bag is not sterile, and the reservoir is the warmest and quietest place in the system for organisms to multiply. Bacteria travel upward along the tubing from the bag toward the bladder, and the mucus layer that lines the catheter gives them a surface to climb. The longer a bag stays in place the more biofilm accumulates on its walls, and biofilm resists both antibiotics and disinfectants. The bag is therefore a consumable with a defined life, and the interval at which it is replaced varies with the manufacturer and the local protocol, so that interval must be confirmed before the system is changed. The clinical sign is urine that turns cloudy, foul, or bloodstained, and the characteristic event that follows is fever, so every bag must be treated as a device with an expiry.

The urometer is a graduated chamber inserted between the catheter and the collection bag so that urine output can be measured hour by hour rather than guessed. Its principle is that a narrow column of fluid magnifies the height of a small volume, so a few milliliters spread over a wide bag become a readable number in a tall chamber. A typical urometer holds 200 to 500 milliliters, and that capacity, together with the reading interval, must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the first measurement is taken. The device has no electrical alarm, so its alarm is the number that falls outside the expected range and stays there.

Hourly urine output is one of the earliest signals that a patient is losing circulation volume, developing kidney injury, or recovering from either. A fall in output that persists is the characteristic alarm of this device, and a sudden rise may signal the return of kidney function. The common operator error is to read the bag instead of the chamber, which averages away the very change the urometer was fitted to detect. The second error is to leave the tubing looped below the bladder, so that urine pools in the loop and the hourly reading lags behind the patient. The third error is to open the chamber valve without recording the volume, which erases the measurement permanently, so the tubing, the chamber, and the connection must be checked before every reading.

An ostomy pouch solves a problem that no valve can solve, because a stoma has no sphincter and the bowel empties on its own schedule. The physical principle is adhesion rather than control, since a sealed pouch is stuck to the skin around the stoma and the seal, not a muscle, prevents the output from reaching the clothing and the wound. That seal must hold against moisture, movement, and the digestive enzymes of the effluent, which is a demanding combination for any adhesive. When the seal fails the effluent contacts the skin, and the resulting excoriation is painful, slow to heal, and difficult to attach a new pouch to.

Ostomy pouches come in two fundamental arrangements, and the difference is where the adhesive lives. A one-piece pouch carries the adhesive wafer on the back of the bag, so the whole pouch is removed and replaced at every change. A two-piece system has a flange, also called a baseplate, that adheres to the skin, and a separate pouch that clicks or sticks onto it, so the pouch may be changed while the baseplate stays in place for several days. A typical wear interval is 1 to 3 days for a one-piece pouch and up to 7 days for a baseplate, and those intervals must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol for the individual patient. The common operator error is to cut the aperture by eye, which leaves a crescent of exposed skin that the effluent will find within hours.

Stoma care uses a small pharmacy of barrier products whose physics is the creation of a dry, protected surface. A barrier ring or a strip of barrier paste fills the crease between the stoma and the surrounding skin, so that the adhesive meets a level surface rather than a valley. A barrier powder absorbs moisture on skin that is already weeping, and it is dusted on and sealed with a barrier film before the pouch is applied. The safety hazard is skin denuded by repeated removal of adhesive, and the common operator error is to apply a new pouch over wet skin, so the choice of product and the wear interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

Emptying a pouch is a small operation with a large effect on the patient's comfort. The pouch is emptied when it is about one third full, because a heavy bag pulls on the adhesive and a full bag can lift the seal away from the skin. The character of the output carries clinical information, because a colostomy produces formed stool, an ileostomy produces a loose and enzyme-rich effluent that is corrosive to skin, and a urostomy produces urine. The safety hazard for the worker is the splash of ileostomy effluent into the eye, and the common operator error is to flush a pouch into a hand basin rather than a toilet, so the emptying routine and the disposal route must be confirmed against the local protocol.

Wound drainage uses the same physics as urine drainage, but the fluid is blood, serum, and lymph, and the collection vessel is often a canister under suction. A closed wound drain is a perforated tube placed in the wound bed and brought out through a separate incision, and it is attached either to a compressible bulb reservoir or to a flat drain joined to a spring-loaded cylindrical evacuator. The bulb or the spring holds a small negative pressure, which pulls fluid from the wound into the reservoir and keeps the wound edges together. A typical bulb reservoir holds 100 to 150 milliliters, and that volume must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the drain is activated.

Measuring wound drainage means recording the volume, the color, the consistency, and the odor, because each of these carries a different message. The volume is read from the graduations on the reservoir when the drain is emptied, and the empty volume is subtracted to obtain the output. A rising output of bright blood is the characteristic alarm of a wound drain, and it demands immediate attention because it may signal a bleeding vessel. The common operator error is to compress the bulb and then reconnect it without restoring the vacuum, so that the drain lies in the wound doing nothing, and the acceptable output volume and the action limits must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

The suction canister is the reservoir that stands between the patient and the vacuum source, and its design protects both the patient and the machine. It is a rigid jar of 1,000 to 3,000 milliliters, usually with a lid that carries an inlet for the patient tubing, an outlet to the regulator, and a float or shut-off valve that rises when the canister is full. A hydrophobic filter or a float closes the outlet before liquid can be drawn into the vacuum line, because fluid reaching the pump damages the machine and aerosolizes infectious material. A disposable liner fits inside the canister so that only the liner and its tubing are discarded, while the rigid outer jar is cleaned and reused. The fill line is usually marked at three quarters of the height, and that limit must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the canister is put into service.

Measuring what a suction canister holds is more difficult than it looks, and the difficulty is a recognized source of error. The canister often stands on the floor or hangs below the eye of the nurse, and the graduations are read at an angle that distorts the level. The safety hazard is the overflow valve that fails, so the canister overfills and the fluid reaches the regulator and the pump. The characteristic warning is a change in the sound of the suction or the whistle of a float valve as it closes, and an electronic regulator may add a low-vacuum alarm, so the measurement method, the fill limit, and the alarm threshold must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

A specimen trap is a small container inserted into the suction line so that a sample of secretion is captured before it is diluted in the canister. The trap has three ports, one from the patient, one to the suction source, and one that is sealed and used to send the specimen to the laboratory. The common operator error is to insert the trap upside down or to let it lie on its side, so that the specimen is drawn straight through and the trap arrives at the laboratory empty. The second error is to fill the trap so completely that the secretion is aspirated into the tubing and contaminates the whole circuit, so the trap must be labeled at the bedside and the labeling rule confirmed against the local protocol.

Stool and bile collection each have their own container and their own timing, and both are less trivial than they appear. A formed stool specimen is placed in a clean, wide-mouthed container with a tight lid, and a small quantity is sufficient for most tests, while a liquid stool may need to be transported within a short interval because the organisms and parasites in it change as it cools. Bile is collected after biliary surgery or when a T-shaped tube is placed in the common bile duct, and it follows the same gravity and closed-circuit rules as urine, with the bag hanging below the patient and the daily volume and color recorded. The safety hazard is the container that leaks in transit, and the common operator error is to collect stool from a bedpan after it has mixed with urine, so the label, the storage temperature, and the transport interval must be confirmed against the local protocol.

Emptying a collection bag or canister is the task that puts the worker in closest contact with the fluid, so the technique matters as much as the device. The person empties the reservoir into a designated toilet or sluice, wearing gloves and a plastic apron, and eye protection when a splash is possible. The spout is kept below the rim of the receiving vessel and never allowed to touch it, and the spout is wiped with a disinfectant after emptying and before it is closed. The hands are washed after the gloves are removed, and the gloves are removed without touching their outer surface. The safety hazard is the splash of urine or wound fluid into the eye or onto broken skin, and the common operator error is to carry an uncovered jug of fluid through a corridor, so the receiving vessel, the disinfectant, and the disposal route must be confirmed against the local protocol.

Some collection hardware is disposable and some is reusable, and the two demand completely different handling. A disposable bag, liner, or trap is used once and discarded into the infectious waste stream, and it must never be rinsed and returned to the shelf. A reusable rigid canister, rack, or trolley is cleaned of visible soil, then disinfected with a chemical agent such as a hypochlorite solution at a measured concentration and contact time, then dried and stored inverted. If a reusable item is to be sterilized it is processed by steam, and a common cycle is 134 degrees Celsius for a short hold, and that temperature, that hold time, and the chosen method must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol. The safety hazard is the canister that is wiped but not cleaned inside, because dried protein shelters organisms, and the common operator error is to top up a disinfectant bath rather than replace it.

A collection system consumes a steady stream of small items, and their absence stops the system as surely as a broken pump. The list includes drainage bags of several sizes, leg bags with straps, catheter securement devices, non-return valves, tap caps, canister liners, hydrophobic filters, specimen traps, ostomy pouches, barrier rings, barrier powder, adhesive remover, wound drain reservoirs, and the labels that identify every one of them. These items must be stored clean and dry, away from direct sunlight, and rotated so that the oldest stock is used first. The expiry date matters most for the sterile items and for the adhesives, because a barrier that has passed its date may not stick and a sterile bag stored damp may no longer be sterile. The safety hazard is the ward that improvises a missing part with a non-sterile substitute, and the common operator error is to store bags beside a heat source, so the par levels, the storage conditions, and the expiry rules must be confirmed against the local protocol.

The failure modes of a collection system are mostly quiet, and the quiet ones are the dangerous ones. A bag can leak at a seam, a tubing connection can separate, a non-return valve can stick open, a tap can be left open, a canister float can jam, a filter can become wet and block the suction, and a catheter can be pulled out by a confused patient. The characteristic alarm of the electrically driven part is the low-vacuum alarm of the regulator, which sounds when the circuit is blocked or disconnected and the pump cannot reach its set pressure. The mechanical canister warns of fullness with the whistle or the click of a float valve, and the gravity bag has no alarm at all, so its warning is the wet sheet or the swollen leg. The safety hazard common to all of them is fluid that reaches the vacuum pump or the electrical socket, and the common operator error is to silence an alarm without finding its cause, so the alarm limits and the response to each alarm must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

The operator who starts a shift with a collection system should be able to answer a short list of questions about it. Is the reservoir below the source and above the floor, is the tubing free of loops and kinks, is the connection secure, is the non-return valve working, is the tap closed and capped, and is the volume inside the bag recorded? For a suction system the questions add the level of the canister against its fill line, the condition of the liner, the seal of the lid, the setting of the regulator, and the patency of the filter. For an ostomy they add the condition of the skin, the fit of the aperture, and the age of the baseplate. The answers are best written down, because a system checked by memory alone is a system checked only on the days when nothing is wrong, so the check and the corrective action must be confirmed against the local protocol.

The field variant of these systems keeps the physics and discards the plumbing. Where a hospital has wall vacuum, sterile bags, and a sluice, a mobile or frontier clinic has a bottle, a length of tubing, a bucket, and a hand pump. What the field gives up is convenience, measurable accuracy, and the assurance of a sterile circuit, and what it demands instead is discipline, improvisation, and a clear understanding of what the substitute cannot do. Every substituted component must be checked for the property it is meant to provide, because a bottle that leaks does not drain and a bag that is not closed does not protect.

Field urine collection usually means a bottle or a plastic bag hung on the bed frame at the foot of the mattress. A clean bottle with a hole in the cap and a short length of tubing can serve as a closed reservoir, and it must hang below the bladder and be emptied at a fixed interval, and that interval must be confirmed against the local protocol. A mobile patient has no leg bag and no catheter strap, so the reservoir is carried in a cloth bag or held in the hand, and the tubing is secured to the thigh with tape. The safety hazard is the container hung above the patient, which allows urine to run back into the bladder, and the common operator error is to rest the container on the floor beside the bed where it is kicked over.

Field suction relies on a hand pump, a foot pump, or a battery-powered pump rather than a wall vacuum line, and the reservoir is usually a bottle in series with the pump. The principle is unchanged, because the pump lowers the pressure in the bottle and the secretion travels from the patient into the bottle, while a float or a simple trap prevents the liquid from reaching the pump. The vacuum is generated by strokes of the pump, so the suction is intermittent and the operator must watch the secretion rather than trust a gauge. The safety hazard is fluid drawn into a manual pump, which cannot be cleaned internally and becomes a reservoir of organisms, and the pressure setting and the maximum suction must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

An ostomy patient in the field often manages with a plastic bag and adhesive tape, and the substitution demands more of the patient rather than less. A clean plastic bag is cut to fit the stoma, the skin is washed and dried thoroughly, and the bag is fixed with a wide tape that overlaps the skin by a comfortable margin. The bag is changed as soon as it leaks, because effluent left against the skin causes excoriation within hours, and the interval between changes must be confirmed against the local protocol. The safety hazard is the reuse of a bag that has been rinsed but not disinfected, and the common operator error is to cut the aperture too large, so the worker must teach the patient to inspect the skin at every change.

Field wound drainage is often an open drain and a gauze dressing rather than a closed suction canister, and the measurement becomes an estimate. A strip of latex or silicone is placed in the wound and covered with an absorbent dressing, and the dressing is changed when it is saturated, so the output is judged by the number of dressings rather than by a volume. The safety hazard is the drain that is pulled out when the dressing is changed, and the common operator error is to pack the wound so tightly that the drain cannot function. The character of the fluid still matters, so the worker records the color and the smell, and the drain type and the dressing change interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

Sample collection and disposal complete the field system, and both are steps where improvisation carries the greatest risk. A suction specimen can be caught in a small sterile bottle placed in the line, and the bottle must stand upright and be sealed immediately after the sample is taken. Stool is collected in a wide-mouthed jar with a lid and kept out of the sun, while bile from a T-shaped tube is collected in a bottle hung below the patient and changed with the same care as a urine bag. Disposal means pouring urine and wound fluid into a latrine or a soak pit and never into a stream, a well, or a garden, while bags, liners, and traps are buried or burned as infectious waste. The safety hazard is the child or the scavenger who finds a discarded bag or a needle, and the common operator error is to label a container at the end of the round, so the burial depth and the separation distance from water must be confirmed against the local protocol.

In the hospital, disposal is designed to keep the fluid and the container on separate paths, and maintenance keeps both paths working. The fluid is emptied into a sluice or a dedicated hopper that discharges to the sanitary sewer, while the container is placed in the infectious waste stream, and a canister liner is closed with its own lid and discarded as infectious waste. Trolleys and racks are cleaned on a schedule, regulators and pumps are tested for the vacuum they can hold, and reusable parts are inspected for cracks, worn washers, and loose lids. The safety hazard is the reusable canister stored wet, because a wet filter blocks the suction and a wet jar grows organisms, so the sluice design, the vacuum test value, and the filter replacement schedule must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

To summarize, a collection and drainage system is the far end of every tube, and it decides whether the fluid leaving a patient is contained or released into the ward. Gravity, a pressure difference, and a one-way valve are the physical ideas that make these systems work, and a closed circuit is the design choice that keeps organisms from traveling back toward the patient. The hospital version provides graduated bags, urometers, sealed canisters with liners and overflow valves, specimen traps, ostomy pouches with engineered barriers, and a waste route that separates fluid from container. The field version provides a bottle, a bag, a hand pump, and a bucket, and it gives up accuracy and sterility in exchange for availability, while demanding more discipline from the person who empties it. In both settings the safety hazard is the splash and the reflux, the common operator error is the broken circuit and the unrecorded volume, and the characteristic alarm is often the absence of one, so every setting, volume, pressure, and replacement interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before it is used on a patient.
