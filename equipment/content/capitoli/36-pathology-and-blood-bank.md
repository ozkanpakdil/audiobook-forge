# Chapter 36 — Pathology and Blood Bank

Pathology and the blood bank look nothing alike, yet both rest on one idea: tissue and blood must be stabilized before they can be examined. The pathology laboratory turns tissue into a glass slide thin enough for light to pass, using chemistry, heat, wax, and a sharp blade. The blood bank keeps cells and fragile proteins usable outside the body, using cold, gentle motion, identity, and an unbroken temperature record. Both are chains, judged by the weakest link rather than the finest instrument. This chapter describes the hospital-grade machines of each department, then the field version. In the field, one refrigerator, one thermometer, and one log sheet carry the whole burden, and every setting, temperature, time, and alarm threshold named here must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The first step of pathology is fixation, and its principle is chemical cross linking. Formaldehyde in buffered water diffuses into the tissue and bonds proteins together, locking the structure of life in place. Diffusion advances only about 1 millimeter each hour, so a thick specimen fixes at its surface while its center stays soft. Blocks are therefore trimmed thin and fixed at about 10 parts fixative to 1 part tissue, a ratio and a time that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The vapor irritates the eyes and airway and is a human carcinogen, so fixation belongs under a fume hood, and the common error is impatience, because a block fixed for an hour stays raw at its center while no alarm warns anyone.

The tissue processor replaces the water in a specimen with wax, because only a solid block cuts into slices a few micrometers thick. It moves cassettes through heated pots: alcohols remove water, a clearing agent such as xylene mixes with both alcohol and wax, and molten paraffin replaces the clearing agent. A schedule commonly runs overnight for 8 to 12 hours, a time that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The vapors are the hazard, since xylene is flammable and neurotoxic and formaldehyde is a carcinogen, so the machine needs extraction and the operator needs personal protective equipment, PPE for short. Its characteristic alarms are a low reagent level, an over temperature condition, and a blocked drain, each confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The embedding station bridges processing and cutting, with a heated paraffin bath, a dispenser, a warm surface, and a cold plate. The operator orients the tissue in a mold, fills it with molten paraffin, and sets it on the cold plate, where the wax hardens around the chosen plane. The principle is support: the wax must resist the blade without shattering, and the tissue must lie where the pathologist needs to see it. The hazard is the burn, because the bath holds wax hotter than boiling water. The common errors are air trapped against the tissue and wax that is too hot, which shrinks as it cools, and the bath temperature and hardening time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The microtome gives pathology its name, and its principle is a precise advance. A block holder carries the wax block past a steel blade while a micrometer screw advances it a set distance each pass, lifting a ribbon of sections. The sections are usually 3 to 5 micrometers thick, a setting that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The hazard is the blade, which cuts to the bone in one movement, so blades are handled with forceps and the holder is locked whenever the hand leaves it. The common errors are a blunt blade, a section cut too thick, and a vibration called chatter, and the microtome has no alarm, so all of these must be recognized in the ribbon.

The cryostat answers questions that cannot wait a day for processing, and its principle is that freezing hardens tissue without chemical fixation. A fresh specimen is frozen on a chuck and cut inside a chamber held near minus 20 degrees Celsius, a temperature that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The surgeon can then have an answer in minutes about a margin or a lump. Cutting generates an aerosol of infectious material, so the operator wears gloves and a mask, works behind the window, and disinfects the chamber with a tuberculocidal agent afterward. The common errors are freezing too slowly, which grows ice crystals that tear cells, and setting the chamber too cold, which shatters the block, and the characteristic alarm is a chamber temperature alarm, confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The staining station gives the colorless section its contrast, because charged dyes bind oppositely charged structures. Hematoxylin, a basic dye, colors the acidic nuclei blue, while eosin, an acidic dye, colors the basic cytoplasm pink. A manual station is a row of dishes, and a rack of slides is dipped from dish to dish on a timetable of seconds and minutes, which must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The automated stainer performs the same journey with a robot arm and buys consistency at the price of complexity. The hazards are solvent vapors and the carryover of one reagent into the next, and the characteristic alarms are a low reagent level and a jammed carrier, each confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A stained section is fragile, and the coverslipper seals it under thin glass with a drop of mounting medium. The medium has a refractive index close to that of glass, so light crosses the coverslip, the medium, the section, and the slide with little scattering. A manual coverslipper demands a steady hand because the section dries in seconds, while the automated instrument places the drop, lowers the slip, and warms the slide. The hazard is the xylene based solvent, which needs ventilation, and the common errors are trapped bubbles, too little medium so the section darkens, and a slip placed off center, with drying time and volume confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The microscope turns a stained section into a diagnosis, and its principle is the bending of light by lenses. The condenser focuses light on the specimen, the objective forms a magnified image, and the eyepiece magnifies it again. Resolution is set by the wavelength of light and the numerical aperture, and a good light microscope resolves about 0.2 micrometers, a limit that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The oil immersion objective reaches its high aperture because a drop of oil, with the refractive index of glass, replaces the air above the slide. Eye strain and lamp heat are the everyday hazards, and a fluorescence microscope adds ultraviolet light that can damage the eye. The common errors are oil on a dry objective, a lowered condenser that destroys contrast, and a lens wiped with a dry tissue, and the microscope has no alarm, so the warning is the image itself.

The slide scanner converts a glass slide into a digital image, and its principle is a camera on a moving stage. The stage carries the slide under the objective in a raster pattern, the software focuses each field, and the tiles are stitched into one large image. The value is remote review, consultation, teaching, and an archive that cannot fade, and the price is a large file and a dependence on power, storage, and network. Identity is the hazard, because a scanner will digitize a mislabeled slide and the error travels into the record unquestioned, so a barcode is read and matched before the scan. The common errors are scanning a wet or warped slide, which throws the focus off, and omitting the daily calibration slide, and the characteristic alarms are a focus failure, a barcode mismatch, and a full storage warning, each confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The immunohistochemistry platform detects specific proteins, and its principle is the lock and key fit of an antibody to its antigen. An antibody binds its target, a second reagent carries an enzyme, and a colorless substrate becomes a colored deposit at the exact site. Many targets need antigen retrieval, in which heat or an enzyme reopens the cross links made by fixation, and the retrieval method and antibody dilution must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The platform is a heated humid chamber that dispenses reagents on a timed program, and it is unforgiving of a section that dries out, because drying concentrates salts and creates background staining. The chromogen, commonly diaminobenzidine, is a suspected carcinogen whose waste needs a defined route, and the characteristic alarms are a reagent level, a temperature, and a waste container full alarm, each confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The pathology laboratory is a chemical workplace as much as a clinical one. Formaldehyde is a carcinogen and an irritant, xylene is flammable and neurotoxic, the alcohols are flammable, and the acids used to soften bone are corrosive. All of them need bench extraction, closed containers, a spill kit, and a flammable storage cabinet, because the vapor is the danger. Personal protective equipment, PPE for short, means gloves chosen for the specific chemical, eye protection, and a gown, and the choice must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The common error is complacency with a familiar smell, because a nose that no longer notices formaldehyde is not protected from it.

The blood bank is a chain of temperatures and identities, and its principle is that cold slows living processes. Refrigeration slows the metabolism of red cells and the growth of bacteria, freezing suspends the decay of clotting factors, and constant motion keeps platelets from clumping. A unit held at the wrong temperature for the wrong time is wasted at best and dangerous at worst. The department is built around a refrigerator, a freezer, an incubator with an agitator, a refrigerated centrifuge, a thawing device, a crossmatching system, an irradiator, and a record tying every unit to a patient. Every temperature, time, and alarm threshold in this part of the chapter must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the characteristic failure of a blood bank is quiet, discovered only when the unit hangs at the bedside.

The refrigerated centrifuge separates whole blood into components, and its principle is density under a spinning load. A bag held at a fixed radius and spun at high speed feels a force many times that of gravity, so red cells pack at the bottom and plasma remains on top. The chamber is held near 4 degrees Celsius because spinning generates heat and warming blood damages cells, a temperature that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A heavy spin separates red cells from plasma, while a light spin first separates platelet rich plasma. Imbalance is the hazard, because a rotor carrying unequal masses at high speed can destroy itself and its bowl, and its alarms are an imbalance alarm and a lid interlock, each confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The blood bank refrigerator looks domestic and is nothing of the kind. Its principle is forced air cooling with a circulating fan, so every shelf stays within a narrow band instead of the wide swing of a household thermostat. Red cells are held between 1 and 6 degrees Celsius, a range that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, because below it cells freeze and above it bacteria multiply. Its defining feature is the alarm system: high and low temperature alarms, a power failure alarm, a door ajar alarm, and often a remote alarm repeated at a nursing station, all confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The temperature chart is the legal memory of the unit, and the common errors are silencing the alarm instead of answering it, storing food beside the blood, and overloading the shelves so that air cannot circulate.

Plasma is frozen because its clotting factors are proteins that lose activity at refrigerator temperature, and cold below the freezing point of the water in plasma suspends that decay. The storage temperature is minus 30 degrees Celsius or colder, a setting that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The freezer is a thick walled cabinet that depends on its alarm and its log exactly as the refrigerator does. The hazard is frost, which builds on the evaporator and acts as insulation, so the compressor runs longer and the cabinet warms without any fault until a limit is crossed. Its characteristic alarms are a high temperature alarm and a power failure alarm, each confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the common errors are placing warm plasma into the freezer and stacking units against the walls.

Platelets are living fragments without a nucleus, they cannot be frozen, and they are therefore stored warm and moving. Their storage temperature is 20 to 24 degrees Celsius, and they rest on an agitator that rocks them gently and continuously, a temperature and a motion that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The principle is gas exchange and suspension, because platelets clump when they are still and the rocking lets oxygen reach them. Because they are stored warm and the bag has been entered, bacteria are the dominant risk, so the storage period is commonly limited to 5 days, a limit that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Its alarms are a temperature alarm and an agitation failure alarm, and the common errors are switching off the agitator during a power cut and stacking bags so the lower ones cannot move.

Frozen plasma must be returned to body temperature before transfusion, and the thawing device is where the cold chain meets the patient. Its principle is conduction, because heat passes from a warm medium into the bag until the ice becomes liquid, and the medium is held at about 37 degrees Celsius, a temperature that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A water bath thaws quickly, but water is a contamination risk, so the ports of the bag stay above the surface and the bath is cleaned on a schedule. A dry plasma thawer uses warm plates or circulating air and removes that risk at the cost of a slower thaw. Overheating destroys the clotting factors the plasma was frozen to preserve, and its characteristic alarms are an over temperature alarm and a low water alarm, each confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Crossmatching asks whether the recipient's serum will destroy the donor's red cells, and the tube method shows the answer as clumping. Its principle is that an antibody binds two cells at once and bridges them into a lattice that settles as visible agglutination. The stages each have a purpose: an immediate spin detects the strong antibodies of the major blood groups, and incubation at 37 degrees Celsius, a temperature that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, lets weaker antibodies bind. Antihuman globulin then reveals antibodies that coated the cells without bridging them, which is why the cells are washed first. The hazard is clerical, because a sample from the wrong patient gives a perfect result for the wrong person, and no laboratory test can detect that mistake. The common errors are reading too quickly and missing a weak reaction, and shortening the wash so that free antibody consumes the reagent, and the tube method has no alarm, so the result is only as good as the hand holding the rack.

The gel column system replaces the tube with a card holding a column of gel beads, and its principle is filtration by size. Serum and cells are mixed and incubated above the column, and the card is spun; unagglutinated cells pass between the beads and form a button at the bottom, while agglutinated cells are trapped higher up. The height of the red cell layer reports the strength of the reaction, and because every card is spun for the same time at the same force the reading is less subjective than a tube. The common errors are a card with a broken seal or a bubble in the gel, the wrong volume of cells or serum, and a centrifuge not calibrated to the card setting, all confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The hazard remains the human one, because a clean automated card invites trust, and it can only be trusted if the sample belongs to the patient on the label.

An electronic crossmatch replaces the physical mixing of serum and cells with a calculation by a validated computer system, and its principle is that compatibility can be proven from data when the data are complete. The system holds two independent blood group results from two separate samples, with the donor unit's group, and compares them by the rules of the major blood group and rhesus systems. The prerequisites are strict: two concordant results, no history of a clinically significant antibody, a current negative antibody screen, a validated system with secure identity and interfaces to the laboratory and the refrigerator, and a written procedure that returns to a serologic crossmatch the moment any prerequisite fails. The hazard is silent, because a wrong result here looks exactly like a right one, and the cause is a data entry error rather than a reaction in a tube. Its characteristic alarm is a mismatch flag that blocks the release of a unit, confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Some immunosuppressed patients can be attacked by the white cells in a donated unit, a complication called transfusion-associated graft-versus-host disease, and the irradiator prevents it. Ionizing radiation from a sealed source of cobalt 60 or cesium 137, or from a high energy X-ray tube, breaks the deoxyribonucleic acid of donor lymphocytes so that they cannot multiply, while red cells, platelets, and clotting proteins survive. The dose is in the range of 25 gray to 50 gray delivered to the center of the bag, and both the dose and the calibration must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Irradiation is not sterilization, so it never replaces aseptic technique. The radiation is the hazard, so the source sits behind a shielded door interlock, the operator wears a dosimetry badge, and the characteristic alarms are a door interlock, a timer, and a source position alarm, each confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The cold chain does not end at the blood bank door, and the transport box is a device in its own right. A validated passive shipper holds units between panels that change phase at a chosen temperature, so the box absorbs heat without letting its contents warm, and a data logger records the journey. The principle is thermal mass and insulation: the box is a battery of cold, with a capacity measured in hours at a stated outside temperature, figures that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The hazard is the box opened at every stop, because each opening exchanges cold air for warm. The common errors are packing units that were not pre cooled, placing frozen packs in direct contact with red cells so they hemolyze, and sending a box without a logger, and acceptance limits at receipt must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Blood banking is a discipline of small routines, and the routines are equipment. Every refrigerator and freezer carries a thermometer whose calibration is checked against a reference on a schedule, because a thermometer reading 1 degree low lets a unit drift out of range while the log looks perfect. Every alarm is tested by simulating the fault, because an alarm that has never been heard is an assumption rather than a safeguard, and the test interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The units that hold blood usually sit on an uninterruptible power supply, UPS for short, and on a circuit separate from the general lighting, so one breaker does not silence the department. The chart, the alarm log, the maintenance record, and the unit history are reviewed together, because a flat line at an impossible value is a broken pen rather than a stable cabinet, and signing a log that was not read is the common failure.

In a mobile clinic or a district hospital with no blood bank refrigerator, this department becomes one refrigerator, one thermometer, one log sheet, and the discipline that makes them work. The refrigerator is usually a domestic or pharmaceutical unit, and it must be understood as neither, because its thermostat swings widely, its coldest spot freezes red cells, and its door opens many times a day. The thermometer sits at the warmest usable point, found by trial with a second thermometer on each shelf, and the reading is written twice a day at fixed hours, with the range of 1 to 6 degrees Celsius confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The unit is dedicated to blood, kept locked, placed in the coolest room away from the sun and the generator, and never used for food, drink, or specimens. Because there is no alarm, the thermometer and the log are the alarm, and a missed reading is a gap in the safety of every unit inside. The field blood bank also needs a written plan for the power cut, naming who moves the units, where they go, and how long the door may stay closed, and that time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Transport in the field is usually a cold box carried by hand, by motorcycle, or on a bicycle, and it obeys the same physics as the hospital shipper. A vaccine carrier with frozen water packs and a lining of cloth or cardboard holds a few units for hours, provided the packs never touch the bags directly, because contact freezing damages red cells. The box has a budget of cold, and every opening spends some of it, so the most distant delivery is opened last. The hazard is heat, because a box left in the sun or a closed vehicle loses its reserve far faster than the plan assumed. The common errors are packing the box the night before, trusting a hand on the outside instead of a thermometer inside, and sending it without a written record of departure and arrival temperatures, and the packing arrangement and maximum journey time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The field version of pathology is not a small histology laboratory, because no field clinic runs a processor, a microtome, and a cryostat. What it can do is the smear and the stained preparation, in which a drop of blood or a touch of a lesion is spread thinly on a slide, fixed in alcohol, stained, and read under a light microscope. The field microscope is often a robust monocular instrument with a mirror or a battery powered light emitting diode, LED for short, and it gives up automated illumination, multiple objectives, and the camera of the hospital instrument. The hazards are the flammable fixative and stain and the sharps used to make the smear, so the work happens on a clean surface with gloves and separated waste. The common errors are a smear that is too thick, a stain left on too long, and a pencil label that rubs off in transit, and the staining times and fixative choice must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Anything that needs tissue architecture rather than single cells must travel, and the transport pot is the field pathologist's most important equipment. A biopsy is placed in a wide mouthed container of buffered formaldehyde at least 10 times its own volume, sealed, labeled with the patient's identity and the site, and packed so it cannot leak, and the volume ratio and fixation time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Fixation continues during the journey, so the tissue is being processed while the vehicle moves, and a specimen that arrives after two warm days is usually fixed even if not ideal. The hazard is the leak, because formaldehyde is a carcinogen and a spill inside a vehicle is a serious event. The common errors are a container that is too small, ink that runs, and a request form that travels separately from the specimen, and reagents must stay within their storage temperature and expiry, both confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Certain failures recur in these two departments often enough to be named as a group. The alarm that everyone silences and no one investigates, on a blood refrigerator or a tissue processor, is the most costly device in the room because it trains the staff to ignore the next alarm. The chart recorder with a flat line, the thermometer that was never calibrated, the gasket gone hard, and the condenser coils matted with dust are quiet failures that leave no trace until an audit or an accident. The refrigerator on a circuit switched off at night, the freezer that thaws and is quietly refrozen, and the thawed plasma returned to the freezer turn a cold chain into a story. In pathology the matching failures are the exhausted stain bath, the blunt blade, the processor interrupted by a power cut, and the cryostat disinfected with a chemical that does not kill mycobacteria, so the operator must check the reading, the date, the seal, the blade, and the reagent at the stated interval.

The consumables of this chapter are easy to overlook because they are silent and small. The histology bench depends on cassettes and lids, embedding molds, disposable blades, glass slides and coverslips, mounting medium, stains and solvents, antibodies and retrieval solutions, and paraffin itself, and every one carries a lot number and an expiry that must be recorded. The blood bank depends on crossmatch tubes or gel cards, reagent red cells, antiglobulin reagent, saline, blood bags and labels, irradiation indicators, loggers, thermometers, chart paper, and log sheets, and a reagent is part of the test, so an expired card changes the result rather than merely the supply. The routine maintenance an operator can do is short: clean the microscope lenses, change the lamp, defrost the freezer, clean the thawing bath, check the agitator motion, clean the centrifuge chamber, change the chart paper, and test the alarm. Cleaning the cryostat and the staining bench uses a tuberculocidal disinfectant and the manufacturer's named agent, with contact time confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and no operator should open a refrigeration circuit or a sealed radiation source.

To summarize, the pathology laboratory and the blood bank are both chains of preparation, and every link is a device with a physical principle behind it. Pathology fixes tissue by chemical cross linking, replaces its water with wax, cuts it on a micrometer screw, freezes it when speed matters, colors it with oppositely charged dyes, seals it under glass, and reads it through lenses whose resolution is set by wavelength and numerical aperture. The blood bank keeps cells and proteins alive by cold and gentle motion, separates them by density in a refrigerated centrifuge, proves compatibility by the bridging of antibodies or by validated data, and protects the immunosuppressed recipient with ionizing radiation. In the hospital these functions rest on narrow temperature bands, audited alarms, calibrated thermometers, and written records, while in the field they rest on one refrigerator, one thermometer, one log sheet, and a person who reads them at the stated hour. The hazards are formaldehyde, xylene, sharp blades, infectious aerosols, cold surfaces, and radiation, and the recurring errors are the silenced alarm, the uncalibrated thermometer, the unread log, the mislabeled specimen, and the overridden computer stop. The single habit that holds both departments together is confirming every number against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before it is used on a patient.
