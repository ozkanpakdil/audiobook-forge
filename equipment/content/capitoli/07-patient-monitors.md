# Chapter 7 — Patient Monitors

A patient monitor is a machine that watches the body and turns its activity into lines and numbers. It is worth saying at the outset what the monitor actually does, because its name is misleading. The monitor measures very little by itself. Every channel begins with a sensor that converts one physical event into a small electrical signal: a voltage generated by heart muscle on the skin, a pulse of light passed through a fingertip, a pressure wave carried along a column of fluid, a change in the absorption of infrared light in a breath. The monitor receives those signals, amplifies them, removes what does not belong, converts them into digits, and presents the result on a screen. This is why a fault in a sensor can look exactly like a fault in a patient, and why an experienced clinician always asks which of the two is more likely before reacting.

The screen is the part everyone recognizes: a dark panel with bright traces, numbers beside each trace, and a row of small symbols for alarms and battery state. Most hospital monitors use a liquid crystal display, LCD for short, lit from behind by a light emitting diode, LED, so that it can be read at an angle in a bright room. The traces scroll from right to left, and the speed of that scroll is a setting. The numerics are updated on their own schedule, often once every second, and they can freeze in a plausible position when a sensor fails, which makes a still number far more dangerous than an empty one. The listener should picture a monitor as two different devices sharing one case, a waveform display for shape and rhythm, and a numeric display for trend and threshold. Reading only the numbers is a common and quiet error, because the shape of a waveform carries information that no number can hold.

Behind the screen sit the modules, the removable blocks that give the monitor its channels. A basic unit may hold only electrocardiography and cuff blood pressure. A fuller chassis accepts a plug in module for invasive pressures, another for exhaled carbon dioxide, another for temperature, another for cardiac output or depth of anesthesia. Each module has its own connector block on the front or side, its own patient cable, and often its own calibration and self test. The physical hazard at this level is electrical. The patient cable is designed to isolate the patient from the mains supply, and any monitor that has been dropped, wetted, or repaired outside the manufacturer's instructions may leak current. Every electrical safety parameter and test interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is returned to a patient.

Inside the chassis the signal path is much the same for most channels. The sensor voltage passes through an amplifier that raises a signal measured in millionths of a volt to something a circuit can use, then through filters that remove muscle noise, mains hum, and drift, then through an analog to digital converter that samples the signal many times each second. A typical electrocardiography channel samples hundreds of times per second, for example 500 times per second, and the sampling rate, the filter settings, and the display gain are all configurable. These are settings, and every one of them must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The chassis also holds the alarm logic and the network connection, which is why a monitor with a dead network card can still display perfectly while no one at a distance can see it. Standards from the International Electrotechnical Commission, IEC, and the International Organization for Standardization, ISO, describe how these devices must behave, but a standard on paper does not inspect a cable.

The electrocardiography channel, ECG for short, is the channel most people mean when they say monitor. Heart muscle generates a small voltage as it depolarizes, and that voltage spreads through the body to the skin, where electrodes pick it up. Because the signal arrives at different skin points at slightly different times and sizes, the monitor can subtract one electrode from another and build a view of the heart's electrical activity from a particular direction. A 3 electrode set gives a single view, a 5 electrode set gives several, and the classic 10 electrode arrangement gives the 12 views of a diagnostic ECG. The monitor is not a diagnostic ECG machine, but it uses the same physics. The quality of the trace depends first on the skin, second on the electrode, and only third on the electronics.

An electrode is a small disc of silver and silver chloride with a conductive gel, pressed onto clean dry skin. Skin is a poor conductor, so the person applying the electrode should abrade the surface lightly, remove oil, and clip hair rather than shave it, because a nick can bleed and a bleeding site conducts badly. The lead wires then carry the signal to the module, and those wires are the most common point of failure in the whole system. A loose electrode, a dried gel, a cracked wire, or a wire crossing another produce artifact: a wandering baseline, a thick fuzzy trace, or a spike that looks like a dangerous rhythm. The characteristic alarm is the lead failure alarm, which the monitor sounds when it cannot find a stable electrical path. The common operator error is to blame the monitor and change the lead wire when the real fault is a dry electrode under a sweating patient, and the hazard is a decision made on a trace that was never the heart.

Blood pressure by cuff is the second everyday channel. The cuff is a bladder inside a fabric sleeve, wrapped around the upper arm and inflated above the systolic pressure, then deflated slowly while a pressure sensor watches the small oscillations that the artery transmits into the cuff. This is why the method is called oscillometry, and why it estimates rather than measures. The cuff must fit the arm: a bladder that is too narrow reads falsely high, and one that is too wide reads falsely low, so the correct bladder width is roughly 40 per cent of the arm circumference, a figure that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The common operator errors are placing the cuff over clothing, wrapping it loosely, letting the arm hang, and leaving the cuff on a limb that carries an intravenous line. The characteristic alarm is the cuff overpressure or cuff timeout alarm, which protects the arm from a bladder that will not stop inflating.

Invasive arterial pressure is the third pressure channel and the most demanding. A catheter sits in an artery, connected by stiff tubing full of saline to a transducer, a small chamber whose diaphragm flexes as the pressure wave arrives and converts that movement into a voltage. The monitor displays a waveform with a sharp upstroke and a dicrotic notch, and it derives the systolic, diastolic, and mean pressures from that wave. Two rituals make the reading trustworthy: leveling the transducer at the level of the heart, and zeroing it to the atmosphere. The tubing must be short, stiff, and free of bubbles, because a soft or bubbly system damps the wave and understates the systolic pressure. A square wave test, performed by briefly flushing the line and watching how fast the trace settles, tells the operator whether the system is damped or resonant. The transducer position, the flush pressure, and the test criteria must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The hazards of an arterial line belong with the line, not with a later chapter. If the tubing disconnects, the artery can bleed quickly and invisibly under drapes, so the line is always secured, labeled, and inspected. If air enters, it can travel, and if the line is flushed carelessly, a bolus of saline or clot can reach the hand or the brain. Infection follows any line that stays in place, so the insertion site is observed every shift. The characteristic alarms are the low pressure alarm, which may signal a disconnected line, and the high pressure alarm, which may signal an occluded catheter or a patient who is coughing. The common operator error is to silence a low pressure alarm before looking at the arm, when the correct sequence is to look at the patient, then the tubing, then the screen.

Oximetry is the channel that most changed how clinicians think, because it made continuous oxygen monitoring possible without a blood sample. A probe shines red light and infrared light through a translucent part of the body, usually a fingertip, and a detector on the other side measures how much of each wavelength arrives. Oxygenated and deoxygenated hemoglobin absorb those two wavelengths differently, so the ratio of the two signals reveals the proportion of hemoglobin that is carrying oxygen. Only the pulsatile part of the signal is used, the part that swells with each heartbeat, and this is why a poor pulse gives a poor reading. Probes exist for the finger, the ear, the forehead, and the newborn foot, and each has its own placement rule and its own risk of pressure injury. The alarm limits and the averaging time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The errors of oximetry are worth memorizing because they all produce a confident number that is wrong. Motion makes the probe read a moving finger rather than a pulse. Cold, low blood pressure, and vasoconstriction reduce the pulsatile signal until the monitor either drops the reading or latches onto a poor one. Nail polish, artificial nails, dirt, and bright light can distort the optics. Carbon monoxide poisoning and abnormal hemoglobin can make the saturation look reassuring while the patient is starved of oxygen, and a probe placed on a limb with a blood pressure cuff cycling above it will read the cuff, not the patient. The characteristic alarm is the low saturation alarm, and it is the alarm most often silenced without a look at the patient. A default low saturation limit is often near 90 per cent, and that example, like every default threshold for that alarm, must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The temperature channel is small but not trivial. A thermistor or a thermocouple changes its electrical behavior as it warms, and the monitor converts that change into a temperature after the probe's own characteristics have been entered or recognized. Skin probes lie on the surface and read low; core probes sit in the esophagus, the bladder, the rectum, or the nasopharynx and read closer to the body's true center. The difference between the two is itself useful information, because a rising skin to core gap suggests poor perfusion. Probes are fragile, they are a known source of pressure injury in the mouth and rectum, and they are a documented route for cross infection when they are reused without proper reprocessing. A usual alert range is 36 to 38 degrees Celsius, and those temperature limits must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Exhaled carbon dioxide is the channel that proves a tube is in the windpipe and that the lungs are moving air. Carbon dioxide absorbs infrared light at a specific wavelength, so a small sensor or a sampling line pulls a breath into a chamber and measures how much light is absorbed. The result is a waveform with a flat start, a steep rise, a plateau, and a steep fall, and the highest point of the plateau is the end tidal value. A sudden drop to zero in a patient with a breathing tube is a disconnection or a lost airway until proved otherwise. A gradual rise may mean the patient is not breathing enough, and a rising value during chest compressions is a sign that the compressions are working. The end tidal value in a healthy resting adult is usually between 35 to 45 millimeters of mercury, and that range, the sampling rate, and the calibration method must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The practical problems of capnography are mostly plumbing. Water vapor from the breath condenses in the sampling line, so a water trap collects it and must be emptied before it fills and blocks the airway. Secretions, blood, and vomit can block the line, giving a flat trace that looks like apnea. The characteristic alarm is the apnea alarm or the low end tidal alarm, and both must be answered by looking at the patient's chest and at the tube before the sensor is touched. The common operator error is to accept a flat trace as a technical fault and disconnect the circuit, when the correct first act is to confirm ventilation by hand and by sight.

Alarms are a subsystem, and the monitor's alarm system is the part most likely to hurt a patient by being ignored or disabled. Most monitors grade alarms by priority: a lethal rhythm, a very low saturation, or an apnea is highest; a mild limit breach is lower; a technical fault is lower still. The screen shows the priority by color and the speaker changes tone, and the alarm settings include limits, volume, delay, and whether the alarm is on at all. Default limits are written for an average adult and are wrong for many patients, so the discipline is to set limits deliberately for the person in the bed and to write them down. Typical default limits for an adult might be a systolic pressure below 90 millimeters of mercury, a mean pressure below 65 millimeters of mercury, or a saturation below 90 per cent, and those example figures must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Alarm fatigue is the predictable result of too many false alarms, and it harms people when staff stop looking up. The hazard is a monitor whose alarm has been silenced or whose limits were widened to stop a nuisance, and every limit, delay, and volume must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Central stations and telemetry extend the monitor beyond the bedside. A central station is a computer that receives the waveforms from many bedside monitors over the hospital network and displays them on one large screen, usually with a technician or a nurse watching. Telemetry is a small transmitter worn by a walking patient that sends a single ECG view by radio to a receiver. Both systems introduce delay: the picture at the central station is a little behind the picture at the bedside, and a network fault can leave the bedside monitor perfect while the central station shows a frozen patient. The characteristic hazard of the central station is the belief that someone is watching, and the common operator error is to treat the screen as a substitute for walking to the bed. Monitoring a patient means touching the patient; watching a screen means watching a screen.

Every monitor carries a battery, and the battery decides what happens during transport. Older units use sealed lead acid cells, newer ones use lithium chemistry, and their runtime varies with the number of channels, the screen brightness, and the age of the cells. A monitor that has been on charge for months may still hold only a few minutes, because capacity falls silently with every cycle. Before any transport, the operator confirms the charge state, carries a spare battery where the design allows, and knows which channels will be shed to extend runtime. The hospital supports the bedside unit with an uninterruptible power supply, UPS, and a circuit on the emergency generator, but neither follows the patient to the scanner. The advertised runtime, which may be given as 4 to 8 hours, and the low battery alarm threshold must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Cleaning between patients is where a monitor becomes an infection risk. The screen, the case, the buttons, the cables, and the reusable sensors all touch hands that have touched patients. The manufacturer's approved disinfectant is the only safe choice, because alcohol, bleach, and quaternary ammonium compounds attack different plastics, soften cable jackets, and cloud screens at different rates. Cuffs are a special problem: the fabric holds moisture, the bladder holds air, and a cuff wiped only on the outside has not been cleaned. Cables should be wiped along their whole length, connectors inspected for corrosion, and probes cleaned according to their own instructions. Contact times, which are often stated as 1 minute for a wipe and 10 minutes for a soak, must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The common error is a hurried wipe of the screen while the cable, the cuff, and the probe go untouched to the next patient.

The consumables of monitoring are few, but their failure is frequent. Electrodes come in adult, pediatric, and neonatal sizes with different gel formulations, and their gel dries with time and heat, so stock must be rotated and expiry dates read. Lead wires and patient cables fail at the connector and at the point where they bend, and a spare set is part of a functioning monitor. Cuffs come in a range of sizes and must be matched to the arm; probes exist for every site and every age; carbon dioxide sampling lines and water traps are single use in most designs. Recording paper, where a monitor still prints, is thermal and fades with light and heat. Storage should be dry, out of direct sun, and away from the disinfectant shelf, because vapor ages adhesive and gel.

Maintenance and calibration are what keep the numbers honest. A preventive schedule checks the case, the screen, the connectors, the cables, the battery, and the alarm speaker, and it tests the module against a simulator: a device that generates a known ECG, a known pressure, and a known saturation. The pressure channel is checked against a manometer or a calibrated reference, the cuff is checked for leaks, and the electrical safety test measures leakage current at the points the standard requires. Calibration intervals, commonly every 6 to 12 months, and the tolerances and safety limits themselves must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The records matter as much as the tests, because a monitor without a maintenance history cannot be trusted in a crisis and cannot be defended in an inquiry.

The characteristic failures of a monitor are worth listing because they repeat. A cracked or delaminated screen loses a corner of the display. A battery that no longer holds charge cuts transport short. A module fails its self test and takes one channel with it. A cable breaks internally and produces artifact only when the patient moves. A monitor with a full memory or a corrupted configuration may freeze the display or forget the alarm limits, and one with a failed network card drops off the central station without any bedside warning. The common operator error in all of these cases is to keep using the device because it looks alive. The safe response is to move the patient to a working monitor, label the faulty unit, and remove it from service.

The field variant of this chapter is the portable monitor of a mobile or frontier clinic. It is a single box with a handle, a smaller screen, and a battery that must last a day, and it usually offers ECG, cuff blood pressure, oximetry, and temperature, with exhaled carbon dioxide appearing only on the better models. It gives up a great deal: the invasive pressure module, the central station, the telemetry link, the printer, the large screen with room for many traces, and the service contract. Its sampling and filtering are simpler, so its traces are noisier. Its cuffs and probes are reusable and must survive dust, heat, and repeated disinfection. Its internal battery is often the only power source, and a monitor that cannot be charged overnight is a monitor that stops working at the worst moment.

What the field monitor demands instead is discipline. Someone must own the charging routine, the spare battery, and the solar panel, because a portable monitor is only as reliable as the last person who plugged it in. The case must be padded and carried, not thrown into the back of a vehicle, and the screen must be protected from sun and from impact. Consumables must be adapted: reusable cuffs in the sizes the clinic actually sees, a supply of electrodes stored away from heat, and a cleaning routine that uses what is available without destroying the plastic. Settings, alarm limits, and battery thresholds must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and in a field clinic the local protocol may be a single laminated sheet that someone wrote carefully.

The field alarm problem is different from the hospital one. There is no central station, so the alarm must be loud enough to cross a small ward and distinctive enough to be recognized from a distance, and the staff may be one person who is also mixing a drug. Sharing one monitor between several patients creates its own hazards, because the cable and the cuff travel and carry organisms with them. The common error is to leave a monitor on a patient who no longer needs it, which exhausts the battery and trains everyone to ignore the alarm. The characteristic hazard is an alarm that is not heard because the device is under a blanket, in a corridor, or on a bench across the room.

When the battery finally dies, manual observations replace the machine, and they are not a lesser form of medicine. The pulse is counted by palpation for a full minute, the respiratory rate is counted by watching the chest, blood pressure is taken with an aneroid manometer and a stethoscope, temperature is taken with a clinical thermometer, and consciousness, urine output, and skin color are recorded on paper. The manual schedule is written down with a time for each set of observations, and the trend matters more than any single value. The aneroid gauge itself must be checked against a known reference, and the cuff sizes are the same sizes the monitor used. Manual observation demands attention, but it demands nothing from the electricity supply.

To summarize, a patient monitor is a set of sensors and a screen, and the sensors are the part that fails first. The hospital-grade device brings modules, invasive channels, a central station, and a service contract, and it repays that infrastructure with precision. The field variant brings a handle and a battery, and it repays that simplicity with demands on the person who carries it. In both settings the same rules hold: set alarm limits deliberately, look at the patient before the screen, clean every surface between patients, calibrate on schedule, and keep a manual observation routine ready for the moment the battery dies.
