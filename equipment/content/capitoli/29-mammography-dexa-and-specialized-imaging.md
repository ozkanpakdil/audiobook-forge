# Chapter 29 — Mammography, DEXA, and Specialized Imaging

Most imaging machines can be pointed at almost any part of the body, but a small group is built to answer one question extremely well, and mammography, bone densitometry, and the specialized units around them are the clearest examples. The reason is contrast, because the difference between one soft tissue and another, or between bone mineral and the fat around it, is far smaller than the difference between bone and air. To see such a faint difference the machine trades flexibility for optimization, using a narrow energy band, a short distance to the detector, and a fixed geometry. The benefit is the detection of a disease that is otherwise silent, and the cost is a room of its own, a phantom, and a strict quality program. This chapter describes those machines and their failures, and then describes what a mobile or frontier clinic can carry in their place.

The breast is a difficult radiographic subject because nearly everything inside it attenuates X-rays almost equally. Contrast comes from two measures at once, from photons of low energy, where small differences in absorption are magnified, and from compression, which spreads the tissue thinner and separates structures that would otherwise overlap. The mammography tube is therefore dedicated to a narrow band of low energy photons, with an anode of molybdenum, and sometimes rhodium or tungsten, whose characteristic emission lies at about 17 to 20 kiloelectronvolts at a tube voltage usually between 25 and 32 kilovolts, values that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The focal spot is very small, about 0.1 millimeter for magnification views and about 0.3 millimeter for contact views, dimensions that must also be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The window is beryllium because glass would absorb the very photons the examination needs, and the anode must cool between patients because the weak beam needs a long exposure.

Compression is the most important action the technologist performs, and it is a mechanical intervention with a measured force. The paddle descends under motor power, and the force it applies is displayed in newtons, with a typical examination using roughly 100 to 200 newtons, a figure that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Compression thins the breast so that less radiation is needed, spreads the glandular tissue so that overlapping structures separate, and holds the breast still so that motion does not smear the image. Excessive force causes pain, bruising, and in a woman with an implant the risk of rupture, while the common operator error runs the other way, because too little compression produces a soft and unreadable image at a higher dose. The paddle must release instantly when the pedal or the emergency release is pressed, and that release is tested every day, a routine that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Between the breast and the detector lies an anti-scatter grid of fine lead strips that absorbs photons deflected inside the breast. Scatter carries no positional information and veils the faint contrast between glandular tissue and fat, so the grid is essential, and it usually moves during the exposure so that its own lines do not appear. Behind the detector the automatic exposure control ends the exposure when a preset quantity of radiation has arrived, and it therefore adapts the technique to the thickness and composition of each breast. The dose is reported as the mean glandular dose, usually between 1 and 3 milligray for a single view, and that value must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. An automatic exposure control that has drifted silently changes every dose in the room, and only a phantom of known thickness and composition will reveal it.

The receptor has passed through the same generations as general radiography, with film, photostimulable phosphor plates, and flat panels of amorphous selenium or a scintillator on a thin film transistor array. A digital panel reads each picture element within seconds and sends it to the picture archiving and communication system, called PACS, and the elements are commonly between 50 and 100 micrometers, a dimension that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Digital detectors hide overexposure, because a detector that receives too much radiation still produces a clean looking image. Tomosynthesis refines the same machine, because the tube sweeps through an arc, commonly 15 to 50 degrees, and makes between 10 and 25 low dose exposures whose shift and addition bring one thin plane into focus and blur the rest, and those values must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The listener should picture a fan of thin slices through the breast, each a few millimeters thick, but the longer compression and the reconstruction mean more data and a longer reading time.

The examination is a set of views, and each view is defined by the anatomy it must show. The craniocaudal view compresses the breast from above, and the mediolateral oblique view compresses it from the side at an angle that follows the pectoral muscle, so that the muscle appears as a triangle at the top of the image. The common operator error is poor positioning, above all an oblique view with too little pectoral muscle, which leaves the upper outer quadrant unexamined and is a well documented reason for a cancer found between screens. The hazard is radiation risk, weighed differently because the breast is radiosensitive and the examination is offered to women who feel well, so a screening program fixes an age range, an interval, and a dose for each view, values that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The pregnancy question is asked before the examination, the characteristic alarm is a tube overload tone or a fault message that stops the exposure, the correct response is to let the tube cool rather than repeat at once, and the emergency compression release is tested before the first patient.

A dedicated mammography room is arranged around the posture of the patient and the reach of the technologist. The machine is a single column with the tube above, the detector below, the paddle between them, a pedal for height, and hand controls on both sides, while the console may sit behind a lead glass screen. The room also holds a magnifier, a chair, a private changing area, and a viewing station whose monitor is calibrated for reading. The consumables are a gown, a skin wipe, and a single use paddle cover for every patient, a permitted disinfectant for the detector window, and for biopsy a sterile drape, a local anesthetic, needles commonly between 14 and 9 gauge, a vacuum canister, specimen containers with fixative, and marker clips. The gauge, depth, and throw of every needle must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the list ends with sharps containers, hand hygiene supplies, and a written record of every specimen and marker.

Cleaning here is patient safety rather than housekeeping, because bare skin presses against the same surfaces all day. The paddle, detector window, face shield, and handles are wiped between patients, the paddle cover is changed even when the skin looked clean, and the biopsy tray and needle guide are disinfected after every procedure. The concentration, contact time, and material compatibility of every disinfectant must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Quality control is unusually strict because the contrast is faint, so a phantom containing simulated fibers, specks, and masses is imaged at a fixed technique and scored against previous images, the compression force is measured with a calibrated scale, and the reading monitor receives a luminance and gray scale check. A warning from the quality control software, or a phantom that fails its scoring criteria, is a fault to be investigated rather than a nuisance to be cleared.

A dying tube arcs, heard as a sharp crack and shown as a fault code, and an aged tube loses output so that the automatic exposure control raises the dose. A compression mechanism that drifts applies less force than the display claims, a flat panel with dead elements shows the same dots on every image, and a scratched plate shows a line that travels with the plate rather than with the machine. The viewing station is a common source of silent failure, because an uncalibrated monitor or a bright room changes the decision of the reader with no technical alarm at all. The start of shift check covers the whole chain, warming up the tube, raising and lowering the paddle, testing the emergency release, imaging a phantom, and confirming that the images of the last patient reached PACS. The warm-up, phantom schedule, and daily check list must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

When an image shows something suspicious, the next device takes a piece of it, because a needle must reach a lesion a few millimeters across inside a moving organ without cutting anything it should not. Guidance relates the lesion to a coordinate system the operator can see, either stereotactically with pairs of angled X-ray views or in real time under ultrasound, and the choice follows the lesion. A stereotactic system exploits the geometry of two eyes, because two angled images of the same compressed breast reveal the depth of the lesion from its displacement, and the machine computes three coordinates and drives a needle guide to that point on a prone table. The common operator error is to accept a lesion that is not clear in both angled views, or to let the breast shift between the scout image and the sampling, because the computed depth then points at normal tissue. Bleeding, infection, and a needle advanced too far into the chest wall are the hazards, the characteristic alarm is a position or verification failure that stops the sequence, and the needle gauge, depth of throw, and verification sequence must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A vacuum assisted device collects more tissue than a spring loaded needle through a single insertion, drawing tissue into a side aperture with vacuum while a rotating cutter advances. The operator can rotate the aperture and take many cores around the lesion, which is why the technique can remove a small mass completely and why it samples a cluster of microcalcifications well. A small metal marker clip is then deployed, because the lesion may no longer be visible after sampling and the clip identifies the site for later surgery and for the pathologist. The common operator error is to lose track of the clip or to omit the image that proves its position, and every specimen must be radiographed or examined to prove that the lesion itself was sampled. The needle size, the vacuum level, the number of cores, and the clip deployment must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Breast ultrasound rests on the reflection of high frequency sound at the boundaries between tissues, and its physics explains both its strengths and its blind spots. A probe emits short pulses of sound, commonly between 7 and 15 megahertz, and the returning echoes are timed to build a picture of depth, a frequency range that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A higher frequency gives finer resolution but is absorbed more quickly, so the frequencies that suit the superficial breast would fail at the depth of the abdomen. The method is real time and free of ionizing radiation, it shows whether a mass is solid or fluid, and it is the natural guide for a needle, but it cannot reliably show microcalcifications. The probe is a semi-critical device, so it is wiped and disinfected between patients, the gel is never taken from a large multiuse container onto broken skin, and the common operator error is to press so hard that the breast is flattened and the lesion is displaced.

Dual energy X-ray absorptiometry, called DEXA, solves a problem of two unknowns with two measurements. Attenuation depends both on the amount of material and on its atomic composition, so a single measurement cannot separate bone mineral from the soft tissue around it. If the same region is measured at two different photon energies, the soft tissue contribution can be calculated and subtracted, leaving the bone mineral content. The energies used are commonly around 40 and 70 kiloelectronvolts, produced by switching the tube voltage or by filtering the beam, and those values must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The result is not a true density but an areal density, the bone mineral content divided by the projected area, reported in grams per square centimeter, a unit that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The densitometer is a table, an arm carrying a collimated source and a detector, and a computer that moves the arm across the patient. A pencil beam system moves the source and detector together in a raster, while a fan beam system sweeps a wider fan and scans faster, which reduces the chance that the patient will move. The standard sites are the lumbar spine and the proximal femur, because those are where a fragility fracture most often occurs and where the reference data are strongest. The result is compared with a reference database matched to the sex and the ethnic group of the patient, and this is where a machine can be badly misused, because a database from a different population gives a number that looks precise and means something else. The report must state the site, the value, the comparison score, and any artifact that limits the analysis, and the reference database, the sites measured, the scan length, and the analysis thresholds must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Quality control for DEXA is built around a phantom that contains a known amount of bone mineral, usually simulated vertebrae embedded in a block of tissue equivalent plastic. The phantom is scanned on a fixed schedule, and its measured mineral content is plotted on a control chart, so that a slow drift of the calibration, the detector, or the software is detected before it changes a patient result. The acceptable variation is small, a few per cent over the life of the machine, and the control limits, the scanning frequency, and the reference values must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The characteristic alarm is a phantom result outside the control limits, and the correct response is to stop patient scanning and investigate rather than to repeat the phantom until a better number appears. The same discipline applies to the precision of the operator, because a technologist whose positioning varies will produce a variation larger than the disease being followed.

The radiation hazard of DEXA is minute, but not every hazard of the examination is radiation, because a patient left on the table while the arm moves can be struck by the arm. The common operator error is misanalysis rather than bad scanning, because an edge placed through a vertebra, a rib or the iliac crest included in the bone map, or a compressed vertebra left in the calculation will change the result in either direction. Metal implants, a recent contrast study, and severe degenerative change all inflate the measured density, and the report must say so rather than presenting the number as if it were clean. Consumables are modest, consisting of a cover on the table for each patient, a positioning block for the leg, a foot support, and disinfectant wipes, and the table pad is cleaned between patients because skin and feet touch it. Failure modes are quiet, because a drifting detector or a stale calibration shifts every result, a table that travels at the wrong speed stretches the skeleton, and an edited analysis template places the bone edges wrongly, so the daily check includes a phantom scan, an inspection of the table and arm, a review of the templates, and a reading of the control chart, all of which must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy uses sound to break a stone without an incision, and its principle is the focusing of energy in space and in time. A generator creates a pressure pulse of very short duration, and an ellipsoidal reflector or an acoustic lens brings the waves to a focus, so that a large area of low pressure at the skin becomes a small point of very high pressure inside the body. The pulse is commonly between 30 and 100 megapascals at the focus and lasts only a few microseconds, and the stone fractures because the sudden change of pressure creates tensile stress within it while the surrounding tissue tolerates the same wave, and that pressure range must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The three common sources are the spark gap, which discharges a high voltage across electrodes in water, the electromagnetic source, which drives a metal membrane with a coil, and the piezoelectric source, which makes an array of crystals expand together. The patient is coupled to the wave path through a water bath or a water filled cushion, and any air between the cushion and the skin reflects the wave and wastes the treatment.

The first hazard of lithotripsy is cardiac, because a shock wave passing through the chest can trigger a disturbance of the heart rhythm. The pulse is therefore delivered in synchrony with the electrocardiogram, called ECG, and gated to a point in the cycle when the heart is electrically quiet, and a patient with a cardiac pacemaker needs a cardiology opinion first. Other hazards are pain, which requires analgesia and often sedation, blood in the urine that clears in a day or two, and rarely a hematoma around the kidney. The common operator error is poor coupling, because a fold of the cushion, a bubble of air, or a smear of soap reduces the energy reaching the stone and leads the operator to raise the output unnecessarily. The characteristic alarms are a gating failure, a coupling or water fault message, and an electrode wear warning, and the gating window, sedation plan, maximum energy, electrode replacement interval, and water quality must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The radiotherapy simulator is a diagnostic X-ray unit built to imitate the geometry of a treatment machine, and it answers one question before treatment begins, which is where exactly the beam will enter the patient. It has the same rotational gantry, table movements, and often the same lasers and isocentric distance as the treatment unit, but it uses kilovoltage X-rays and a flat panel or image intensifier instead of a therapy beam. The field edges are set with lead shutters, the patient is marked on the skin with ink or small tattoos, and verification images are stored so the position can be reproduced on the treatment machine. Alignment depends on the isocenter, the point around which the gantry and table rotate, and a laser that has drifted by even 1 or 2 millimeters moves every field in the department, a distance that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The hazard is that a mark on the skin looks authoritative even when it is wrong, so the common operator error is a field marked from a mislabeled image, and the isocentric distance, laser alignment, shutter calibration, and marking procedure must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Computed tomography, called CT, has largely replaced the radiographic simulator for planning, because a scan gives the planner the density of every tissue along the beam path as well as the geometry. The older simulator survives where a CT scanner is not available or where a simple field must be verified quickly, and its quality control resembles that of a radiographic room but with an obsession about geometry. The isocenter is measured with a front pointer, the lasers are checked against a fixed reference, the light field is compared with the radiation field, and the table travel is measured in all three directions. A mechanical fault that would be trivial in a diagnostic room becomes serious here, because an error of a few millimeters becomes the same error in the treatment of every patient, and the characteristic alarms are a door interlock, an exposure fault, and a laser or gantry warning. The isocenter tolerance, laser check, field size test, and table travel limits must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

All of these units share a pattern the listener can carry from one machine to the next, because each one fixes its geometry, narrows its energy band, and defends that design with a phantom, a control chart, and a dedicated room. Each one also concentrates the skill of the operator, so that a technologist who works only in that room can be excellent while a visitor can be dangerous. The reading environment belongs to the device as much as the machine does, because a calibrated monitor and a dim room are part of the imaging chain in mammography and densitometry reporting. When any of these devices is absent the clinical question does not disappear, and the field must answer it with different equipment. Mammography itself does not travel, because the machine is heavy, it needs a stable power supply, its detector is fragile, and its quality control needs a phantom and a service visit, so a working unit in a remote setting should be protected rather than moved.

Portable bone densitometry is the field variant that travels best, because the physics of two energies can be squeezed into a small device that measures the heel, the forearm, or a finger. The machine is a bench top or handheld unit with a small source and detector, a cradle for the limb, and a battery or a mains adapter, and it reports an areal density within minutes. What it gives up is the axial skeleton, because the spine and the hip cannot be measured by a peripheral device, and the diagnosis and monitoring of treatment are defined at those axial sites. What it demands instead is honesty about the reference database, since a peripheral measurement compared with a database from another population may be meaningless. The site measured, the reference database, and the interpretation limits must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the report must state that the result is a screening estimate.

Portable ultrasound is the workhorse of breast assessment where mammography cannot reach, because a handheld unit with a linear probe can show a palpable mass, distinguish a cyst from a solid lesion, guide aspiration, and measure a lesion for later reassessment. What it gives up is the detection of microcalcifications, the reproducibility of a standard set of views, and the archival image a reader can review later, because a handheld examination is only as good as the person holding the probe. What it demands instead is a trained and supervised operator, a written record of the probe position and the finding, clean gel, a disinfected probe face, and a referral pathway for every lesion that cannot be resolved on the spot. Infection control is the hazard that portable ultrasound brings to the field, because a probe that is wiped once a day, a large container of gel that is refilled rather than replaced, and a machine carried between wards all transfer organisms from one patient to the next. The remedy is a single patient wipe for the probe, a small gel bottle for one patient where possible, hand hygiene before and after every examination, and a clean cover for any probe that touches broken skin, and the gel, disinfectant, and cover must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Where no mammography exists, a clinical breast examination by a trained examiner can find a mass that is large enough to feel. A fine needle aspiration with an ordinary syringe can show whether a lump is a cyst, an abscess, or a solid tumor, which is information that ultrasound alone cannot give. The limits must be spoken aloud, because a normal examination does not exclude an early cancer, a sample can be inadequate or falsely reassuring, and neither method can find a cluster of microcalcifications. Lithotripsy cannot be improvised either, because it demands a focused pressure pulse, a water coupling system, and a source that erodes as it works, so a stone that needs shock wave treatment must be referred while a stone causing obstruction with infection is drained first. Radiotherapy planning follows the same logic, because a mark made without the geometry of the treatment machine is worse than no mark at all, so the field sends a complete record with the patient, and the referral criteria and record format must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Quality assurance in the field is proved with a simple phantom, a fixed setting, and a written record rather than with a service contract. A portable ultrasound unit is checked against a tissue equivalent phantom, so that a failing probe or a drifting gain announces itself before a patient is examined, and a portable densitometer is checked with its own calibration block against the value from the week before. Troubleshooting begins with the question of whether the fault is in the machine, the operator, or the environment, because a blurred image may come from a cracked probe, a bad coupling layer, or a setting changed by the previous user. A unit that reports a calibration failure is not repaired by repeating the test, because the warning exists to prevent a wrong measurement from reaching a patient, so the device is labeled out of service and referred to a biomedical technician when the fault cannot be identified. Power, storage, and people are the rest of the system, because a portable unit is ruined by a voltage spike, a leaking battery, or a hot vehicle, while training, supervision, and a culture in which uncertainty is spoken aloud are the real equipment of the remote clinic.

To summarize, a specialized imaging device is a general purpose machine deliberately narrowed so that a faint contrast becomes visible. The mammography tube uses a low energy beam from a molybdenum or rhodium target, a small focal spot, a moving grid, an automatic exposure control, and a paddle that compresses the breast at a measured force, while digital detectors and tomosynthesis separate superimposed planes and a guided needle takes the tissue that the image has found. Bone densitometry uses two photon energies to subtract soft tissue from bone mineral and compares the areal density with a reference database, while lithotripsy focuses an acoustic pulse onto a stone and the radiotherapy simulator reproduces the geometry of a treatment machine. Every one of these devices has a characteristic hazard, a common operator error, and an alarm that should be obeyed, and every one depends on cleaning, consumables, quality control, and a start of shift check that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. In the field the honest survivors are portable ultrasound and peripheral bone densitometry, used by trained hands for triage and referral, while mammography, lithotripsy, and the simulator stay in the hospital and the patient travels to them.
