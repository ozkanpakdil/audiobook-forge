# Chapter 8 — Basic Examination Tools

Basic examination tools are the small instruments that extend the unaided senses of the examiner. Each couples a property of the body to a sense and bends sound, light, or touch into a form the ear, the eye, or the hand can read. A stethoscope gathers faint pressure waves, an otoscope concentrates light, a reflex hammer delivers an impulse, and a tape compares the body against a known length. Because they are simple they fail quietly, so this chapter describes each hospital-grade tool and then its field substitute, naming the hazard, the common error, and the warning sign of each.

Sounds inside the chest are pressure waves of very small amplitude, and they reflect back when they meet the skin, because the change from tissue to air is abrupt. The stethoscope defeats that boundary. Its chest piece collects the vibrations, a closed column of air carries them up the tubing, and earpieces seal the ear canal against room noise. The diaphragm is a taut disc pressed firmly to the skin and answers the higher sounds of breathing and most murmurs, while the bell is an open cup pressed lightly that answers the low sounds of blood flow.

Using the instrument well depends on knowing which face is listening. The diaphragm needs enough pressure to seal, while the bell needs only light contact, because pressing the bell stretches the skin into a diaphragm and hides the low murmurs the examiner wants. The common operator error is exactly this. The instrument is shared, so the chest piece and tubing are wiped between patients with the disinfectant named in the local protocol, and any cleaning agent must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Tubing cracks and develops pinholes where it bends, and a pinhole weakens the instrument without making it look broken, while hardened earpieces stop sealing. The characteristic warning sign is not an alarm but a change in sound that becomes obvious when the 2 ears are compared. The checks are to look along the tubing for cracks, listen with both earpieces at once, and confirm that the diaphragm is neither torn nor hardened. The interval for those checks must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The electronic stethoscope replaces the air column with a microphone, an amplifier, and headphones. The microphone contacts the skin, the signal is filtered and amplified, and the sound reaches the ear at a volume the examiner can adjust. A quiet murmur can be made audible, the signal can be stored, and the instrument can feed a recording or a remote consultation. Its characteristic alarm is a low-battery tone or a flashing indicator, and its common error is to trust the volume control so far that a genuine sound and an amplified artifact cannot be told apart. The housing is wiped rather than immersed, and any battery or cleaning agent must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

In a field clinic the stethoscope is often the cheapest single-head instrument available, sometimes a single-tube model with a plastic chest piece and stiff tubing. It gives up the choice of face, and with it the deliberate separation of low and high frequencies, so the examiner must learn what it can and cannot hear. It also gives up durability, because the chest piece cracks and the thin tubing splits at the bend. Where a fetal heartbeat must be heard, the acoustic fetal stethoscope, a simple funnel pressed on the abdomen, needs no tubing and gives a clear low sound to a trained ear. Any specification or replacement interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The otoscope turns a small lamp into a beam and a cone into a speculum. Light from a bulb or a light-emitting diode travels along the handle and head, is directed down a funnel-shaped speculum, and illuminates the ear canal while a magnifying lens enlarges the view. The speculum is tapered so that it seats in the opening of the canal, and it must be wide enough to see past its own walls and narrow enough to enter without pain. The pneumatic attachment adds a rubber bulb and a tube, and its principle is that a sealed column of air can test the mobility of the eardrum, because a squeeze of the bulb changes the pressure and a healthy eardrum moves while a stiff or perforated one does not. Any bulb specification and any cleaning or sterilization cycle must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Using the otoscope requires a technique the design assumes. The examiner selects the largest speculum that will enter comfortably, braces the back of the hand against the patient's cheek or temple, pulls the pinna upward and backward in an adult and backward and downward in an infant, and advances only as far as the canal allows. The brace is a safety device, because a patient who turns the head can drive a rigid speculum through the eardrum if the examiner's hand is free in the air. The common error is to push toward the eardrum instead of following the curved canal, and the second is to choose a speculum so small that it fits easily but shows nothing. Cerumen, a failing bulb, and a dim light limit the view rather than announce themselves, so the warning sign is a dark field that the examiner wrongly blames on the patient. Specula are single-use or reprocessed according to the local protocol and the lens and head are wiped between patients, with any bulb, cleaning agent, or sterilization cycle confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The ophthalmoscope solves a harder optical problem, because the examiner must look into the eye almost along the line by which light enters it. Its essential target is the red reflex, the soft red glow that appears when light returns from the retina, and the same instrument inspects the optic disc, the retinal vessels, and the macula. The apertures change the beam, so a small round spot is used through an undilated pupil, a larger spot gives a wider view once the pupil is dilated, a red-free filter removes red light to make vessels stand out, and a fixation target gives the patient something to look at. The lenses are measured in diopters, and the setting that matches a particular patient and examiner must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the examination is relied upon.

The technique the ophthalmoscope assumes is easy to describe and easy to get wrong. The examiner holds it in the right hand for the right eye and the left hand for the left eye, keeps it close to both faces, and follows the red reflex at about 15 centimeters before moving in toward the pupil. The common error is to flood the eye with a wide bright beam, which makes the patient blink and squeezes the pupil to a pinhole so that nothing can be seen. The characteristic warning sign of a failing instrument is a dim or uneven beam, usually an aging bulb or a dirty window. The forehead rest and housing are cleaned between patients, with any bulb, battery, aperture, or lens specification confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The reflex hammer is a controlled mechanical impulse in the shape of a small mallet. Its head strikes a tendon and stretches the muscle spindle, the spindle sends a burst of signals along a sensory nerve to the spinal cord, and the cord returns a signal that makes the muscle contract. The technique that makes the test meaningful is a short, loose swing of the wrist that delivers a brisk tap rather than a heavy blow, with the limb relaxed and supported, because a tense muscle cannot show its reflex. The common error is to strike too hard or repeatedly, which produces pain and voluntary guarding that the examiner misreads as a reflex. Because the hammer has no alarm or calibration, the warning sign of a problem is a difference between the 2 sides caused by technique rather than by the patient, with the strength of the tap confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the finding is trusted.

The tuning fork is a piece of metal shaped so that it vibrates at a single known frequency when struck. The 2 tines swing toward and away from each other, the stem transmits that vibration into whatever it touches, and the frequency is fixed by the length, thickness, and material of the tines. A clinical set commonly contains forks at 128 hertz, 256 hertz, 512 hertz, and 1024 hertz, and the frequency of each fork and the tests performed with it must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the examination is interpreted. In the first hearing test the fork is held near the ear canal and then its stem is pressed against the mastoid bone behind the ear, and the patient reports which sound lasts longer. In the second test the vibrating stem rests on the middle of the forehead and the patient reports which ear hears it better, which helps separate a problem of the outer or middle ear from one of the inner ear.

The results depend entirely on how the fork is excited and held. The correct stroke is a firm tap against the heel of the hand or a rubber pad near the elbow, and the correct hold is by the stem alone, because a finger touching a tine stops the vibration. The common error is to strike the fork against a hard metal or wooden surface, which gives a loud overtone instead of a clean tone, or to strike so hard that the tines are damaged and the pitch changes. A fork that has been dropped may be cracked at the stem, and a cracked fork sounds dull, so the warning sign is a tone that dies quickly and unevenly. Tuning forks are cleaned by wiping and never by immersion, and their storage, replacement interval, and cleaning agent must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A penlight concentrates a small battery current into a narrow beam that can be aimed at a pupil to test the light reflex, at the skin to look for a rash, or through the mouth or sinuses to transilluminate tissue, so that a fluid-filled cavity appears dark against a glowing field. The tongue depressor holds the tongue down and out of the line of sight so that the throat can be seen without provoking a gag. The common error with the depressor is to push it too far back on the tongue, which causes retching and can injure the mouth if the patient bites down. The common error with the penlight is to shine it from an angle that puts the examiner's own shadow across the view, or to use a beam so wide that the pupil is flooded. Both items touch mucous membranes, so a wooden depressor is used once and discarded, a plastic one is reprocessed or discarded according to the local protocol, and the penlight is wiped between patients.

A measuring tape is a physical standard carried to the patient. The tape is marked in equal divisions, usually centimeters and millimeters, and a measurement is a comparison between the patient and those divisions, so the only thing that makes a tape trustworthy is that its length does not change. A cloth or paper tape stretches with use and moisture, and a stretched tape reads high, which is a silent error no examiner can see. Tapes come in different forms for different jobs, including a firm tape for limb circumference, a narrow flexible tape for the head and chest of an infant, and a long tape for the length of a child. The measurement depends on the landmark, because a mid-upper-arm circumference or a head circumference means nothing unless the tape is placed at exactly the point the local protocol defines. The common errors are to use a stretchy tape, to measure over clothing, to pull until the tissue is compressed, and to read the wrong mark, with the specification, the landmark, and the cleaning agent confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the measurement is used to make a decision.

The neurological pin and the cotton wisp test the sensory side of the nervous system, which no machine in this book can examine for the clinician. A pin delivers a sharp stimulus and a wisp of cotton delivers a light touch, and the patient's report of what is felt and where maps the pathway from skin through peripheral nerve and spinal cord to brain. The common error is to push the pin hard enough to draw blood or to test so lightly that nothing is felt anywhere, since a test that is too weak is as misleading as one that is too strong. A disposable pin is used for one patient and discarded, a reusable pin must be disinfected between patients because any object that breaks the skin can carry infection, and a cotton wisp is used once or replaced.

Examination gloves are a barrier, and their principle is that a continuous, intact film of polymer does not let organisms pass from the patient's skin to the examiner's hands or the other way. Gloves do not replace hand hygiene, so hands are cleaned before they go on and after they come off, and a single pair is used for a single patient and then discarded. The common error is to wear one pair through a whole round of patients, or to touch a telephone, a keyboard, or a door handle with a contaminated glove and then continue the examination, because the glove then carries organisms exactly as a bare hand would. The examination surface has its own rule, since a couch, a table, or a chair is touched by every patient, and a paper cover, a clean sheet, or a disinfectant wipe is the barrier between them. The choice of glove material, the cleaning agent, and the interval for changing covers must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the examination begins.

Every small tool in this chapter is cleaned and reprocessed according to a rule that depends on what it touches. A tool that touches intact skin needs cleaning and low-level disinfection, one that touches mucous membranes needs a higher level, and one that enters sterile tissue must be sterile. The principle is that disinfection reduces the number of organisms while sterilization removes or destroys all of them, and that a disinfectant works only after the surface has been cleaned of blood, wax, and oil, because organic material shields organisms from the chemical. Contact time matters as much as the chemical, and the time, concentration, and temperature of any disinfectant or sterilization cycle must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the process is trusted. A tool whose reprocessing method is unknown should not be used on the next patient.

Between 2 patients the smallest tools carry the greatest risk, because they are touched, shared, and forgotten. The sequence that works is to keep clean items away from contaminated ones, to clean the hands between patients, and to reprocess or discard each tool according to what it touched. The common error is to clean the visible tool and forget the invisible one, the telephone, the pen, the door handle, and the earpieces handled while the stethoscope was put away. The warning sign of a broken routine is a tool that is warm from the previous patient or still damp from a hasty wipe, and both are reasons to stop and clean properly.

Storage keeps a small tool working, and the enemies of small tools are heat, moisture, sunlight, and dust. Instruments are kept in a clean, dry case where the tubing is not kinked, the specula are protected, and the bulbs are not crushed, and batteries are stored outside the device when it will not be used for some time, because a leaking cell destroys the contacts. The common error is to fit the wrong bulb or battery because it fits the socket, which can damage the instrument or produce a light too dim to be useful. A simple inventory with a spare bulb, a spare battery, a set of specula, and a replacement set of earpieces prevents most of the interruptions that make an examination incomplete.

The same tools must be adapted to very different patients, and the smallest patients punish the largest errors. An infant or small child needs a stethoscope with a smaller chest piece, because a large diaphragm covers the whole chest and blends the sounds of both lungs and the heart into one. An infant needs the smallest otoscope speculum, and the ear is pulled backward and downward rather than upward because the canal is straighter and shorter. The tape for an infant is narrow and flexible so that it can follow the curve of the head and chest, and the landmarks for the head, the chest, and the mid-upper arm differ from those of an adult. The correct size, the landmarks, and the cleaning method for each patient group must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

In a mobile clinic or a frontier health post the instruments are fewer, lighter, and chosen for what they can survive rather than for what they can hear or see. The field kit usually holds one stethoscope, one otoscope or a substitute for it, a penlight, a supply of tongue depressors, a tape, gloves, and a small power source, packed so that it can be carried on a bicycle or a shoulder bag. What the field gives up is precision, the right instrument for the task, the spare part, and the certainty that the next patient will find a clean tool. What it demands instead is skill, because the examiner's own senses and memory carry the work the missing instrument would have done, and discipline, because an improvised tool must still be cleaned and used with the same care as a manufactured one.

The flashlight built into a mobile telephone replaces the penlight for many field examiners, and its physics is the same light-emitting diode in a smaller package. It gives up the shaped beam of a clinical penlight, the colored or red-free filter, and the ability to be cleaned as a sealed instrument, because the telephone is a porous object that should not be wiped with aggressive chemicals. The common error is to hold the telephone so close that the examiner's fingers and shadow block the view, or to use the brightest setting, which makes the patient turn away and closes the pupil. The warning sign of a failing telephone light is a beam that flickers or dims when the telephone moves, which usually means a weak battery or a loose connection. The telephone must be cleaned according to its own instructions and kept out of the examination field where it can contaminate the gloves.

The camera of a mobile telephone can serve as a magnifier, and its principle is that a small lens focuses an image onto a sensor and the image can be enlarged on the screen, stored, and shown to another clinician. In the field this turns a telephone into an improvised otoscope for the outer ear, a magnifier for a skin lesion, a reader for a faint label, and a means of sending a picture to a distant colleague for advice. It gives up the stereoscopic view, the controlled illumination, and the magnification of a true instrument, and it adds a hazard, because a photograph can flatter a lesion or hide its depth. The common error is to rely on digital zoom, which enlarges the pixels rather than the optics and produces a blurred picture that looks detailed on a small screen. The warning sign of a poor photograph is uneven light, a color cast, or a shadow across the subject, all of which change what the remote colleague is being asked to judge.

An improvised tuning fork is one of the more demanding substitutions, because the pitch of the substitute is not known. Any stiff metal object that vibrates, a heavy table fork, a spoon, or a piece of spring steel, can produce a tone when struck, and the vibration can be felt when its base is pressed against a bone. It cannot be used for the tuning fork tests of hearing, because those compare air conduction with bone conduction at a known frequency, and an uncalibrated piece of metal gives neither the frequency nor the purity the comparison requires. Other substitutions follow the same logic, because a rolled cone of paper with a flashlight behind it forms a crude speculum, the edge of a hand or the head of a stethoscope can elicit a reflex, and a length of string laid along a limb and measured against a ruler can replace a tape. Each demands that the examiner knows what the real instrument would have shown, states the limitation in the record, and confirms every frequency, method, and cleaning rule against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The field kit lives or dies by its consumables and its power. Specula, wooden depressors, cotton, alcohol wipes, gloves, replacement earpieces, bulbs, and batteries are the items that run out first, and a kit that is not restocked becomes a bag of useless instruments. The common field error is to store the kit with the batteries inside, so that a leaking cell corrodes the contacts and the tool is found dead at the moment it is needed. Another error is to reuse a single-use speculum or a wooden depressor because the supply is low, which is exactly the moment when the risk of transmission is highest. The warning sign of a failing field light is a beam that is yellow rather than white or that dims when the lamp is moved, and the response is to replace the battery or the bulb before the next patient.

Every measurement and observation made with these small tools ends as a record, and the record is part of the device system. A head circumference, a limb circumference, a reflex grade, the result of a hearing test, and the appearance of an eardrum are written into a chart or an electronic health record, or EHR, and the value depends on the units, the landmark, and the date being stated. Around that record sits a program of maintenance, in which a scheduled round checks the stethoscope tubing and earpieces, the otoscope bulb and lens, the ophthalmoscope beam and lenses, the hammer for cracks, the tuning forks for a clean tone, the penlight for a steady beam, and the tapes for stretch and legibility. The common failure of such a program is to treat these small tools as consumables that need no attention, so that they are replaced only when they break and never when they quietly degrade.

Almost none of the tools in this chapter has an alarm, and that absence is the central safety fact of the chapter. A monitor announces a failing battery and a ventilator announces a disconnected circuit, but a stethoscope with a pinhole, an otoscope with a failing bulb, an ophthalmoscope with a dirty window, and a tape that has stretched all continue to look and feel exactly as they should. The examiner therefore has to be the alarm, and the only way to be the alarm is to test the tool deliberately rather than to assume that yesterday's performance continues today. The simple tests are a visual inspection, a comparison with a second instrument, and a check against a known standard, and they take less time than the examination itself. The common error is to explain away a poor finding as a difficult patient, a narrow canal, a small pupil, or a quiet chest, when the difficulty lies in the tool.

Returning to the field, the value of these tools is not that they are simple but that they are the only instruments that always work. The field examiner must therefore protect them with the care a hospital gives to a scanner, because a cracked tube or a dead battery ends the diagnostic capability of an entire clinic. The substitutes described here, the telephone light, the telephone camera, the rolled paper speculum, and the improvised vibrating metal, are honest tools as long as their limits are stated and their hygiene is maintained. What they demand is a clear understanding of what each can show and what each can hide, and the discipline to record the difference, with every substitute, cleaning method, and improvised material confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before it is used on a patient.

To summarize, the basic examination tools are small devices that extend the ear, the eye, and the hand, and each works by a physical principle that can be stated plainly. The stethoscope couples chest vibrations to a column of air and offers a bell for low frequencies and a diaphragm for high ones, the otoscope funnels light into a narrow canal, and the ophthalmoscope follows the path of light into the eye to find the red reflex. The reflex hammer delivers a controlled stretch, the tuning fork supplies a known frequency, and the penlight, the tongue depressor, the tape, the pin, and the cotton wisp test the surface of the body against simple standards. In the field the same work is done with a telephone light, a telephone camera, a paper cone, and a piece of vibrating metal, each of which gives up precision and demands skill in return. Whatever the setting, every specification, every cleaning method, and every threshold must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the tool is used on a patient.
