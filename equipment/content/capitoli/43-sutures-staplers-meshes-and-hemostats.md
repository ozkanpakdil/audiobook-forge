# Chapter 43 — Sutures, Staplers, Meshes, and Hemostats

Every wound closed by hand is held by something that carries tension, and that task is the physics of a rope under load. Tissue heals by laying down collagen across a gap, but only while the edges stay in contact and the collagen matures. A closure device must bring the edges together, hold them against the forces that pull them apart, and keep holding while healing is weak. Four families answer that demand, the suture, the staple or clip, the mesh, and the hemostatic agent. A cough can place kilograms of force across an abdominal incision, so a closure that only looks neat may still fail.

The behavior of a suture is decided by what it is made of, whether the body dissolves it, and whether it is one strand or many. The useful strength of a suture is not its strength when implanted but the strength left after days or weeks in tissue. Absorbable materials lose strength as the body breaks the polymer apart, while non-absorbable materials keep their strength but stay as a foreign body. A monofilament is one smooth strand that slides through tissue easily and resists bacteria because there is nowhere to hide. A braided suture ties secure knots, but its interstices can harbor organisms and wick fluid along the strand.

A knot holds because friction between the strands resists the pull of the wound. A stiff, smooth monofilament has little friction, so it needs more throws and must be tied flat, while a soft braided material grips itself and needs fewer. The square knot, in which the strands cross left over right and then right over left, is the standard because it resists capsizing under load. A suture tied too loosely leaves the edges apart and one tied too tightly strangulates the tissue, so the throws must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Absorbable sutures are made from materials the body can dismantle, and they differ in how fast they lose strength. Catgut is made from the collagen of animal intestine, and its plain form loses strength within about seven to ten days while the chromic form holds for two to three weeks, intervals that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Synthetic absorbables are polymers broken down by hydrolysis, the reaction of the polymer with water, so their behavior is more predictable and tissue reaction is smaller. A braided synthetic such as polyglactin holds for two to three weeks, while a long-lasting monofilament such as polydioxanone retains strength for about six weeks. Absorbable materials suit tissue that cannot keep a foreign body, such as the urinary tract and the biliary tree.

Non-absorbable sutures are the permanent materials, chosen where strength must outlast healing. Silk handles superbly and ties securely, but it is braided, it provokes chronic inflammation, and it loses strength slowly over years. Nylon is a strong monofilament with low tissue reaction, but it is stiff and its knots tend to slip, so it needs several throws. Polypropylene is smoother and chemically inert, and it is the common choice for fine vascular work and for permanent reinforcement. Polyester is a strong braided synthetic, and stainless steel wire is the strongest and stiffest of all, used in sternal closure and in orthopedic work where nothing else holds.

A suture reaches the table as a sterile package, and everything about that package matters. It is delivered with a swaged needle that is pressed onto the strand instead of threaded through an eye. The swaged junction is stronger and smoother than an eyed needle, because it is the same diameter as the strand and leaves no doubled thickness to drag through tissue. The package states the material, the size, the needle type, the curvature, the length, and the expiry date, and those details must be confirmed before the pack is opened. A package that is torn or past its date must be discarded, and a suture cannot be reprocessed after it has been in tissue.

Suture sizes follow a convention that confuses almost everyone, because the numbers run in two directions from a central zero. A size written as zero is the reference, and sizes larger than zero are written as 1, 2, 3, and 4 and beyond, each step a thicker strand. Sizes smaller than zero are written as 2-0, 3-0, 4-0 and so on, each step a finer strand, and the finest microsurgical sutures reach 12-0. Beside this number stands a metric size, which is the diameter in tenths of a millimeter, so a 3-0 suture is about 0.2 millimeters across. The size for a given tissue must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Needles are where the closure meets the tissue, and the shape of the point decides what the needle does as it passes. A cutting needle has sharp edges that slice through dense tissue such as skin and fascia. A reverse-cutting needle puts the flat surface on the inside of the curve so the cut is directed away from the wound edge. A taper needle has a rounded point that spreads the fibers apart rather than cutting them, and it is used in bowel, blood vessel, and liver. The curvature is quoted as a fraction of a circle, and the choice depends on how much room the surgeon has to rotate the wrist.

The needle is the sharpest object in the operating room and it carries the greatest risk to the operator. A needle-stick injury can transmit bloodborne infection from a patient to a member of the team, and the risk is highest with a needle that has just been in a deep vessel. The safety hazard is a needle left loose on the field, handed from hand to hand, or forgotten on a drape. The common operator error is to reach for a dropped needle with bare fingers instead of a forceps and a sharps container. The recommended practice is the neutral zone, in which the needle is placed on a tray by one person and picked up by another so that no two hands ever hold it at once. The characteristic alarm is the sudden absence of the needle from its counted mat, and the needle count must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Suturing is a two-handed skill, and the instruments that support it are as important as the strand. A needle holder is a hinged clamp with a short, strong jaw and a ratchet. Its jaws are cross-serrated or fitted with tungsten carbide inserts that grip the needle without letting it rotate. The needle is grasped about two thirds of the way from the point for a curved needle, so enough length is exposed to pass through tissue but the grip is not on the sharp end. The holder is rotated through the arc of the needle rather than pushed straight, because pushing a curved needle follows a different path and tears the tissue.

A surgical stapler replaces the tied loop of a suture with a small piece of bent wire, and its physics is a controlled plastic deformation. The staple is a U-shaped piece of metal held in a cartridge, with a driver above it and an anvil below it that has two curved depressions. As the legs are driven down they curl inward and close into a B shape. That B shape matters, because the closed staple must be tight enough to hold the tissue but loose enough not to crush the blood supply inside its loop. The height of the finished staple is set by the thickness of the tissue and by the gap designed into the cartridge, so the tissue must match the stapler. The correct staple height must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, because a cartridge too short will crush thick tissue and one too long will not close thin tissue.

The stapler family divides by the geometry of the closure it makes. A skin stapler fires one staple at a time across an incision, and it is fast, strong, and well tolerated, though its staples must be removed later with a dedicated extractor. A linear stapler places rows of staples across a length of tissue and divides it with a blade in the same instrument, and it is used to close the stomach, the bowel, and the lung. A circular stapler fires a ring of staples around a circular knife, and it is the instrument of the colorectal anastomosis, where a donut of tissue is cut from the inside and two bowel ends are joined by a ring the surgeon can inspect. A linear cutter combines rows of staples with a knife on the same pass, and its reload cartridges come in lengths and colors. The cartridge code must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Staplers fail in ways that are particular to mechanical devices, and a misfire is often discovered only when the tissue is already divided. A cartridge that is not fully seated, a safety interlock that has not been released, or a piece of tissue caught in the mechanism will produce an incomplete staple line. A malformed staple has its legs splayed outward instead of curled inward, so it holds nothing and leaves a hole. The characteristic signals are the feel and the sound of firing, because a completed line gives a firm sweep of the handle and a distinct click. The safety hazard is a staple line across a hollow organ that later leaks, and the common operator error is to fire without confirming that the anvil and cartridge are aligned and the tissue lies flat. Reloads are single-use, and a partly fired cartridge must be discarded because its remaining staples are no longer in a known position.

Skin clips are the simplest form of staple, and they are a device that the ward nurse as well as the surgeon must understand. A clip is a piece of wire shaped into a wide squared U, and it is applied across the skin with an applier that holds the two ends and bends them inward, so the clip lies over the wound edges like a bridge. The advantage is speed and strength on a scalp, an abdomen, or a limb. Removal is done with a clip remover that hooks the center of the clip, squeezes the ends apart, and lifts it away, and the common operator error is to pull a clip out with a forceps and scissors, which tears the fresh wound. The interval between clips and the timing of removal must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Surgical mesh answers a different problem, because it does not close a gap but reinforces a wall that cannot be trusted to hold. The physics is the distribution of load, because a flat sheet spreads the tension of the abdominal wall across a large area instead of concentrating it where sutures pass through tissue. A synthetic mesh is a woven or knitted sheet of polymer, usually polypropylene, polyester, or expanded polytetrafluoroethylene, and the knit pattern gives it a pore size, which is the distance between the strands. A macroporous mesh has pores larger than about 75 micrometers, a dimension that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and it allows immune cells and blood vessels to grow in so the mesh integrates with the tissue. A microporous mesh bridges the gap effectively but allows bacteria to hide in its interstices where immune cells cannot reach them.

Meshes come in several families, and the choice between them is a bet about infection. Permanent synthetic meshes are the workhorses of hernia repair and abdominal wall reconstruction, and they are strong and durable, but once infected they usually cannot be saved and must be removed. Bacteria adhere to the polymer and form a biofilm that no antibiotic can reach. Biological meshes are made from animal collagen or human donor tissue, processed to remove the cells, and they act as a scaffold that the body repopulates and eventually replaces with native tissue. They are useful in a contaminated field, but they are expensive, weaker at the outset, and can stretch. Composite meshes combine a permanent layer for strength with an absorbable or anti-adhesive layer that faces the bowel, so the mesh does not adhere to the intestine.

Sutures can tack the mesh at the corners and edges, but each suture is a point of tension that can pull through. Tacks fired from a mechanical device hold faster but can injure a nerve or a vessel if placed blindly. Some repairs rely on the pressure of the abdomen to hold the mesh, with only a few absorbable tacks to keep it in place during healing, and tissue glue has its own role in the groin. The common operator error is to cut a mesh to fit the defect exactly, because the mesh must overlap the defect by several centimeters to carry the load. A mesh that touches the skin is contaminated and should not be implanted, so the sterile field is not broken until the mesh is in place.

A hemostatic agent helps blood to clot where a suture or a ligature cannot reach, and its physical principle is either to concentrate the clotting machinery, to provide a scaffold for the clot, or to press on the bleeding surface. A topical hemostat is a dry sheet, sponge, or powder placed on the bleeding area. It works by absorbing water from the blood so that clotting factors and platelets become concentrated at the surface. Oxidized cellulose and oxidized regenerated cellulose are acidic sheets that swell into a brown gel when they meet blood, and the acidity itself helps to denature proteins and start the clotting cascade. A gelatin sponge is a porous foam that holds many times its own weight in blood and provides a physical plug. A collagen sponge or a microfibrillar collagen powder works in a similar way, with a surface that attracts platelets.

The hazards of hemostatic agents come from two properties, which are that they swell and that they are foreign material. An oxidized cellulose or gelatin sponge swells as it absorbs blood, and a sponge left in a confined space such as the spinal canal, the orbit, or a closed joint can press on a nerve or a vessel. The resorbable sponges are broken down over weeks, but a large mass of them can become a nidus for infection. The common operator error is to pack a wound and close it without removing the excess, so the retained mass later mimics an abscess. A powder sprinkled into a blind bleeding cavity can also clump and swell, so it must be delivered to the bleeding point rather than poured in.

Beyond the passive agents stand the active ones, which supply the components of the clotting reaction rather than merely concentrating them. Thrombin is an enzyme that converts fibrinogen into fibrin, and applied with a gelatin or collagen carrier it forms a clot within seconds, but it is inactive in a patient whose blood has no fibrinogen. A fibrin sealant supplies both fibrinogen and thrombin in two syringes that meet at the tip, and the mixture forms a gel that glues tissue surfaces and seals small leaks. Topical powders and sprays are designed for the broad oozing surface of a burn, a liver bed, or a tumor resection, where no single vessel can be ligated. The characteristic signal of these products is the rapid formation of a firm clot, and their storage temperature must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Bone wax is a special hemostat for bleeding from cut bone, and it is a device whose misuse is a classic error. The wax is a malleable mixture based on beeswax, and it works purely by mechanical tamponade. It is pressed into the open ends of bleeding vessels and the spaces of cancellous bone, blocking the flow without any clotting chemistry. The problem is that bone wax is not absorbed, so it remains in the bone forever, and it can interfere with the union of a fracture, act as a foreign body, and increase the risk of infection. The safety hazard is the patient who has wax left in a sternum or a long bone and later develops a chronic infection or a non-union. The common operator error is to smear wax across a whole bone surface instead of pressing a small amount into the specific bleeding point, so the amount used must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Drains are the last element of a closure, because a closed wound that accumulates fluid under pressure will open or become infected, and a drain gives that fluid a path out. The simplest drain is a flat strip of soft rubber or plastic, called an open drain, which works by capillary action and gravity, so fluid flows along its surface into a dressing. A closed suction drain is a tube with holes along its end and a bulb or canister that maintains a gentle negative pressure, and the pressure difference is perhaps 50 to 100 millimeters of mercury, a value that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The relationship between the drain and the closure is intimate, because the drain site is a hole in the sterile barrier and a path for bacteria. A drain is therefore brought out through a separate stab incision rather than the main wound where possible. The safety hazard is a drain that erodes, falls out, or stays too long, and the common operator error is to anchor it so loosely that it migrates or so tightly that it cuts the skin.

A drain is useful only while it is patent, and its failure modes are easy to miss. The tube can kink, the holes can be blocked by clot or omentum, the suction bulb can be left uncompressed, and the canister can fill so that the negative pressure is lost. Each of these turns an apparently working drain into a closed cavity with a foreign body inside. The characteristic signal of a blocked drain is a sudden fall in output while the wound swells and the patient becomes febrile. The common operator error is to flush the drain blindly with saline, which can push infection into the cavity rather than clear the tube. A drain is also a retained item if its anchoring suture is removed and the tube is forgotten, so it is counted with the sponges and the needles. The count at the beginning of the case, at the first closure of a cavity, and at the final skin closure is the standard that prevents a retained item.

The hospital-grade supply of closing materials depends on an infrastructure that a small clinic does not have. The central sterile services department receives the reusable instruments, cleans them of blood and tissue, inspects them, packs them with an indicator, and sterilizes them. A steam sterilizer at 134 degrees Celsius for about 3 minutes, or at 121 degrees Celsius for about 30 minutes, is the common cycle for wrapped instruments, temperatures and times that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A tightly wrapped or overfilled pack fails even when the sterilizer works, and a pack on a damp shelf loses sterility over time.

The consumables of this chapter are numerous, and each has a defined life and a defined failure. Suture strands and needles, staple cartridges, skin clips, clip removers, mesh sheets, tacks, hemostatic sheets and powders, bone wax, drains and canisters, needle counter mats, and sharps containers pass through a case once and are replaced. The reusable closing instruments, which include the needle holders, forceps, scissors, staple removers, and the staplers themselves, need their own care. Blood and saline left in the joint of a needle holder corrode the hinge, and a dropped stapler must be inspected and its alignment verified before use. Cleaning is with the approved detergent in an ultrasonic bath or by hand with a soft brush, followed by rinsing, drying, lubrication where the maker allows, and sterilization by the correct cycle. The characteristic sign of a worn needle holder is a needle that rotates in the jaws under load, and the replacement criteria must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Before any closing material is used, the operator must perform a short series of checks that takes seconds and prevents the worst errors. The package must be intact, dry, within its expiry date, and marked with a sterilization indicator that has changed as expected. The strand must be inspected for fraying, for a kink that will weaken it, and for discoloration, and the needle must be firmly attached and straight, because a bent needle makes a curved path. A staple cartridge must be seated fully, must match the tissue thickness, and must be of the type the case plan calls for, and the stapler must be checked for the alignment of the anvil. A mesh must be opened only onto the sterile field and must never be touched by an ungloved hand, and each of these checks must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

In the mobile or frontier clinic the same needs are met with a much smaller inventory, and the first thing given up is the choice of material. The field kit usually carries one or two sutures only, most often a braided absorbable for deep tissue and a monofilament nylon for the skin. The size is chosen by the tissue rather than by a catalogue, with 2-0 and 3-0 for fascia and 4-0 for skin being a common range that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The needles are more often reusable, and they are sharpened, straightened, and inspected between cases, so they are duller than a new swaged needle and demand more force and a firmer grasp. Where a manufactured suture is unavailable, the team may use a monofilament made for another purpose, but any substitution must be sterile, of a known diameter, and tested for knot security before it is trusted in a wound. The characteristic alarm of the field is the snap of a suture under tension, and the common operator error is to compensate for a dull needle or a weak strand by pulling harder, which tears the tissue.

The field variant of this chapter is less a device than a method, because sterile technique is what makes an affordable closure safe. Without a steam autoclave, the instruments and the reusable needles are sterilized in a pressure cooker at about 121 degrees Celsius for about 30 minutes, a cycle that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The sterile field is built from a boiled or chemically disinfected cloth, and the operator works with gloves if any are available and with a strict no-touch technique if they are not. Skin preparation with an alcohol or chlorhexidine solution, a clean drape, and a firm separation between the clean and dirty hands are the substitute for the missing operating room. The characteristic signal of a break in technique is a wound that becomes red and hot on the second or third day.

What the field clinic gives up beyond the choice of suture is the mechanical closure and the advanced reinforcement. A linear or circular stapler requires cartridges, calibration, and a supply chain, so the field surgeon performs a hand-sewn anastomosis with a continuous or interrupted suture, which is slower and demands more skill but needs nothing beyond a needle and a strand. Mesh is often unavailable or unaffordable, so a hernia is repaired by tissue approximation with a suture, which has a higher recurrence rate but leaves no foreign body and no biofilm to remove when it becomes infected. The hemostatic sheet and the fibrin sealant are replaced by pressure, by packing, by a ligature, and by the oldest agent of all, which is a hot instrument applied to a bleeding point. Adhesive strips and tissue adhesive become the field closure for a small clean laceration, and they spare the suture entirely when the wound edges can be brought together without tension.

The field variant demands more of the operator in three respects, which are skill, judgment, and stock discipline. A hand-tied knot must hold on the first attempt, because there is no assistant with a stapler to rescue a leaking anastomosis, and a wound closed under tension will fail in a patient who cannot return for a second operation. The judgment is about when not to close primarily, because a contaminated wound is better left open and packed than closed over an infection. The stock discipline is about expiry and storage, because a suture package that has been damp, sun-baked, or crushed in a vehicle is no longer guaranteed. The characteristic signal of poor stock control is a case that is underway when the last 3-0 strand is found to be frayed.

To summarize, closure is a mechanical bet placed on biology, because every strand, staple, mesh, and hemostatic plug must carry load only until the tissue can carry it alone. The material decides how long that help lasts, the needle and the instrument decide how cleanly it is delivered, and the knots and the staple line decide whether the help holds. Mesh and hemostatic agents extend the same principle to reinforcement and to bleeding, and both bring a foreign body whose infection and swelling must be respected. The hospital version buys certainty with cartridges, biological indicators, and a sterile supply chain, while the field version buys access with a needle, a reusable strand, and a sterilizing pot. In every setting, the operator's checks, the counts, and the confirmation of every setting against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol are what turn the hardware into a safe closure.
