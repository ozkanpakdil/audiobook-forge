# Chapter 58 — Disaster and Mass-Casualty Equipment

Disaster and mass-casualty equipment exists for the day when the number of injured people arriving at one door is larger than the number the building was built to hold. The governing physics is throughput. Patients arrive at a rate, and each one consumes a share of staff, oxygen, blood, and operating time. The system fails when the arrival rate exceeds the rate at which those shares can be delivered. This chapter describes the hospital assets an institution keeps ready, and then the field variant. The field variant is the tented and truck-borne hospital that does the same work with a fraction of the infrastructure.

Triage converts a clinical impression into a color and a destination. It rests on a hard judgment about who can still be saved. A patient who is not breathing after a simple airway maneuver will not survive, however much time is spent. A patient who breathes rapidly and cannot follow a command needs an immediate intervention. A patient who walks and speaks can wait. A respiratory rate above 30 per minute, a capillary refill longer than 2 seconds, and an inability to follow a command are the classic cut points, and every one of those values must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the scoring system is used on a patient.

The common operator error in triage is to sort by the loudest voice or the most visible blood. The triage tag is the physical record of the sorting decision, and it must survive water, blood, mud, and being dragged along the ground. It carries a tear-off stub that stays with the patient and a duplicate that goes to the command post. The colors are a language, with red for immediate, yellow for delayed, green for walking wounded, and black for deceased or expectant. The safety hazard is a tag attached where it cannot be seen, such as inside clothing, and the common operator error is to fill in the tag after the patient has moved rather than at first contact.

The operator must check the tag for legibility, firm attachment, and a written time. A tag without a time cannot show how fast a patient is deteriorating. A tag that uses radio frequency identification or a printed barcode carries the same number in machine-readable form. Its read range and durability must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before it is relied upon. A well-resourced hospital prepares with fixed and cached assets that are tested on a schedule. The fixed assets include a decontamination suite with a separate entrance, a floor sloping to a sealed drain, a holding tank for the run-off, and an exhaust that filters air before release.

The cached assets include surge carts holding airway, hemorrhage control, and vascular access supplies. Inside the building the surge ward is made by converting spaces that already carry piped oxygen and medical vacuum. A recovery room or a day surgery unit can be converted in an hour, while a corridor fitted with cylinders cannot. Every ventilation rate, pressure cascade, and oxygen outlet pressure must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before a patient enters a converted space. The common operator error is to store the cache in a basement that floods or behind a door the disaster has blocked.

The field variant begins with a frame and a sheet of fabric. The physics of that frame decides whether the hospital stands or becomes a hazard. A shelter frame of aluminum or steel tubes is held in tension by guy lines, and its curved shape sheds wind by leading the load into the ground. The flooring matters as much as the frame, because a hospital floor must be level, cleanable, and dry. It must separate the patient from mud, water, and the insects that follow both.

The safety hazard of the shelter is a stake driven through an unseen buried cable or water pipe. The operator must check the anchor points, the guy line tension, the roof for water pooling, and the ground for the high-water mark of the last flood. The wind load and the anchoring specification must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the shelter is occupied. Electricity in the field comes from a generator. A generator is a rotating machine that converts the chemical energy of fuel into a magnetic field sweeping past coils of wire.

A field generator for a tented hospital is typically rated between 5 and 100 kilowatts. Its output is 230 volts at 50 hertz or 120 volts at 60 hertz depending on the region. Every one of those values must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the generator is connected to a patient care area. The safety hazards are the exhaust, which contains carbon monoxide and must be piped away from every occupied space, and the electrical hazard. The electrical hazard requires an earth connection and a residual current device for every circuit that touches a patient. The characteristic alarms are low oil pressure, high coolant temperature, and overload. The common operator error is to load one generator with heaters, sterilizers, and imaging equipment at once.

The operator must check the fuel level, the oil level, the air filter, the battery charge, and the earthing before every shift. Lighting a field hospital is a problem of shadows rather than of brightness. A clinician who stands between the lamp and the wound casts the shadow that hides the work. A lighting tower raises a mast carrying several light emitting diode heads. The physics of the mast is simple, because doubling the height quarters the light on the ground. Battery-powered head torches and hand lamps light a single task and keep both hands free. An area light must be placed to the side and above so that the operator's body does not intercept the beam.

The safety hazard of lighting is glare, which destroys the dark adaptation of staff who must work at night. The second hazard is a mast that contacts an overhead power line. The operator must check the mast for a firm footing, the cable for damage, and the battery charge before the light is needed. The illuminance required for a dressing station and for an operating table must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Water is the heaviest and most continuously consumed supply in a field hospital. It is needed for drinking, for hand hygiene, for cleaning instruments, and for decontamination. It is stored in collapsible tanks and jerrycans close to the point of use, and every transfer from a large container to a small one is an opportunity for contamination.

Treatment of water follows filtration, sedimentation, and disinfection. A chlorine solution is added at a concentration and left in contact for a period that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the water is used for drinking or for wound care. Waste is a public health problem before it is a housekeeping problem. Sharps go into a rigid puncture-resistant container carried to the point of use. Human waste goes into a latrine sited downhill and downwind from the water source and the kitchen. The safety hazards are open burning of plastic, which releases toxic fumes, and a filled sharps container left beside a walkway where a child can reach it. The operator must check the latrine for a lid and the incinerator for a clear area and a working thermometer.

Oxygen in a fixed hospital arrives through a pipeline. A field hospital must carry its oxygen in cylinders or make it on the spot. A cylinder stores the gas at high pressure, so the gauge is a fuel gauge, and a concentrator instead passes air through a zeolite sieve that adsorbs nitrogen and releases oxygen. A typical portable concentrator delivers up to about 5 liters per minute at a purity it holds above 90 per cent. The flow rate, the purity alarm threshold, and the cylinder pressure limits must each be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient. The safety hazards are common to tent and ward, because oxygen accelerates combustion and any oil or grease on a fitting can ignite.

Cylinders must be secured upright away from heat, and the tubing must be kept free of grease. The characteristic alarm of a concentrator is a low purity or low flow alert. The common operator error is to run it from a generator that is already overloaded. The operator must check the gauge, the regulator gasket, the tubing for kinks, and the intake filter before every use. Mass-casualty kits are designed to be opened once, and that single decision explains almost everything about them. The pack is sealed at the factory, and it is consumed and replaced as a whole rather than restocked item by item during an incident.

Modularity is the organizing principle of the mass-casualty kit. A trauma module, an airway module, a burn module, and a pediatric module can be combined. The safety hazard is a pack whose seal was broken for training and then taped shut. The common operator error is to improvise from the pack and leave the remaining contents scattered.

The operator must check the seal, the expiry dates, and the contents list at every rotation. The pack must be stored where it can be reached without moving anything else. The consumables that decide a mass-casualty day are the inexpensive ones, because hemorrhage and airway obstruction kill faster than any other consequence of injury. A windlass tourniquet, a compression dressing, a hemostatic gauze, and a chest seal for a penetrating wound of the chest are the first-line items. Their mechanism is mechanical rather than pharmacological, because they work by occluding a vessel with pressure, sealing a hole, or promoting the formation of a clot. Vascular access supplies, crystalloid fluids, and a limited formulary of analgesia, antibiotics, and tetanus prophylaxis follow. So do splints, pelvic binders, burn dressings, and simple warming blankets.

The safety hazard is a tourniquet left in place without a written time, because a limb can be lost to a tourniquet that no one remembers. The common operator error is to loosen a tourniquet to check the bleeding and restart it in the process. Every drug dose, fluid volume, and permitted duration of tourniquet use must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Decontamination is the removal of a hazardous substance from a person or an object. It works by dilution, by chemical neutralization, or by physical removal. None of these reaches a substance still trapped in clothing, so the removal of clothing is often the single most effective step.

A decontamination corridor is arranged in zones. There is a dirty zone where the patient arrives, a shower zone, and a clean zone. A one-way flow of people and water keeps anything on the dirty side from travelling back to the clean side. The shower is a row of heads on a frame, and its water temperature, pressure, and duration all matter. The run-off is contaminated and must be captured in a bunded area or a holding tank. The water temperature, contact time, shower duration, and disposal limits must all be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the system is used on people.

The common operator error in decontamination is to let a patient walk out of the shower into the clean zone wearing the same shoes. The people who run a decontamination line wear a chemical-protective suit, gloves chosen for the specific agent, boots, and a respirator. That ensemble blocks the three routes of exposure, which are the skin, the lungs, and the eyes. An impermeable suit traps heat and water vapor, so the wearer's core temperature rises during the work. The safe duration of a single shift inside it must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the shift begins. The safety hazard of the ensemble is the doffing step, because the outer surface is contaminated and a glove that touches the face during removal defeats the whole barrier.

The common operator error is to reuse a suit that has been splashed, or to work alone at the shower. The characteristic alarm is a worker who stops talking or stops sweating, because both are signs of heat illness inside equipment that hides the face. The operator must check the suit for tears and perished seams, check the respirator filter against the agent, and enforce a buddy system and a work and rest cycle. Communication equipment in a disaster works on radio waves. Radio waves behave in ways a user must understand before the network fails. Very high frequency, or VHF, and ultra high frequency, or UHF, signals travel mostly in straight lines.

Lower frequency signals bend around obstacles and follow the ground for long distances but carry less information. A satellite telephone bypasses the terrestrial network and depends on a clear view of the sky. A global positioning system receiver turns signals from satellites into a position and a time. The safety hazard is a radio left on the wrong channel or locked in a vehicle. The common operator error is to transmit a long message on a shared channel during an emergency, because a channel is a single resource.

Morgue capacity is an equipment problem usually discovered too late, because the dead consume space and cold at a steady rate while the living consume attention. A body begins to decompose when its temperature falls to that of the environment and the bacteria it carries begin to multiply. Refrigeration slows that process by holding the tissue a few degrees above freezing. A refrigerated container is a standard transport unit with its own compressor and its own power demand. A fixed hospital morgue uses a refrigerated room with racking and a lifting trolley. Both need an alarm that sounds when the temperature rises above its set point. The body bag is the primary containment, and it must be leak-proof, strong enough to be lifted by its handles, and labeled with an identifier matching the tag and the written record.

The safety hazard is a failure of cooling during a power cut. The common operator error is to overload a rack so that a bag blocks air circulation. The operating temperature, high temperature alarm setting, and maximum storage time must all be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before bodies are accepted. The operator must also plan for identification, because a container without a chain of custody for every bag is a container of unknown people. Blood in a disaster is a supply chain that must be kept cold from the donor to the patient. Red cells are stored between 2 and 6 degrees Celsius, plasma is frozen, and platelets are stored warm and agitated, and each of those ranges must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before a unit is issued.

The cold chain has three vulnerable points, which are the blood bank refrigerator, the transport box, and the point of transfusion. The safety hazards are the wrong unit, which is fatal, and the cold chain break, which is silent. Every unit must be checked against the patient's identity and the storage history before transfusion. When the cold chain cannot be kept, the alternative is the walking donor. A walking donor is a person tested within minutes who gives blood directly to a patient who needs it. Fresh whole blood carries red cells, plasma, and platelets at once and needs no cold chain if it is transfused quickly. A donor may carry an infection that a rapid test cannot detect during the window period, so the practice must be reserved for a patient whose death from hemorrhage is more certain than the risk of a transfusion-transmitted infection.

The screening test, anticoagulant volume, collection volume, and maximum storage time before transfusion must all be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A stockpile is equipment that is doing nothing, and that is precisely its value and its danger. The physical enemies of a stockpile are heat, moisture, sunlight, pests, and time. Every item must be stored off the floor on pallets, away from the walls, and in a stack that lets the oldest stock be reached first. The principle governing the order of issue is first expiry first out.

The safety hazard is a stockpile raided for a routine shortage and never restored. The common operator error is to issue from the front of the stack, which leaves the oldest stock buried at the back. The storage temperature, humidity limit, and expiry rules must all be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the stock is accepted. The operator must check the stock card against the physical count, check the seal on every kit, and check for rodent droppings and water stains on every pallet. What a stockpile contains is decided by the events it is meant to answer.

The safety hazard of a stockpile is a pack that assumes a skill the responding staff do not have, because a device that is not understood is either unused or misused. The common operator error is to fill a kit with a large quantity of one item and none of its essential partner, such as a chest drain without a drainage bottle. Maintenance in a field hospital is a daily ritual rather than a monthly event. A machine that is not serviced in a shelter will fail within days rather than months. The generator needs its oil checked and its fuel filtered, the concentrator needs its intake filter cleaned, and the refrigerator needs its condenser cleaned and its temperature logged. The shelter needs its guy lines retensioned after every storm.

Preventive maintenance is organized around the running hours of each machine rather than the calendar. A generator that runs for 20 hours a day reaches its service interval in a fraction of the time the manual assumes. The safety hazard is a repair made with the wrong part or with a length of wire instead of a fuse. The common operator error is to skip the log because the day was busy, which removes the only evidence that the machine is being cared for. Every service interval, tightening value, and filter change period must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the work begins. Cleaning follows the same discipline, because reprocessing is a sequence whose order matters more than the equipment used to perform it. Instruments are collected at the point of use, kept moist, washed with detergent and a brush, rinsed, inspected, dried, packed, and sterilized.

A pressure cooker used as a sterilizer reaches a higher temperature than boiling water because the sealed lid raises the internal pressure. A typical field cycle is 121 degrees Celsius for 30 minutes at the corresponding pressure. That temperature, that time, and that pressure must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before any load is processed. The safety hazard is a cooker opened before the pressure has fallen, which can release a jet of scalding steam. The operator must check the gauge against a known reference, check the lid gasket for cracks, and record the load, temperature, time, and chemical indicator result for every cycle. The arithmetic of surge capacity is the most important calculation of the day, and it is made before the day arrives. The number of patients a facility can treat is the smallest of several numbers.

The limiting numbers are the staffed beds, the available staff, the oxygen supply, the blood supply, the operating room time, and the rate at which patients can be moved onward. A hospital with 200 beds and 20 nurses can hold 200 patients but cannot monitor more than a fraction of them. The effective capacity is therefore set by the nursing ratio rather than by the bed count. The safety hazard is a plan that assumes staff will work beyond a safe number of consecutive hours, because exhausted staff make errors at a rate that rises faster than the hours they add. The common operator error is to count equipment without counting the consumables that the equipment consumes. A ventilator without tubing and a monitor without electrodes are both counted as capacity that does not exist. The nursing ratio, the maximum shift length, and the monitoring standard must all be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the plan is adopted.

Diagnostics in the field must be chosen for what they change, because a test that does not alter treatment consumes time and battery. A handheld ultrasound device, a portable blood analyzer, a hemoglobin meter, and a glucose meter each trade accuracy for speed and independence. The physical principle of the handheld ultrasound probe is that a vibrating crystal sends a pulse of sound into tissue and listens for the echo. The depth of the echo and the time it takes to return produce the image. The safety hazards are the probe used without a cover on broken skin and the device charged from a generator whose voltage is unstable. Every analyzer calibration, control interval, and reference range must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the result is used in a clinical decision.

Patient tracking works on the principle of the triage tag, which is that identity must be attached to the patient and duplicated elsewhere. A numbered wristband, a duplicated card, a photograph, and a written log all carry the same identifier for family reunification. The electronic health record may be unavailable because the network is down, so a paper form that mirrors it must exist. The characteristic alarm of a failing tracking system is a patient who cannot be found. The equipment of a mass-casualty response must be scaled for the people who actually arrive, and children and pregnant women are always among them. Pediatric airway equipment, smaller bag and mask sets, weight-based dosing charts, and warming devices are commonly missing from an adult-oriented cache.

Their absence is a failure of planning rather than of supply. A newborn delivered in a field hospital needs warmth, a clean cord, and a way to support breathing. The physics of that warming is the same as in a fixed nursery, because a wet newborn loses heat by evaporation and radiation faster than a dry one. The safety hazard is a dose calculated from an adult chart. The common operator error is to use an adult tourniquet or an adult cervical collar on a small child. Every pediatric dose, device size, and warming temperature must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Planning must also include the equipment for survivors of sexual violence, the medications for hemorrhage after birth, and the means to keep a mother and her newborn together.

The characteristic failures of a field hospital are rarely the failures of its most complicated machine. The generator fails because of fuel contamination or a clogged filter. The refrigerator fails quietly during the night. The radio fails because its battery was never charged. The sterilizer fails because its gasket was cracked, and the shelter fails because one guy line was loosened by a vehicle. Each of these failures produces a signal before it produces a catastrophe. The generator gives a warning of low oil pressure, the refrigerator gives a temperature alarm, the radio goes silent on a scheduled check, and the sterilizer fails its chemical indicator.

The safety hazard is a monitor whose alarm has been silenced at the panel and never reset. The common operator error is to treat a recurring alarm as a nuisance rather than as a pattern. The operator must investigate every alarm at the device rather than at the panel, and must not return a machine to service until the cause is identified. The last piece of equipment is the drill, because a cache that has never been unpacked in the dark is an unknown quantity. The safety hazard is a drill that damages the cache without restoring it. The operator must time each step, record each failure, and check that every expendable item has been replaced, every battery charged, and every fuel can refilled.

The field variant gives up the things a building provides without thought. What it demands in return is redundancy, simplicity, and a willingness to work by hand. A fixed hospital assumes mains power, piped oxygen, piped water, drainage, climate control, and a network. A field hospital carries its own generator, its own cylinders or concentrator, its own tank, its own waste route, and its own radio. Each substitution removes a layer of convenience and adds a layer of maintenance. The safety hazard is the assumption that a simpler device is a safer device.

Human beings are the least replaceable equipment in the response, and their limits must be planned with the same care as the fuel supply. Rotation, shade, water, food, a place to sleep, and a clear rule about the maximum length of a shift are clinical equipment for the staff, and their numbers must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the roster is written. The characteristic alarm of an overworked team is a delay in the routine tasks rather than an error in the dramatic ones. It is easy to miss until the errors begin.

To summarize, the equipment of disaster and mass-casualty response is the equipment of arithmetic under pressure. The field half is a shelter on a frame, a generator, a light tower, a water tank, an oxygen supply, a radio, and a set of packs designed to be opened once and replaced whole, and its virtue is speed while its cost is fragility. The triage tag is the smallest and most important object in the chapter, because it carries the decision that sends a patient to the right place at the right time. Every threshold, temperature, pressure, dose, and alarm setting named here must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before it is used on a patient.
