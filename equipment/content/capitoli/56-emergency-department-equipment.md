# Chapter 56 — Emergency Department Equipment

An emergency department receives patients whose diagnosis is unknown, so its equipment must support a decision before it supports a treatment. The problem is sorting, because twenty people may arrive within an hour with complaints that range from a sprained ankle to a ruptured aorta, and attention and machines must be divided in proportion to risk. Every device has two jobs at once, to measure something quickly and to make that measurement visible to whoever decides next, so the room shortens the distance between the patient and the next action. The department is built around time, because the interventions that save lives in trauma, stroke, sepsis, and heart attack lose their value as minutes pass, and this chapter describes the hospital arrangement before the field version, where a table, a light, and a bag do the work of a building.

Triage is the first instrument, and its principle is that scarce attention is allocated by a rule rather than by the order of arrival. A scale takes a few variables, such as the ability to walk, the respiratory rate, the oxygen saturation, the heart rate, and the level of consciousness, and combines them into one level that says how soon the patient must be seen. A five-level arrangement is common, from a patient who needs resuscitation at once to one who can safely wait, and every threshold between the levels must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The scale asks the same questions in the same order for every patient, which keeps the last dramatic case from coloring the present judgment, and the commonest error is to grade behavior rather than physiology.

The tools at the triage desk are chosen for speed rather than precision. A pulse oximeter shines two wavelengths of light through a fingertip and compares how much of each is absorbed, because oxygen-rich blood absorbs the two differently. A reading below 90 per cent is a common trigger for immediate assessment, and that figure and every other threshold in this chapter must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A thermometer reads infrared energy, a blood pressure cuff measures the pressure at which an artery in the arm is occluded and released, and a glucose meter reads a current produced when glucose reacts with an enzyme on a strip, so a reading below 3.9 millimoles per liter in a confused patient changes the plan.

The trauma bay follows one rule, that everything needed in the first 10 minutes must be reachable without stepping away from the patient. The patient lies on a bed that can be raised, tilted head down, and dropped flat in seconds, and working surfaces sit on both sides because two teams must work at once. A hard board or backboard gives a firm surface for chest compressions and spinal precautions, because a soft mattress absorbs the force the hands intend for the heart. The overhead light illuminates a deep wound without the operator's head casting the crucial shadow, so a boom-mounted light with a sterile handle is used, and suction, oxygen, the monitor, the defibrillator, and the airway trolley are mounted so a hand finds them by habit.

The trauma bed is chosen for access and for compatibility with imaging. It is narrow enough that a rescuer can reach across the patient, and a radiolucent section under the torso lets a film cassette or a fluoroscope be positioned without moving the patient. The head drops away so the airway can be managed from above, and the side rails fold flat so a board can slide underneath. The commonest error is to leave a backboard in place after the resuscitation, and the second is to imagine that lying on a board immobilizes the neck without a collar and blocks.

The surgical light gathers light from a lamp through a fiber bundle or a light-emitting diode array and focuses it with a reflector, so it delivers a bright beam with little heat at the wound. The patient monitor measures several signals at once, and its alarms are the department's early warning system, so a repeated alarm must be treated as data rather than as noise. A repeated lead-off tone means an electrode has lost contact, while a low saturation tone means a real physiological change until proven otherwise, and every alarm threshold must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The commonest error is to silence the alarm and walk away, which turns a warning into silence.

The rapid infuser turns the trauma bay from assessment into treatment, and its physics is pressure applied to a warmed fluid. A bag hung on a pole flows by gravity at a slow rate, so the machine uses a pump to push fluid through wide-bore tubing and a heater to bring it close to body temperature. A typical device delivers several hundred milliliters per minute and warms the fluid to about 38 degrees Celsius, and every flow rate and temperature setting must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Warming matters because large volumes of cold fluid make the patient colder and cold stops the clotting enzymes, and the commonest error is to connect the set to a narrow cannula that cannot carry the flow, while the characteristic alarms are air in the line, occlusion, and a heater fault.

Suction removes blood, vomit, and secretions by creating a pressure lower than the pressure in the airway, and the vacuum is usually set between 80 and 120 millimeters of mercury for an adult, a value that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The airway trolley carries laryngoscopes, blades in several sizes, tracheal tubes, supraglottic airways, a bag and masks, and a carbon dioxide detector, arranged so the hand moves in the order the procedure is performed. A chest drain set contains a large-bore tube and a sealed drainage bottle that lets air and fluid leave the chest while preventing air from returning, and a drain on suction uses about 20 centimeters of water below atmospheric pressure, a setting that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The characteristic alarm is a swinging column that stops moving, which means the tube is blocked or the lung has re-expanded.

Access to imaging is part of the equipment of the bay, and the resuscitation room is its twin, because moving or keeping a patient is itself a clinical risk. A portable X-ray machine can be brought to the bed, while a machine for computed tomography, or CT, and one for magnetic resonance imaging, or MRI, cannot, so the department has a short, wide route to the imaging suite. The resuscitation room is optimized for the medical arrest, with a fixed hard table, the monitor and defibrillator on one side so a single operator controls both, and a large clock facing the team. An electrocardiogram, or ECG, is recorded through adhesive electrodes, and its principle is that the heart's electrical activity reaches the skin as a small voltage that the machine amplifies and traces against time.

The defibrillator belongs in both rooms, and its principle is the capacitor, two conducting plates separated by an insulator that store charge and release it across the chest in a few thousandths of a second. The stored energy is measured in joules, and a typical adult biphasic shock delivers 120 to 200 joules, an energy level that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The pads are adhesive electrodes that both sense the rhythm and carry the shock, so their gel must be moist and their expiry date current, because a dried pad burns the skin and conducts poorly. An automated external defibrillator, or AED, removes the rhythm decision from the operator and shocks only when its algorithm recognizes a treatable rhythm, and the characteristic sounds are a rising charge tone, a discharge, and a steady low-battery tone.

The difficult airway cart answers the patient whose larynx cannot be seen, and it is organized as a sequence of escalating options rather than as a box of tools. It holds a video laryngoscope with a camera at the blade tip, which shows the cords on a screen, and it holds supraglottic airways, bougies, stylets, a surgical airway kit, and a carbon dioxide detector. Confirmation of any airway placement is by watching the chest rise, listening over both lungs and over the stomach, and detecting exhaled carbon dioxide, which is the only sign not shared with an esophageal placement. A waveform capnograph displays about 35 to 45 millimeters of mercury when the tube is in the trachea, a range that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the commonest failure is a dead battery in a handle left switched on.

Spinal immobilization holds the injured column in the position it already occupies, because a fractured vertebra can injure the cord when the head moves. A rigid cervical collar supports the chin and the occiput and limits forward and backward bending, but it does not stop rotation, and it must be sized to the patient rather than stretched to fit. A spine board is a flat rigid surface with straps and head blocks, and its purpose is to transfer a patient as a single unit, not to serve as a bed for hours. The log roll turns a patient onto the side while the spine stays in line and needs at least three people, and the commonest error with a collar is a size that is too large, which lets the chin sink and the neck flex.

Splints, traction devices, and pelvic binders treat the fractures that bleed, and their physics is alignment, compression, and the reduction of movement at a broken surface. A rigid splint holds a limb in the position found and stops the bone ends from grinding, while an inflatable splint presses evenly and is light enough for a bag, though it can lose pressure with a slow leak. A traction splint pulls the lower leg lengthwise against a padded ring at the groin, which reduces a fractured femur and relieves the pain and bleeding that muscle spasm produces. A pelvic binder is a wide strap applied around the hips at the level of the greater trochanters, and it closes a disrupted pelvis, a maneuver that can stop venous bleeding that would otherwise fill the abdomen, and every strap tension and traction force must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Minor procedures have their own tray, and the suture set has barely changed in a hundred years because its principle is mechanical. It contains a needle holder, fine forceps, scissors, a scalpel handle, a syringe for local anesthetic, and a bowl for cleaning solution, and the suture is a thread with a sharpened end that pulls the wound edges together while the body heals beneath. The needle is curved so it can be rotated through tissue along the arc of its own shape, which is why the needle holder grips it at the middle and turns the wrist rather than pushing. Suture material is chosen by how long the wound needs support, and the size and type must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, while the commonest error is to close a wound that should be left open, because a contaminated wound closed tightly becomes an abscess.

Casts hold a reduced fracture still, and their chemistry is a setting reaction that gives off heat. Plaster of Paris becomes a paste when mixed with water and then hardens as crystals form, and the reaction releases heat, so a thick cast can become uncomfortably warm and, rarely, burn the skin. Synthetic tape sets by a different reaction, is lighter and stronger, and becomes permeable to water once cured, and a typical plaster sets in 5 to 10 minutes, so every setting time and thickness must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The cast is applied over a stockinette and padding and is split if the limb swells, because a cast that is too tight stops the blood and becomes a compartment syndrome, and the commonest error is to leave a finger or a toe unpadded over a bony prominence.

Point-of-care testing brings the laboratory to the bedside, and its principle is that a small disposable sensor is calibrated against a known reference so a whole instrument fits in a hand-held device. A blood gas analyzer passes a tiny sample across electrodes that generate a voltage proportional to the acidity, the carbon dioxide, and the oxygen, and it reports values such as an acidity between 7.35 and 7.45, a range that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A lactate strip measures a chemical produced when tissue lacks oxygen, and a raised value in a patient with infection signals that the circulation is failing before the blood pressure does, while a test for troponin detects a protein released by injured heart muscle. The commonest error with all of these is a sample that is clotted, diluted, or delayed, which produces a number that looks authoritative and is wrong.

Ultrasound in the emergency setting became a piece of bedside furniture, and its principle is that a crystal changes shape when an electric field is applied and generates a pressure wave that reflects from every boundary. The machine measures the time each echo takes to return and builds a picture of the interfaces, so fluid, bone, and soft tissue appear differently because they reflect differently. A low-frequency probe penetrates deeply and a high-frequency probe resolves fine detail, so probes are chosen for the question, and a typical abdominal probe works between 2 and 10 megahertz, a range that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The applications include looking for free fluid in the abdomen, judging the size of the aorta, and guiding a needle into a vein, and the hazard is that a negative scan is treated as certainty.

Eye and ear examination sets address two small spaces that hide serious disease, and their instruments share the principle of a light channel and a magnifying lens. An ophthalmoscope projects a beam into the eye and lets the examiner look at the retina through a hole in a mirror or a beam splitter, and the pupils must be dilated or the room darkened to see past the iris. A slit lamp produces a thin sheet of light and a microscope on a common axis, which reveals a foreign body or an ulcer that a penlight misses. An otoscope funnels light down a speculum into the ear canal and is braced against the patient's head, and a foreign body is removed with a moistened swab or a small needle under magnification, never with a jet of water if the object may be a battery.

Documentation equipment and the tracking board are the department's memory, because a patient in a crowded room becomes invisible unless an object stands for that patient. A whiteboard or a digital tracking board lists every patient with the location, the complaint, the triage level, the assigned clinician, and the results that have returned, so the whole room shares one picture of who is waiting. Notes on paper, labels for specimens, and the electronic health record, or EHR, carry the clinical information, and the department must be able to document when the network fails. The commonest error is a result seen on a screen and never recorded or handed over, so the finding exists only in one person's memory, and every scanning, labeling, and identity check must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The consumables make the machines useful, and because the department treats the undifferentiated patient, its stock must cover almost every possibility at once. The core list runs from gloves and other personal protective equipment, or PPE, through cannulae, syringes, needles, giving sets, and fluid bags, to dressings, sutures, splints, catheters, specimen containers, and the forms that record care. Sharps containers must sit within arm's reach of every place a needle is used, because the commonest injury is a needle recapped by hand or left on a tray. Suture cartridges, chest drain tubes, airway tubes, and defibrillator pads all carry expiry dates, and stock is rotated on a first in and first out rule, while restocking happens after every patient rather than at the end of a shift, because a missing item is discovered at the worst possible moment.

Cleaning and reprocessing follow the logic of every department, which is that visible soil must be removed before disinfection can work. Reusable instruments such as laryngoscope blades, forceps, scissors, and specula are cleaned with a brush and an enzymatic detergent, rinsed, inspected, and then disinfected or sterilized by a validated method. A common steam cycle is 134 degrees Celsius for 4 minutes or 121 degrees Celsius for 30 minutes, and those cycles must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Surfaces touched by many hands, such as trolley tops, monitor cables, and bed rails, are wiped between patients, and a chlorine solution of 0.5 per cent available chlorine with a contact time of 10 minutes is used for blood spills, a concentration and time that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, while the commonest error is to wipe a device with a cloth that is already dirty, which moves organisms rather than removing them.

Maintenance is a scheduled program involving a biomedical technician, because a device that is never used still ages and the department cannot test everything at the moment of need. Defibrillators are checked for delivered energy and battery condition, monitors for calibration and alarm accuracy, infusion devices for flow accuracy, and ultrasound probes for lens cracks and cable damage. Suction and oxygen regulators are checked against a known gauge or leak tested, and every battery is tracked by the date it was installed, and the intervals for these checks must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The commonest maintenance failure is not a broken machine but an absent record, because a device whose last test cannot be found is treated as untested, and a technician should keep a small stock of the parts that fail most.

The failure modes that matter most appear during a resuscitation and turn out to have been present for weeks. A laryngoscope whose battery was left switched on is dead when it is needed, an oxygen cylinder used for transport was never exchanged, defibrillator pads whose gel has dried adhere poorly, a suction canister left full produces no vacuum, and a monitor whose alarm limits were set to unreachable values is effectively silent. The characteristic alarms each mean something specific, so a monitor repeats a lead-off tone, a defibrillator gives a low-battery tone, a rapid infuser alarms on air or a heater fault, a suction unit blocks at the canister float, and an infusion pump signals an occlusion. Every one of those thresholds must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the commonest operator error is to silence an alarm without naming its cause, which turns a warning into silence.

What the operator must check is a short, fixed routine performed at the start of every shift rather than when the patient arrives. The defibrillator is switched on and allowed to run its self-test, the pads and their expiry dates are inspected, the monitor is checked against a simulator or a known signal, the oxygen cylinders are weighed or their gauges read, the suction is run against a sealed test cap, the laryngoscope light is switched on, the bag is squeezed to test its valves, and the drugs are counted. The seal on the resuscitation trolley is read against the log, and a cart whose seal number does not match is treated as tampered. Every reading and threshold in this check must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the commonest error is to check the front row of a drawer and the labels that face outward, since the items that fail are the ones that cannot be seen.

In a mobile or frontier clinic the emergency department is not a building but a system a person carries, and its principle is that the sorting function must survive the loss of every wall and pipe. The fixed furniture becomes a sturdy table that can be wiped, a battery or kerosene lamp with a bright focused beam, and a flat surface for instruments, and the rest is a set of bags that one or two people can lift. The hospital's spatial memory, in which a hand knows which drawer holds the airway and which holds the drugs, is replaced by a written inventory and a fixed order inside each bag, because a soft bag has no compartments that keep an item in place. Power comes from a battery or a small generator, water comes from a container, and oxygen comes from a cylinder rather than a wall outlet, so every device must be chosen for independence from infrastructure, and every pressure, flow, energy, and temperature setting must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Triage in the field does the work of the building, because there is no separate room in which the stable can wait and the unstable can be treated. The system is a printed scale or a set of colored cards, and the first decision is whether the patient can walk, which sorts the many minor cases from the few serious ones in a single minute. The next decisions use the same variables as the hospital scale, the respiratory rate, the heart rate, the ability to respond, and the presence of severe bleeding, and the color assigned determines the order of treatment. The colors correspond to actions rather than diagnoses, from treat now through treat soon and can wait to beyond the resources available, while the commonest error is to treat the loudest patient first and a written tally substitutes for the tracking board.

The field bag is built around weight and the questions that will actually be asked, and its contents compress the trauma bay. It holds a small monitor or a hand-held oximeter, a portable defibrillator with adult and pediatric pads, a bag and masks in several sizes, oral airways, a supraglottic airway, suction that is manual or battery driven, a small oxygen cylinder with a regulator, splints, a pelvic binder, dressings and tourniquets, a suture set, a few drugs, and laminated checklists. The weight is the central design problem, because a bag that is too heavy is left in the vehicle, so the kit is built around what one person can carry for a kilometer over rough ground, and drugs are chosen for stability in heat and carried in an insulated pouch. Every dose, energy level, and flow setting in the bag must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the bag is sealed after each restock so its seal number can be checked like a closed drawer.

The field variant gives up a great deal, and an honest list of the losses is more useful than optimism. There is no CT scanner and no MRI scanner, no laboratory on site, no blood bank, no rapid infusion device, no piped suction, no wall oxygen, and usually no capnograph to confirm a tracheal tube, so the clinician must decide with the hands, the eyes, and a few numbers. What remains is the clinical examination, a small number of tests, an ultrasound probe if one is carried, and the judgment to move the patient toward the hospital rather than wait for equipment to arrive. Maintenance is built around weather rather than a calendar, because heat shortens battery life and softens pad adhesive, dust clogs suction filters, humidity corrodes contacts, and vibration loosens connectors, so batteries are charged whenever power is available and the state of charge is written on a tag each day.

Reusable instruments are cleaned of all visible soil and then disinfected or sterilized by a method validated in that setting, and the cycles used must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Because the field has fewer alarms, the operator must supply the monitoring by repeating observations at fixed intervals and writing them down, and some interventions cannot be improvised at all, so the field clinician must know that boundary before the patient arrives. A kit that is checked once a week is not checked, and the person who carries it must confirm that every expiry date and setting inside it is still valid. A discharged defibrillator is an empty bag with a handle, so the battery state is the first thing checked and the last thing recorded, and a bag that has never been opened in a drill is only a collection of unverified assumptions.

To summarize, the equipment of the emergency department exists to sort the undifferentiated patient and to shorten the time between arrival and the right treatment. The trauma bay and the resuscitation room hold a hard bed, an overhead light, a monitor, a rapid infuser, suction, an airway trolley, a chest drain set, and a route to imaging, arranged so every device sits within reach of the role that needs it. Splints, collars, boards, and binders hold the injured body still, suture sets and casts protect the wounded part, and point-of-care tests and ultrasound bring the laboratory and the image to the bedside. In the field the building becomes a table, a light, and a bag, and the operator supplies the monitoring the alarms would otherwise provide. Whatever the setting, the discipline is the same: know the order of the kit, check the seal, confirm every setting against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol, watch the patient and the clock, and restock before the next arrival.
