# Chapter 68 — Labels, Wristbands, and Documentation Supplies

Hospital care is held together by marks on surfaces, and a wristband, a specimen label, a medication label, and a log sheet are the same device in different clothes. Each is a durable mark that carries a fact from where it was established to where a decision is made, and the principle is adhesion plus legibility, because a mark works only if it stays attached to the right object and remains readable until that object is used. When the mark fails the failure is silent, and the harm appears later as a wrong drug, a wrong blood unit, or a wrong patient. Ink is a pigment or a dye in a solvent that sinks into the paper or sits on the surface as a layer that can be rubbed away, while the substrate and the adhesive decide whether the mark holds on skin, glass, plastic, or cold metal. Every material, temperature, humidity level, and storage interval named in this chapter must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before it is relied upon.

The hospital-grade identification band solves one problem with one physical trick, because it cannot be removed and refitted without being destroyed. It is closed with a single-use snap, a one-way rivet, or an adhesive overlap whose backing tears, so moving it to another person leaves visible evidence, while a reusable strap with a buckle can be moved in silence. Adults usually wear a soft strap of vinyl or a flexible polymer printed to resist water, alcohol hand rub, soap, and body oils, while newborn and pediatric bands are narrower, softer, and often made of a spun-bonded synthetic paper that will not cut a delicate limb. Placement is part of the function, because a band that cannot be seen will not be checked, so the band goes on a visible limb and may be duplicated on another site. The fit must allow one finger beneath the strap and must not slide over the hand, and that fit must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the band is fastened.

An adult band carries at least the patient's full name and a unique record number, and many hospitals add the date of birth, the sex, and the admitting service. The unique number matters more than the name, because names repeat and names are misspelled under pressure, while a generated number is never reused. Printing must be large enough to read at arm's length in dim light, and characters must not be abbreviated, since an abbreviation creates a second name that only some staff will recognize. The band is checked before every medication, every transfusion, every specimen, and every procedure, and the check is made against a second source rather than against the band alone. A band that is read and agreed with itself proves nothing.

Children grow, they pull, and they are often uncooperative at the moment of application, so pediatric bands are smaller and are placed on an ankle as often as a wrist. The safety hazard is a band tight enough to press on a small vessel, so the limb is inspected at every shift for swelling, color, and marks. A newborn has no trustworthy name in the first hours, so two bands are placed on the baby and a matching band is placed on the mother, all carrying the same number. A band applied later is a weaker record because the interval is unaccounted for, and the characteristic alarm is a number on the baby that does not match the number on the mother. The placement method and the matching routine must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the first band is closed.

Alert bands carry a warning rather than an identity, and they are a convenience with a serious trap. A red band commonly means allergy, a yellow band a fall risk, and a purple band a resuscitation preference, but the meanings are not universal and change between hospitals and countries. The physical hazard is that color is read faster than text and is therefore trusted more, yet color vision deficiency affects a substantial minority of men and dim ward lighting alters every hue. A color must therefore never be the only carrier of the warning, and the condition must also be written on the band or in the record. The local color scheme and its meanings must be confirmed against the local protocol before any alert band is applied.

Band failure is usually a slow process rather than a dramatic event. Direct thermal printing fades with heat, sunlight, friction, and alcohol, so a band that was legible in the morning can be pale by the evening, while the plastic strap stretches with edema and repeated pulling until it slides over the hand. A band that is too tight leaves an indentation that can progress to skin breakdown, and a band cut by hand leaves a sharp corner that scratches the patient and the worker. The characteristic alarm is a mismatch between the band and the record, and the common operator error is to write a new band from the old band instead of from the record, which copies an error forward. Every band must be inspected at the start of a shift and replaced when it is illegible, damaged, loose, or has been removed.

A barcode is a pattern of dark bars and light spaces that a machine converts into characters. The scanner illuminates the pattern with a light-emitting diode or a laser, a photodetector measures the light that returns, and the dark bars absorb while the light spaces reflect, so the returning signal rises and falls in a rhythm that the decoder reads. The pattern works only if the contrast is high, the bars are sharp, the quiet zones at each end are clear, and the whole symbol lies inside the scanner's field, and a checksum makes a misread fail loudly rather than pass in silence. At the bedside the scan is a second reader of identity, because scanning a medication and then a band forces two independent records to agree before a drug is given, and a specimen tube is scanned against the band while the needle is still in the arm. Where scanning is used, the local policy must define when it is required and what to do when it fails, and that policy must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

A scanner is a small optical instrument and it needs the care of any other lens. The window collects dust, adhesive residue, and the sticky film left by a label peeled from a gloved hand, and a scratched or cloudy window produces the failed reads that staff blame on the labels. The housing is wiped with a soft cloth and an approved cleaner and is never soaked, while the cable, the connector, and the battery contacts of a cordless unit are checked for wear and charge at the start of a shift. The characteristic alarm is the single low beep or the amber light of a failed read, and the common operator error is to keep scanning the same label instead of inspecting it. The cleaning agent and the maintenance interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

Radio-frequency identification replaces the visible pattern with an invisible conversation. A tag holds a small chip and an antenna, a reader emits a radio field, the field induces a tiny current in the antenna, the chip wakes, and it answers by modulating the field it reflects, so a sealed tag needs no battery and no window to scratch. The read range depends on the reader's power, the antenna design, the orientation of the tag, and the material around it, because metal reflects the field and water absorbs it. Common bands are the low frequency near 125 kilohertz, the high frequency near 13.56 megahertz, and the ultra high frequency between roughly 860 and 960 megahertz. Any frequency named here must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the system is installed.

In a hospital the technology is used to count rather than to read, so it tracks pumps, trolleys, instruments, and linen as they pass a doorway. Its worst property is that it fails quietly, since there is no beep when a tag is not seen, only an absence in a list, and an absence is easy to mistake for a correct count. A tag can be detached, left on a shelf, or moved to another object, and the system will still report it as present, while two tags in the field at once can collide so that neither is reported. The common operator error is to treat a successful read as proof that the physical object is correct, rather than as proof that a tag was in range. The correct discipline is to reconcile the electronic list against a visual count at defined intervals, and that interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

A label printer is a device that applies heat to a controlled pattern. In thermal transfer printing, a printhead of many tiny heating elements presses against a ribbon, the heated spots melt a wax or resin coating, and the coating transfers to the label stock beneath, so a wax ribbon smears while a resin ribbon resists abrasion and solvents. Direct thermal printing skips the ribbon and darkens a chemically treated paper with heat alone, which is convenient, but the image fades with light and heat and disappears entirely on a hot dashboard. The stock has a face material, an adhesive, and a liner, and the adhesive may be permanent, removable, or a medical grade made for skin and for cold surfaces, so the wrong choice either falls off a specimen tube or tears the skin of a newborn. The print resolution, the ribbon type, and the adhesive must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before a printer is put into service.

Printer maintenance follows the path of the heat. The printhead is cleaned with an approved swab and a recommended solvent, because its hard coating is removed by an abrasive cloth, and the platen roller beneath it collects adhesive and paper dust until a flat spot produces a light band across every label. The ribbon path is checked for wrinkles, since a wrinkled ribbon leaves pale streaks and wastes stock. The characteristic failure is a label printed at the wrong position or split across two labels, and the common operator error is to adjust the tension without recalibrating the printer. Stock control is a clinical activity here, because a hospital that runs out of band stock or specimen labels improvises, so the par level is set from measured use plus a reserve the night shift can reach. The cleaning agent, the calibration routine, the storage temperature, and the shelf life must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

A specimen label is the identity card of a biological object, and it must carry enough to survive every hand that touches it. The minimum set is the patient's full name, the unique record number, the date and time of collection, the type of specimen and the site it came from, the tests requested, and the identity of the collector. The label is attached to the container rather than to the lid alone, because the lid is the part most often swapped between tubes. The adhesive sticks when it can wet the surface it meets, which is easy on dry glass and hard on a cold, wet, or dusty tube, so the surface is dried, the label is pressed firmly from the center outward, and the adhesive is never touched with a gloved finger. The data set required by the receiving laboratory must be confirmed against the local protocol before a specimen is dispatched.

A mislabeled specimen is a special kind of harm, because the error is invisible when it is made and irreversible when it is discovered. Two tubes drawn at the same bedside and labeled away from the patient can be exchanged, and the exchange appears only when a result contradicts the patient in front of the clinician. The physical barrier is printing at the bedside, because a label produced at the moment of collection cannot be moved to another patient without the act being noticed, and where bedside printing is unavailable the rule is to label one tube at a time, in the room, with the patient's band in view. The common operator error is to pre-label a batch of tubes before the round begins, which separates the label from the person for the whole round. The characteristic alarm is a laboratory result that does not fit the patient.

A medication label must carry everything another person would need to give the dose safely if the preparer were suddenly unavailable. That list includes the drug name, the strength and the total dose, the diluent and the final volume, the route, the date and time of preparation, the expiry after preparation, the storage requirement, the patient's identifiers, and the initials of the preparer, with warnings for high-alert drugs and for drugs that look or sound like another. The most dangerous label in a hospital is the missing one, because a syringe without a label and a basin of clear fluid without a label both ask a future person to guess. The common operator error is to believe the drug is obvious because the preparer remembers it, which is true for the preparer and useless to everyone else. The correct habit is to label the container before drawing up the drug, and the exact list, the expiry after preparation, and the label size must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

A log sheet is a device that converts a measurement into evidence, and a refrigerator log is the clearest example. It records the temperature of a cold space at a stated time with the initials of the reader, and a complete entry carries the date, the time, the temperature with its unit written out, and a note of the action taken when the value was outside the accepted range. A vaccine or a blood product can be damaged by a single excursion above or below its range, and the damage may not be visible in the vial. A hospital may use a continuous recorder or a data logger with its own display and audible alarm, and the thermometer must be checked against a reference at defined intervals because an uncalibrated instrument records a comfortable fiction. The characteristic alarm is an out-of-range temperature, an audible beep, or a gap in the record, and any of the three requires a documented response. The failure modes of a paper log are human, and the classic is retrospective filling, in which a week of readings is written at one sitting with one pen and one posture, while a missing sheet breaks the series and leaves the fate of the contents unknown. The accepted range, the reading interval, the alarm threshold, and the calibration interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

Consent and chart forms record a decision and a clinical course, and a consent form is not the consent itself, because consent is a conversation and the form is the evidence that the conversation occurred. The common form is a pre-printed sheet with a carbonless copy, in which pressure from a pen ruptures microscopic capsules of dye and transfers the writing to the sheet below, while a modern version is printed from the record and carries a barcode so that the signed page is matched without retyping. The hazards live in the details left blank, because a form signed by the wrong patient, a form dated later, and a form signed by a relative without documented authority can all invalidate the record. The patient must have capacity and must receive the information in a language and at a level they understand, so an interpreter is required when the clinician and the patient do not share a language. Paper records are also a physical problem that outlives the stay, because paper is hygroscopic, mold grows on it in a warm and humid room, and light fades the ink while rodents eat the paper and the glue on the spine, so access is controlled, retention is decided by law, and destruction must be documented when the period ends.

Digital documentation moves the record into a network, where it is faster to find and easier to lose. The electronic health record holds orders, results, medication administration, and notes, and it is backed by servers, a network, and a power supply protected by an uninterruptible power supply and a generator. The characteristic failure is the screen that will not load, and its alarm is often a silence, because no printer sounds and no label appears until a task cannot be completed. The correct response is a rehearsed downtime routine with paper forms in a known place, a defined way of numbering the paper record, and one person responsible for reconciling paper against the digital record afterward. The common operator error during an outage is to postpone documentation on the assumption that the information will be remembered, and the downtime procedure, the printed forms it requires, and the reconciliation interval must be confirmed against the local protocol before the system is used on patients.

The field variant begins where the network, the printer, and the reliable mains supply end. A mobile or frontier clinic has no server to fall back on, no barcode scanner, and often no electricity at the moment a record must be made, so the system is reduced to paper, ink, and a discipline that substitutes for the machine. What it gives up is speed, searchability, and the automatic cross-check that a scan provides. What it demands instead is redundancy, because one lost page is a lost patient history, and a written culture in which every entry is legible, dated, signed, and read back by a second person.

The spine of the field record is the register, which is a bound book rather than a loose-leaf folder, because a bound book cannot lose a page without leaving a stub. Its pages are numbered in sequence so that a missing page is immediately visible, and each entry carries the date, the time, the patient's name, an identifying number, the findings, the treatment, and the signature of the writer. The register stays in one place and travels only with a responsible person, and a second copy of the most important entries in a separate book is the cheapest backup in medicine. The common operator error is to keep the register as a pile of loose sheets, which can be removed, reordered, or lost without evidence. The numbering scheme and the fields required must be confirmed against the local protocol before the register is put into use.

Ink is the field's most underrated consumable. A water-based ink sinks into the paper and binds to the fibers, so it is difficult to remove without damaging the page, but it runs and blurs when the page gets wet, while a pigment sits closer to the surface and can be dissolved by alcohol hand rub. The permanent marker that writes beautifully on a plastic band can be erased from a paper page by the alcohol used at every bedside, which is a quiet way to lose a record. The safest common choice for a register is an oil-based ballpoint pen, whose paste is pressed into the paper and is not dissolved by water, and the pen is carried with a spare because a dry pen stops the clinic. Where no pen exists, a graphite pencil survives water but is faint and erasable, and a wax marker writes on plastic and wet paper but smears with heat, so whatever instrument is used must leave an entry that is still legible years later.

When commercial bands are unavailable, the field improvises them, and the improvisation must be honest about what it loses. A strip of adhesive tape written with a permanent marker can carry a name and a number, but the adhesive lifts in heat, humidity, and sweat, and the ink smears on the slick backing, so the tape must be applied to clean, dry skin, wrapped so that it cannot slide, and checked at every contact with the patient. A card pinned to a cot is not a band, because it identifies a place rather than a person and can be moved without evidence. Color is the field's favorite shortcut and its most reliable trap, because perceived hue depends on the light and the eye, so a color must always travel with a written word that is readable at night without a lamp. Where an improvised band is used, a second identifier and a spoken confirmation with the patient or the family are mandatory.

The photograph of the page is the field's most portable backup, because light reflected from the page is captured as a grid of measurements that can be copied without loss. The photograph is only as good as its legibility, so the whole page must be in the frame including the page number and the edges, the light must be even with no shadow across the writing, and the camera must be steady and close enough that individual letters are clear. A folded page needs both sides photographed, and a second frame with the register's cover makes the copy traceable. The photograph is a copy rather than the original, so where the original is required for a legal purpose the paper must still be kept, and a photograph taken in a hurry with the writing at the edge of the frame preserves nothing but the fact that a page once existed. The storage location and the copying routine must be confirmed against the local protocol before the photographs are treated as a record.

Power and adhesive are the two boundaries that divide the hospital half of this chapter from the field half. A hospital label printer runs from protected mains power and fails only when the network or the stock fails, while a field printer runs from a battery charged by a solar panel or a generator and fails whenever the sun does not return. A thermal printer draws a large current in short bursts as the printhead heats, so a battery that runs a lamp will not necessarily run a printer, and the capacity must be matched rather than guessed. Heat softens an adhesive until a label slides, cold stiffens it until it lifts at one edge, and humidity attacks the paper from underneath, so a label on a dusty jerrycan or a wet vaccine vial may live for only hours, and the field habit is to write the identification on the object itself as well as on the label. The battery type, the charging routine, and the adhesive life must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

Maintenance in this chapter is mostly inspection, because these objects have few moving parts. Bands are single-use and are inspected rather than repaired, since a mended band is a band that can be moved, and scanners are cleaned with their windows checked for scratches and their cables and cradles examined for wear. Printers are cleaned at the printhead and the platen, calibrated to the stock, and checked for ribbon wrinkles and for the pale band that marks a failing heating element. The start-of-shift check is short and cannot be skipped, and it begins with every patient's band, which is inspected for presence, legibility, correct data, and correct fit. It continues at the printer, where stock and a clean printhead are verified with one test label, and at the scanner, where a charged battery and a successful test read are confirmed. The temperature log is then checked for the current entry and for the action taken after any out-of-range reading, the forms cupboard is checked for consent sheets, downtime forms, and specimen labels, and the cleaning agent and the full check list must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

The failure modes of documentation supplies share one signature, and it is silence. A faded band, a smeared specimen label, a misread barcode, and a log filled retrospectively all present themselves as ordinary work, and the harm appears later as a wrong result or a disputed record. The characteristic alarm is therefore a discrepancy noticed by a person, which is why a second check is built into every step of this system, and the common operator error is to sign the second line without performing the second look. Traceability is the reason all of this work exists, because a mark joins a substance, a container, or a person to a time and an author, so that an error can be found and prevented from recurring. The economics are deceptive, since these items cost little individually and can halt a hospital collectively, so the par level is set from measured use rather than from a yearly budget line, and the stock levels, the audit trail, and the retention of supporting documents must be confirmed against the local protocol and the applicable regulation.

To summarize, labels, wristbands, and documentation supplies are the memory of the hospital, and they work because a mark can be fixed to an object and read later. The hospital-grade version relies on durable bands, thermal transfer printing, barcodes and radio-frequency identification, networked printers, structured medication and specimen labels, and temperature logs that turn a routine reading into evidence, all supported by stock control, cleaning, and calibration. The field version gives up the printer, the network, and the scan, and demands instead a bound register with numbered pages, permanent ink, a second reader, and a photograph as a copy against loss. In both settings the physics is the same, since ink must bind, adhesive must hold, and a reader must see what the writer intended. The safety hazard is the silent mismatch, the common operator error is to trust a mark instead of verifying it, and the characteristic alarm is a discrepancy that only a person can notice. Every material, temperature, interval, and threshold named in this chapter must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before it is used on a patient.
