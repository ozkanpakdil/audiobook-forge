# Chapter 2 — Classification of Medical Devices

Classification sorts medical devices by the harm they can do, and it is the first practical question anyone can ask about a machine. Two devices may look equally complex and cost the same, yet one may be sold from any catalog while the other may be sold, installed, and serviced only under conditions a government has examined. The difference is not prestige and it is not price; it is the probability and the severity of injury if the device fails. This chapter explains the physical reasoning behind the classes, the regulatory frameworks that name them, and the categories that cut across them. It then walks the same ground in a mobile or frontier clinic, where the classification still applies but the infrastructure that made the device safe does not travel with it. Understanding the class of a device is how a clinic decides what it may accept, what it must refuse, and what it must demand in writing.

The physical principle underneath every scheme is that harm is a product of energy, access, and time. A device that delivers no energy and touches only intact skin can fail with little harm, while one that sends energy to the heart, or holds a sterile path into a vein, can kill within minutes. Access matters because the same energy is far more dangerous when it reaches tissue the body does not protect. Skin resists current, while the bloodstream carries a substance everywhere at once and the heart responds to currents too small to feel. Time matters because an instrument that touches a patient for 5 minutes and an implant that remains for 10 years raise different problems of material, infection, and removal. Classification therefore asks how deeply the device enters, how long it stays, and how much rides on its correct function, and every step up that ladder tightens the rules around sale, installation, and servicing.

The class of a device determines 5 practical things, and a buyer who does not know them cannot write a competent specification. It determines who may place the device on the market and therefore which paperwork must accompany it. It determines who may install it, because a higher-class device often requires the manufacturer's own engineer to commission it. It determines who may service and calibrate it, because work on a life-supporting machine by an untrained hand is a hazard rather than a saving. It determines how the device must be tracked, so that a faulty batch can be traced to the patient who received one. It determines what the buyer can demand, including the manual, the training, the spare parts list, the calibration certificate, and a defined period of support. The class is therefore a set of obligations that travels with the device to the bedside.

In Europe the scheme is built around a marking the manufacturer applies to declare that the device meets the applicable legal requirements, and that marking is the European conformity marking. Its logic is that independent scrutiny should rise with the class. For the lowest-risk devices the manufacturer may assess conformity itself, compile a technical file, and issue a declaration of conformity on its own authority. For the middle and highest classes an independent notified body must examine the design, the manufacturing quality system, or both, and its certificate becomes part of the evidence that the device is fit to be sold. The marking is therefore a claim with a paper trail behind it, and the paper trail, not the mark alone, is what a hospital should demand. The safety hazard is a mark without the file behind it, and the common operator error is to accept the mark as proof and file the paperwork unread, which leaves the hospital unable to prove what it bought or to trace a fault.

The United States uses a different structure built on the same logic, and its regulator is the United States Food and Drug Administration, or FDA. The lowest-risk devices reach the market under general controls that cover labeling, manufacturing practice, and reporting, and most need no separate review before sale. Devices of moderate risk are usually cleared through a pathway that asks the manufacturer to show that its device is substantially equivalent to one already legally on the market, which is a comparison rather than a fresh proof of safety and effectiveness. The highest-risk devices require a premarket approval, in which the manufacturer submits clinical and technical evidence and the regulator examines the design and the manufacturing process in detail. Alongside these pathways run the duties of registering the establishment, listing the device, and reporting adverse events. The word cleared and the word approved do not mean the same thing, and a buyer who treats them as equal may accept a moderate-risk device as though it had passed the strictest examination.

Many countries have no regulatory authority of their own, or have one too thinly staffed to examine every device, and for them the World Health Organization publishes guidance describing how a national system should be built. That guidance sets out the essential principles of safety and performance, the assessment required before a device is permitted on the market, and the surveillance that detects harm after sale. It also publishes a list of priority medical devices for the interventions a health system is expected to deliver. The physical reasoning behind the list is that a device is useful only when the whole chain around it exists, including the consumable, the power, the trained operator, the spare part, and the repair. The hazard of the list is that it can be read as a guarantee, when it only states that the device suits the intervention and not that the clinic can sustain it. Appropriateness is therefore a property of the setting rather than of the device, and the honest question before any purchase is whether this clinic, with these staff and this supply chain, can hold the device at the standard its class requires.

Begin at the bottom of the ladder, with the devices that present the least risk. These touch only intact skin or never touch the patient at all, such as the tongue depressor, the manual stethoscope, the examination glove, the reusable bedpan, and the simple tourniquet. Their physical character is that they deliver no energy into the body, create no sterile path into a cavity, and depend only on the shape of the material and the hand that holds it. The rules are correspondingly light: they may be sold widely, often need no independent review, and demand ordinary cleanliness rather than a validated sterilization cycle. This does not mean they are harmless, because a contaminated bedpan or a glove used on 2 patients can still cause infection. The classification speaks only to the risk inherent in the device when it works as designed, and the operator's judgment about cleanliness remains the defense that no class can replace.

The middle of the ladder holds the great majority of hospital equipment, and it is the region where classification becomes genuinely difficult to apply. These deliver a measured amount of energy or fluid, enter the body through a natural opening or a small puncture, or keep a patient alive while the patient cannot keep themselves alive. An infusion pump, a ventilator, an X-ray unit, and a dialysis machine all sit in this broad band. They are grouped together because each can cause serious harm by delivering too much, too little, or at the wrong moment, yet none is intended to remain inside the body for years. The rules are intermediate: independent review before sale, a manufacturing quality system, calibration and preventive maintenance, and traceability of the consumables that touch the patient. The common operator error is to treat such a device as though it were simple, skipping the calibration, ignoring the alarm limits, or substituting a consumable the device was never validated to use. A characteristic alarm usually announces the error that inattention would hide, and its threshold must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.

At the top of the ladder sit the devices whose failure is most likely to kill or to cause permanent harm, among them the implantable pacemaker, the heart valve, the hip replacement, and the catheter that rests inside the heart. Their character is intimate contact, long duration, and large consequence: the material sits in living tissue for years, its surface becomes a site for infection and clotting, and the flow it controls is central to survival. The rules are the strictest in the system: examination of the design and the manufacturing process before approval, a validated quality system, traceability to the individual patient, and often a register that follows every recipient for years. The hazard is not only the failure of the device but the failure of the material, including wear particles, corrosion, and slow fracture. The operator error at this level is a lapse in the chain that protects the patient, such as a missing implant card or a recall notice that never reaches the ward. For these devices the paperwork is not bureaucracy, because it is the only way the system can find the patient when something turns out to be wrong.

A division that cuts across the risk classes is the split between active and non-active devices, and it rests on a simple physical question: does the device convert an external energy source into an effect on the patient? An active device takes electricity, gas pressure, or another form of energy and turns it into a physiological change, so a ventilator moves air, a defibrillator delivers charge, and a dialysis machine moves fluid across a membrane. A non-active device adds no energy of its own and relies instead on gravity, on the patient's own body, or on the hand of the operator, so a syringe, a catheter, and a walking frame are all non-active. A non-active device fails by breaking, by blocking, or by being used wrongly, while an active device can also fail by delivering the wrong amount of energy. Active devices therefore carry alarms, interlocks, and automatic limits that non-active devices lack, and they depend on a power source that must itself meet its own standard. A defibrillator that stores 200 joules is a clear example, and that energy must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.

A separate family never touches the patient and yet carries its own classes: the in vitro diagnostic devices, whose measurement is performed on a specimen outside the body. Their physical principle is that a chemical or biological reaction produces a signal proportional to the amount of the substance measured, so the result is an inference rather than a direct observation of the patient. The harm is indirect but severe, because a wrong result leads to a wrong treatment, a missed infection, or a transfusion of infected blood, and no alarm announces it. Their classes follow the consequence of an incorrect result rather than contact with the body, so a test that screens donated blood sits at the highest level while a test for a harmless marker sits far lower. The common operator error is to run a test with expired reagents or with a specimen stored outside its stated conditions, because the device will still produce a number that looks authoritative. A point-of-care test is subject to the same classes as a laboratory analyzer, and its storage temperature and reading time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before it is used on a patient.

Sterility is another property that cuts across the classes, and it is best understood as a probability rather than a state. A sterile device is one on which the number of viable microorganisms is so low that the probability of any single one surviving is acceptably small, and the process is validated to reach that probability. A sterile barrier blocks the passage of microorganisms while allowing the sterilizing agent to enter and the air to leave, so a package that is damp, torn, or crushed may no longer be a barrier even though it looks intact. The hazard is a wound, a bloodstream, or a sterile cavity receiving an organism the barrier was supposed to exclude, and the consequence is an infection that appears days later and cannot be traced to the moment. The operator check is visual and happens at the point of use: the package is dry, undamaged, within its expiry date, and marked with the process indicator that shows it passed the sterilization cycle its material requires. A cycle at 134 degrees Celsius for 4 minutes is a common example, and any such cycle must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before an item is released for use.

Reusable against single-use is the next cut, and it is decided by the material and the geometry of the device rather than by its class alone. A reusable instrument survives repeated cleaning and sterilization, and its surfaces and channels can be reached by the cleaning process. A single-use device is often made of plastics that soften or fatigue after one cycle of heat or chemistry, and it may contain narrow lumens or bonded joints that no cleaning process can reliably reach. The physical hazard of reuse is therefore 2 hazards at once: the material no longer performs as designed, and a residue of the previous patient, including blood, protein, and biofilm, survives inside the device. The common operator error is to reuse a device because it appears undamaged and the correct replacement is out of stock, which trades a certain saving today for an uncertain infection tomorrow. The rule that governs reprocessing is written in the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol, and it must be confirmed before the device is used on a second patient.

Capital equipment against consumables is an economic division with a physical consequence, and it often decides whether a ward can function. A capital device is the machine itself, bought once, installed, maintained, and expected to last for years, while a consumable is the item that is used up and replaced for every patient or procedure. The physical consequence is that the machine is useless without a chain of consumables that fit it exactly, and the chain is usually specific in its geometry even when this book refuses to name the maker. A ventilator needs its breathing circuit and its filter; an infusion pump needs its dedicated giving set; a dialysis machine needs its dialyzer and its blood lines. The hazard is the device that is purchased and then stands idle because a consumable cannot be bought, which is a common and expensive failure of planning. The buyer who understands this division writes the specification around the supply chain, asking how long a consumable takes to arrive, how many suppliers exist, and what the device does when its consumable is absent. Total cost of ownership is therefore a classification question, because the class usually predicts the cost and the scarcity of everything the device consumes.

The class of a device then decides who may do what with it, and the obligations are concrete. The seller must hold whatever authorization the jurisdiction requires, and for the higher classes that authorization comes only after an examination of the design and the factory. The installer must be qualified to a standard that matches the risk, so an imaging suite is commissioned by engineers who prove the output and the shielding, while a weighing scale may be checked by the ward. The servicer must have access to the manufacturer's procedures, the correct software, and genuine parts, because a repair that restores the outside of a machine without restoring its calibration has made the device more dangerous. The device must be tracked in a register that records its location, its unique identifier, its maintenance history, and the patients who received any implantable part of it. The buyer may demand the technical documentation, the declaration of conformity, a manual the staff can read, the training, the spare parts list, and a defined period of support. A clinic that receives a device without this paperwork holds an object it cannot prove, cannot calibrate, cannot repair, and cannot recall, so the paperwork is part of the device and not an accessory to it.

Installation is where the class meets the building, because every higher-class device expects an environment the clinic must supply. An imaging device expects a stable power supply, a room whose temperature and humidity stay within a range, and shielding that keeps the beam and the scatter inside the room, and those conditions are part of the declared operating envelope. A life-supporting machine expects a supply that does not fail, which means an uninterruptible power supply behind it, and it expects ventilation that removes heat and waste gas. The hazard of a poor environment is a device that works well on the day of commissioning and drifts or fails months later, with the failure blamed on the machine rather than on the room. Maintenance continues the same obligation, because every device wears invisibly until it crosses a threshold: a seal hardens, a filter clogs, a battery loses capacity, and the machine keeps working while its margin shrinks. The intervals and tolerances that apply must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol, and the record of each service belongs in the equipment register.

Cleaning and reprocessing follow the tissue the device touches, and this is a classification the operator applies every day whether or not a regulator does. A device that enters sterile tissue or the bloodstream is called critical, and it must be sterile at the moment of use, which is why surgical instruments, implantable parts, and intravascular catheters travel through a validated sterilization cycle. A device that touches mucous membrane or non-intact skin is called semi-critical, and it must receive at least a high-level disinfection that kills all organisms except perhaps a large number of bacterial spores. A device that touches only intact skin is called non-critical, and it needs cleaning and a low-level disinfectant. The physical reason the tiers exist is that the number and the type of organisms able to reach a vulnerable site differ by orders of magnitude, so the effort should match the risk rather than the appearance of the object. The common operator error is to clean to the wrong tier, usually by treating a semi-critical item as non-critical because it looks clean, or by storing a critical item while it is still wet. The cycle or the chemical concentration that applies to each tier must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before any item is reprocessed.

The failure modes that classification is designed to prevent can be grouped by how they announce themselves. Some are loud, and these are the ones an alarm system was built for: the pump that detects an occlusion, the ventilator that loses its supply pressure, the defibrillator that reports a low battery, the analyzer that fails its internal control. Some are silent, and these are the most dangerous, because the device continues to produce an output that no longer means what it says, such as the sensor that drifts, the flowmeter that reads high, the thermostat that holds a temperature 3 degrees away from its setting, or the timer that runs slow. Some are intermittent, appearing only when the device is warm, when a cable is bent, or when the supply voltage dips, and these are the hardest to find because the device passes every test on the bench. The class of a device predicts which of these failure modes matters most, and the higher the class, the more the system must assume the failure will be silent and must therefore test for it. Any tolerance or temperature named here is an example only and must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before it is used as a limit.

The checks an operator performs before use are short, and they change with the class of the device rather than with the brand. The operator confirms that the device is clean and dry, that it is within its service date, and that it carries no visible damage to its housing, cable, or connector. The operator confirms that the consumable is the one the device was designed for, that it is within its expiry date, and that its sterile barrier is intact if it has one. The operator confirms that the device powers on, completes its self-test, and has its alarms enabled rather than silenced, because a silenced alarm is indistinguishable from a working one until the moment it matters. Traceability turns classification into protection after the fact, and it works only when the identifiers are recorded at the moment of use, because a recall becomes an action only if the lot number was linked to the patient. The common operator error is to record the device type but not the lot, which breaks the chain at exactly the point where it is needed, and the identifiers that apply must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

Now walk the same ground in a mobile or frontier clinic, where the classification still applies but almost none of the supporting system does. The clinic may be a room, a tent, a vehicle, or a table under a tree, and its devices are chosen by donation or by what a district store happens to hold. Those frameworks were written for a market and an authority, and neither is present at the end of the road, so the clinic must act as its own regulator with the information it can obtain. The most common situation is a device that arrives with no paperwork, and the first job is to reconstruct what it is, using the plate on the housing and the markings on the accessories to establish its generic name, its power requirement, its consumable, and its class. The second job is to decide honestly whether the device can be kept within the standard its class requires, and where the answer is no, the device is best converted into a source of parts rather than pressed into clinical service. The practical rule that follows is to choose the lowest class that meets the clinical need, and to prefer a passive device over an active one whenever the passive device can do the job. A manual suction pump, a gravity infusion set, a hand-operated resuscitator, and a battery-powered device are all chosen because they fail in ways the clinic can see. Any marking, setting, or storage requirement on such a device must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before it is used, and where neither exists, the device should not be used until an authoritative source is found.

In the field the same class carries a heavier weight, because the hospital services that absorb the risk are absent. A middle-class device in a hospital sits behind a stable supply, a maintenance schedule, a technician, and a stock of consumables, and the same device in a clinic sits behind none of them. Consumables are the tightest constraint and the place where classification is most often broken, because the correct item is frequently unavailable and the pressure to reuse a single-use device is constant. A single-use item that touches only intact skin and holds no lumen may often be cleaned and reused without great risk, while an item that enters a vein, a sterile cavity, or the airway may not, because its lumen cannot be cleaned and its material cannot be sterilized without damage. Where reuse is unavoidable, the clinic needs a written policy, a validated method, and a record for every patient, and a pressure cooker used as a sterilizer is the common field answer whose cycle must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before any item is released. Maintenance and traceability fall back on paper, and a log kept beside the device, recording the date, the fault, the action, and the name of the person who acted, does most of what a hospital register does. Where no alarm exists, a person must take its place, which means a written observation schedule and a handover that names what to watch for and how often to look. The field gives up the certified installer, the calibration laboratory, and the central alarm, and demands understanding and redundancy instead, because the physics of harm does not change at the end of the road.

To summarize, classification sorts medical devices by the harm they can do, and that harm is a product of the energy the device delivers, the access it gains to the body, and the time it stays there. The lowest class holds the instruments that touch only intact skin and deliver nothing, the middle class holds the pumps, ventilators, imaging units, and monitors whose output can injure if it is wrong, and the highest class holds the implants and life-supporting machines whose failure is intimately and often permanently dangerous. Around these risk classes run 4 other divisions that matter just as much: in vitro diagnostics, whose harm arrives through a wrong result; sterile devices, whose sterility is a probability protected by an intact barrier; reusable against single-use devices, decided by the material and the lumen; and capital equipment against consumables, decided by the supply chain that must follow the machine. The regulatory frameworks, whether the European conformity marking system, the American clearance and approval pathways, or the guidance of the World Health Organization, exist to make the class visible before the device reaches a patient, and the class then decides who may sell, install, and service it, how it is tracked, and what the buyer can demand. The field variant gives up the scrutiny, the installer, the calibration laboratory, and the central alarm, and demands understanding and redundancy in their place. Read the marking, ask the class, ask who supports it, check the consumable, and never use the device beyond what its class allows, with every limit confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the patient is exposed.
