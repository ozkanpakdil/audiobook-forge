# Chapter 47 — Nephrology

The nephrology chapter gathers the machines that stand in for a kidney when the organ has stopped working. A kidney performs many jobs through about a million filtering units, and the ones that kill quickly are the removal of waste, the control of water, and the control of potassium. In physical terms it is a selective filter that runs blood at pressure against a membrane, letting water and small dissolved molecules pass while holding back cells and large proteins. When those filters fail, waste accumulates, the acid-base balance drifts, potassium rises to a level that stops the heart, and water overloads the lungs.

Every dialysis membrane works on two movements of dissolved particles, and those movements explain almost every setting on the machine. Diffusion is the movement of particles from a place of higher concentration to a place of lower concentration, and it needs only a gradient and a path through a membrane whose pores let water and small molecules cross while holding back cells and large proteins. Blood runs on one side of that membrane and a fluid called dialysate on the other, so urea and creatinine leave the blood at a speed set by the concentration difference, the membrane area, the membrane thickness, and the contact time. The second movement is convection, in which water is pushed through the pores and drags dissolved molecules with it, and that pressure-driven flow becomes ultrafiltration when it is used to remove water.

In a well-resourced hospital the kidney machine is a fixed installation rather than a portable object, standing beside a dedicated chair or bed. It is connected to treated water, to drainage, and to a reliable electrical supply, and it consumes single-use tubing, a filter, and prepared fluid. It needs a trained operator who can answer its alarms and a technician who can maintain the water plant and calibrate its sensors. The field clinic rarely has any of that, so the second half of this chapter describes peritoneal dialysis, which uses the patient's own abdominal membrane and a bag of fluid and asks for clean hands instead of a water plant.

The hemodialysis machine is a console with two fluid circuits, and those circuits meet only at the filter. The blood circuit takes blood from the patient, pushes it through the filter, and returns it, while the dialysate circuit makes a warmed fluid of the correct composition from concentrate and treated water. Between them sits the dialyzer, the membrane device where diffusion, convection, and ultrafiltration occur, and the pump that moves the blood is almost always a peristaltic roller pump. In that pump, rollers press a flexible tube segment against a curved track and push the trapped volume ahead of them, and a common blood flow is 200 to 500 milliliters per minute, a range that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, while the characteristic failure is the pump segment that ruptures or walks along the track.

Pressure is measured at several points of the blood circuit because pressure tells the machine whether it is pulling or pushing too hard. The pressure before the pump falls when the access is poor or the line is kinked, while the pressure after the pump rises when the venous needle is misplaced or a clot is forming. The characteristic alarm is a high venous pressure alert, and the common error is to silence it without inspecting the needle, the line, and the filter in that order. The exact alarm limits for the venous and arterial lines must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The dialysate circuit begins with proportioning, the mixing of a concentrate with treated water in a ratio that most machines hold with two balancing chambers. The fluid is then warmed and stripped of dissolved air, because bubbles inside the dialysate compartment can blanket part of the membrane and reduce clearance. Conductivity is measured continuously as the quickest proof that the ratio is correct, since dissolved salts carry current in proportion to their concentration, and a typical value of about 13 to 14 millisiemens per centimeter must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A mix that is too weak draws sodium out of the blood and one that is too strong drives sodium in and can damage red cells, so the conductivity alarm diverts dialysate away from the filter and must never be reset without checking the concentrates.

Dialysate must be warmed to body temperature, and a temperature near 37 degrees Celsius is normal, a value that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Blood returned too cold causes shivering and a rise in blood pressure, while blood returned too hot damages red cells, so the heater is controlled by a thermistor and guarded by a thermal cutout. The characteristic alarms are the temperature alarm and the blood leak alarm, the second detecting blood in the dialysate if the membrane has ruptured. The common error is to drape a blanket over the machine vents, which overheats the electronics while the patient complains of feeling cold.

Ultrafiltration control decides how much water is removed, and it is the part of the machine most directly tied to the patient's wellbeing. A common maximum removal rate is 10 to 15 milliliters per kilogram of body weight each hour, and a common session target is the difference between the weight before the session and the dry weight agreed by the clinical team, both of which must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The machine removes fluid by making the outgoing dialysate volume greater than the incoming volume or by measuring the difference with a flow sensor, and removal that is too fast drops the blood pressure and causes cramping and fainting. The characteristic alarm is the ultrafiltration error alarm, which usually means the balancing chambers no longer agree with the set target, and the common error is to record an incorrect starting weight.

The venous line carries blood back to the patient, so it is the place where air must never pass. Air enters through a loose connection, an empty saline bag, an open syringe port, or a needle that has come out of the vessel while the pump keeps running. The machine guards against this with an air detector, a drip chamber that lets bubbles rise out of the blood, and a venous clamp that closes when the detector alarms, because a larger volume of air reaching the right side of the heart can block the pulmonary circulation and kill the patient within minutes. The characteristic alarm is the air detector alarm with the pump stopped and the venous clamp closed, and the common errors are to override the detector and to leave the drip chamber nearly empty.

The check before a session is a fixed sequence, and it is the operator's real protection against a machine that is quietly wrong. The machine runs a self-test of its pumps, valves, and sensors, and a pressure test then proves that the blood circuit holds and that the dialysate does not leak across the membrane. The conductivity and temperature are read against the prescribed values, the alarm limits are reviewed for this patient, and the operator confirms that the correct concentrate is connected to the correct port. Priming follows, in which the sterile blood lines and dialyzer are filled with sterile saline so that no air remains and no blood meets a dry surface, and the common error is to initial the check sheet without reading the values.

The dialyzer is the membrane device itself, and its usual form is a cylinder packed with thousands of hollow fibers through which blood flows while dialysate flows around the outside in the opposite direction, so the concentration difference is maintained along the whole length. The wall of each fiber is the membrane, made of regenerated cellulose, a modified cellulose, or a synthetic polymer such as polysulfone, and the fibers are potted at each end in resin so the two compartments stay sealed. The size of the dialyzer is given by its membrane surface area, typically 1.0 to 2.2 square meters, and the character of the membrane by its flux, which states how freely water and larger molecules cross, both of which must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A low flux membrane restricts the middle molecules, a high flux membrane passes them and far more water, and the common error is to knock or drop a dialyzer, because the potting can crack.

In many units the dialyzer is reprocessed and used more than once for the same patient, and reuse is a controlled process rather than a rinse. The fibers are flushed with clean water and then a disinfectant, the volume of the fiber bundle is measured against its first-use value, and the device is tested for membrane leaks before storage. A fall in fiber bundle volume beyond the local limit, a failed leak test, or a damaged header means the dialyzer is discarded, and each device is labelled with the patient's identity and the number of uses and never used for another patient. The common errors are to accept a dialyzer whose volume has fallen below the limit and to omit the test for residual disinfectant, and the acceptable number of reuses and the minimum fiber bundle volume must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The vascular access is the connection between the patient and the machine, and it is the component that fails most often. The best access is an arteriovenous fistula, created by joining an artery to a vein in the arm so that the vein receives arterial pressure and enlarges over weeks into a thick vessel that must mature before it is used, a period that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The operator checks it every session by feeling the thrill of turbulent flow and by listening for the bruit through a stethoscope, and a fistula that has lost its thrill, that hurts, or that keeps bleeding must be assessed before it is used again. The common error is to accept a weak thrill as normal, because a narrowing that is tolerated today can clot the access tomorrow.

The needles that puncture a fistula are large, because the flow must be high and the resistance low, and they are usually 14 gauge to 17 gauge, where the gauge number falls as the needle becomes thicker. The venous needle always points toward the heart, and each needle is inserted at a shallow angle with the bevel facing up, because a needle pressed against the wall reduces flow while one that has passed through it lets blood leak into the surrounding tissue. The common error is to cannulate the same spot at every session, which weakens the wall and produces an aneurysm, and the correct practice is to rotate the sites in a ladder pattern. The needle gauge, the insertion angle, and the rotation plan must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the characteristic alarm is a rising arterial pressure with a falling flow.

A dialysis catheter is a large bore tube placed in a central vein, usually the internal jugular vein, with two or three lumens so that blood can be withdrawn through one and returned through another. A tunneled catheter is passed under the skin to a cuff that anchors it and seals the tract, a non-tunneled one serves for days to weeks, and the lumens are locked between sessions with heparin or citrate whose concentration must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The catheter is the access most likely to become infected, so the hub is scrubbed and the connection protected, and the common errors are to leave a clamp open, to let air enter, and to use the catheter for other purposes without a deliberate decision. When no fistula is possible, a graft of expanded polytetrafluoroethylene, or PTFE, is sewn between an artery and a vein, and its characteristic problems are a narrowing at the venous end and a pseudoaneurysm, so the common error is to press too hard after the needles are removed.

In a dialysis unit the water is not a utility but a clinical device, because the patient's blood meets it across a thin membrane many times each week. Ordinary drinking water carries chlorine, chloramine, calcium, magnesium, iron, bacteria, and bacterial fragments, so the treatment chain is a sequence in which each stage removes something the next cannot tolerate, usually a sediment filter, carbon tanks, a softener, and a reverse osmosis unit. The sediment filter traps particles, rust, and grit before they reach the finer stages, and the carbon filter removes chlorine and chloramine by adsorption onto activated carbon. The carbon stage is the most safety-critical of all, because those substances destroy red blood cells, so the water must be tested before every shift, and the test method and permitted concentration must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A softener removes calcium and magnesium by ion exchange, passing the water through a bed of resin that holds those ions and releases sodium in their place. Hard water forms scale on the reverse osmosis membrane and reduces its output, so the softener protects the plant and also the patient, because calcium and magnesium in the wrong proportion can disturb the heart, and a hardness test of the water leaving the softener shows whether the resin is still working. The resin is regenerated by drawing brine from a salt tank through the bed, so the salt level must be checked and the tank kept clean, and the common error is to let the salt tank run dry, after which the resin is exhausted and hard water passes unnoticed. Reverse osmosis forces water at a pressure greater than the osmotic pressure through a membrane that rejects dissolved salts, producing purified product water and a stream of reject water carrying the impurities away, and the rejection rate and product flow must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Deionization passes water through ion exchange resin that replaces dissolved salts with water, and the resin is monitored by measuring the resistance of the water, because a fall means it is exhausted. Beyond the chain the water is stored in a tank and moved through a loop of piping to each machine, and that loop is itself a device that must be maintained. Stagnation, rough joints, dead ends, and warm spots encourage biofilm, a community of bacteria in a slime layer that disinfectant cannot easily reach, so the water is monitored by counting colonies and by testing for endotoxin, a bacterial fragment that produces fever and shock in the blood. Common action limits of below 100 colonies per milliliter for water used to prepare dialysate and below 0.25 endotoxin units per milliliter for the same water must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The characteristic failure is a biofilm that is invisible in the water, and the common errors are to continue past the capacity of the resin and to sample from a tap that is not the point of use.

Dialysate is made from two concentrates and treated water, and the reason for two is chemistry: the acid concentrate is acidic so that the bicarbonate in the other concentrate does not precipitate with the calcium and magnesium in the water. The bicarbonate concentrate is usually a powder dissolved on site or a ready liquid, it is the part most vulnerable to bacterial growth because it is a warm alkaline solution, and the machine mixes the concentrates at a fixed ratio that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The composition is prescribed for each patient, and potassium is usually set low because the session aims to remove the potassium a failing kidney has retained, with a common value of 2 millimoles per liter that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The common errors are to connect the acid container to the bicarbonate port, to use a bicarbonate batch that has stood warm, and to use powder that has not dissolved completely.

Between patients the machine is cleaned and disinfected, and that schedule is part of treatment rather than housekeeping. The exterior is wiped with a compatible disinfectant, the blood pump and the ports are cleaned, and the internal fluid path is disinfected with heat or a chemical agent and then rinsed, with citric acid commonly used to descale the heater at a concentration and contact time that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The machine is also a measuring instrument whose sensors drift, so the conductivity cell, the temperature sensor, the pressure transducers, and the ultrafiltration balance are calibrated against reference instruments on a schedule, and the blood pump is checked for the flow it delivers with the tubing in use. The common error is to skip the internal disinfection because the machine looks clean when the growth is inside the fluid path, and the second error is to treat the annual service visit as the only maintenance when the daily checks catch the drift.

Peritoneal dialysis uses the patient's own peritoneum as the membrane, and it is a fundamentally different device from a hemodialysis machine even though the physics are the same. The peritoneum is a thin moist membrane that lines the abdominal cavity and covers the organs, and it is richly supplied with blood vessels, so it is a dialysis membrane already inside the body. Dialysate is instilled through a catheter, left to dwell, and then drained, and during the dwell diffusion removes the small waste molecules while dwell time decides how complete that removal is, while fluid removal is produced by osmosis because the dialysate contains glucose at a higher concentration than the blood. The solution comes in a clear plastic bag, its glucose concentration is chosen for the amount of water to be removed, with common concentrations of 1.36 per cent, 2.27 per cent, and 3.86 per cent and common adult bag volumes of 1.5 to 2.5 liters, values that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the common error is to warm a bag in a microwave or in water that is too hot.

The transfer set is the tubing that joins the bag to the catheter, and the technique of connecting it is the single most important skill in peritoneal dialysis. In a twin bag system the sequence is drain, flush, and fill, and the flush step pushes any bacteria that touched the connector out of the fresh line before the new fluid enters the patient. The connector is disinfected, the clamps are opened in the correct order, and the hands never touch the sterile ends, because the peritonitis that follows a break in technique is the characteristic complication of this therapy and presents as a cloudy drained bag, abdominal pain, fever, and a loss of ultrafiltration. The first equipment step is to examine the drain bag, because turbidity is the earliest sign, the effluent is sent for a cell count and a culture, treatment is usually started before the culture returns using antibiotics given into the peritoneum through the catheter, and the common error is to attribute a cloudy bag to fibrin and to wait.

The catheter is a soft silicone tube with side holes near its tip and one or two polyester cuffs near the exit site, and the cuffs provoke fibrous tissue that anchors the catheter and blocks bacteria from tracking along its outside. The exit site is kept dry and undisturbed, a red, tender, or discharging exit site is an infection that must be reported at once, and the common error is to pull or rotate a new catheter, which disturbs the cuff and opens the tract to bacteria. The cycler automates the therapy while the patient sleeps, using a pump or valves, a heater plate for the bags, scales to measure what is drained, and a controller that runs a program of fills, dwells, and drains. The prescription sets the total volume, the number of cycles, the dwell time, and the final fill, and each of these along with every alarm limit must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, while the characteristic alarms are the low drain volume alarm and the fill volume alarm, whose common cause is the catheter tip lying against the bowel.

Continuous renal replacement therapy, or CRRT, is dialysis delivered slowly and without interruption for a patient who cannot tolerate the blood pressure swings of standard hemodialysis. The patient is usually in an intensive care unit, and instead of removing two days of fluid in 4 hours the machine removes the same volume over 24 hours or more. The circuit is a set of tubing, a pump for blood, pumps for replacement fluid and effluent, and a filter much smaller than a hemodialyzer, and a common blood flow is 100 to 200 milliliters per minute, a range that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The effluent rate is prescribed according to the patient's weight, a common prescription is 20 to 35 milliliters per kilogram each hour, a value that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and unfractionated heparin or a regional citrate infusion keeps the circuit open. The characteristic alarm is a rising filter pressure or a falling effluent volume, and both mean the membrane is clotting, so the common error is to ignore a slow drift in the pressures until the filter fails.

In a mobile or frontier clinic the hospital-grade hemodialysis machine is almost never present, and the reason is infrastructure rather than cost. The machine needs a continuous supply of treated water that meets a strict chemical and microbial standard, a drain for hundreds of liters in a session, a stable electrical supply for several hours, a vascular access that can be cannulated with large needles, and a patient who can return three times each week for years. When any of those is missing the realistic answer is peritoneal dialysis, which needs no water plant, no electricity during the dwell, and no needle, but what it gives up is speed and clearance, because the peritoneum clears small molecules far more slowly than a dialyzer. What it demands instead is a clean room, scrupulous hand and connection technique, a reliable supply of bags and transfer sets, and a trained patient or family member; the manual exchange is performed with the bag hung above the patient, commonly 2 liters instilled, left to dwell for 4 to 6 hours, and then drained, repeated 4 times each day, figures that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The frontier clinic must also recognize what it cannot do, and the most important decision is when to refer. A patient who needs rapid removal of potassium, who has pulmonary edema from fluid overload, or who has taken a poison that requires fast clearance cannot be treated by slow peritoneal dialysis and must reach a hemodialysis unit. In the meantime the clinic restricts fluid and potassium intake, gives medicines that remove potassium through the gut, treats the cause of the kidney injury, and watches the heart rhythm, because the conservative care of acute kidney injury is itself a set of equipment decisions from accurate urine collection to a reliable scale. The clinic must know its referral pathway in advance, and the indications for starting dialysis and for urgent referral must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The consumables of nephrology are heavy, bulky, and perishable in ways that make stock control a clinical matter. The dialyzers, blood lines, fistula needles, saline, concentrates, disinfectants, and peritoneal dialysis bags each have a shelf life and a storage condition, and the peritoneal dialysis bags must be kept cool, upright, and out of direct sunlight, while a bag that has frozen or been stored hot must be discarded. The concentrates have a limited life once opened or once mixed, the bicarbonate is the most perishable of all, and the reverse osmosis membranes, the carbon, and the ion exchange resin are consumables of the plant whose stock must be tracked as carefully as the supplies at the bedside. The minimum stock level, the storage temperature, and the shelf life of each item must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the common error is to order only the visible items and to forget the filters and the salt that keep the water plant alive.

To summarize, the machines of nephrology do outside the body what a kidney does inside it, and they do it by moving dissolved particles across a thin membrane. Hemodialysis depends on a peristaltic blood pump, a proportioning system, a dialyzer chosen for its surface area and flux, a vascular access, and a water plant whose carbon stage is the difference between safe treatment and hemolysis. Diffusion removes the small waste molecules, convection and ultrafiltration remove the middle molecules and the accumulated water, and every pressure, temperature, conductivity value, and alarm limit must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The operator's duties are decisive: the pre-session test, the chlorine test before the first patient, the trend of the pressures, the inspection of the drain bag, and the refusal to silence an alarm whose cause has not been found. Peritoneal dialysis is the device that travels, because it uses the patient's own membrane and demands technique instead of infrastructure, while CRRT serves the unstable patient in the intensive care unit, and in every setting the rule is to understand the physics, check the machine, and treat the patient rather than the alarm.
