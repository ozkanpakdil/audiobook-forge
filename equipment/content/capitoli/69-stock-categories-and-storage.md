# Chapter 69 — Stock Categories and Storage

Everything a hospital consumes must wait somewhere in a known quantity and condition, so a storeroom is a controlled environment rather than a room full of objects. Four agents destroy what is stored inside it, and they are heat, water, light, and oxygen, with time as the multiplier. Almost every form of damage is a chemical reaction, and reactions run faster when they are warm, so temperature is the first lever, while water moves into materials according to the difference in vapor pressure, which makes humidity the second. Light carries energy in packets that can break a drug molecule, which makes darkness the third, and oxygen attacks oils, rubbers, and metals, which makes a sealed package the fourth. Many reactions roughly double in rate for every rise of 10 degrees Celsius, and that figure must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before a storage temperature is chosen.

Relative humidity is the water vapor the air holds compared with the greatest amount it could hold at that temperature. Water condenses on any surface colder than the dew point, so a cold item opened while still cold draws water into its packaging and onto the powder inside, and hydrolysis then splits the bonds of many drugs. Mold grows readily when relative humidity stays above 65 per cent, a figure that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before a room is approved. Metal rusts, cardboard collapses, and paper becomes unreadable, so humidity damages the container as well as the contents.

Light damage depends on energy and exposure time, and ultraviolet light breaks bonds most readily, which is why many drugs arrive in amber glass or opaque foil. Time converts these agents into an expiry date, and that date is a promise made from stability studies at defined conditions of temperature and humidity. A store hotter or wetter than those conditions shortens the real life of the product without changing the date on the box, so the date is conditional and the storage conditions on the label must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the stock is accepted. A storeroom is also an ecosystem, and warmth, moisture, and food attract life, since mold needs a damp surface, insects arrive in corrugated cardboard, and rodents and ants are drawn by paper, starch, and sugar. The defenses are exclusion and dryness, which mean sealing the store, keeping stock off the floor, rotating the oldest material outward, and cleaning shelves on a schedule.

The organization of the store begins with a distinction that the accounting makes and the shelf must respect. Capital equipment is durable hardware bought once and used for years, such as an infusion pump, a ventilator, or a sterilizer, and it is depreciated over its useful life, carries a serial number in an asset register, and needs a maintenance schedule, spare parts, calibration, and often a service contract. A consumable is used once or used up, charged to the operating budget in the year it is bought, and small in unit price even when its total cost is enormous. A capital item belongs in the equipment store with its history and accessories, while a consumable belongs in the supply store with its quantity and expiry, and the common error is to blur the two, so that a pump sits on a consumable shelf without an asset number.

Wound care supplies are the gauze, dressings, bandages, tape, and antiseptics, and vascular supplies are the catheters, needles, syringes, infusion sets, tubing, and collection bags. Surgical supplies are the sutures, gloves, gowns, drapes, and blades, and laboratory supplies are the reagents, tubes, slides, culture media, and quality control material. Cleaning supplies are the detergents, disinfectants, personal protective equipment, or PPE, and waste bags. Some consumables belong to one machine, such as the tubing set for an infusion pump, the transducer for a patient monitor, the breathing circuit for a ventilator, and the electrode for an electrocardiogram, because a device without its dedicated consumable is a sculpture rather than a therapy.

The store classifies its stock by value and by volume, which means asking how much one unit costs and how many units move in a month. An item that costs a great deal and moves rarely, such as an implant or a specialized spare part, deserves a locked cabinet, a batch record, a count at every shift change, and two signatures at issue. An item that costs little and moves constantly, such as a gauze swab or a pair of examination gloves, deserves a generous par level, an open bin near the issue point, and a count quick enough to be done often. The items in the middle are the difficult ones, because they are expensive enough to attract theft and common enough that a shortage stops work, so they deserve a cycle count at a fixed interval. Fast movers belong at waist height near the issue counter, while slow movers belong out of prime space but easy to reach and often checked for expiry.

A par level is the quantity that must be on the shelf at the start of a period, and it is calculated rather than guessed. A safety stock is then added to cover variation in both consumption and lead time, because a route that is usually five days long is sometimes fifteen, and a clinic without safety stock is holding a stock-out on credit. The reorder point is the level at which the order must be placed, and it equals the consumption during the lead time plus the safety stock, so an item used at 10 units a day with a 7 day lead time has a reorder point of at least 70 units before any provision for delay. Every consumption figure, lead time, and safety margin must be confirmed against the local protocol and the manufacturer's instructions before the order is placed.

Shelving should be a rigid frame anchored to the wall or floor, loaded from the bottom upward, with the heaviest material at waist height and the lightest above, because a heavy box lifted from a high shelf is a back injury and a heavy box that falls is a head injury. Nothing should be stored above the height that the shortest member of staff can reach with both feet flat on the floor. Aisles must be wide enough for a trolley and a person to pass, in practice a clear width of at least 90 centimeters, and that figure must be confirmed against the local protocol and the manufacturer's instructions for the shelving before the room is laid out. The access route must stay clear, and a shelf that creaks, bends, or shows rust has lost its margin and must be unloaded and repaired.

Circulating air is the cheapest environmental control, because it carries heat and water vapor away from the stock, while air trapped in a solid stack allows the middle to stay warm and damp for weeks. A gap of 10 to 15 centimeters should be left between the shelving and the wall, and stock should stay below the ceiling and below any sprinkler head by the clearance the fire code requires, figures that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the room is arranged. A room with an inlet low on one wall and an outlet high on the opposite wall ventilates by convection, while a room with a single sealed door does not. Where a mechanical system exists, the heating, ventilation, and air conditioning, or HVAC, unit must be maintained and its filters changed on a schedule, and the characteristic alarm of that equipment is the filter differential pressure alarm, whose threshold and response must be confirmed before the system is trusted.

The store must prove what its environment has been, and that proof comes from instruments rather than memory. A maximum and minimum thermometer and a hygrometer record the extremes since the last reset, while a continuous data logger can be downloaded to show the exposure of the stock over a whole month. A general medical store is commonly held between 15 and 25 degrees Celsius and between 40 and 60 per cent relative humidity, and both ranges must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol for the products on the shelf. The place of the store matters as much as its equipment, because a room under a metal roof in direct sun, above a boiler, or beside a laundry runs hotter than the rest of the building, and stock stacked on the floor is the most hazardous arrangement of all, because the floor is where water collects and where pests travel.

The cold chain is a separate physical system inside the store, and it works by moving heat rather than creating cold. A refrigerant evaporates in a low pressure coil inside the cabinet, absorbing heat from the load, and then condenses in a high pressure coil outside, releasing that heat into the room. A full cabinet warms slowly during a door opening or a power failure while an empty one warms quickly, and the latent heat absorbed by melting ice is what lets a cold box hold its temperature for hours without power. This is why a vaccine carrier is packed tightly, why the ice packs go against the walls and the vaccine in the center, and why a thermometer in the middle of the load tells the truth while one taped to the door does not.

Cold chain storage is divided into bands, and each band has its own equipment and its own risks. The most common band is 2 to 8 degrees Celsius, the range for most vaccines, many drugs, blood components in transit, and clinical specimens, and that range must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol for each product before it is stored. A freezer band of minus 20 degrees Celsius is used for some vaccines, frozen plasma, and certain reagents, and a still colder band near minus 70 degrees Celsius is used for specialized biological material, and each of those temperatures must likewise be confirmed. The hazard that operators underestimate is freezing, because an adsorbed vaccine that has been frozen looks exactly like one that has not, yet its potency has been destroyed, so freeze sensitive items must be kept away from the evaporator plate, the cabinet wall, and direct contact with ice packs.

Cold chain monitoring is continuous, and the characteristic alarm of this chapter belongs to it. A vaccine refrigerator carries a panel that watches the cabinet temperature and the power supply, and it sounds a high temperature alarm when the load spends too long above the upper limit, a low temperature alarm when it approaches freezing, a door alarm when the door is left ajar, and a power failure alarm when the mains supply is lost, with a battery that keeps the alarm alive after the lights go out. The threshold, the delay before the alarm sounds, and the response to each alarm must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the cabinet is loaded, because an alarm that is silenced has removed the only warning the stock will ever receive. The second line of defense is a manual reading recorded twice each day, and the third is the vaccine vial monitor, a small square of heat sensitive material on the label that darkens cumulatively as it is exposed to heat.

The cold chain depends on maintenance, and its failures are usually mechanical and gradual. Condenser coils clogged with dust make the compressor run hotter and longer, a hardened door gasket lets warm air in with every closure, ice on the evaporator reduces the surface for heat exchange, and a cabinet in a hot alcove cannot reject the heat it has collected. The gasket should be checked by the paper test, which is to close the door on a strip of paper and feel whether it can be pulled out easily, and the coils should be cleaned on the interval the manufacturer specifies, and both checks and intervals must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before they become routine. The power supply is the weakest link in most settings, so a refrigerator that protects vaccines should sit on an uninterruptible power supply, or UPS, or on a circuit backed by a generator. When the mains fails and no backup exists, the plan is physical, which means keeping the door closed, moving the load to a conditioned cold box, and recording the time of every action.

Expiry rotation keeps stock moving in the right order, and its rule is that the item closest to its expiry leaves first, regardless of when it arrived. The expiry date faces outward on the shelf, the newest delivery is placed behind the older stock, and the front row is the row that will be issued next. The operator error is to place the new box in front because it is easier to reach, which buries the older box at the back where it is found only after it has expired. An item that has been opened has its own clock, because a multidose vial, a bottle of reagent, or a breached sterile pack has a shelf life after opening shorter than the date on the box, and the rotation rule and that shelf life must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol for each product.

Quarantine is where doubt is kept, and it must be a physical place rather than an intention. The hazard is the quiet return, in which a well meaning worker decides that a recalled batch looks fine and puts it back on the active shelf, which defeats the recall and may reach a patient. Stocktaking reconciles the register against the shelf, and its value lies in the difference between the two. A persistent variance points to a specific cause such as an unrecorded issue, a wrong unit of measure, an expiry discarded without a record, a return never booked in, or a theft the controls have not stopped. Barcode scanning and radio-frequency identification, or RFID, reduce the labor and the error of counting, but neither removes the need for a physical verification, and the record must hold the stock card, the ledger, the requisition, the issue voucher, the receipt, the temperature log, and the short-dated list, each carrying a date, a quantity, a batch number, and a signature.

Theft and shrinkage are facts of every storeroom, and the response must protect the stock without insulting the people who handle it. The safeguards that work are structural, and they include the separation of duties so that the person who orders is not the person who receives, the two-person issue for high-value items, a locked cabinet with restricted keys, and a stock card that makes a loss appear at the shelf. The safeguards that do not work are the ones that treat every member of staff as a suspect, because they destroy the cooperation the store needs, and a lock protects the honest worker from suspicion as much as it protects the stock. A store full of cardboard, paper, plastic packaging, alcohol based hand rub, and aerosol cans is a fuel load waiting for a source of ignition, and a store that also holds oxygen cylinders under pressure holds the oxidizer that makes a fire burn fiercely.

Certain operator errors recur in every store, and naming them is the fastest way to prevent them. The first is the unlabeled opened pack returned to the shelf without a date or batch, so its identity and its life are both lost. The second is the private reserve, in which a worker hides a small stock of a scarce item for the shift that will need it, which distorts the par level and creates the shortage it was meant to prevent. The quiet alarms have no buzzer and no light, and they decide whether the store actually works. An empty space on the shelf is the first, because it announces a stock-out before the last item is issued, and a date that has passed is the second, because an expired item is a loss recorded as an asset.

Maintenance in the store is mostly preventive, and its purpose is to keep every control working between failures. The refrigerator needs its condenser coils cleaned, its door gasket replaced when it hardens, its defrost drain cleared, and its interior washed on a schedule, and the intervals must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before they are written into a schedule. Thermometers and data loggers must be verified against a reference at fixed intervals, and the classic field reference is an ice bath, which is crushed ice and water stirred until it holds a steady reading that any accurate thermometer should agree with. Cleaning is a dry task first and a wet task second, because water added to a storeroom is water added to the stock, and the store also consumes its own supplies, which include labels, bin cards, marking pens, bins, pallets, desiccant sachets, ice packs, logger batteries, and chart paper.

Distribution is where the central store meets the ward, and the exchange is governed by the same discipline. A ward requests stock with a requisition and receives it with an issue voucher, and the ward holds its own par level calculated from its own consumption, because a central store that tries to serve every bedside directly becomes a corridor of traffic and a source of error. The emergency trolley is a special case, because it must be complete at every moment and yet its contents expire, so it is sealed with a dated lock, checked on a fixed schedule, and restocked from a written list that names every item and its minimum quantity. The two-bin system holds each item in two containers and treats the empty one as the reorder signal, and every ward par level, exchange quantity, and trolley check interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the system is adopted.

A cold box is an insulated container whose walls slow the entry of heat, and its performance depends on the quantity and temperature of the ice packs, the tightness of the packing, and the number of times the lid is opened. The ice packs should be conditioned, which means allowing them to begin to melt so that they sit near zero degrees Celsius rather than far below it, because a pack still at freezer temperature in contact with a vaccine will freeze and destroy it, and the conditioning method and temperature limits must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the box is loaded. The packs go against the walls and the product in the center, with a thermometer among the product, and the box is filled so that little air remains to carry heat. The lid must be latched, the box carried in the shade, and the temperature recorded at the start, at the end, and at intervals during a long journey, and the power failure plan names the backup power, the cold boxes, the ice supply, and the person authorized to move the load.

The field variant of this chapter begins where the building ends, and it changes the equipment rather than the principle. A mobile or frontier clinic has no ventilated store, no air conditioning, no backup generator, no calibrated data logger, and often no refrigerator at all, so the environment that a hospital creates with machinery must be created with judgment. What it demands is discipline, because every control a machine performs automatically must be performed by a person on a schedule, and a missed check is a loss that no alarm will announce. The cupboard must stand off the floor on a wooden pallet, a concrete plinth, or a metal frame, because water, ants, and rodents travel along the ground, and it must stand away from the wall by a hand's width so air can move behind it. Where there is no electricity, evaporative cooling still works, so a clay pot inside a larger pot of wet sand, or a box wrapped in a wet cloth and set in a breeze, will hold a temperature below the surrounding air while the water lasts, and every arrangement must be checked with a thermometer whose acceptance range is confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

The field cold chain is built from whatever can hold a temperature, and each option carries a cost. A solar direct drive refrigerator has no battery, storing cold in a phase change material while the sun shines and releasing it at night, which removes the battery as a failure point but demands a panel shaded by nothing and cleaned daily. The cold box with ice packs remains the universal fallback, and the vaccine vial monitor remains the field's only cumulative record of heat exposure, because it is attached to the vial and travels with it. The field temperature log is a thermometer standing in a bottle of water inside the cabinet, which gives the reading the thermal mass of the load rather than the temperature of the air, and it must be read and written twice each day without exception.

The field cupboard must function as a pharmacy as well as a store, and the pharmacy rules survive the loss of the building. Look-alike and sound-alike medicines must be separated on the shelf and labeled in full, because a tired worker reading by hand torch will confuse two names that differ by one letter. Controlled substances should be held in a second locked box, with a register that records every dose given, the patient, and the witness, because a double lock and a dual signature are the only controls that survive where there is no pharmacy department to appeal to. Security in the field is a social arrangement as much as a physical one, so the cupboard should carry two locks with the keys held by two people, the register should be signed by the borrower, and a public monthly count with a witness turns the stock into a shared fact rather than a private secret.

Field storage fails in ways that are slower, wetter, and more biological than the failures of a hospital store. Mold appears as dark spots and a musty smell on cardboard and cloth, rust appears on instruments and cans, insects and rodents puncture packaging, and water arrives from a leaking roof, a wet floor, or a cloth put away damp. Heat damage is invisible at first and appears as a melted suppository, a discolored tablet, a separated cream, or a vial whose monitor has darkened. The daily check is short and physical, which means reading and writing the temperature, looking at the front row for the nearest expiry, looking for dampness, rust, and pest signs, counting the high-value items, and confirming that the lock and the register are intact.

Whether the store is a hospital building or a locked cupboard, the operator's checks follow the same order and take only minutes. The first is the environment, which means reading the thermometer and the hygrometer, comparing the readings with the limits for the stock on the shelf, and writing them down at the time of reading. The second is the cold chain, which means reading the cabinet and the logger, looking at the alarm history, checking the door seal, and confirming that the ice packs and cold boxes are ready for an outage. The third is the stock itself, which means walking the front row, reading the nearest expiry date, and looking for damage, dampness, and pests.

A supply failure is a clinical event, and it is worth saying plainly at the end of this chapter. The most capable ventilator, the most precise analyzer, and the most sophisticated imaging system all stop working when a filter, a reagent, or a catheter is missing, so the store that holds those consumables is as much a part of the therapy as the machine they serve. A stock-out of a dressing, an antibiotic, or a vaccine is a patient harm with an administrative cause, and it is invisible in the equipment inventory even though it stops treatment as completely as a broken device. Heat, water, light, oxygen, and time are working on every shelf in the building at every moment, and the operator who measures them is protecting the patient as surely as the clinician at the bedside.

To summarize, a storeroom is a controlled environment whose enemies are heat, water, light, and oxygen, multiplied by time, and its organization begins with the difference between capital equipment, which is depreciated and maintained, and consumables, which are used up and expensed. Stock is classified by value and by volume, fast movers and slow movers are shelved differently, and the par level and the reorder point are calculated from consumption and lead time rather than guessed, so that the shelf never empties and never buries its oldest stock. The room itself must be dry, ventilated, cool, and secure, with heavy items low, air behind the shelves, stock off the floor, and instruments that record temperature and humidity as evidence rather than as impression. The cold chain is a separate system that moves heat rather than making cold, and it depends on insulation, thermal mass, continuous monitoring, a tested alarm, a maintained cabinet, and a plan for the power failure that will come. In the field, the same principles are carried by a locked cupboard on a plinth, in the shade, with a desiccant, a thermometer in a bottle of water, a conditioned cold box, a double lock, a public register, and a daily check performed by a person rather than a machine. Every temperature, humidity, clearance, interval, and alarm threshold named in this chapter must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before it is used, because the numbers are a guide and the patient's safety is the standard.
