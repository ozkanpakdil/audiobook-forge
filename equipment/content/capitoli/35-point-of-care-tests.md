# Chapter 35 — Point-of-Care Tests

Point-of-care tests carry the laboratory to the bedside, to the clinic bench, and to the tent where a patient has just arrived. The physical principle is always the same in outline: a chemical or immunological reaction happens inside a small disposable vessel, and the signal it produces becomes a number the operator can act upon. What changes is how much machinery is left behind. In the hospital laboratory the reaction occurs in a cuvette and a photometer or electrode reads it, while at the point of care it occurs in a strip or a cartridge and the reading may be a line the eye can see, a color matched against a chart, or a current measured by a pocket instrument. This chapter describes the hospital grade program with its governance, its connectivity, and its quality control, and then the field position, where these devices often are the only laboratory that exists.

The handheld glucose meter is the most familiar point-of-care device in medicine, and its principle can be pictured as a current produced by an enzyme. A single-use strip carries dried glucose oxidase or a glucose dehydrogenase, a mediator molecule, and at least 2 electrodes printed on plastic. When a drop of blood fills the strip, the enzyme oxidizes glucose and the mediator ferries the freed electrons to the electrode surface, so a current flows whose size is proportional to the glucose present. The meter applies a small voltage, measures that current, and converts it into a concentration using a calibration stored in its memory. Some older designs let the reaction produce a color and read it by reflectance, but the electrochemical design dominates because it needs no optics or lamp and very little power. The strip is the true reagent vessel, because everything that makes the reaction specific is dried onto it, and moisture, heat, and age destroy those reagents long before the plastic looks damaged. The operating temperature range, the sample volume, and the measuring range must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.

Because strips are manufactured in lots that vary slightly, the meter must be told which lot it is reading, and this is the meaning of coding. The match is made by a code chip, by a number on a keypad, or by a code printed on the vial that the meter reads automatically, and a meter set to the wrong lot reports a number that is precise, plausible, and wrong. A control solution at one or more levels must be run at the interval the local program defines, whenever a new vial is opened, and whenever a result is doubted. The classic distortions are physical and hematological rather than electronic. Anemia lowers the red cell content and can push the reading upward, a very high red cell count or dehydration can push it downward, and ascorbic acid, high doses of acetaminophen, extreme altitude, and extreme temperature disturb the chemistry unpredictably. The control limits, the coding method, and the correction between whole blood and plasma readings must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.

The common operator errors at the bedside are small and repetitive. A fingertip still wet with water, fruit, or alcohol dilutes the drop; a drop too small to fill the strip gives a low current; squeezing the finger hard expresses tissue fluid and dilutes the blood; a strip from a vial left open in humid air has absorbed its own destruction; and a strip past its expiry or left in a hot vehicle may never have been reliable. The characteristic alarms are a strip error, a low battery warning, a temperature out of range message, and a result shown as below or above the measuring range rather than a number, and each is a refusal to report rather than a reading to argue with. The operator must resist repeating the test on fresh drops until a preferred number appears, because that converts a measurement into a search for reassurance. The error codes, the sample volume, the storage temperature, and the response to each alarm must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.

In a hospital the glucose meter is not a loose object in a pocket but a managed instrument inside a program. Each meter is assigned to a unit, tied to its operator by a login or badge, and connected by a docking station or wireless link so that every result flows into the electronic health record, called EHR, stamped with the patient, operator, strip lot, and any quality control result that accompanied it. The program keeps meters cleaned and charged, distributes control material, records the training and competency of every operator, and periodically compares meter results with the central laboratory using the same blood sample, because agreement with the reference method is the only meaningful definition of accuracy at the bedside. The consumables are strips, control solutions, lancets, alcohol pads, batteries, and cleaning supplies, whose storage temperature and expiry are the program's constant concern. The connectivity standard most often used is Health Level Seven, called HL7, and the patient is identified by a barcode or radio frequency identification, called RFID, wristband rather than by asking the name again. The temperature limits, the comparison schedule, and the data fields required must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.

The rapid diagnostic test, which most people simply call a rapid test, is built on the lateral flow strip, and its principle is capillary action doing the work of a pump, a pipette, and a wash station. A plastic housing contains a strip of nitrocellulose, a pad holding dried labeled antibody, a test line where a capturing antibody is fixed, and a control line further along. When the sample is applied it wicks along the strip, rehydrates the labeled antibody, and carries it forward; if the target molecule is present, the labeled antibody binds it, the complex is caught at the test line, and a colored line appears. The control line catches antibody that traveled past regardless, and so proves that the liquid moved and the reagents were alive. Some tests work in the opposite way, with a labeled copy of the analyte competing for limited capture sites, so a strong test line means a low concentration, and the operator must know which format is in hand. The result is qualitative or semi-quantitative, which is why a lateral flow test cannot report a concentration as a laboratory immunoassay can. The reading time and the interpretation of each line must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.

The tests that matter most share this same body. A malaria test detects an antigen released by the parasite, a human immunodeficiency virus test detects antibody or antigen, a syphilis test detects antibody to the bacterium, a pregnancy test detects human chorionic gonadotropin in urine, a dengue test detects viral antigen or antibody, and a streptococcal test detects the group A carbohydrate. Storage is a condition of validity rather than a detail. The dried antibodies and the nitrocellulose are destroyed by heat and moisture, so the sealed pouch must be kept within its stated temperature range, often below 30 degrees Celsius and never below freezing, opened only immediately before use, and never left in a hot vehicle or a humid room. Expiry must be read on the pouch itself and not the carton, and a test that has been frozen, baked, or soaked should be discarded rather than tried. Reading must occur within the exact window printed by the maker, because a line appearing after the window may mean nothing, and a line absent at the closed window means the test is negative. The storage temperature, the expiry rule, and the reading window must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.

The operator errors that damage rapid tests are as consistent as the tests themselves. Too much or too little sample, a buffer volume guessed rather than measured, a strip read in poor light, a strip left on the bench while other patients are seen, and a result recorded from memory rather than from the strip are the ordinary failures, and the most dangerous habit is reading the strip again later and accepting whatever appears. A test with no control line is invalid, and a faint test line in a patient with a low probability of disease may be a false positive just as a clear negative in early disease may be a false negative. The safety hazards are the lancet, which belongs in a sharps container at once, the blood or urine on the strip, which is infectious material, and the used strip itself, which belongs in infectious waste rather than an ordinary bin. The characteristic alarms of the few readers that exist are a low battery warning, a temperature out of range message, and a device error, because the strip alone is silent and its only warning is the missing control line. The sample volumes, buffer volumes, reading windows, and waste rules must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.

Blood gas and electrolyte analyzers measure the gases, the acid base state, and the ions of whole blood with a cluster of electrodes rather than with light. A glass electrode measures hydrogen ion activity and therefore acidity; a Clark type electrode measures oxygen by letting it diffuse through a membrane and be reduced at a cathode, producing a current proportional to its partial pressure; and a Severinghaus type electrode measures carbon dioxide by letting it change the acidity of a thin bicarbonate film behind its membrane, and that shift is read as a partial pressure. Sodium, potassium, chloride, and ionized calcium are read by ion selective electrodes, and some instruments also measure hematocrit by conductivity, glucose and lactate by enzyme electrodes, and hemoglobin or bilirubin by photometry. The instrument must be calibrated before each measurement, and the calibrating solutions, wash, and gas mixtures are held inside the cartridge or reagent pack, which is why a blood gas analyzer can sit in an intensive care unit rather than a laboratory. The electrode temperatures, the calibration intervals, and the sample volume must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.

The accuracy of a blood gas result is decided mostly before the sample reaches the electrode. The syringe must hold the correct amount of heparin, because too much dilutes the blood and shifts the values, and the sample must be free of air bubbles, because air equilibrates with the blood and moves the oxygen and carbon dioxide readings toward room air. The measurement should be made quickly, and the sample kept cool if it must wait, because leukocytes and platelets continue to consume oxygen and produce carbon dioxide inside the syringe. A capillary tube filled from a heel or earlobe must be sealed and mixed without introducing a bubble, and the operator must record whether the patient was breathing room air or supplemental oxygen, and at what concentration, because a blood gas without that information cannot be interpreted. The common operator errors are a clotted sample, a bubble under the cap, a sample drawn into an ordinary tube instead of a heparinized one, and a specimen that waited on a warm bench through a shift change. The heparin concentration, the maximum delay, the storage temperature, and the acceptable bubble size must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.

A cartridge based blood gas analyzer removes most of the plumbing from the operator's view, but it does not remove the maintenance. Every cartridge holds its own calibrants, wash, and waste chamber, and it must be stored within its stated temperature range and used before expiry, with the first measurement after installation usually reserved for calibration or quality control rather than for a patient. The electrodes foul with protein and clot and must be cleaned or replaced on the schedule the maker gives; the sample probe clogs with fibrin; tubing and seals age and admit air; and a battery that has not been charged will interrupt a measurement exactly when it is needed. The characteristic alarms are a calibration failure, a sample not detected message, a clot detected flag, a temperature out of range warning, and a low battery or replace cartridge notice, and each demands a defined response before another patient sample is run. The cartridge storage conditions, the calibration and quality control schedule, the electrode life, and the response to each alarm must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.

Troponin testing at the point of care is an immunoassay compressed into a cartridge or strip, and it exists because a cardiac marker is useful only if it is known while the patient is still in the emergency area. A quantitative device applies a measured volume of blood or plasma to a cartridge, lets a labeled antibody bind the cardiac troponin, captures the complex on a defined zone, and reads the signal by fluorescence or by an electrochemical or magnetic method, while a simpler qualitative strip produces only a line. The physical limits of the format are the source of its clinical limits. Sensitivity is lower than in a central laboratory, so a negative result early after the onset of pain does not exclude myocardial injury, and the test must be repeated at the interval the protocol defines while the patient is observed and an electrocardiogram, called ECG, is obtained. Cross-reaction, a very high concentration that overwhelms the capture, and poor temperature control all produce results that mislead. The decision cutoffs, the repeat interval, the storage temperature, and the handling of an invalid cartridge must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.

The coagulation meter that reports the international normalized ratio exists to manage anticoagulant therapy, and its physical principle is the detection of the moment blood turns from liquid to gel. A drop of capillary or venous whole blood is mixed with a thromboplastin reagent dried on a strip or held in a cuvette; the reagent activates the extrinsic clotting pathway, and the instrument detects the clot by a mechanical method that senses a small magnet or plunger, by an optical method that senses light scatter, or by an electrochemical method that senses the fall in current as the mixture solidifies. The time from the start of the reaction to the clot is the prothrombin time, and that time is divided by the mean normal prothrombin time of the reagent and raised to the power of the reagent's international sensitivity index, which yields the international normalized ratio and makes results comparable between laboratories and reagent lots. This arithmetic is why the reagent lot and the calibration matter so much, and why a meter and a strip must be treated as one system rather than interchangeable parts. The reagent sensitivity index, the calibration procedure, and the therapeutic range must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.

The errors that distort a coagulation meter are partly the patient's and partly the operator's. Hematocrit changes the viscosity of the sample and shifts the result, so a very anemic or very polycythemic patient may need a different method; a drop obtained by squeezing the finger is diluted with tissue fluid; a strip that has been warm, moist, or expired has lost its reagent; and a sample from a line flushed with heparin will be altered. The operator must keep the meter clean, since dried blood on the strip channel and around the optical window is a common cause of failed readings, and must run the control solutions at the interval the meter provides rather than only when a result looks wrong. A result outside the measuring range, or inconsistent with the patient's dose history and previous values, should trigger a venous sample and laboratory confirmation rather than a change in dose. The characteristic alarms are a strip error, a temperature out of range message, a low battery warning, and a result flagged as out of range. The control schedule, the hematocrit limits, the measuring range, and the alarm responses must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.

The urine dipstick is the oldest point-of-care test in continuous use, and it is a row of small reagent pads on a plastic strip, each pad carrying a different chemical system and each judged by a color. Glucose is detected by an enzyme and a dye that darken in proportion to the sugar; ketones by a nitroprusside reaction that turns purple; blood by the peroxidase like activity of hemoglobin, which produces a greenish blue color; protein by a dye that changes color when it binds albumin; nitrite by a diazotization that reveals nitrate reducing bacteria; leukocyte esterase by an enzyme reaction that reveals white cells; and bilirubin by a diazo coupling. The specific gravity pad works differently, using a polyelectrolyte whose apparent acidity changes with the concentration of ions. The color must be read at the exact interval the maker states, because the reactions continue and a pad read late deepens and overstates while a pad read too early understates. The reading interval, the lighting, and the interpretation of each pad must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.

Read by eye, the dipstick is cheap and everywhere, and it is also a device that punishes haste. The common operator errors are dipping the strip too long so that reagents from one pad run into another, dragging the strip along the rim of the container, reading the pads in dim or colored light, reading them at the wrong time, and comparing a wet pad against the chart on the bottle instead of blotting and holding it flat. A sample left standing at room temperature grows bacteria that consume glucose and raise the acidity, so an old specimen can be reported as sugar free when it was not, and a specimen contaminated by vaginal discharge or cleaning fluid gives a result unrelated to the urinary tract. A reflectance meter removes some lighting and timing variation and prints a record, which is a real advantage in a busy clinic, but the meter must itself be calibrated and cleaned, and urine on the optical window can damage it. The dip time, the reading window, the specimen age limit, and the meter's calibration schedule must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.

A hemoglobin meter answers a single question and does it almost anywhere, because anemia is common, blood donation programs depend on it, and a laboratory hemoglobin is not always available. The common hospital design takes a small volume of capillary or venous blood into a disposable cuvette that already contains a reagent, converts the hemoglobin to a stable colored form such as azide methemoglobin, and reads it photometrically through the cuvette wall; a second design measures whole blood by conductivity or photometry without dilution, and older color comparison scales still exist in field kits. The measurement depends on the blood being well mixed and the cuvette filled to its mark, because a short fill or a bubble in the light path changes the result, and the cuvette must be wiped clean on the outside but never the inside. The common errors are a drop from a squeezed finger, blood left to settle so that cells and plasma separate, a dirty or expired cuvette, and a meter read while it is still stabilizing. The sample volume, the cuvette storage temperature, the calibration interval, and the measuring range must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.

Connectivity turns a collection of small devices into a point-of-care system, and its purpose is that the result reaches the record with the right patient attached. A meter linked to the EHR through a docking station or a wireless network can transmit the value, the time, the operator identity, the device serial number, the strip or cartridge lot, and any quality control result that accompanied it, and that bundle is what allows a supervisor to audit the device later. When a meter cannot connect, the operator must write the result by hand, and handwriting is where identity errors are born, so the local protocol must define who transcribes, how the value is confirmed, and how the paper record is reconciled with the EHR. The data also feed the wider program, because transmission shows which meters produce results outside the expected range, which operators have not run controls, and which lot numbers are associated with failures. A result that arrives without an identity, or that is duplicated because a meter was docked twice, is a defect of the system rather than of the chemistry. The interface specification, the data fields, and the reconciliation procedure must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.

Quality control is what separates a measurement from a guess, and at the point of care it has three layers. The first is built into the device, namely the control line on a lateral flow strip, the electronic self check of a meter, the calibration verification a cartridge performs when inserted, and the internal control solutions some instruments run automatically. The second is the external control material the operator runs deliberately, at more than one level where the program provides it, at the start of a session, after a new lot or cartridge, after the meter is dropped, and whenever a result matters a great deal. The third is external quality assessment, in which an outside body sends samples with unknown values and compares the answers with those of many other sites, and it is the strongest available check because it examines the whole local system rather than one instrument. The honest discussion belongs to the test that is convenient and wrong. A device used under pressure, without controls, with a strip stored in a hot room, and with a result never compared against the patient's appearance or a laboratory value will produce numbers believed because they are printed, and a falsely reassuring result can delay treatment as surely as a false alarm can cause harm. The control frequency, the acceptance rules, the quality assessment schedule, and the response to a failed control must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.

Point-of-care devices are maintained by a routine that fits into the clinical day, and the routine matters more here than in a laboratory because the device travels. Every day the operator should confirm the battery or mains connection, inspect the strip channel and optical window for dried blood and dust, check that strips or cartridges are within expiry and have been stored properly, and verify that the session controls have passed before the first patient is tested. Every week the instrument usually needs its outer surfaces cleaned with the disinfectant the maker approves, since alcohol and bleach damage some housings and optical parts, and the battery contacts, printer, and docking station deserve a look. The consumables list is longer than the device list, and it includes strips, cartridges, cuvettes, lancets, capillary tubes, heparinized syringes, control solutions, calibration codes, printer paper, batteries, disinfectant wipes, sharps containers, and the temperature log for whatever refrigerator or cool box holds the reagents. Storage temperature is the quiet variable that decides whether a strip still works six weeks later, so the refrigerator temperature, typically between 2 and 8 degrees Celsius, must be recorded and watched. Every storage limit, cleaning agent, and replacement interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.

In a mobile clinic or a frontier health post, point-of-care testing is not an addition to the laboratory but the laboratory itself, and its limits must be named before its results are trusted. What the field position gives up is the menu, the throughput, the multiple levels of quality control, the refrigerated storage, the trained technologist, the service engineer, and very often the reliable electricity and connectivity that make a hospital program auditable. What it demands instead is discipline of a different kind. The device must be charged, usually from a solar panel or a vehicle, protected from dust and direct sun, and kept within its operating temperature range, which for many instruments is roughly 15 to 30 degrees Celsius; strips and cartridges must be carried in a sealed dry box and rotated so the oldest are used first; the operator must be trained and re assessed; and every result, control, error message, battery state, and storage temperature must be entered in a written log, because the log is the only substitute for the absent electronic record. The operating temperature range, the charging method, and the log fields must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.

Within those constraints the field bundle is usually small and chosen for robustness rather than range. It contains a handheld glucose meter with strips and control solution; lateral flow tests for the conditions that matter locally, which may include malaria, human immunodeficiency virus, syphilis, pregnancy, dengue, and streptococcal infection; a urine dipstick bottle with a color chart; a hemoglobin meter or a color scale; sometimes a cartridge blood gas and electrolyte analyzer or a small coagulation meter where a generator, a refrigerator, or a cold chain can be sustained; and the consumables that make them work, including lancets, alcohol pads, capillary tubes, buffer vials, a timer, a thermometer, a sharps box, and disinfectant. The limits of this bundle are physical and must be spoken plainly. A lateral flow test negative early in an illness may become positive later, so a single negative result cannot rule out disease; a glucose reading altered by anemia or dehydration may mislead; a cartridge analyzer stored above its allowed temperature may fail without warning; and a device that has been dropped, soaked, or heated may produce numbers that look entirely normal. The operator must know which tests the site can do well, which must be sent to a reference laboratory, and when clinical judgment must override a printed number, and the menu limits, cold chain requirements, and referral rules must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before any device is used on a patient.

The failure modes of point-of-care testing are worth naming as questions the operator can ask before every test: is the device calibrated or coded for the strip in use; is the strip or cartridge within expiry and has it been stored at the stated temperature and humidity; is the control solution in date and did it pass; is the sample the right kind, the right volume, and free of bubbles, clot, and dilution; is the patient identified by a wristband or record number rather than by assumption; has the reading been taken within the exact window; and is the result plausible against the patient in front of the clinician. The hazards that accompany these devices are equally concrete, and they include a sharps injury from a lancet or needle, a blood splash to the eye or mouth, a chemical burn from a reagent, an allergic reaction to a disinfectant, and the infectious waste that a used strip represents. All of these require personal protective equipment, called PPE, hand hygiene, and a waste stream that keeps used sharps and contaminated disposables out of ordinary refuse. The battery state, the strip storage conditions, the control schedule, and the waste rules must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient.

To summarize, point-of-care tests are the laboratory reduced until it fits in the hand of the person at the bedside, and each works by one plain physical principle the operator can picture. A glucose strip turns an enzyme reaction into a current; a lateral flow strip turns antibody binding into a colored line moved by capillary action; a blood gas analyzer turns the diffusion of gases and the selectivity of membranes into voltages and currents; a coagulation meter times the formation of a clot; a dipstick turns several small chemical reactions into colors; and a hemoglobin meter turns a blood pigment into absorbed light. The hospital version of these devices is embedded in a program of connectivity, training, control material, comparison with the central laboratory, and documented maintenance, while the field version trades that scaffolding for portability and must repay the loss with storage discipline, charging discipline, a written log, and an honest awareness of what it cannot measure. In both settings the governing rule is the same, because a convenient result is not the same as a correct one, and the value of a point-of-care test is decided less by the instrument than by the person who prepared the sample, respected the storage, ran the control, and asked whether the number matches the patient.
