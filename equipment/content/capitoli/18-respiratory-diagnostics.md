# Chapter 18 — Respiratory Diagnostics

Breathing looks effortless and is in fact a problem in fluid mechanics, and every instrument in this chapter is an attempt to measure one part of that problem. Air moves into the lung only because the pressure inside the chest falls slightly below the pressure in the room, and it moves out again because the elastic recoil of the lung raises the pressure above the room. The rate at which the gas travels is the flow, the amount that has passed is the volume, and the two are linked by a simple relationship, because flow is the rate of change of volume. The opposition to that movement is the resistance of the airways, and the stiffness of the lung and the chest wall is the compliance. Disease changes one or more of these numbers by narrowing the tubes, stiffening the tissue, weakening the muscles, or destroying the surface where gas exchange happens. A respiratory diagnostic device does not look for a disease by name; it measures pressure, flow, volume, or the concentration of a gas, and the clinician reads the pattern. Understanding which of these the instrument actually senses is what separates a trustworthy result from a plausible-looking number.

The spirometer is the central instrument of lung function testing, and the first thing to understand is that most modern spirometers are flow meters that calculate volume rather than volume collectors that measure it directly. A pneumotachograph places a small, known resistance in the path of the breath and measures the tiny pressure difference that develops across it. When the flow is smooth and orderly the pressure difference is proportional to flow, a condition called laminar flow, and the instrument can then be calibrated with a known volume of air pushed through it by a syringe. A screen of fine mesh, a bundle of parallel tubes, or a small hole can all serve as that resistance, and each behaves predictably only within a limited range of flows. A turbine or vane spirometer instead lets the breath spin a light rotor and counts the rotations, while an ultrasonic spirometer measures the time taken by pulses of sound to cross the moving stream. All of these approaches answer the same question in different ways, and each has a characteristic weakness that the operator must know.

A complete spirometer is a chain of parts, and every link can degrade the result. At the patient end there is a mouthpiece, usually a disposable rigid tube or a flanged cardboard type, and between the mouthpiece and the sensor sits a filter that traps particles and microorganisms. The filter adds a small resistance and a small dead space, and a wet, clogged, or wrongly seated filter changes both, so the filter specified by the maker must be used and the resistance it adds must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Beyond the sensor there is the tubing, which must be short, wide, and free of kinks, because a long narrow hose distorts the flow signal and encourages water to collect. The console holds the pressure transducer, the amplifier, and the processor, together with sensors for room temperature, humidity, and barometric pressure. A calibration syringe of exactly 3 liters is the standard tool for checking the sensor, a nominal value that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and it must be stored where its plunger seal cannot dry out or crack. Consumables include mouthpieces, filters, nose clips, and the disinfectant for the sensor head, and a spirometer without them will be idle within a week.

The maneuver itself is the part of spirometry that depends most on the patient and the coach, and a poor maneuver produces a wrong diagnosis rather than a missing one. The patient sits upright, applies a nose clip, seals the lips tightly around the mouthpiece, breathes in fully to total lung capacity, and then blasts the air out as hard and as long as possible until no more will come. The effort must be maximal from the opening instant, because the volume expelled in the first second is the number most often used, and a slow start understates it badly. The patient then breathes in fully again so that the maneuver can be repeated, and the series is performed at least 3 times until the best 2 efforts agree closely, a requirement that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A bronchodilator may then be given and the test repeated after a fixed interval, so that a change in the numbers can be attributed to the drug, and the interval and the dose must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The coach must demonstrate, encourage, and watch the screen rather than the patient's face, because the shape of the curve tells immediately whether the effort was acceptable. A leak at the lips, an extra breath in the middle, or a tongue partly blocking the mouthpiece all spoil the effort, and the quality messages on the display should be read rather than dismissed.

Quality criteria exist because a spirometer will happily report a number from a worthless effort, and the criteria are mathematical rather than a matter of opinion. The back-extrapolated volume, found by extending the steepest part of the curve back to the start, shows how much air escaped before the blast began, and a large value means a hesitant start. The effort must last long enough for a plateau to appear, otherwise the patient simply stopped early and the total is falsely low. Repeatability means that the best 2 acceptable efforts differ by only a small margin, and the acceptable margin must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The great hazard of the procedure is infection, because a forced exhalation turns whatever is in the airway into a fine mist that reaches the sensor, the tubing, and the room, so a filter and careful hand hygiene are not optional. The common operator error is to accept the first effort because it looks reasonable, and the second common error is to mistake a leaking mouth seal for genuine obstruction. A spirometer signals its displeasure with prompts such as blow harder, blow longer, or a message that the effort was not acceptable, and the quality limits and alarm thresholds must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The peak flow meter is the simplest respiratory diagnostic instrument in existence, and its principle is a variable opening. As the patient blows through the device, a piston, a vane, or a small flap is pushed against a spring or along a calibrated slot, and the distance it travels depends on the greatest flow reached during the blast. The reading is held by a ratchet or a marker until it is reset, so the patient and the clinician can both see it, and the scale is usually printed in liters per minute with numbered bands alongside. The device measures only one point, the highest flow achieved, and it says nothing about the total volume or the shape of the effort, which is both its strength and its limitation. Because the measurement depends on effort and technique, the patient must stand or sit upright, take the deepest possible breath, seal the lips, and blow as hard and as briefly as possible, and the best of 3 attempts is recorded, a convention that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Each patient should have a personal best, established while well, so that later readings can be expressed as a fraction of it rather than compared with a stranger's prediction. The common operator error is to read the marker after the patient has already lowered the device, or to let the patient blow with the tongue partly in the way, and both produce a number that is low for the wrong reason.

In asthma care the peak flow meter earns its place by being cheap, portable, and repeatable in the patient's own hands, and by turning a symptom into a number that can be followed over time. A fall in the peak flow often begins hours or days before the patient feels breathless, so a daily morning reading can warn of deteriorating control while there is still time to act. Action plans therefore divide the personal best into zones, most often a green zone of normal function, a yellow zone of caution, and a red zone of danger, and the boundaries and the instructions attached to each must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. During an acute attack the peak flow helps to grade severity and to show whether treatment is working, and a value that fails to improve after repeated bronchodilator doses is a strong argument for transfer to a higher level of care. The hazard is a false sense of security, because a patient who cannot perform the maneuver properly, or who uses a meter clogged with dust, may record a low value while well or a falsely acceptable value while ill. Meters must be washed or wiped according to the instructions supplied with them, allowed to dry completely, and compared with a second meter when results seem inconsistent, and a cracked or sticky meter must be discarded rather than repaired. The characteristic failure is silent rather than audible, because there is no alarm, only a scale that sticks, which is why the operator must test the device before it is trusted.

Pulse oximetry was introduced as a monitor, but in respiratory diagnostics it becomes a measuring instrument, and its physics must be understood before its numbers are believed. The sensor shines light of 2 wavelengths, one red and one infrared, through a translucent part of the body, usually a fingertip, and a detector on the other side measures what survives the journey. Oxygenated and deoxygenated hemoglobin absorb those wavelengths differently, and the ratio of the absorbed amounts is converted into a percentage saturation. The device separates the pulsatile part of the signal, which comes from arterial blood arriving with each heartbeat, from the steady part, which comes from skin, bone, and venous blood, and that separation is why a good pulse waveform is required for a good reading. This is also why the technique fails in the cold, in shock, with movement, with dark nail polish, and with a badly placed sensor, because in each case there is too little pulsatile signal for the arithmetic to work. The reading is a saturation and not a content, so a patient with severe anemia can show a normal percentage while carrying far too little oxygen in absolute terms. It also cannot distinguish oxygen from carbon monoxide, because the 2 forms of hemoglobin look alike to the 2 wavelengths, so a survivor of a fire may show a reassuring number while being poisoned. The characteristic alarm is the low saturation tone or message, and the threshold that triggers it must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The walk test turns oximetry from a snapshot into a diagnostic procedure by adding exertion, and it is one of the most useful low-technology tests in respiratory medicine. The standard version asks the patient to walk as far as possible along a measured corridor for 6 minutes, and the distance covered, the lowest saturation reached, the heart rate, and the symptoms are all recorded, with the exact duration and the stopping rules confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A fall in saturation during walking, particularly below the threshold set by the local protocol, which must itself be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, identifies a patient who desaturates with activity and who may benefit from oxygen away from the bed. A simpler version, sometimes called the step test or the shuttle walk, is used where there is no corridor, and it still reproduces the essential feature, which is that the measurement is taken while the patient works rather than while the patient rests. A continuous recording during the walk is more informative than a single reading at the end, because the lowest value often occurs during the effort and recovers within seconds of stopping. The common operator error is to let the patient stop at the first sign of breathlessness, which conceals the very desaturation that the test was designed to find, or to hold the sensor still with a hand, which removes the pulse and blanks the display. The operator must also record whether the patient used oxygen during the walk, because a test performed on oxygen answers a different question from one performed on room air.

Blood gas analysis measures the pressures and the acidity of arterial blood directly, and it does so with electrodes rather than with light. The sample is drawn into a syringe, introduced into a small chamber, and brought into contact with a series of sensors, each of which produces a tiny electrical signal in proportion to the quantity it senses. The acidity is measured with a glass electrode, a thin membrane of special glass across which hydrogen ions distribute themselves and create a voltage that the instrument compares with a stable reference. The partial pressure of carbon dioxide is measured with a modified glass electrode covered by a membrane that lets the gas diffuse into a thin film of bicarbonate solution, where it changes the acidity, and that change is read as a voltage. The partial pressure of oxygen is measured with a different electrode, in which oxygen diffuses through a membrane and is consumed at a small electrode, producing a current proportional to the amount arriving. Newer analyzers use fluorescent dyes printed on a disposable cartridge, where the presence of a gas or an ion changes the light the dye emits, and optics replace the wet electrodes.

A blood gas analyzer is only as good as its calibration, its quality control, and the sample that reaches it. The instrument holds a set of calibration gases and buffer solutions, and it performs a full calibration at intervals, with a shorter calibration before each sample, and the composition, the values, and the schedule must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Control material with known values is run at fixed intervals and after every maintenance visit, and the results are charted, because an analyzer can drift slowly and still appear to work. The sample syringe is itself a critical consumable: it must contain a small amount of an anticoagulant, usually a salt of heparin, it must be sealed against air, and it must be mixed by gentle rolling rather than shaking. Air bubbles are the classic error, because a bubble exchanges gas with the blood and falsely raises the measured oxygen pressure while lowering the carbon dioxide pressure, and a bubble left in a syringe for a few minutes can change the interpretation entirely. The sample must be analyzed quickly, and the acceptable delay and the acceptable temperature must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, because the cells in the blood continue to consume oxygen and produce carbon dioxide after the sample is drawn. The operator must also confirm that the sample is arterial, which is judged from the color and the pulsatile flow at the time of puncture and from the plausibility checks built into the analyzer, since a venous sample sent as arterial is a dangerous misdiagnosis waiting to happen.

Maintenance of a blood gas analyzer is a discipline of membranes, fluids, and cleanliness, and its failures are usually gradual rather than sudden. The electrode membranes are thin films that can dry out, crack, or become coated with protein, and each of these faults produces a drifting or sluggish reading rather than an obvious breakdown. The reference electrode and the internal filling solutions must be replenished on schedule, and the tubing, the clot traps, and the sample path must be flushed and cleaned to remove fibrin and blood cells, because a partial blockage can produce a falsely low oxygen reading and erratic results. The instrument must be kept at a stable temperature, because the electrodes are sensitive to it and the sample is warmed to the electrode temperature during analysis, and the set temperature must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Waste fluid and rinse solutions must be emptied and replaced, and the used syringes and needles must be handled as infectious material. The characteristic alarms include a calibration failure, an electrode drift or slope error, a sample error when the analyzer detects a clot or too little blood, a low reagent warning, and a temperature warning, and every threshold must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The common operator error is to keep reporting results after a calibration flag has appeared, or to override a quality control failure because the sample, in the operator's judgment, is urgent. A result from an analyzer whose control material is failing is not a result at all, and the correct response is to suspend testing, use a backup analyzer or send the sample onward, and write the event in the log.

Exhaled carbon dioxide measurement sits between monitoring and diagnosis, and it deserves attention here because it answers questions that oximetry cannot. The usual instrument is an infrared analyzer, which passes a beam of infrared light through a small chamber filled with the patient's breath and measures how much of one particular wavelength is absorbed by carbon dioxide molecules, because the absorption is proportional to the number of molecules in the path. The sample may be drawn through a thin tube to a remote chamber, or the chamber may sit directly in the breathing circuit, and a filter and a water trap protect the optics from mucus and condensation. The result is displayed as a waveform and as a number, the end-tidal value, which is the concentration at the very end of exhalation when the gas has come from the deepest part of the lung. The difference between that value and the arterial partial pressure of carbon dioxide is normally small, and a widening gap suggests that some of the exhaled gas is coming from parts of the lung that are not exchanging gas well, which is a ventilation problem rather than a metabolic one. A flat waveform, a sudden fall, an unexpected rise, or the loss of the characteristic shape all carry meaning, and a colorimetric detector that changes color on contact with the gas is the simplest field version. In diagnostics the measurement is used to confirm that a breathing tube is in the airway, to follow the severity of an asthma attack, and to judge the response to treatment, and the normal range and the alarm thresholds must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Bronchoscopy is the invasive end of respiratory diagnostics, and it exists because some questions can only be answered by looking inside the airway and taking a piece of it. The flexible instrument is a long tube built around bundles of glass fibers, and it works by total internal reflection, because light travelling along a fiber strikes the boundary at a shallow angle and is reflected back into the fiber rather than escaping, so an image can be carried around a curve without lenses along the way. One bundle carries light from the console to illuminate the airway, and a second bundle, with its fibers arranged in the same order at both ends, carries the image back to the eyepiece or the camera. A separate channel runs the length of the scope for suction, for the passage of instruments, and for the instillation of fluid, and a set of wires at the tip allows the operator to bend the end in one or 2 directions. The rigid instrument is a hollow metal tube with a lens and a light source, and it gives a wider view and a larger working channel at the cost of requiring deep anesthesia and an extended neck. The consumables are numerous, including biopsy forceps, cytology brushes, needles for sampling through the airway wall, grasping tools, guide wires, specimen containers, and a suction trap. The field variant of this instrument barely exists, and its absence is one of the sharpest limits on what can be diagnosed far from a referral hospital.

The hazards of bronchoscopy are immediate and must be managed during the procedure rather than after it. Bleeding can follow a biopsy, particularly in a patient with a clotting problem, and the operator must be ready to instill a cold solution, to suction, and to press on the area, with the exact agents and the protocol confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Spasm of the vocal cords and coughing are provoked by the instrument touching them, and the oxygen saturation commonly falls because the scope occupies part of the airway and suction removes oxygen along with secretions. Monitoring of saturation, heart rate, and blood pressure, and the presence of resuscitation equipment, are part of the procedure and not a separate concern. The characteristic alarm is the falling saturation tone or the low saturation message on the monitor, and it must be treated as an instruction to withdraw the scope, suction briefly, and give oxygen, not as a signal to continue and watch. The common operator error is to advance the scope against resistance, which can perforate the airway, or to use suction continuously, which collapses the airway, damages the lining, and provokes bleeding. Cleaning is a multistep process: bedside pre-cleaning of the insertion tube, leak testing before immersion, manual brushing and flushing of every channel, enzymatic cleaning, rinsing, high-level disinfection or sterilization, drying, and vertical storage, and the concentrations, the contact times, and the temperatures must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A leak test is the single most valuable step in that sequence, because fluid entering a damaged scope destroys the optical bundle, and a scope that fails its leak test must be removed from service at once.

In a mobile or frontier clinic the respiratory diagnostic toolkit shrinks to almost nothing, and the clinician must decide what to carry and what to do without. A peak flow meter, a pulse oximeter, a watch, a measuring tape for the walk test, and a supply of mouthpieces and filters weigh little and cost little, and together they cover a surprising amount of respiratory assessment. What disappears is the blood gas analyzer, the spirometer with its calibration syringe, the exhaled carbon dioxide monitor, and the bronchoscope, each of which needs electricity, reagents, calibration gases, trained maintenance, and a patient volume large enough to keep it honest. The respiratory rate counted over a full minute, the use of the muscles of the neck and ribs, the ability to speak in whole sentences, the position the patient chooses, the color of the lips, the presence of sweating, and the response to a trial of oxygen all carry information that a machine would otherwise supply. Because these signs are judged by the observer, they must be recorded at each visit in the same way, so that change over time, rather than any single reading, drives the decision. Where an arterial blood gas is impossible, the clinician reasons from the pattern: a patient who is breathless at rest, who cannot finish a sentence, or whose saturation falls with the slightest effort is treated as seriously ill and moved onward, while a patient whose saturation is stable and whose work of breathing is normal can be observed and treated locally. The absence of a number is a real loss, and the honest response is to say so and to transfer the patient when the clinical picture is uncertain.

Whatever survives in the field must be maintained with the same seriousness as a hospital instrument, and the checks are simple enough to be done on every clinic day. The pulse oximeter needs clean and undamaged sensors, a battery that holds its charge, and a check that the displayed pulse matches the pulse felt by hand, and a device whose readings cannot be reproduced must be taken out of use. The peak flow meter needs a free-moving piston or vane, a clean mouthpiece area, and a periodic comparison with a second meter, and it must be stored where it cannot be crushed or filled with dust. A portable spirometer with a disposable flow sensor needs fresh sensors, a charged battery, a check with a known volume, and a clean and dry sensor port, and its results must be read only when the quality message or the curve shows an acceptable effort. Consumables must be counted before the clinic, because a missing mouthpiece or a dead battery turns a diagnostic visit into a wasted journey, and the stock must be rotated so that expired filters and sensors are not carried for months. The operator must also know the failures that mimic disease, because a battery losing power can make a spirometer under-report, a dirty sensor port can make it over-report, and a cold finger can make an oximeter under-report, and each of these errors sends the clinical reasoning in the wrong direction. Every device should carry its own log of checks, faults, and repairs, and any instrument that fails a check must be labeled and set aside rather than used with an explanation. Where no spirometer is available at all, the peak flow meter and a careful bronchodilator trial remain the substitute, and the change in peak flow after treatment is the most useful single piece of evidence the clinic can generate.

To summarize, respiratory diagnostics begins with the simple physics of pressure, flow, and volume, and each instrument in this chapter senses one of these quantities and converts it into a number. The spirometer measures flow across a known resistance and adds it up over time to obtain volume, and its value depends entirely on a maximal effort that meets defined quality criteria. The peak flow meter measures one point of that effort against a spring or an opening, and it is most useful as a personal baseline tracked over time rather than as a single reading. Pulse oximetry uses 2 wavelengths of light and the pulsatile signal to estimate saturation, and the walk test extends it into a diagnostic procedure by adding exertion. Blood gas analyzers use electrodes or fluorescent dyes to measure acidity and the partial pressures of oxygen and carbon dioxide directly, and they are only as reliable as their calibration, their quality control, and the care taken with the sample. Exhaled carbon dioxide measurement adds a view of ventilation and of the shape of the breath, and bronchoscopy adds direct vision and tissue at the cost of sedation, bleeding, infection, and a demanding cleaning routine. In the field the toolkit contracts to a peak flow meter and an oximeter, and clinical reasoning must take over from the missing laboratory, with serial observation, a measured walk, and the honest recognition of uncertainty guiding the decision to treat locally or to transfer.
