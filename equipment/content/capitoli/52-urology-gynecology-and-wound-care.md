# Chapter 52 — Urology, Gynecology, and Wound Care

This chapter gathers three fields that share a physics before they share a corridor. Urology and gynecology both work through natural openings into cavities that are normally collapsed, so both depend on instruments that distend a soft-walled space enough for a lens and a tool to work inside it. Wound care works in the other direction, on a cavity that is kept open to drain while its surface is sealed against the air, and its central machine controls pressure across a film. In all three the outcome depends on lumen size, applied pressure, and the cleanliness of a long narrow channel.

The urinary tract is plumbing with a chemical factory at one end and a valve at the other. Each kidney makes urine continuously, and the urine leaves through a funnel called the renal pelvis and then through the ureter, a muscular tube about 25 centimeters long that pushes it downward in small waves. That tube is narrowest at three points, where it leaves the kidney, where it crosses the pelvic blood vessels, and where it enters the bladder, so a stone usually lodges at one of them. The bladder stores urine at low pressure and empties by contraction, and the urethra is the outlet. When the path is blocked, pressure rises behind the blockage and travels back to the kidney, which is why so much urological equipment exists simply to relieve pressure, and the catheter size and drainage technique must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Most instruments in this field are measured on the French scale, and the scale is worth understanding rather than memorizing. A single French unit equals one third of a millimeter of external diameter, so 18 French is about 6 millimeters across and 30 French is 10 millimeters across. A change of 2 French is therefore a real change in the bore, and a tube that passes easily at one size may tear the lining at the next, because the urethral wall is thin and its blood supply is easily compressed. A catheter that is too small drains poorly, and one that is too large presses on the lining and can cause a stricture months later. The size chosen and the balloon volume that comes with it must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The cystoscope is the eye of urology, and its shape follows from the fact that the urethra is a curved tunnel with no view of its own walls. A rigid instrument is a metal tube carrying a lens system and a light, and it is passed with the patient's legs supported and the urethra well lubricated. Beside the telescope runs a working channel for a catheter, a wire, or a small forceps, and a separate channel admits irrigation so the bladder can be filled and blood washed away until the lining is visible. A flexible instrument replaces the rigid lenses with a bundle of fine glass fibers or a chip camera at the tip, so it follows the curve of the urethra without leverage. The diameter, the angle of view, and the irrigation flow must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A rigid cystoscope is assembled from parts that must match exactly, and a mismatch is the first thing to check. The sheath is the outer tube, with a blunt obturator filling its lumen during insertion so that no sharp edge can catch the lining, and a seal at the near end stops irrigation running over the operator's hands. Forcing the instrument when resistance is felt can create a false passage or perforate the bladder, and over-distension causes pain, bleeding, and fainting. Every pass of the instrument carries bacteria upward, so disinfection and technique are the real infection control. The over-temperature alarm of the light source is the characteristic alarm of the system, and the light output, the temperature limit, the sheath size, and the alarm behavior must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The resectoscope is a cystoscope given a cutting loop, and it removes tissue from inside the bladder or the prostate by sliding a wire loop across the surface while a high-frequency current passes through it. The current concentrates at the thin wire and raises the temperature of the tissue in contact with it until the cells separate, and a lower current density produces coagulation instead of division. The loop sits on a spring carriage worked by the thumb, so the cut is a controlled sliding movement, and the speed of that movement decides whether the surface is clean or charred. A continuous flow sheath has inner and outer tubes so irrigation enters through one and leaves through the other, keeping the view clear while bleeding continues. The loop size, the current, and the irrigation rate must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The irrigation fluid is a safety decision, because the choice follows from the electrical circuit. A monopolar loop needs a non-conducting solution such as glycine, sorbitol, or mannitol, and those dilute fluids behave like water when they are absorbed; a bipolar loop keeps the current between two electrodes at its tip, so ordinary salt water is safe. Absorption happens because the open veins of the resected surface are bathed in fluid under pressure, and a large absorbed volume of a dilute solution lowers the blood sodium and can cause confusion, nausea, visual disturbance, seizures, and death, a pattern known as transurethral resection syndrome. A monopolar generator also supervises the large return pad on the patient's thigh, and it sounds a return electrode alarm when the contact is poor, which is a warning of a skin burn rather than a nuisance. The fluid, the resection time, the pressure, the pad position, and the alarm behavior must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The urethrotome treats a stricture, which is a scar that narrows the urethra into a tight ring. The instrument places a blade at the narrow point and draws it through the scar in controlled cuts, so the ring is opened and the channel widened; a laser fiber or a knife under direct vision does the same work another way. A blind instrument carries a graduated scale along its shaft, so the operator reads how deep the blade has advanced instead of judging by feel. Cutting too deeply opens the erectile tissue around the urethra and causes severe bleeding or a fistula, while cutting too little lets the stricture narrow again. This is a purely mechanical device with no alarm of its own, so the only warnings are resistance and blood, and the blade depth, the instrument size, and the intended incision length must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

An extracorporeal lithotripter breaks a stone with a shock wave made outside the body and focused on the stone. Most machines make that wave by discharging a spark across a gap under water, by driving a metal plate with a magnetic pulse, or by contracting a piezoelectric array, and most focus it with an ellipsoid reflector that has two focal points. The patient is positioned so the stone sits at the second focus, and the energy is concentrated there while the tissue along the path is largely spared. Some machines time each shock to the heart rhythm so that it avoids a vulnerable phase of the cardiac cycle. The number of shocks, the energy level, and the coupling of the water cushion to the skin must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Intracorporeal lithotripsy carries the energy to the stone through a probe or a fiber. A pneumatic probe fires a small projectile at the stone, an ultrasonic probe vibrates it apart and sucks the fragments away, and a laser fiber delivers light that water and stone absorb, creating a tiny plasma bubble that drills and cracks the stone. The laser is the most versatile, because a thin flexible fiber reaches almost any stone, and its energy is set in joules per pulse and its rate in pulses per second, so a typical setting might be 1 joule at 10 hertz, a value that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The hazards are a fiber fired against the ureter wall, a fragment driven into tissue, and a laser triggered outside the field of view. A lithotripter also reports coupling, water, and calibration faults, and it usually refuses to fire rather than fire at the wrong energy; the common error is to raise the energy instead of repositioning the fiber.

Urodynamic equipment asks not what a structure looks like but how it behaves while it fills and empties. Two fine catheters, one in the bladder and one in the rectum or vagina, each connect by a column of fluid to an external pressure sensor, and the machine records bladder pressure and abdominal pressure against time. Because the two pressures are recorded together, the pressure made by the bladder muscle itself can be found by subtracting the abdominal pressure from the bladder pressure. A pump fills the bladder at a controlled rate, a flowmeter weighs the urine as it leaves, and surface electrodes record the electrical activity of the pelvic floor. An air bubble flattens the trace, a catheter tip against the wall records a false pressure, and a sensor not zeroed at the level of the pubic bone drifts, so the filling rate, the pressure limit, the calibration, and the alarm behavior must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The urinary catheter is the most common device in this field, and its physics explain both its usefulness and its dangers. A Foley catheter is a flexible tube with two channels, one draining urine and one inflating a small balloon near the tip with sterile water, so the balloon holds it in the bladder. A three-way catheter adds a channel for irrigation fluid, which is used after bladder surgery or when clots must be washed out, and a coudé catheter has a curved tip that can be steered past an enlarged prostate. Catheters are made of latex, silicone, or polyvinyl chloride, and the material decides how stiff the tube is, how long it may stay in place, and whether a patient with a latex allergy can tolerate it. The size, the material, the balloon volume, and the intended dwell time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The balloon must never be inflated until urine returns, because a balloon filled inside the urethra tears the lining and creates a false passage. Lubricant reduces friction and pain, and in a man the penis is lifted to remove the curve of the urethra before steady gentle pressure is applied; pushing against resistance is the common error that causes the injury. The drainage bag hangs below the bladder so urine runs downhill and never flows back, and a dependent loop of tubing where stagnant urine collects is a defect to correct, not a detail of tidiness. Every day an indwelling catheter remains raises the chance of a catheter-associated urinary tract infection, so it is removed as soon as it is no longer needed and the closed circuit is never opened to take a sample. An electronic urine meter may carry a full-bag alarm, and its alarm volume, bag capacity, and emptying interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

When the ureter itself is blocked and a catheter cannot pass, pressure is relieved with a tube placed directly into the kidney. A fine needle is guided into the collecting system under ultrasound or X-ray, a guidewire is threaded through it, and a pigtail catheter is passed over the wire and coiled in the renal pelvis, where a locking thread keeps it from falling out. A ureteric stent takes the other route, running inside the ureter from kidney to bladder with two curled ends that give it the name double J. Both are quiet devices whose failures are noticed by their consequences: a blocked or dislodged nephrostomy stops draining and the kidney distends, and a stent left in place for many months becomes encrusted and may break when it is finally removed. The diameter, the length, the fixation, and the planned removal date must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and every stent belongs in a register.

The urological equipment of a frontier clinic is chosen by what can be cleaned without a machine and repaired with ordinary tools. A rigid cystoscope with a battery handle, a set of metal sounds, catheters in every size, a manual irrigation syringe, and a supply of sterile water and gel carry most of the urgent work, which is the relief of retention and the drainage of an obstructed kidney. Stones are managed with analgesia and referral unless a portable lithotripter is available, because the machine is heavy and needs power, water, and a trained operator for every session. The clinic gives up diagnostic endoscopy and gives up urodynamics entirely, and in exchange it demands a stricter rule about which patients are kept and which are sent onward. What is never improvised is prolonged irrigation with tap water into an open bladder or prostate, because the fluid is absorbed and the electrolyte disturbance can kill; the sterilizer cycle and the chemical disinfectant contact time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Gynecology begins with the speculum, the simplest and most misunderstood instrument in the specialty. Its purpose is to hold the vaginal walls apart so the cervix can be seen, and it does this with two blades that open like a beak when the handle is squeezed, held by a screw or a ratchet so the operator's hands are free. Blades come in different lengths and widths, from a narrow instrument for a postmenopausal patient to a wide one for a woman who has delivered, and the choice follows from what the examination requires. A metal speculum is reusable and sterilized between patients, while a plastic one is single use, which matters where reprocessing is unreliable. The size, the material, and the sterilization or disposal method must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and a speculum that is cold, dry, or roughly inserted is remembered for years.

The speculum has no gauge and no alarm, so all of its safety lies in technique. It is inserted closed and angled along the axis of the vagina, and it is opened only after it is in place, because opening it early catches the vulva and the urethra; the screw is then set so the blades cannot close on the cervix. A lubricant is used when it is appropriate, but not when a cervical sample is being taken, because the gel dilutes the specimen. The colposcope goes further by magnifying the cervix under a strong light, using two eyepieces on a long arm to give a three-dimensional view from 20 to 30 centimeters away, and a green filter that makes the blood vessel pattern stand out. Dilute acetic acid turns abnormal tissue white, and iodine stains mature tissue dark while leaving abnormal areas unstained; the magnification, the light level, the acetic acid concentration, and the reading time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A colposcope is an optical instrument on a movable stand in a room used for many purposes, which is exactly the combination that damages it. Its lenses are cleaned only with a soft cloth and a solution the maker permits, its joints and counterbalance are checked, and a spare lamp is kept on hand because the usual warning of lamp failure is a dimming image rather than a sudden blackout. The common operator error is to mistake normal immature tissue that whitens with acetic acid for a lesion, or to examine without enough light and magnification and record a confident normal finding. Tissue is taken whenever the view is abnormal, using a punch forceps with two sharp opposed jaws, or a thin spoon for the endocervical canal, and the physical action must be a clean shear rather than a tear because a dull instrument crushes the specimen beyond interpretation. The sampling site, the fixative, and the label on each pot must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The hazard of cervical biopsy is bleeding, because the cervix has a generous blood supply. Pressure from a swab usually stops it, with a silver nitrate stick or a cauterizing paste ready, and a suture or a vaginal pack is the next step; a dark discharge and light bleeding for a few days are normal, while fever and heavy bleeding are not. The hysteroscope looks into a cavity whose walls touch one another, so its central problem is distension rather than view. A sheath carries fluid in and lets it out, and the pressure of that fluid holds the cavity open; salt water is used for a bipolar instrument, while a monopolar electrode needs a non-conducting medium that behaves like water if it is absorbed. The distension medium, the inflow pressure, the flow rate, and the maximum fluid deficit must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The most serious complication of hysteroscopy is an absorbed fluid load, because the uterus has an open venous drainage that accepts distension fluid under pressure. A large absorbed volume of a dilute non-conducting medium lowers the blood sodium and can cause confusion, nausea, visual disturbance, and seizures, while a large absorbed volume of salt water overloads the circulation and fills the lungs. The defense is to count what goes in and what comes out continuously, to keep the inflow pressure as low as the view allows, and to stop when the deficit reaches the limit set for that patient, because a woman with heart disease tolerates far less than a healthy one. The distension pump carries a fluid deficit alarm and an over-pressure alarm, and both are stop signals; the inflow pressure, the deficit limit, the flow rate, and the alarm behavior must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The loop electrosurgical excision procedure removes the abnormal transformation zone with a thin wire loop carrying a high-frequency current, and its value lies in the control the wire gives over depth and shape. A loop is chosen so that one pass removes the lesion and a margin of normal tissue while leaving a specimen the pathologist can read. The current is a blend of cutting and coagulation, with a low voltage and a high current density at the wire, so the tissue divides with little thermal damage at the edge; too slow a pass cooks the specimen and too fast a pass cuts without sealing the small vessels. The generator also supervises the return pad on the thigh and sounds a pad alarm if the contact becomes poor, and the smoke is removed by an evacuator close to the field because it carries fine particles and chemicals. The power, the loop size, the pad position, the smoke evacuation, and the alarm behavior must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Cryotherapy destroys tissue by freezing it, and the mechanism is the formation of ice inside and between the cells. A probe cooled by a gas that expands through a small nozzle, usually nitrous oxide or carbon dioxide, is pressed against the cervix, and the expanding gas absorbs heat from the metal tip through the Joule-Thomson effect. The ice ball ruptures cell membranes and stops the small vessels, and the cycle is repeated as a freeze, a thaw, and a second freeze, because the second freeze kills more cells than the first. Thermal ablation reaches a similar result with a probe held at about 100 degrees Celsius, and its temperature, contact time, and probe size must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Both methods destroy tissue in place, so they are used only for a small, fully visible lesion confirmed non-invasive by biopsy, and never when cancer is suspected.

The gynecological field kit is built around the eye and the hand rather than around electricity. A set of specula, a good lamp, dilute acetic acid, and a trained examiner will find most visible cervical lesions, and the technique of inspection after acetic acid is deliberately taught because it needs no machine at all. A battery-powered colposcope or a magnifying loupe improves the view, and a portable thermal coagulator running from a battery or a small inverter can treat a lesion in a clinic with no reliable mains supply. The clinic gives up cytology, histology, and the precise excision a loop provides, and in exchange it demands a rigid referral rule for anything that looks invasive, bleeds on contact, or extends into the canal. The disinfectant concentration, the contact time, and the sterilizer cycle must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Wound care rests on a principle that is physiological rather than mechanical, because a wound heals best in a moist, warm, clean environment. Cells migrate across a moist surface, while a dry scab forces them to travel underneath it, and a wound allowed to cool and dry loses the enzymes that drive repair. Modern dressings therefore control moisture rather than dry the wound out, and the choice follows from how much fluid the wound produces and whether the surrounding skin can tolerate being wet. Clean water or salt water at low pressure removes loose debris, and the force of the irrigation matters more than the solution, because a high-pressure jet drives bacteria into the tissue while a gentle flow washes them away. The irrigation pressure, the solution, and the dressing choice must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The instruments of wound care are simple and mostly about seeing and cleaning. A probe measures the depth and direction of a tract, forceps remove loose debris, and a scalpel or curette takes away dead tissue, with good light and a measuring tape as important as the blade. Sharp debridement is a controlled surgical act and is never done blindly in a wound over a vessel, a tendon, or a joint, and the dressing that follows must not macerate the surrounding skin or press on a bony prominence. The wound is measured and described at each visit, with its size, depth, base, edges, and the state of the surrounding skin, because that record shows whether the treatment is working. The common error is to leave a dressing in place because it looks clean while the wound underneath deteriorates, so the dressing is lifted and the wound inspected on the schedule the protocol sets; the cleaning agents, the debridement technique, and the dressing interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Negative pressure wound therapy is the most technical device in wound care, and it turns an open wound into a closed system under a controlled subatmospheric pressure. The wound is filled with an open-celled foam, covered with a semipermeable film that sticks to the surrounding skin, and joined by a tube to a pump that removes air and fluid; the film lets water vapor escape while keeping air and bacteria out, so the seal is what makes the system work. The usual range is a negative pressure of 75 to 125 millimeters of mercury, a value that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The pressure gradient draws the wound edges together, pulls fluid from the tissue, and increases blood flow to the wound bed, while the mechanical deformation of the cells appears to stimulate granulation tissue. The foam also gives the new tissue a scaffold, which is why the choice of foam is not arbitrary.

The system has four parts, and each can fail in its own way. The foam is either a black open-pored polyurethane sponge with pores between roughly 400 and 600 micrometers, used for most granulating wounds, or a white denser polyvinyl alcohol sponge with smaller pores, used where the wound is painful or the tissue fragile; the pore size and material must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The film must overlap the skin by several centimeters on every side and must not be stretched across a crease or a joint, because a wrinkle becomes a leak. The tubing carries fluid to a canister that must be seated correctly, and the pump runs in continuous or intermittent mode with set cycle times, carrying a leak alarm, a canister full alarm, a blockage alarm, and a low battery alarm, each naming a different physical fault. The pressure, the mode, the cycle times, the canister size, and the alarm thresholds must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The most serious hazard of negative pressure wound therapy is hemorrhage, because the negative pressure can erode a blood vessel in the wound bed, and a patient taking an anticoagulant can bleed catastrophically into the dressing. Foam is never placed in direct contact with an exposed large vessel, a graft, or a fresh anastomosis, and a bleeding wound is an emergency rather than a dressing problem: the pressure is stopped, the dressing removed, and direct pressure applied. Infection and retained foam are the next hazards, so every piece of foam is counted and recorded and the dressing is changed on schedule rather than when it begins to smell. The common operator error is to keep raising the pressure to overcome a leak instead of finding it, and the pressure setting, the dressing interval, and the alarm responses must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A frontier clinic can sometimes build a version of this therapy from what it owns, and the honest account must say what such a system cannot do. The improvisation uses sterile gauze or foam in the wound, a flat drain or small tube laid over it, an adhesive film sealing the dressing to the skin, and a wall suction or portable suction unit with a regulator, and the pressure is judged by the gauge and by bubbling in the canister rather than by a pump that measures it. It removes fluid and holds the surface under some negative pressure, but it has no leak compensation, no fluid measurement, and no alarm at all, so a blocked tube or a lost seal is silent and the therapy is simply not happening. The dangers are an uncontrolled suction pressure, a hemorrhage nobody is warned about, a vacuum line contaminated with wound fluid, and a dressing left unchanged too long. The improvised system must never be used over a vessel, and the delivered pressure, the seal, the suction setting, and the observation interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

All three fields carry the same reprocessing burden, and the fragile instruments at the center of it are the ones most often damaged by cleaning. Rigid telescopes, cystoscopes, and hysteroscopes are leak tested before immersion, because fluid entering the optical tube destroys it, and the test takes less time than the repair it prevents. Reusable metal instruments are cleaned before blood dries, inspected for cracks and stiffness, lubricated where the maker requires it, and sterilized in a cycle set for the load, a typical steam cycle being 134 degrees Celsius for 3 to 4 minutes, a number that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Single-use devices are not reused even when they look intact, because their materials were never designed to survive cleaning and their internal channels cannot be inspected. Flexible scopes are hung vertically to drain and dry, since a scope stored wet grows a biofilm that no later disinfection will remove; the cleaning agents, the test method, and the storage conditions must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Maintenance across this equipment is mostly inspection, and the inspection is mostly of the parts that touch the patient. Light cables lose fibers and grow dim, camera cables break at the strain relief, seals and gaskets harden and leak, balloons weaken and burst, pump tubing stretches and delivers a wrong volume, and suction regulators drift so that the number on the gauge is no longer the number delivered. A preventive schedule names what is checked daily, monthly, and at service, and each check is recorded in a logbook that belongs to the device rather than to the ward. The consumables that must never run out are the ones that stop a procedure in its middle: sterile water for balloons, lubricant, biopsy pots and fixative, return pads, smoke filters, foam and film, and canisters of the right size. A recurring theme of this chapter is that the most dangerous failures are silent, since a catheter that no longer drains, a forgotten stent, an absorbing uterus, and a dressing that has lost its seal all look normal while they fail; the maintenance interval, the spare parts list, and the calibration method must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

To summarize, these three fields meet in the physics of soft tubes, closed spaces, and controlled pressure. Urology sees with the cystoscope and the resectoscope, distends with irrigation, cuts with controlled current, fragments stones with focused energy, measures function with pressure catheters, and drains with catheters, nephrostomy tubes, and stents sized on the French scale. Gynecology examines with the speculum, magnifies with the colposcope, takes tissue with sharp forceps, distends the uterus with a measured fluid, and destroys small lesions with a wire loop, with cold, or with heat. Wound care keeps a wound moist, cleans it gently, and where the wound is large or slow, seals it and applies negative pressure through foam and film. In each field the hospital version is a system of pumps, sensors, sealed circuits, and trained reprocessing, while the field version trades that machinery for portability and gives up measurement, alarm, and certainty in return, demanding stricter selection, closer observation, and a written protocol instead.
