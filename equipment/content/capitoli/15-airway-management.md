# Chapter 15 — Airway Management

An airway device is any instrument that keeps the path between the outside air and the lungs open, and the whole family follows from one physical fact. The upper airway is a soft, collapsible tube, and in a person who is unconscious or deeply sedated the muscles that hold it open relax, so the tongue falls backward against the throat. Air must travel from the higher pressure of the atmosphere toward the lower pressure the chest creates, and no amount of oxygen at the mask will help if that path is pinched shut. Every device here holds soft tissue out of the way, seals over the entrance to the voice box, or passes a tube through the vocal cords. The most feared event in this field is a tube placed in the food pipe while the team believes it is in the windpipe, and every habit described here exists to prevent that error. The pressures, volumes, sizes, and temperatures named below must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

To use these devices well, the operator carries a short map of the airway in mind. Air enters the nose or mouth, crosses the pharynx, and reaches the larynx, where the epiglottis, a leaf-shaped flap, guards the entrance to the trachea. The vocal cords lie just below, and beyond them the trachea divides into the two main bronchi at a ridge called the carina. Behind the larynx lies the esophagus, normally closed and easy to enter by mistake, and its position is the reason a tube can pass smoothly and still be in the wrong place. In adults the distance from the teeth to the vocal cords is roughly 15 centimeters, a value that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A tube advanced too far enters the right main bronchus more often than the left.

The simplest device is the oropharyngeal airway, a rigid curved plastic channel that follows the curve of the tongue and holds it forward. It is made in a series of lengths, and the correct one is chosen by measuring from the corner of the mouth to the angle of the jaw, a measurement that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The principle is mechanical, because the device occupies the space the tongue would otherwise fill, so the tongue cannot fall back and air passes around the channel. A device that is too short pushes the tongue backward and worsens the obstruction, and one that is too long can press on the epiglottis or enter the larynx and provoke spasm. It is inserted along the roof of the mouth and rotated into place. It holds tissue but does not seal, so it offers no protection against vomit or blood.

The oropharyngeal airway is the wrong device for a patient who still has protective reflexes, because a conscious or lightly sedated person will gag, retch, and may vomit or develop a spasm of the vocal cords. The operator must judge the depth of unconsciousness before inserting it, and the common error is to force it into a patient who is only partly unresponsive. The device does not prevent aspiration, so a patient wearing one still needs to lie on the side or be watched for vomiting. Cleaning is by hand washing followed by high-level disinfection or sterilization, and the temperature and duration of that cycle must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The field variant is a single rigid airway carried in a bag, and its danger is that one size must serve every patient.

The nasopharyngeal airway is a soft tube passed through a nostril into the pharynx, and it is tolerated by patients who would never accept the rigid oral device. Its principle is that it slips behind the tongue and holds the soft palate and the tongue base forward from inside the air column, and because it is soft it moves with the tissues. It is measured from the tip of the nostril to the tragus of the ear, a value that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and it is chosen by internal diameter or by length in the French scale. It must be lubricated with a water-soluble gel and advanced gently along the floor of the nostril with a slight rotation, never forced, because the lining of the nose bleeds easily. The contraindications are a suspected fracture of the base of the skull, a severe bleeding disorder, and a blocked or deformed nasal passage.

Ignoring those contraindications is the common operator error, and it can drive the tube into the cranial cavity through a fractured skull base or start a hemorrhage in a patient whose blood does not clot. Petroleum jelly must never be used as the lubricant, because it damages many plastics, so a water-soluble gel is correct, a point that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The tube is secured at the nostril with a manufactured flange and tape, and the position is rechecked after every movement of the patient. In the field the device is often improvised by cutting a softened endotracheal tube to the measured length, a practice that demands a smooth cut and the same lubricant. The improvisation gives up the flange and the exact sizing, and it demands that the cut end be inspected for sharp edges.

The supraglottic devices sit above the larynx and solve the airway problem without ever showing the operator the vocal cords. The best known is the laryngeal mask airway, a wide tube whose end carries a small elliptical mask with an inflatable rim that sits over the entrance to the larynx and forms a low-pressure seal around it. The principle is a seal rather than a plug, because the inflated rim presses against the pharynx and closes the space around the laryngeal inlet, so positive pressure from a bag reaches the lungs. It is made in sizes numbered from 1 to 5, matched roughly to the weight of the patient, and both the sizes and the weights must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The mask is deflated, lubricated, and slid along the hard palate until resistance is felt, and only then is the rim inflated with the recommended volume of air. The seal is limited, and if the pressure needed to move air rises above the seal pressure, often around 20 centimeters of water, a value that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, gas leaks out instead of entering the lungs.

The characteristic failure of a supraglottic device is therefore a poor seal, and it has two sounds, an audible leak around the mask as the bag is squeezed and a gurgling of gas entering the stomach that distends it and raises the risk of vomiting. The common operator error is to overinflate the rim in an attempt to stop the leak, which presses harder against the pharyngeal tissues without improving the seal. The device does not protect the airway against regurgitated stomach contents, so it is a rescue tool and a bridge rather than a substitute for a tube with a sealed cuff in a patient at high risk of aspiration. A rising airway pressure alarm on the ventilation device, if one is in use, is often the first sign that the seal has failed, and the second sign is a falling oxygen saturation. The operator's response is to stop squeezing, recheck the position and the size, and prepare the next device rather than to add more air.

Several refinements of the laryngeal mask airway are now standard in hospital practice. A second-generation device adds a separate channel that reaches down to the esophagus, so that any regurgitated fluid can be diverted away from the larynx and vented, and it adds a bite block that keeps the tube from being occluded by the teeth. An intubating version is shaped so that an endotracheal tube can be passed through it and guided toward the vocal cords, which makes it a useful bridge when the cords cannot be seen. Reusable versions are made of silicone and are sterilized by steam at 134 degrees Celsius for a cycle whose temperature and duration must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the rim is checked for cracks before every use. The field argument for this family is strong, because it needs less skill and less equipment than passing a tube through the cords.

A laryngoscope is the instrument that turns a hidden opening into a visible one, and its principle is direct mechanical displacement. The operator places a rigid blade in the mouth, advances it along the tongue, and lifts the handle upward and forward, which pulls the tongue and the jaw forward and brings the larynx into a straight line of sight. A curved blade is placed into the vallecula, the pocket between the base of the tongue and the epiglottis, and lifting there pulls the epiglottis aside indirectly. A straight blade is placed directly under the epiglottis and lifts it, which is often preferred in infants and in patients with a long, floppy epiglottis. The light at the tip comes either from a small bulb on the blade or from optical fibers fed by the handle, and the handle holds the batteries. The most common equipment failure at the worst possible moment is a dead battery or a loose bulb, so the light is tested before every attempt and spare batteries and a spare handle travel with the set.

The characteristic hazard of the laryngoscope is mechanical rather than electrical, because the upper front teeth are the fulcrum a hurried operator instinctively uses. The blade must be used as a lever against the jaw, never against the teeth, and the common operator error is to pry backward on the handle when the view is poor, which chips or dislodges teeth that can then fall into the airway. The video laryngoscope removes the need for a straight line of sight by placing a small camera and a light at the tip of the blade and showing the image on a screen. It improves the view in many difficult patients and reduces the force applied to the teeth, but it adds a screen, a battery, and a cable, and it fails when the battery is flat or the lens is coated with blood. The operator must look at the screen rather than into the mouth, and that change of habit is itself a common error during the first attempts.

The endotracheal tube is the definitive airway, and its principle is simple, because a flexible tube passed between the vocal cords into the trachea creates a sealed conduit through which the lungs can be inflated and which is protected from the contents of the stomach. The tube is sized by its internal diameter in millimeters, and adult sizes commonly run from 7.0 to 8.0, with smaller sizes for children chosen by age, all of which must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Around the lower end sits a cuff, a thin balloon inflated through a small channel to a pilot balloon outside the mouth, and the cuff seals the trachea against the tube. Modern cuffs are high in volume and low in pressure, and the recommended cuff pressure is commonly between 20 and 30 centimeters of water, a range that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A small side opening near the tip, called a Murphy eye, allows air to pass even if the main opening is pressed against the tracheal wall.

Three simple tools make the difference between a failed and a successful attempt. The stylet is a malleable metal rod inserted into the tube to give it a chosen shape, and its tip must stay inside the tube, because a projecting wire can cut or perforate the airway. The bougie is a long, thin, flexible introducer with a curved tip, and it is passed through the cords when the view is poor; its entry into the trachea is often felt as clicks as the tip rides over the cartilage rings, and the tube is then threaded over it. A curved intubating forceps, traditionally called a Magill forceps, guides a tube or a nasal tube through the cords and removes a foreign body, and it works because its long curve follows the pharynx while the operator's hand stays outside the line of sight. A tube exchanger is a long hollow catheter passed through an existing tube before that tube is removed, so the new tube can be railroaded over it and the airway is not lost in between. The common error with all of these tools is to use force when the view is poor, which drives the instrument into tissue instead of guiding it.

Confirming that the tube is in the trachea is the single most important act in this chapter, and no single sign is trustworthy on its own. Listening over both sides of the chest and over the stomach is traditional, and it is unreliable because breath sounds can be conducted from one side to the other and because a tube in the esophagus can produce sounds that imitate them. Watching the chest rise, seeing the tube mist with each breath, and listening for air escaping are useful supporting signs, and none of them settles the question. The reference standard is the detection of carbon dioxide in the exhaled gas, because carbon dioxide comes from the body and is present in every breath from the lungs, while the esophagus contains none. A small colorimetric detector clipped between the tube and the bag changes color in the presence of carbon dioxide, and its color change over several breaths must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A capnograph draws that concentration as a waveform against time, and a sustained waveform across many breaths is the most reliable confirmation available outside direct vision of the cords.

The carbon dioxide detector can lie in both directions, and knowing how it lies is part of using it. A very low reading, or none at all, may occur during cardiac arrest, when almost no blood reaches the lungs, so a negative result in a patient with no circulation does not prove that the tube is misplaced. A false positive can occur when the tube has just been in the esophagus after the patient swallowed air containing carbon dioxide, and the color then fades over a few breaths. The rule that protects the patient is to keep watching the waveform or the color for several breaths and to recheck after every movement, after every transfer, and after any sudden change in the patient's condition. Any loss of the carbon dioxide signal is treated as a lost airway until proven otherwise, and the alarm on the monitor is the falling oxygen saturation, which is a late and dangerous sign of a misplaced tube.

A tracheostomy tube is placed directly through the front of the neck into the trachea, and it exists for patients who need a secure airway for weeks or months or who have an obstruction above the larynx. The tube has an outer cannula that stays in the trachea, an inner cannula that can be lifted out for cleaning, a cuff on some designs, a flange that rests against the skin, and ties that hold the whole assembly in place. The inner cannula is removed and cleaned on a schedule, commonly every eight hours, which must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, because dried secretions narrow the tube silently and can block it completely. The cuff pressure is checked with a gauge and kept in the same low range as an endotracheal cuff, a range that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The stoma is cleaned and dressed, and a spare tube of the same size and one size smaller must live at the bedside.

Tracheostomy care is a routine that conceals an emergency, and the operator's checks are the defense. The obturator, the rounded guide used to insert the tube, must be at the bedside, because without it a replacement tube cannot be threaded back through the stoma. A source of suction, a manual resuscitation bag, and oxygen must be immediately available, and the alarm that matters most is the patient's own breathing, because a blocked inner cannula announces itself as increasing effort and noisy breathing rather than as a machine alarm. The common operator error is to clean the inner cannula less often than the schedule requires because the patient looks comfortable, and the second common error is to change the ties without a second person holding the tube. A late, brisk bleed from the stoma is an emergency because a fistula can form between the trachea and a major artery.

The surgical airway is the last resort, and it exists for the situation in which the upper airway cannot be opened, a tube cannot be passed, and the patient cannot be ventilated by any other means. The principle is to enter the airway below the vocal cords through the cricothyroid membrane, a small, relatively bloodless sheet of tissue stretched between the thyroid cartilage above and the cricoid cartilage below. The operator identifies the membrane in the midline of the neck, stabilizes the larynx with one hand, and either passes a needle, a guidewire, and a dilator to make a passage for a small tube, or makes a short incision and inserts a small cuffed tube directly. The tube used is much smaller than an oral tube, commonly 6.0 millimeters internal diameter, a size that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The hazards are bleeding, damage to the cartilages, and placement outside the trachea, and in small children the membrane is tiny and the procedure is far more dangerous. The common operator error is delay, because the decision is postponed while other attempts continue and the patient's oxygen falls.

The consumables that surround these devices matter as much as the devices themselves, and they are the items that run out first in a busy unit. Gloves, eye protection, and a gown protect the operator from the blood and secretions that accompany every airway attempt, a working suction unit with a rigid catheter is essential before any attempt begins, and oxygen tubing, a mask, and a self-inflating bag with a reservoir feed the patient during and between attempts. A tube of the chosen size, a syringe for the cuff, a water-soluble lubricant, a stylet, a bougie, a carbon dioxide detector, and a stethoscope complete the set, together with tape or a tie to secure whatever is placed. Every one of these items has an expiry date, and every setting, pressure, and alarm threshold associated with them must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Contaminated tubes, suction catheters, and gloves are discarded as clinical waste, and hand hygiene before and after every contact remains the simplest and most effective measure in the room.

Reprocessing the reusable parts is where cross-contamination is prevented or allowed. Laryngoscope blades and handles are wiped free of visible soil, cleaned, and then disinfected or sterilized, and the cycle, the temperature, and the concentration of the disinfectant must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A common and costly error is to immerse a blade still wet with disinfectant into the handle, because the fluid runs into the battery compartment, corrodes the contacts, and destroys the light that the next patient will need. The blade and the handle are separated before cleaning, the batteries are removed and checked, and the handle is wiped rather than soaked. Reusable supraglottic devices are washed, inspected for cracks and leaks, and sterilized by steam at 134 degrees Celsius for a cycle whose temperature and time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Single-use tubes, stylets, suction catheters, and detectors are discarded after each patient, and no amount of cleaning makes them safe to reuse.

Before any airway is managed, the operator performs a check that is short, spoken, and systematic. Suction is switched on and tested against the hand, oxygen is flowing and the reservoir of the bag is full, and a bag and mask of the right size are assembled and squeezed once to confirm the valves move. The laryngoscope light is clicked on and the brightness is judged, spare batteries and a spare handle are within reach, and a tube of the expected size and one size smaller are checked by inflating the cuff and watching the pilot balloon hold its shape. A stylet is prepared but kept inside the tube, and a bougie, a lubricant, and a carbon dioxide detector are laid out with a syringe for the cuff. Intravenous access, monitoring, and the drugs for sedation and paralysis are confirmed with the team, and the plan for a failed attempt is stated aloud, including a limited number of attempts, commonly three, a limit that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Pre-oxygenation before the attempt buys time, and the operator must recognize the features that predict difficulty, including a short neck, a large tongue, and limited mouth opening.

Airway devices fail in a small number of recognizable ways, and each has a signature the listener can learn. A tube can be obstructed by thick secretions, by a kink at the teeth, by a cuff that has herniated over the tip, or by the patient biting it closed, and the sign is a sudden rise in the pressure needed to move air together with a fall in oxygen saturation. A tube can become dislodged outward until the cuff sits at the cords, and the sign is a new leak and a change in the sound of the breathing. There can be a leak from a torn cuff, announced by the loss of the seal and by a pilot balloon that will not stay inflated. The bag can fail to refill because a valve is stuck, and the mask can fail to seal because it is the wrong size or the patient has no teeth. The most dangerous failure of all is unrecognized misplacement, which shows no mechanical sign and is caught only by the carbon dioxide detector and repeated examination.

The field decision is which of these devices earns its place in a bag carried up a mountain, and the answer is driven by weight, volume, power, and the skill of the person who will use it. The reliable core is a self-inflating bag with masks, a small set of oral and nasal airways, two or three sizes of supraglottic device, a small number of endotracheal tubes, a laryngoscope with spare batteries or a battery-powered video device that can be recharged from a portable power bank, a carbon dioxide detector, and a manual suction pump. A cricothyrotomy kit is carried by those trained to use it, and a spare tube and a means of securing it are always included. Where no device is available the operator still has the hands and the position, because a head tilt with a chin lift or a jaw thrust pulls the tongue forward and the recovery position keeps the tongue and vomit away from the opening. What this kit gives up is the range of a hospital trolley, the monitoring, the humidification, and the sterile processing, and what it demands instead is that every item be checked before departure and that the carrier maintain the skill to use it. A device carried for years without practice is a liability rather than a safety net, and a clinician who once intubated regularly may go months without passing a tube, so scheduled practice on a model matters as much as the equipment list.

To summarize, airway management is a family of devices built on three physical ideas, holding the soft tissue of the upper airway open, sealing over the larynx without seeing it, and passing a tube through the vocal cords to replace the upper airway. The oral and nasal airways hold the tongue forward but seal nothing, the supraglottic devices seal over the larynx and tolerate only limited pressure, and the endotracheal tube seals inside the trachea and remains the definitive airway when it is placed correctly. Around these devices sits a set of consumables, a laryngoscope whose light and batteries must be tested, and a carbon dioxide detector whose signal is the only reliable proof of placement. Tracheostomy tubes and surgical airway kits extend the same principles to the neck and to the last resort, each with its own hazards and its own checks. In the field, the choice is governed by weight, power, and skill rather than by ambition, and the kit that is carried must be the kit that is practiced. Every size, pressure, volume, temperature, and alarm threshold in this chapter must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, because a number on a package is not a substitute for the number in the protocol.
