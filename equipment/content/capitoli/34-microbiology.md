# Chapter 34 — Microbiology

The microbiology laboratory does not merely measure a substance already present in a specimen but persuades a living organism to show itself, by growing it, by watching how it feeds, and by testing what stops it. The work has three physical tasks, which are to keep an organism alive outside the body, to separate it from everything else in the specimen, and to measure its response to a drug. This chapter describes the hospital-grade equipment of those tasks, from the incubator, the anaerobic jar, and the media kitchen to the blood culture instrument, the identification and susceptibility benches, the molecular platform, the biosafety cabinet, and the sealed centrifuge. It then turns to the field variant of a microscope, a gram stain, and a trained eye, and every temperature, atmosphere, cycle, and alarm threshold named here must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Everything here rests on one idea, which is that a microorganism is a small chemical engine that runs only when its conditions are supplied. It needs water, a source of carbon and nitrogen, a correct temperature, the right gas mixture, and time, so the first job of the laboratory is to supply those in a controlled way. Growth becomes visible because one cell that divides repeatedly becomes a colony, and a colony is large enough to see, count, and touch, which converts an invisible population into an object. Identification exploits the fact that different organisms run different chemical reactions, so a substrate that changes color when consumed becomes a question whose answer is a clue, and susceptibility testing asks whether the organism can still grow while a drug is present. Every set point and incubation interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The incubator is the simplest and most important machine in the department, and its principle is negative feedback. A temperature sensor reports the air temperature to a controller, the controller switches a heating element on or off, and a fan stirs the air so that every shelf sits at nearly the same temperature, while thick insulation and a well fitting door hold the heat in. The usual setting for clinical bacteriology is 35 to 37 degrees Celsius, close to the human body. A carbon dioxide incubator adds a second loop, in which a sensor measures the gas and a valve admits carbon dioxide to hold the atmosphere near 5 per cent, because organisms that cause pneumonia and meningitis need that gas on a primary plate. The temperature, the gas concentration, and the humidity of each incubator must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

An incubator fails in ways that are quiet before they are loud, so the operator's check is a routine rather than an event. The door seal hardens and leaks, the fan slows, the sensor drifts, and the shelf nearest the door sits cooler than the shelf at the back, so the chamber must be mapped with an independent thermometer rather than trusted to its display. A high temperature alarm and a low temperature alarm are the characteristic alarms, and a carbon dioxide deviation alarm belongs to the gas incubator, but by the time one sounds, plates have often been at the wrong temperature for hours. The common operator error is to overfill the chamber, which blocks the fan and strangles circulation, and the next is to stack plates so thickly that the air cannot reach them. The alarm set points, the acceptable range, and the recording interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

An anaerobic jar is a heavy sealed vessel that removes oxygen from the atmosphere around the plates. A gas pack inside, once its seal is broken, consumes oxygen and releases carbon dioxide and hydrogen, and the hydrogen then combines with the remaining oxygen on the surface of a palladium catalyst to form water, leaving the jar almost free of oxygen. A strip of indicator paper changes color to show that the conditions are correct, and the catalyst and the gas pack are the consumables that make the method work. The common operator error is a poor seal at the gasket or the lid clamp, which leaves oxygen inside and lets the anaerobic organisms die while the facultative ones grow and give a misleading picture. The gas pack type, the catalyst condition, the indicator reading, and the incubation time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Media preparation begins with water, a powdered nutrient base, and agar, a polysaccharide from seaweed that melts when hot and sets into a firm gel when it cools. The mixture must be sterilized, and the device that does it is the autoclave, whose principle is saturated steam under pressure. Steam above the boiling point of water carries latent heat, and when it condenses on a cooler load it releases that heat into the material, which coagulates the proteins of any organism present and kills it faster than dry heat at the same temperature. A typical cycle holds the load at 121 degrees Celsius for about 15 minutes, while a faster cycle may reach 134 degrees Celsius for a few minutes. The temperature, the pressure, the holding time, and the load arrangement of every cycle must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Once the agar is sterile it must be cooled and poured, and the two devices here are a water bath and a pouring station. The molten agar is held in the bath at about 45 to 50 degrees Celsius, warm enough to stay liquid and cool enough not to injure the blood or the additives mixed into it, and it is poured into shallow dishes to a depth of roughly 4 millimeters. A mechanical pourer gives the same volume to every plate. A plate with bubbles, cracks, a wavy surface, or growth before use is discarded. The bath temperature, the volume per plate, the agar depth, and the drying time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The inoculating loop is a small wire with a handle whose physical job is to carry a known volume of liquid or a single colony from one place to another. A calibrated loop delivers a fixed volume, commonly 1 microliter or 10 microliters, so that the colonies that grow after streaking can be counted and multiplied into a concentration that quantifies the specimen. Disposable loops are molded in plastic and used once, while metal loops are re used after being heated to redness in a flame, which sterilizes them by burning away every organism on the wire. A metal loop must be allowed to cool before it touches the agar, and the commonest operator error in the department is to touch a hot loop to a plate, which kills the organisms and leaves a scar in the agar. The loop volume, the streaking pattern, and the sterilization method must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The blood culture system is the most sensitive culture device in the hospital, and its principle is continuous surveillance rather than a single reading. A bottle holds nutrient broth, an anticoagulant so the blood does not clot, and often a resin or charcoal material that absorbs the antibiotics the patient is already receiving, with a headspace of gas above the broth. The bottle is incubated at 35 to 37 degrees Celsius with gentle agitation, and an instrument watches continuously for the physical signs of growth, which may be a change in the fluorescence of a sensor, a change in the pressure of the headspace, or a change in a color indicator. When growth is suspected it raises a positive alert, and a sample is taken for a gram stain and for subculture onto solid media, while the decisive variable is the volume of blood in the bottle. The incubation temperature, the blood volume, the number of bottles in a set, and the alert response time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The blood culture instrument has its own maintenance and alarms, and both belong to the daily round. The incubation block must be held at its set temperature, so a temperature fault alarm is characteristic, and a bottle not loaded alarm tells the operator that a specimen is not being watched as it should be. The consumables are the bottles, and a bottle past its expiry, stored too warm, cracked at the neck, or overfilled will not support growth or will flood the sensor. The commonest operator error is contamination by the skin, which produces a positive bottle growing an organism that lives on the skin rather than in the blood, and the next is to take only one set, when two sets from separate sites are needed to separate true bloodstream infection from contamination. the bottle expiry, the storage conditions, the fill volume, and the alarm responses must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Identification by biochemical reaction is the classical method, and its principle is that an organism's enzymes are a fingerprint. A differential plate contains a substrate that changes color when it is attacked, so a fermented sugar produces acid and turns an indicator from red to yellow, a sulfur containing amino acid that is broken down releases hydrogen sulfide and blackens the medium, and an organism that makes urease turns a urea medium alkaline. A strip system multiplies the idea into a row of small wells, each holding a different substrate, and the pattern of color changes after incubation is read as a code matched against a database. The inoculum density, the incubation time and temperature, and the reading of each well must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The newest identification bench uses mass spectrometry instead of chemistry, and its principle is that the mass of a molecule can be measured by the time it takes to fly. A pure colony is mixed with a matrix compound and dried on a target plate, and a laser pulse vaporizes and ionizes the mixture, so the proteins of the organism become charged particles. Those particles are accelerated by an electric field and travel down a long tube to a detector, and the lighter ones arrive first, so the arrival time at each mass to charge ratio builds a spectrum of peaks characteristic of the species. The instrument compares that spectrum with a library of reference spectra, and it is fast, but it demands a pure colony, depends on a current library, and cannot always separate closely related species. The target preparation, the matrix, the laser energy, and the library version must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Susceptibility testing by disk diffusion is the workhorse of the hospital laboratory, and its principle is diffusion against growth. A paper disk carrying a measured amount of antibiotic is placed on a lawn of the organism spread evenly over the agar, and the drug dissolves and diffuses outward, so its concentration is highest near the disk and falls with distance. The organism grows everywhere except where the drug concentration exceeds the level that stops it, and the result is a circular zone of no growth whose diameter is the visible record of that balance. The diameter is measured in millimeters and compared with an interpretive table that converts it into susceptible, intermediate, or resistant, and those breakpoints follow current guidance and are revised as resistance changes. The inoculum density, the agar depth, the disk potency, the incubation conditions, and the breakpoints in use must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Where disk diffusion gives a category, the minimum inhibitory concentration gives a number, and its principle is to expose the organism to a series of drug concentrations and find the lowest that prevents visible growth. In broth dilution, a row of wells or tubes holds a doubling series of concentrations, each is inoculated with the same small number of organisms, and after incubation they are read for turbidity or for a color change that marks growth, and the first clear one is the answer. A strip carrying a gradient of drug gives the same result on an agar plate. Automated systems fill small cards with drug, incubate them, and read the growth optically, which shortens the work but hides the arithmetic inside a machine that can drift. The drug panel, the inoculum, the incubation time, and the breakpoints applied must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Molecular platforms find the organism by its genetic material rather than by growing it. The polymerase chain reaction works by thermal cycling, which is repeated heating and cooling of a reaction mixture. Heating separates the two strands of a piece of deoxyribonucleic acid, cooling lets short primers bind to the sequences they match, and a warm step lets an enzyme copy the strands, so each cycle doubles the target and 30 to 40 cycles turn a few copies into millions that a fluorescent signal can detect. Because the reaction copies whatever it finds, a trace of genetic material from a previous run can contaminate the next, so the physical separation of the preparation room from the detection room is part of the device. Cartridge-based systems close the process into a sealed disposable, so extraction, amplification, and detection happen inside, which reduces contamination, and the cycling temperatures, the number of cycles, the primer and probe concentrations, and the contamination controls must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The biosafety cabinet is the barrier between the technologist and the aerosol a culture can raise, and its protection depends on airflow rather than on a sealed box. Room air is drawn in through the front grille, filtered, and blown down over the work surface as a smooth curtain, while a high efficiency particulate air filter cleans the air that is exhausted or recirculated, and the inward flow at the opening keeps organisms from escaping toward the operator. The commonest operator error is to block the front grille with the hands or a pile of plates. The airflow alarm is characteristic, and the airflow set point, the filter certification, and the lamp timing must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The centrifuge is a hazard as well as a tool, and it earns its place because it concentrates organisms from a liquid so they can be seen or grown. Its principle is that a spinning rotor throws denser particles outward, so cells and organisms gather at the bottom of the tube while the liquid stays above them. The danger is that a broken tube, a loose lid, or an unbalanced rotor throws a fine aerosol of infectious material into the room, which is why sealed safety buckets with tight lids and rubber gaskets are used, and why the rotor is never opened until it has stopped completely. The imbalance detector is the characteristic alarm, and the rotor speed, the run time, the bucket and gasket condition, and the rotor retirement schedule must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Cleaning the department is equipment work, because every surface and machine has a decontamination routine defined by its manufacturer. Bench surfaces are wiped with a disinfectant active against the organisms handled, spills are covered with absorbent material and flooded with disinfectant from the edge inward, and waste leaving the bench is autoclaved before disposal, since a discarded plate is a concentrated culture of whatever the patient carried. Incubators are emptied, cleaned, and dried on a schedule, water baths are drained and disinfected because a warm bath grows its own biofilm, and the centrifuge and the biosafety cabinet are cleaned after each session as well as on the maintenance schedule. The consumables that keep the chain intact include gloves, gowns, eye protection, disinfectant, spore strips and indicator tape for the autoclave, sterile plates and broth, transport media, swabs, saline, immersion oil, slides, stains, pipettes, and the sharps container. The disinfectant contact time, the spill procedure, the autoclave validation method, and the waste route must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Quality control in microbiology is harder than in chemistry, because the result is a living population and the reference is another living population. The laboratory keeps reference strains of known organisms and grows them alongside the patient's specimen, on the same medium and in the same incubator, to prove that the medium supports growth and that the tests behave as they should, and a disk diffusion test is controlled by a reference strain whose zone diameters are known. Thermometers are checked against a reference, loops and pipettes are verified, reagent and medium lots are recorded, and the temperature of every incubator and refrigerator is written in a log at fixed intervals. The common operator errors are to skip the control when work is heavy, to read a strip too early, and to trust a machine's identification without confirming it against a gram stain, and the control organisms, the acceptable zone ranges, the calibration interval, and the recording schedule must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The field variant of this department is not a smaller version of the hospital laboratory but a different idea. A mobile or frontier clinic rarely has an incubator it can trust, never has a mass spectrometer, and cannot run a susceptibility panel, so it gives up species identification and the prediction of resistance and asks instead a smaller set of questions that change treatment. What remains is a microscope, a set of stains, a flame or a battery lamp, clean slides, and a trained eye, and that combination can still answer whether there are acid fast bacilli in a sputum smear, whether cerebrospinal fluid shows organisms and cells, whether a wound smear shows a dominant organism and its shape, and whether a blood film shows a parasite. The field laboratory therefore trades depth for reach, and it demands a discipline the hospital can partly delegate to machines, which is careful collection, careful staining, patient reading, and an honest refusal to guess beyond the smear. Every reagent, stain time, and microscope setting named below must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The microscope is the central instrument of the field laboratory, and its principle is that the wavelength of light and the size of the opening that admits it set the smallest detail that can be resolved. The condenser focuses a cone of light onto the specimen, the objective lens collects the light that passes through it, and the eyepiece magnifies the image the objective has already formed, so magnification without adequate light and aperture produces a large and empty picture. The numerical aperture of the objective measures how wide a cone of light it can accept, and resolution improves as the aperture rises, which is why a drop of immersion oil is placed between the slide and an oil immersion objective, because the oil has nearly the same refractive index as glass and stops the light from bending away at the gap. A field microscope may be lit by daylight through a mirror or by a battery powered light emitting diode, and it must be carried in its case, kept dry, and cleaned of oil with lens paper after every session. The lamp, the condenser setting, the objective, and the immersion oil must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The gram stain is a sequence of dyes that sorts bacteria into two great groups by the structure of the cell wall, and every step has a physical reason. Crystal violet stains all cells purple, iodine is applied and forms a large complex with the dye inside the cell, and alcohol or a mixture of alcohol and acetone is used to decolorize. In a gram positive organism the thick mesh of peptidoglycan in the wall traps the dye complex and the cell stays purple, while in a gram negative organism the thin peptidoglycan and the lipid rich outer membrane let the complex wash out, so the cell becomes colorless and is counterstained pink with a dilute dye such as safranin. The result is read as purple or pink and as round cocci or rod shaped bacilli, in single cells, pairs, chains, or clusters, and the arrangement is itself a clue. The common operator errors are to decolorize too long, which turns gram positive organisms pink, and to make the smear too thick, which leaves the middle of the field a purple mass, and the dye sequence, the decolorizing time, the smear thickness, and the reading of the control slide must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The field laboratory can still culture a little, and its substitutes for the hospital media kitchen are modest but real. A pressure cooker, a lidded pot, or a simple steamer can sterilize a small batch of agar, and a candle jar replaces the carbon dioxide incubator, because a lit candle in a sealed jar consumes oxygen and produces carbon dioxide as it burns out, leaving an atmosphere rich enough for organisms that need that gas. A warm cupboard or a thermos flask can hold a temperature close enough to body heat for a short incubation, though the temperature must be watched with a thermometer and not assumed. For tuberculosis the field method is the acid fast smear, and for malaria it is a stained thick and thin blood film read under the microscope. The field laboratory must also solve with discipline what the hospital solves with infrastructure, which is safe disposal of cultures and sharps, disinfection of the bench, fly proof storage, and a cool dark place for the reagents that need it, and the sterilization method, the jar atmosphere, the incubation temperature, and the storage conditions must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The hazards of the field laboratory are the hazards of the hospital laboratory with the protections removed, and naming them is part of using the equipment. Without a biosafety cabinet, the aerosol raised by an open culture, a flame, or a centrifuge is breathed in the same room, so the field worker uses gloves, a gown, eye protection, and a well ventilated space, and handles the most infectious material on an open bench only when nothing better exists. A centrifuge without sealed buckets is particularly dangerous, and it is safer to spin a capped tube in a shielded or hand powered device than to run an open rotor in a crowded room. The absence of susceptibility testing is a clinical hazard in itself, because treatment must then be chosen from local guidance and the appearance of the smear, and the honest clinician names that limit rather than pretending that a gram stain predicts resistance. The characteristic alarms of the field version are the autoclave pressure indicator and the thermometer in the incubation box, and the personal protective equipment, the ventilation, the spill procedure, and the local treatment guidance must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Between the two extremes stands the district laboratory, and its shape is instructive because most of the microbiology in the world happens there. Such a laboratory lives on its maintenance log, and the daily check is short but not optional, covering the temperature of every incubator, refrigerator, and water bath, the date and sterility of every batch of medium, the condition of the gas packs and the catalyst, the level of disinfectant, and the expiry of reagents, stains, and culture bottles. The failure modes to watch are the ones that produce a confident wrong answer rather than an obvious breakdown, which are an incubator that runs cool, a medium overheated during preparation, a stain diluted or contaminated, a loop that no longer holds its volume, and a breakpoint table years out of date. The recording interval, the acceptance limits for each check, and the replacement schedule must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

To summarize, the microbiology laboratory is the part of the hospital that grows and questions living organisms, and its governing principle is that an organism behaves only when its conditions are supplied, so temperature, atmosphere, nutrient, and time are the measurement rather than the background. The incubator holds a negative feedback loop at body heat and its gas variant adds carbon dioxide, the anaerobic jar strips oxygen with a gas pack and a catalyst, the autoclave kills with saturated steam under pressure, the calibrated loop turns growth into a number, and the blood culture instrument watches a bottle continuously so that the volume of blood in it becomes the decisive variable. Identification moves from color changing biochemical reactions to strip systems to mass spectrometry, susceptibility moves from the diffused zone of a disk to the number of a minimum inhibitory concentration and depends on breakpoints that follow current guidance, and molecular platforms find genetic material by thermal cycling while the biosafety cabinet and the sealed centrifuge contain the aerosol that all of it can raise. The field version gives up species identification and resistance prediction and keeps the microscope, the gram stain, the candle jar, the pressure cooker, and the trained eye, demanding instead careful technique, honest reporting, safe disposal, and a maintenance log kept by hand. The alarms to know are the incubator temperature and gas alarms, the airflow alarm of the cabinet, the imbalance alarm of the centrifuge, the positive alert of the blood culture instrument, and the autoclave pressure or cycle fault, and every temperature, atmosphere, cycle, and threshold named in this chapter must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.
