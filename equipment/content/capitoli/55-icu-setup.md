# Chapter 55 — ICU Setup

An intensive care unit, or ICU, is not a room full of machines but a single bed space repeated, and each bed space is a complete life-support system for a patient who has lost the ability to regulate breathing, blood pressure, temperature, or consciousness. The organizing principle is distribution, since gases, electricity, vacuum, water, data, and alarms each arrive at a fixed point beside the bed, and each delivery has a source, a path, a terminal, and a failure mode. A ventilator without oxygen, a monitor without power, and a suction unit without vacuum are only furniture. A listener who understands the distribution can diagnose most trouble in an ICU before touching any device.

The design of one bed space begins with geometry, because the machines occupy the floor long before they occupy the patient. A common arrangement leaves about 60 centimeters of clear floor on both sides of the bed, a figure that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, but the principle is that a nurse must reach every port, line, and airway from either side while a second rescuer works without stepping over the first. The bed is positioned so the patient's head and chest stay visible from the central desk, because direct sight is the fastest detector of a disconnection or a change in skin color. Storage and the monitor shelf are placed so that no cable crosses the walking path. Lighting comes in layers rather than one bright ceiling fixture, because the room must brighten at once for a procedure and dim at night while the staff still read the monitor.

Medical gas reaches each bed space through a piped system whose principle is that a central source is held at high pressure and regulated down at the point of use. A central plant feeds pipe that passes a zone valve and a regulator to a terminal unit matched to one gas. The connectors of oxygen, medical air, and vacuum are deliberately made non-interchangeable, and the wall color code is a convenience rather than the safety mechanism, so a faded label is a hazard worth reporting. Piped oxygen is commonly delivered at about 4 bar at the terminal, a value that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the flowmeter then reduces it to the few liters per minute the patient receives. The characteristic alarms are the low pressure alarm on the area panel and the high pressure alarm of the plant, and the common error is connecting a device to the wrong terminal because the fittings look similar in poor light.

Electricity follows the same logic, and its principle is that current must have a complete path and a deliberate return. Each bed space draws from circuits sized for its load, and critical care sockets are typically divided into ordinary mains sockets and sockets backed by an uninterruptible power supply, or UPS, with a further set on the emergency generator. The backed socket is the one a life-support device should occupy, because the battery carries the load through the seconds before the generator starts, and its runtime must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The serious hazards are an overloaded socket strip, a cord across the floor, and a damaged plug, and the common error is unplugging a ventilator to charge a telephone.

Everything needed in the first minute must be within arm's reach, because the interval between recognition and action decides the outcome of a resuscitation. The bedside stock holds gloves, gowns, masks, eye protection, alcohol hand rub, suction catheters, syringes, saline flushes, gauze, tape, electrode pads, specimen tubes, and a sharps container. Consumables are grouped by purpose rather than by delivery, so the airway items are together and the vascular access items are together, and each group is restocked after use. The cupboard stays closed, dry, and out of direct sunlight, because many items are degraded by heat, humidity, or ultraviolet light, and storage conditions and shelf lives must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The common error is a cupboard filled at the front with items that expire next year while the item needed tonight sits empty at the back.

The alarm system is the sense organ of the unit, and it exists because a monitor can measure while only a human can respond. A bedside monitor raises its own tone and light, a central station repeats the alarm at the desk, and a nurse call system carries a patient request to the same place, so no single alarm depends on one listener. The serious hazard is alarm fatigue, the dulling produced by hundreds of non-actionable signals each day, and the common errors are widening a limit to silence a nuisance and silencing an alarm without looking at the patient. Alarm limits and the escalation interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and a unit should audit which alarms actually fire.

The ventilator takes over the work of breathing, and its principle is positive pressure. A normal breath is drawn in by negative pressure as the diaphragm descends, but a ventilator pushes gas into the airway, so the pressure inside the chest rises above the pressure around it. The machine creates that push with a blower or a compressed gas supply, controls it with an inspiratory valve, measures flow and volume near the airway, and ends the breath when a set volume, pressure, or time is reached. Because the gas is pushed, pressure and volume are what injure the lung, and overdistension together with the repeated opening and closing of small air sacs explains why ventilation can itself cause harm.

The prescribed settings keep the lung open without stretching it, and common starting points are a tidal volume of 6 to 8 milliliters per kilogram of predicted body weight, an end-expiratory pressure of 5 to 10 centimeters of water, and a plateau pressure below about 30 centimeters of water, all of which must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A common operator error is reading the exhaled tidal volume without checking that the circuit is not leaking or the tube is not partly blocked, because the machine reports what it measures rather than what the patient received. The characteristic alarms are the high airway pressure alarm, the low tidal volume alarm, the apnea alarm, and the oxygen concentration alarm, and each names a cause found by looking at the patient, the tube, and the circuit in that order. The safety hazard of a silenced ventilator alarm is a patient who is no longer being ventilated while the room stays quiet.

The ventilator is only as good as the disposable circuit that carries its gas, and those consumables are the breathing circuit, the humidifier chamber or heat and moisture exchanger, the closed suction catheter, the water traps, and the bacterial filters. Condensation collects at the low points of the circuit, where it can be tipped into the airway or can block the flow sensor, so the traps are drained and the circuit is routed so water runs away from the patient. Cleaning follows the manufacturer's instructions, because the housing and screen tolerate a wipe while the internal flow sensor and valves do not tolerate fluid at all. Preventive maintenance replaces the internal battery, recalibrates the oxygen and flow sensors, and tests the alarms on an interval that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The characteristic failure of neglect is an oxygen sensor that drifts low, so the machine delivers less oxygen than it displays.

The bedside monitor is a small computer that translates several physical signals into one picture. The electrocardiogram, or ECG, measures the tiny voltage generated by heart muscle through skin electrodes, and its trace appears only when electrode, gel, and skin form a stable conductive path. Pulse oximetry shines red and infrared light through a fingertip and calculates oxygen saturation from the different absorption of oxygenated and deoxygenated hemoglobin, which is why motion, cold fingers, nail polish, and poor perfusion corrupt the number. Invasive pressure measurement uses a fluid-filled catheter joined to a strain gauge transducer, and the pressure wave moves the diaphragm of the gauge, changes its electrical resistance, and becomes the waveform on the screen.

The monitor is useful only when its alarm limits suit this patient rather than the defaults, and its characteristic alarms are the low oxygen saturation alarm, the low blood pressure alarm, the high heart rate alarm, and the asystole or lead-off alarm. The common errors are accepting default limits designed for a different patient, leaving a probe on a finger that has been cold and still for hours, placing electrodes over bone or scar tissue. Electrodes dry out and must be replaced before the trace becomes noisy, because a wandering baseline is a signal about the hardware rather than the heart, and a transducer is leveled at the height of the right atrium and zeroed before its pressure is believed. The skin under every electrode and probe is inspected each shift, and the housing and cables are wiped with the approved disinfectant, with alarm limits and cleaning contact times confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Infusion pumps exist because gravity cannot deliver a precise volume against resistance, and their principle separates them into two families. A volumetric pump moves fluid with fingers or a peristaltic rotor that squeezes a dedicated giving set, so the volume delivered equals the number of squeezes multiplied by the volume trapped in the tubing. A syringe pump drives the plunger of a syringe with a lead screw, so the volume equals the cross-sectional area of the barrel multiplied by the distance traveled. Both devices infer a problem from pressure, because a rising pressure at the pump means resistance downstream while a falling pressure means a line has come apart or a vessel has been lost. The characteristic alarms are the occlusion alarm, the air-in-line alarm, the syringe empty alarm, and the low battery alarm, and the common error is clearing an occlusion alarm without tracing the line from pump to patient.

The consumables are the dedicated giving set or syringe, the extension set, the filter where one is required, and the label, and they are not interchangeable between pump families because tubing diameter is part of the calibration. The hazard of a mismatched set is silent underdelivery or overdelivery, since the pump counts its own strokes and trusts the tubing it was told to expect. Free flow is a second hazard, because a set removed from a volumetric pump can siphon its container into the patient by gravity unless the roller clamp or anti-free-flow mechanism is closed. Every pressure limit, bolus volume, and drug library entry must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the common error is overriding the library when a concentration is unusual.

The dialysis machine in an ICU is most often a continuous renal replacement therapy, or CRRT, device, whose principle is slow convection across a filter rather than the fast diffusion of a hemodialysis session. Blood is drawn from a central catheter by a peristaltic pump, pushed through a hemofilter whose membrane passes water and small molecules, and returned to the patient, while replacement fluid and an effluent pump remove the ultrafiltrate. A common prescription is an effluent rate of 20 to 35 milliliters per kilogram each hour with a blood flow of 100 to 200 milliliters per minute, values that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The installation demands treated water if the machine prepares its own fluid, a drain for the effluent, dedicated power, and a circuit of tubing, a filter, replacement fluid, and an anticoagulant. The characteristic alarms are the filter pressure alarm, the transmembrane pressure alarm, and the air detector alarm, and the common error is ignoring a slow upward drift of pressure until the filter clots.

The ICU bed is itself a machine whose principle is a set of electric actuators that raise and lower the platform and articulate the back and knee sections. The low position is the safest, because a patient who falls from a low bed is injured less, and the brakes are applied whenever the bed is stationary, because a rolling bed drags lines and tubes. The special mattress answers a different problem, namely that sustained pressure above the capillary closing pressure stops blood flow to the skin and produces a pressure injury, so an alternating pressure mattress inflates and deflates groups of cells in a slow cycle to move the contact points. A common cycle is about 10 to 20 minutes, a figure that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the common errors are leaving the mattress pump unplugged, covering the surface with a thick pad, and forgetting the cardiopulmonary resuscitation release.

Suction removes what the patient cannot clear, and its principle is a pressure difference. A vacuum pump or a venturi driven by compressed gas lowers the pressure in a canister and a tube, and the higher pressure of the atmosphere then pushes secretions toward the lower pressure. The regulator sets the vacuum and a float valve protects the pump from overflow, so the characteristic alarms are the canister full alarm and the low vacuum alarm. The safety hazards are a vacuum set too high, which tears and bleeds the airway mucosa, and prolonged suction, which removes oxygen along with secretions and can drop the oxygen saturation and heart rate. The common errors are suctioning on a schedule rather than on need and leaving a canister near full, while the vacuum level for an adult, a child, and a neonate differs and must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The resuscitation trolley is a portable collection of everything needed in the first minutes of a cardiac arrest, sealed so that a missing item is visible before it is needed. It carries a defibrillator, airway equipment including a laryngoscope, blades, tubes, and a self-inflating resuscitation bag with masks, suction, intravenous access and fluids, drugs. The defibrillator's principle is the storage of energy in a capacitor and its sudden release through pads on the chest, which sends a current across the heart that depolarizes the muscle at once and allows the natural pacemaker to resume control. A common adult energy setting for a biphasic device is 150 to 200 joules, a value that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the common errors are expired pads, a battery that has never been load tested, and a seal broken weeks ago. The characteristic alarm is the self-test failure of the defibrillator, and the trolley is checked and recorded at the start of every shift.

Behind the wall the ICU depends on a plant the staff rarely see, and its reliability is built from ordinary maintenance. The medical gas plant holds the source, the changeover manifold, the regulators, and the alarm panel that warns the desk when a bank runs low, and a zone valve allows the gas to be isolated. The electrical plant includes the substation, the generator, the UPS batteries, and the distribution boards, and the generator is tested under load at intervals rather than merely started, because an engine that starts and then fails on load is a dangerous illusion of readiness. The heating, ventilation and air conditioning, or HVAC, plant holds temperature, humidity, and pressure relationships, and in critical areas it changes the air often enough to dilute contamination, with room pressure and air change rate confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The characteristic alarms of the plant are the gas pressure alarm, the generator failure alarm, and the UPS low battery alarm, and the common error is treating these distant alarms as the technician's business.

Isolation capability is a property of the room rather than the patient, and its principle is the control of air and contact. An airborne infection needs a room held at negative pressure relative to the corridor, so air flows in and leaves through a filtered exhaust instead of drifting into the hall, and that pressure difference is small enough that an open door or a blocked grille can reverse it without any alarm being heard. A contact or droplet infection needs something simpler, namely a barrier, dedicated equipment, and a place to remove personal protective equipment, or PPE, before leaving the room. The curtain is the most familiar barrier and also the most contaminated object in the unit, because it is touched before and after patient contact and cleaned far less often than the bed rail, so it is changed on a schedule or whenever it is soiled. The negative pressure, the air change rate, and the curtain change interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the common error is opening the isolation door to improve the ventilation.

The consumables of critical care are the most visible cost and the least visible risk, because each has a shelf life, a storage condition, and a single-use rule. Oxygen masks and nasal cannulas, suction catheters and canisters, ventilator circuits and filters, infusion sets and syringes, central line dressings, electrodes and transducers, gloves and gowns, and drainage bags all expire, and stock that is not rotated becomes unusable at the worst moment. Single-use items are never reprocessed in a critical care area, because a residual contaminant in a device that touches blood or airway is not worth the saving. The minimum stock level for each item is set from the daily use rate and the resupply lead time, and the storage temperature, humidity, and expiry of each item must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The common error is hoarding a private drawer of supplies, which hides real consumption and empties the shared shelf.

Cleaning and maintenance decide whether the equipment still is the device the manufacturer tested. Between patients the bed space receives a terminal clean, in which the mattress and cover, the bed rails, the monitor, the pump, the ventilator housing, and every touched surface are cleaned and then disinfected with a product whose contact time is respected, since a sprayed surface that dries in seconds has not been disinfected. Curtains and cloth surfaces are changed or laundered rather than wiped, and a mattress cover with a crack or a stain is replaced because fluid inside the foam cannot be removed. Preventive maintenance is scheduled by the biomedical technician, who tests electrical safety, calibrates pressure and flow sensors, replaces batteries, and records every intervention in the equipment register. The cleaning agent, its dilution, and its contact time, together with every calibration tolerance and service interval, must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A well-run ICU plans for its own failure, because every supply it depends on can stop. The failure modes that matter are a power failure, a gas failure, a vacuum failure, a water failure, a network failure, and the failure of a single machine, and each has a rehearsed answer rather than an improvised one. When power fails the UPS carries the ventilator and the monitor for a measured time while the generator starts, and if it does not start the nurse switches to the self-inflating resuscitation bag and a portable oxygen cylinder, which is why that bag is checked and why the cylinder is never empty. When the gas plant fails the bed space is transferred to cylinder supply and the flow is calculated against the remaining pressure and the patient's demand, while a network failure sends the unit back to a paper chart prepared before the electronic health record, or EHR, goes dark. The operator's check at the start of a shift covers the bedside equipment, the oxygen and suction outlets, the alarm limits, the drugs, the defibrillator, and every running infusion, and the alarms, battery runtimes, and cylinder durations must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The equipment assumes a staffing model, and this is where machines and people must be counted together. A ventilator, a monitor with continuous alarms, and several infusion pumps extend the senses and the hands of a nurse but cannot act, so the unit assumes a nurse who reaches the bedside within seconds and a second trained person for every resuscitation. A common model assigns one nurse to one unstable patient and one nurse to two stable patients, and any such ratio must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The equipment also assumes competence rather than presence, so staff must be trained on each machine and drilled on the failure of power, gas, and airway. The common error is adding a bed without adding a nurse, and the serious hazard of understaffing is an alarm that sounds at a desk where nobody is sitting.

In a mobile or frontier clinic the intensive care unit is usually three beds made from an ordinary room, and the first decision is selection rather than construction. The room must be close to the entrance so a deteriorating patient can reach it and a referral vehicle can reach the door, and it must have a solid floor, a roof, light, enough electrical supply for the devices that matter, and at least one opening for ventilation. The design principle is unchanged, namely that every bed needs power, oxygen, suction, monitoring, and the consumables for the first hour, but in the field each of those is a separate object rather than a wall fitting. What the field gives up is the central station, the piped gas, the continuous vacuum, the building alarm, and the specialist who arrives with a spare part. What it demands instead is a disciplined order of assembly, a written plan for every failure, and staff who have rehearsed that plan.

The order of assembly starts with power, because nothing else can run without it. A generator, a battery bank with an inverter, or a solar array with batteries is sized from the sum of the loads, with the ventilator, the suction, and the lights counted first, and any such calculation must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Oxygen comes next as cylinders with a regulator and a flowmeter, and the amount available is finite, because a full cylinder holds a known volume at a known pressure and its duration equals that volume divided by the flow the patient is receiving. Suction follows, either an electric unit on the generator or a foot operated pump that needs no power, and every bed must have one. The common error is placing the beds before the utilities and then running extension cords and oxygen tubing across the floor, which is both a trip hazard and a source of accidental disconnection.

Monitoring in the field is assembled from handheld devices rather than a central station, and the principle is to cover the measurements that change a decision. A handheld pulse oximeter, a manual blood pressure cuff with a stethoscope, a thermometer, and a disciplined count of respiratory rate and level of consciousness form the minimum set, and a single transport monitor is shared between beds. Ventilation may be a self-inflating resuscitation bag used continuously by a nurse, a simple turbine ventilator on its own battery, or a continuous positive airway pressure, or CPAP, device for a patient who is breathing but cannot maintain oxygenation, and any pressure or flow setting must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Consumables are laid out in one place per bed, with gloves, airway items, intravenous supplies, dressings, and drugs counted before the shift, because in a three-bed unit a missing item is a walk to the store.

The most important field equipment is the plan for the patient the unit cannot hold, because a three-bed intensive care exists to stabilize and refer rather than to finish. The order of assembly is therefore a clinical order as well, namely choose the room, secure the power, position the oxygen, establish the suction, place and brake the beds, set up the monitoring, prepare the airway and ventilation, stock the consumables, write the chart and fix the communication route, and only then accept a patient, with every device setting confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. What the field gives up in precision it must recover in vigilance, so the nurse to patient ratio is often one to one or one to two, and tasks are shared among assistants and family members trained for specific roles. The characteristic limitation is that a patient needing invasive monitoring, prolonged mechanical ventilation, or CRRT cannot be managed there.

To summarize, the intensive care unit is a distributed life-support system built around one bed space, in which gases, power, vacuum, water, data, and alarms arrive at a fixed point and the machines are only as reliable as the building behind them. The ventilator pushes gas and can therefore injure the lung, the monitor translates electrical and optical signals into a picture and can therefore mislead, the infusion pump counts strokes and trusts its tubing, the CRRT circuit clears slowly across a membrane, and the mattress, the suction, and the resuscitation trolley each answer a physical problem with a characteristic alarm and a common error. In the hospital the unit rests on piped gas, backed power, central alarms, isolation, a deep stock of consumables, and a staffing ratio that assumes a responder within seconds. In the field the same care is assembled from a chosen room, a sized power supply, cylinders, manual suction, handheld monitoring, and a plan to refer, and the rule in both settings is the same, namely understand the physics, check the device at the start of the shift, and treat the patient rather than the alarm.
