# Chapter 65 — Catheters, Tubes, and Drains

A catheter, a tube, and a drain are three names for one idea, which is a deliberate channel between a cavity inside the body and a container outside it. Fluid always moves from a region of higher pressure toward a region of lower pressure, and the tube gives that movement a defined path. A bladder that cannot empty, a stomach that will not drain, and a chest cavity filled with air are the same physical problem in different places. What makes these devices difficult is not the flow but the fact that the tube is a foreign body crossing skin and mucosa, so each one trades a drainage problem for a risk of infection, injury, and blockage. This chapter describes the hospital-grade devices with their infrastructure and consumables, and then the field variants that a mobile or frontier clinic can keep in stock. Every setting, pressure, and volume named here must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before it is applied to a patient.

The behavior of every tube follows from a few facts about flow. Flow through a narrow channel rises steeply as the channel widens, so a small increase in internal radius produces a large increase in drainage. Flow falls as the tube lengthens, and it falls again when the fluid is thick or full of debris. This is why a catheter can seem adequate at insertion and fail once the fluid becomes purulent. The operator who understands this examines the channel and the fluid, not only the number on the wrapper.

The simplest driving force is gravity, which costs nothing and never fails, and a container placed below the patient creates a column of fluid whose weight pulls the contents out of the cavity. Suction adds force when gravity is not enough, as with a thick wound or a pleural space that must be kept expanded. Suction carries its own hazards, because a vacuum strong enough to move fluid is also strong enough to injure mucosa and pull tissue into the openings of the tube. Air, not force, is what carries most secretions, so an occluded opening defeats a powerful pump. Every suction level must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Materials decide how a tube behaves in tissue and how it can be reprocessed. Latex is soft, cheap, and elastic, but it provokes allergy in some patients and stiffens and cracks with age. Silicone is smoother, more inert, and tolerates heat, which makes it the material of reusable catheters and long-term tubes. Polyvinyl chloride, usually shortened to PVC, is the stiff, clear plastic of most single-use suction catheters and feeding tubes, and it softens at body temperature. Polytetrafluoroethylene, usually shortened to PTFE, appears as a slippery coating and as a chemically inert guidewire surface. The safety hazard is a latex tube in a sensitized patient, where the reaction can be sudden and severe.

Sizing follows two different traditions, and confusing them is a common and dangerous error. The French scale describes the outer circumference in millimeters, so a catheter of 16 French has an external diameter of about 5.3 millimeters, and one French equals one third of a millimeter. Needle and cannula gauges run the opposite way, with a larger number meaning a smaller diameter. Because a French number measures the outside, two catheters of the same size can carry very different flows if their walls differ in thickness. Sizing and the corresponding flow must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before a device is chosen.

Three habits keep every tube in this chapter safe, and they apply in a teaching hospital and in a tent alike. The tube is secured so that normal movement cannot pull it out or push it in, its depth is marked and recorded, and it is reviewed every day for the moment it is no longer needed. A tube left in place because nobody remembers why it is there is the most common reason for a hospital-acquired infection. Within hours of insertion a film of protein coats the plastic, and bacteria settle into it and produce a slime that shields them from antibiotics and disinfectants. That biofilm is why a catheter can be sterile at insertion and infected a week later without any break in technique, and why the reliable treatment is removal. The characteristic sign is undramatic, usually fever or cloudy urine, and any new fever in a patient with a tube must be treated as possibly tube-related.

Urinary catheters are the most numerous tubes in any hospital, and their purpose is to keep the bladder empty when the patient cannot do it. The urethra is a soft muscular channel and the bladder is a muscular bag, so the design problem is to hold a soft tube in a moving organ without letting it fall out. The familiar balloon catheter solves this with a small balloon near the tip, inflated inside the bladder after the tip has passed the sphincter, so that the tube cannot be withdrawn. The two-way catheter has one channel for drainage and one for the balloon, while the three-way catheter adds a channel for irrigation when the bladder contains blood clots. The curved-tip catheter, whose bent end lets the operator steer past an enlarged prostate, is passed with a slight rotation, and the intermittent catheter has no balloon at all and is removed as soon as the bladder is empty. Adult sizes run from about 12 French to 18 French, and that size must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The retention balloon is inflated with sterile water through a small side channel, and the volume is chosen for the catheter and the situation. A typical adult balloon holds 10 milliliters, while a larger balloon of 30 milliliters is used after some prostate operations to press on the bladder neck. Air must never be used, because air compresses and leaks and the balloon can deflate and let the catheter fall out. The most serious error is inflation before the tip is confirmed to be inside the bladder, which tears the urethra and creates a false passage. Overinflation is the second error, because it can rupture the balloon so that its fragments must be retrieved. The balloon volume and the inflation medium must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Insertion is a sterile procedure, and the sterile field is not a formality. The meatus is cleaned, sterile lubricant is applied to the tip, and the catheter is advanced gently until urine flows and then a little further so the balloon clears the sphincter. The safety hazard is urethral trauma, which begins with bleeding and can end with a stricture that narrows the channel for life. The common operator error is to push against resistance instead of stopping, withdrawing slightly, and asking for help. A catheter that will not pass is a reason to call a more experienced clinician, not a reason to push harder, and the technique and lubricant must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the procedure.

Once the catheter is in place, the drainage system matters as much as the catheter. The bag hangs below the level of the bladder so that gravity and not backflow moves the urine, the tubing avoids dependent loops where stale urine pools, and the junction between catheter and tubing stays closed. Meatal care is gentle washing with water and soap, and vigorous antiseptic scrubbing does more harm than good. Blockage is the quiet failure, and it announces itself through the chart rather than through an alarm, with falling output, suprapubic pain, and a tender bladder. Encrustation is the usual cause, because minerals deposit on the tube and slowly narrow the lumen, and gentle irrigation with sterile saline may restore flow. Irrigation must never be forced, and the drainage position, the emptying schedule, and the irrigation volume must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

Catheter-associated urinary tract infection is the principal hazard of this device, and it is largely preventable. Organisms travel along the outside of the catheter, through the lumen, or across a broken junction, and they find a surface already coated with biofilm. Every extra day of catheterization multiplies the risk, which is why the removal date should be decided at insertion. The signs are fever, cloudy or foul urine, suprapubic pain, and confusion in an older patient. The characteristic alarm is not a sound but a reading, because the monitor may show a rising temperature or a falling blood pressure before anyone looks at the urine. The indication for the catheter and the review interval must be confirmed against the local protocol before the device is left in place.

In a mobile or frontier clinic, the hospital-grade catheter and its closed drainage system are often replaced by clean intermittent catheterization. A clean single-use catheter, or a reusable silicone catheter that is washed, boiled, and stored dry, empties the bladder several times a day and then leaves no foreign body behind. What this gives up is convenience for the staff and the continuous measurement of output, and what it demands is clean water, a way to boil, a private place, and a patient or family member who can be taught the technique well. When the urethra cannot be used at all, a catheter may be placed directly through the lower abdominal wall into the bladder, which bypasses an obstruction but risks bleeding, bowel injury, and leakage around the site. The tract of such a catheter takes time to mature, so it must not be changed early by an inexperienced hand. The reuse interval, the disinfection method, and the first change interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

Nasogastric tubes are the simplest tubes in the hospital and among the most dangerous when misplaced. A nasogastric tube passes through the nose, down the back of the throat, and into the stomach, where it drains the stomach, decompresses it, or delivers liquid feed by gravity and slow drip. Sizes run from about 8 French for a fine feeding tube to 18 French for a wide drainage tube, and the larger the tube the better it drains and the more it irritates the nose, throat, and stomach. Polyvinyl chloride is stiff enough to pass and cheap enough to discard, while silicone or polyurethane is softer and more suitable for feeding over weeks. A weighted tip contains a small heavy bead, usually of tungsten, that helps the tube sink and stay in the stomach, while an unweighted tube relies on the movement of the stomach to carry it along. A fine-bore feeding tube often carries a stiff guidewire to help it pass, that wire must be removed before the tube is used, a nasojejunal tube is longer and sits beyond the stomach in the small bowel, and these sizes and materials must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Confirming that a nasogastric tube is in the stomach and not in the lung is the single most important check in this chapter. The tube can pass through the throat into the trachea, and feeding through such a tube delivers liquid directly into the airway and kills the patient. Auscultation of a whoosh of air over the stomach is unreliable and must not be used as the only test. The reliable bedside test is to aspirate a small amount of fluid and measure its acidity with an indicator strip, because stomach fluid is acidic and lung fluid is not, while radiography remains the reference method when there is any doubt. The safety hazard is the misplaced tube, the common operator error is trusting the whoosh, and the characteristic alarm is the patient coughing, choking, or desaturating during insertion. The acidity threshold and the confirmation method must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before feed is given.

Maintenance keeps a nasogastric tube working and keeps the patient safe. The tube is secured to the nose or cheek with tape or a commercial holder, and the mark that shows its depth is checked at every shift, because a tube that has slipped inward can enter the airway and a tube that has slipped outward pours feed into the throat. The lumen is flushed with water before and after every feed and every medicine, since dried feed and crushed tablets are the usual causes of blockage. Medicines are given one at a time with a flush between, and the tube is never used as a drain and a feed line at the same time. Long-term tubes cause sinusitis, sore throat, and erosion of the nostril, so the tube is moved from one nostril to the other when the protocol allows. The securing method and the flush volumes must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

The field variant of enteral feeding gives up the pump and the sterile packaging and demands patience. A fine-bore feeding tube with a guidewire may be unavailable, so a larger nasogastric tube is used and tolerated less well. A gravity drip is made from a bag, a bottle, or a syringe barrel held above the patient, and the rate is set by the height of the column and a simple clamp rather than by a machine. The safety hazard is the uncontrolled fast feed, which can cause aspiration, vomiting, and diarrhea, so the container is never hung more than a set distance above the stomach. What this variant demands instead is a person who watches the patient during the feed, because no alarm will announce the complication. The drip height and the feed volume must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A rectal tube is a short, soft tube passed through the anus into the rectum to release gas or to deliver an enema. Its physical action is simple decompression, because trapped gas and fluid move down a pressure gradient when a path is offered. The tube is soft and usually has a rounded tip and side holes, and it is passed only a short distance and never forced. The safety hazard is perforation of the rectum or the sigmoid colon, which is a surgical emergency and is more likely in a thin, frail, or inflamed bowel. The common operator error is to insert the tube too far or to leave it in place for hours, which erodes the mucosa. The insertion depth and the dwell time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A chest drain rests on one of the most elegant principles in medicine, because the lungs are elastic and want to collapse while the chest wall springs outward, and the two are held together by a thin film of fluid in the pleural space. When air or blood enters that space the film is broken and the lung pulls away from the wall, so a chest drain restores the coupling by removing the air or fluid and then preventing anything from returning. The water seal is the key, because fluid bubbles out through a column of water while the water rises back and blocks the path inward. Suction is added only when the lung needs help to re-expand, and its level must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before it is applied.

Chest drains differ in size, shape, and stiffness, and the choice follows the fluid and the patient. The straight large-bore tube is the traditional drain for blood and thick fluid, and adults often receive 28 French to 36 French. The angled tube has a bent tip that helps it reach around the lung or into a pocket, while the pigtail is a thin curled catheter of 8 French to 14 French placed for air or simple fluid, which is much less painful. Small-bore drains are increasingly used for pneumothorax, while large-bore drains remain necessary for hemothorax and empyema. The size, the shape, and the insertion technique must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the drain is placed.

The drainage system is a set of chambers that each perform one job. The collection chamber receives the fluid and lets the volume be read from the side. The water seal chamber shows the pressure of the pleural space as a small movement of water called tidaling, which rises and falls with breathing, and if tidaling stops the tube is blocked or the lung has re-expanded. The suction control chamber sets the amount of negative pressure that reaches the chest, usually by bubbling air through a column of water, and continuous bubbling in the water seal means air is still leaking. The suction level and the water seal depth must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before suction is applied.

The hazards of a chest drain begin at insertion and never really stop. The trocar, a sharp point on some large tubes, can injure the lung, the liver, the spleen, or the heart, which is why many clinicians now use a blunt technique with a guidewire. Dislodgement is the next hazard, because air can rush back into the chest through the open tract and produce a tension pneumothorax. Clamping a bubbling drain is a common and dangerous operator error, since it traps air that is still leaking and converts a controlled situation into an emergency. The characteristic alarm is the sudden deterioration of the patient, with falling oxygen saturation, rising heart rate, and a shift of the trachea. The insertion technique and the clamping rules must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

Daily chest drain care is mostly observation and documentation. The dressing at the skin is kept clean and occlusive, the tube is secured so that a turn in bed cannot pull it, and the tubing is kept free of loops and never lifted above the chest. Milking or stripping the tube to move clots is traditional but can generate very high negative pressures, so it is done only when the local protocol allows and never routinely. The output is measured and described, including its color and whether it is blood, pus, or serous fluid, because the rate of bleeding guides the decision about surgery. The insertion site, the drain depth, the swinging, the bubbling, and the volume are recorded at every shift, and the dressing type and measurement interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

The field variant of chest drainage strips the system down to a tube and a one-way valve. The collection chamber, the water seal, and the suction control are replaced by a small flutter valve that lets air and fluid out of the chest and closes against the flow back in. The whole device is light, needs no electricity and no water, and can be taped to the chest wall, which makes it usable during transport and in a tent. What it gives up is the ability to measure suction and to see tidaling, so the clinician loses two important signs. What it demands instead is careful observation of the patient and of the drain, because the valve will not announce a blockage. The valve specification and the drain size must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

Abdominal drains clear the space left by surgery, infection, or injury, and they work by gravity, by capillary action, or by suction. The flat latex strip drain is a soft ribbon with no lumen that relies on capillary action and gravity, and it is usually placed through a separate stab wound and covered with a dressing. The firm cylindrical drain is a perforated tube with a rounded tip that keeps a track open so fluid can enter along its length. The closed suction drain connects a perforated tube to a compressible bulb reservoir, which is squeezed flat so that it draws fluid out and holds it in the bulb. The pigtail drain is a thin curled catheter locked into a cavity with a suture and drained by gravity or gentle suction. Each design has a different removal rule, because a flat strip can be pulled early while a suction drain must be withdrawn gradually, and the dwell time and removal technique must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

Abdominal drain care is a study in watching what comes out. The output is measured and described, and a sudden change from serous fluid to blood, bile, or bowel content is a signal that requires an urgent decision. The common operator error is to advance a drain that has been pulled outward, because pushing it back can carry skin organisms deep into the wound. Another error is to leave a drain in place because the output has stopped, when a stopped drain may mean the cavity is empty or that the holes are blocked against tissue. The safety hazard is erosion, where a rigid drain presses on a blood vessel or the bowel and slowly cuts into it. The output chart, the securing method, and the removal criteria must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

The T-tube drains the biliary tree after surgery on the bile ducts. It is shaped like the letter T from which it takes its name, with a short crossbar that sits inside the duct and a long limb that exits through the abdominal wall. The crossbar keeps the tube in place without a balloon and holds the duct open while it heals. Bile flows out through the long limb into a collection bag, and the volume and color of that bile tell the clinician whether the duct is patent. The tube is usually left for days to weeks and then removed, because the track from the duct to the skin must mature before the tube is pulled. The removal time and the clamping trial that precedes it must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

A tracheostomy tube is a short curved tube placed through an opening in the front of the neck directly into the trachea. It bypasses the upper airway and is used when the airway is blocked, when long-term ventilation is needed, or when secretions cannot be cleared. The outer cannula is the main tube that sits in the trachea, the inner cannula is a removable liner that can be taken out and cleaned, and the obturator is a smooth guide used only during insertion and then removed. A cuff is a balloon near the tip that seals the trachea so that air from a ventilator does not leak past the tube, and the flange and ties hold the whole assembly at the neck. The cuff target is a pressure of 20 to 25 millimeters of mercury, or about 25 to 30 centimeters of water, measured with a dedicated manometer, and that pressure must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

The cuff is a seal and not a tourniquet, and its pressure must be kept within a narrow range. The capillaries in the tracheal wall are perfused at a low pressure, and a cuff that presses harder than that pressure stops the blood supply and damages the mucosa. The safety hazard is the overinflated cuff, which leads to tracheal stenosis, fistula, and necrosis, often noticed only after the tube is removed. The common operator error is to inflate the cuff by feel or by the number on the pilot balloon instead of using a gauge. The cuff pressure and the measurement interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

The characteristic emergencies of a tracheostomy are decannulation, blockage, and bleeding. Decannulation means the tube has come out of the trachea, and if the tract is not yet mature the stoma may close within minutes. Blockage is usually caused by dried secretions in the inner cannula, which is why the inner cannula exists and why it is cleaned on schedule, and bleeding may come from the stoma, from the tracheal mucosa, or from a vessel eroded by the tube. The ventilator announces trouble with a high-pressure alarm when the tube is blocked and a low-pressure alarm when the cuff leaks or the tube is displaced. The operator must check the ties, the cuff pressure, the inner cannula, and the patency of the tube at every shift, and every alarm threshold must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

Routine care keeps a tracheostomy safe, and it begins with suctioning. A sterile catheter removes secretions that the patient cannot cough out, and humidification is essential because the nose no longer warms and moistens the air, so a heat and moisture exchanger or a saline instillation is used according to the protocol. The stoma is cleaned and the dressing changed, and the ties are replaced by two people, one holding the tube while the other works, because a tube released at the wrong moment is a lost airway. A spare tube of the same size, a smaller tube, a suction device, and a bag and mask are kept at the bedside at all times. The suction depth and the humidification method must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

The field variant of the tracheostomy and its care is defined by what can be kept clean and what can be improvised. A reusable metal tube with no cuff is common, because it is durable, easy to boil, and less likely to be damaged, but it cannot seal the airway for positive-pressure ventilation. Humidification may fall back to instilling a few drops of sterile saline and relying on the patient's own cough, and the inner cannula is cleaned by hand with a brush and then boiled or soaked in disinfectant. What this gives up is the precision of a cuff and the convenience of a disposable inner cannula. What it demands instead is a caregiver trained to clean, to suction, and to recognize blockage before the patient becomes exhausted, and the cleaning method and the instillation volume must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

A suction catheter is a thin, flexible tube with a small opening at the tip and a side hole, and it removes secretions from the airway by connecting to a vacuum source. The physical principle is that airflow, not force, carries the mucus, so the catheter needs an opening that lets air move past the tip. The thumb port at the proximal end controls the suction, because while the port is open air enters and the vacuum at the tip is low, and when the thumb covers the port the vacuum acts. The catheter is chosen so that it does not occupy more than about half the internal diameter of the airway or the tracheal tube, because a catheter that fills the lumen blocks the air the patient needs. Suction is applied only while the catheter is withdrawn with a gentle rotation, the pass should last no more than about 10 to 15 seconds, and the patient is allowed to recover between passes. The safety hazard is hypoxia during a long or repeated pass, the common operator error is to apply suction while the catheter is advanced rather than withdrawn, and the characteristic alarm is the falling oxygen saturation on the monitor. The catheter size, the vacuum level, and the pass duration must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

The field variant of suction replaces the wall vacuum and the electric pump with a hand or foot pump, a large syringe, or a simple mouth-operated device with a trap. The principle is unchanged, because a partial vacuum is still created by expanding a chamber. A foot-operated pump gives a more sustained vacuum than a syringe and is preferred for thick secretions, while a large syringe attached to the catheter clears a small amount of mucus but demands two hands and cannot be repeated quickly. What this variant gives up is the steady, adjustable vacuum of a hospital wall outlet. What it demands instead is a second trained person and a reliable method of cleaning the device between patients, and the vacuum produced and the cleaning method must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

The consumables that surround these tubes are as important as the tubes themselves, and a ward that runs out of them stops being able to use its catheters safely. Sterile gloves, sterile lubricant, and antiseptic solution are needed for every insertion, and sterile water or saline is needed to inflate balloons and flush lumens. The correct syringe is needed to inflate a balloon without overpressure, while securing devices, tape, and skin barrier protect the skin and hold the tube. Drainage bags, canisters, and clamps handle what leaves the body, and specimen containers, indicator strips, and labels support the tests and the documentation that make a tube auditable. Stock levels, expiry dates, and storage conditions must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

Reprocessing applies to the reusable minority of these devices, and the rules are stricter than for almost any other item. A reusable silicone catheter or metal tracheostomy tube must be cleaned of all visible soil before disinfection or sterilization, because a film of protein protects organisms from both. Sterilization by steam in an autoclave at 121 degrees Celsius for 20 minutes, or at 134 degrees Celsius for a shorter cycle, is the usual method for heat-tolerant items, and those cycle figures must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol. Single-use catheters are labeled for one use, and reusing them, even with careful cleaning, carries a real risk of infection and of material failure. The safety hazard is the device that looks clean but hides biofilm in a lumen, and the common operator error is to shorten the contact time when the ward is busy.

Documentation is what turns a set of tubes into a safe system. Every insertion is recorded with the type of device, the size, the date and time, the depth or the marking, the indication, and the name of the operator. Every shift records the output, its color and character, the condition of the site, and any complication, and every removal records the date, the reason, the condition of the tube, and whether a specimen was sent. The safety hazard is a tube whose record has been lost, because the next clinician cannot judge whether it is old, infected, or misplaced. The common operator error is to chart the tube only at insertion and then trust memory, and the record format must be confirmed against the local protocol.

The failure modes of tubes are few and they repeat across every device in this chapter. Occlusion happens when the lumen fills with clot, feed, encrustation, or secretions, and dislodgement happens when the securing fails or the patient pulls the tube. Migration happens when the tube moves inward or outward and no longer sits where it was placed, and infection happens when the foreign surface meets organisms and a biofilm forms. Erosion happens when a rigid tube presses on tissue for long enough to injure it, while kinking and leaking are the mechanical failures, and a kinked tube behaves exactly like a blocked one. Every one of these has a sign that a careful observer can find before the patient is in danger.

At the start of every shift, the operator must walk the tubes in their care and check them one by one. The securing is tested gently by hand, the depth mark is read and compared with the record, and the drainage system is checked for position, kinks, and dependent loops. The site is inspected for redness, swelling, discharge, or bleeding, and the output is read and charted with its character. The balloon or cuff is checked if the protocol requires it, and the emergency equipment for a tracheostomy or a chest drain is confirmed to be present and working. Anything that has changed since the last shift is reported in the handover, and the review interval must be confirmed against the local protocol.

Competence with these devices is not memorizing a size chart but understanding a principle and recognizing a failure. A clinician who knows why a balloon holds and why a water seal works will notice when either stops behaving normally. In a low-resource setting the deeper problem is usually the supply chain rather than the skill, because a ward with no catheters, no lubricant, and no bags cannot deliver safe care no matter how well it is staffed. The practical response is to standardize on a small range of sizes, to keep a documented reserve, and to train one person per shift to manage the stock and the records. The reserve level and the resupply interval must be confirmed against the local protocol and the manufacturer's instructions before a shortage is discovered at the bedside.

To summarize, a catheter, a tube, or a drain is a controlled path for fluid, and its behavior follows from pressure, lumen size, and the properties of the material. The hospital-grade version surrounds the tube with sterile technique, closed drainage, calibrated suction, and a chart that records every change, and it depends on clean water, electricity, and a supply chain. The field version replaces that infrastructure with clean intermittent catheterization, gravity, a flutter valve, a hand pump, and a trained caregiver, and it gives up measurement and margin in exchange for portability. Every one of these devices is a foreign body, so the universal rules are to secure it, to record it, to watch it, and to remove it as soon as the need has passed. The hazards that matter most are infection, dislodgement, blockage, and erosion, and the operator errors that cause them are pushing against resistance, trusting a whoosh, clamping a bubbling drain, and inflating a balloon before the tip is in the bladder. The alarms, where they exist, are the falling oxygen saturation, the high and low ventilator pressure alarms, and the rising temperature, and none of them replaces the nurse who looks at the tube. Every setting, pressure, volume, and cycle figure in this chapter must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before it is used on a patient.
