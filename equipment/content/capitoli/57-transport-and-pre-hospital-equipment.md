# Chapter 57 — Transport and Pre-Hospital Equipment

The physical problem of transport is that a device must keep working while the ground beneath it moves, so every transport device is built around stored energy and restraint. A hospital machine draws power from a wall socket and gas from a pipeline, while a transport machine carries its own battery and cylinder and must survive being lifted, dropped, and braked. The second principle is inertia, because a vehicle in a sudden stop keeps its contents moving until something stops them, and the force on a loose object rises with the square of its speed. A transport device is therefore a compromise between the capability of a ward machine and the mass and power that one person can carry. That compromise explains the small screen of a transport monitor and the short cylinder life of a portable ventilator.

An ambulance is a family of vehicles separated by how much medicine they deliver while moving. The simplest is a van or pickup with a flat floor and one stretcher, a vehicle for carrying rather than treating. The most capable is a box vehicle with a permanent oxygen supply, a suction line, mains power from an inverter, and room for two clinicians beside the stretcher. The layout principle is that everything must be reachable from a seated or kneeling position, because the vehicle is a treatment room that is falling over. A bulkhead separates the cab from the patient compartment, and the layout of any class must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the vehicle is placed in service.

Securing is the least glamorous and most important task in the vehicle, and it rests on a calculation of deceleration. A stretcher and patient may weigh well over 100 kilograms, and the restraint must hold many times that in a crash, so fittings are rated in multiples of the load rather than by how firm they feel. The correct fittings are rails, brackets, and quick-release cradles tested to a stated load, and those values must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A cylinder is doubly dangerous because its regulator can shear off and turn it into a rocket, so it travels in a bracket with the valve protected. The commonest error is the hurried departure in which the last item is placed on the bench and never strapped, so a pre-departure check in which every item is touched and named is the only reliable defense.

The wheeled stretcher, or cot, is the central object of the vehicle, and its principle is a platform on a linkage that can be raised, lowered, and locked. The frame carries a mattress on a hinged backrest, the undercarriage collapses for loading, and side rails keep the patient from rolling off. The cot locks into the floor by a capture mechanism, and an unlocked stretcher is a heavy object on wheels that will travel forward in a crash. The commonest error is failing to hear the click of the floor lock, and the warning is a mechanical indicator that the crew must check by pulling the stretcher back before driving. The height, load rating, and locking procedure must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the mattress must be wiped and the straps inspected after every patient.

A spine board is a rigid slab that turns the whole body into a single unit so that the injured spine is not bent. A body on a soft surface flexes at every bump, concentrating movement at an unstable fracture, so a rigid platform spreads the load and limits relative motion between vertebrae. A scoop stretcher applies the same principle in two halves that slide under the patient and lock together, allowing a casualty to be lifted without being rolled onto the side. The commonest error is leaving the patient on the hard board long after transfer, because a rigid surface concentrates pressure on the sacrum, heels, and back of the head and can cause a pressure injury within hours. The correct sequence, the board's weight limit, and the interval before the patient is moved off it must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the straps and blocks must be checked for cracks.

The vacuum mattress is a bag of small beads that becomes a rigid shell, and its physics is friction between grains. When the air is withdrawn, outside pressure presses the beads together, friction rises sharply, and the bag hardens into the shape of the patient around whom it was molded, which distributes pressure far better than a board. The hazard is a slow leak, because a mattress that has lost its vacuum looks inflated and feels firm at the edge while the middle has gone soft, and the patient can shift unnoticed. The check is therefore a vacuum gauge or a firm hand at the start and end of transport, and a softened mattress must be repumped or replaced. The required vacuum level and pump strokes must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the valve and cover must be inspected for punctures and wiped between patients.

A transport monitor is a ward monitor asked to shrink, and what it gives up reveals its physics. It measures the electrocardiogram from adhesive skin electrodes, the blood pressure by an inflatable cuff, the oxygen saturation from the differing absorption of red and infrared light, the breathing rate, and often the carbon dioxide in the exhaled breath. The electronics are packed into a sealed case with a battery, and the screen is small while the alarms must be heard over an engine and a siren. The hazard is motion artifact, because every bump produces a false reading and every loose electrode a false alarm, and the commonest error is to silence the alarm instead of correcting the cause. The upper and lower alarm limits, the alarm volume, and the interval between charges must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The monitor must be strapped to its bracket, because an unsecured monitor is a heavy object in a crash.

A transport ventilator generates a breath without a hospital pipeline, and the way it does so defines its limits. A ward ventilator may use piped gas pressure to drive a bellows or turbine, so it has unlimited gas and mains power, while a transport ventilator makes its own pressure with a small turbine, piston, or compressed-gas injector and therefore spends battery charge or cylinder gas for every breath. The settings are the same family as a ward machine, a volume or pressure for each breath, a rate, a positive end-expiratory pressure, and an oxygen concentration, and every value must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The hazard is disconnection during movement, and the characteristic alarm is a low-pressure or apnea alarm announcing a circuit that has come apart. The commonest error is to watch the screen rather than the chest, and every ventilator must be checked with a test lung before it leaves the station.

Portable oxygen is stored energy in its most concentrated form, and its arithmetic governs every transport plan. A cylinder holds gas at high pressure, and the usable volume equals the water capacity multiplied by the gauge pressure, so a small cylinder holds far fewer liters than its size suggests. A flow of 15 liters per minute through a reservoir mask empties a small cylinder in minutes, while 2 liters per minute through a nasal cannula may last an hour, and both flows must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The regulator reduces cylinder pressure to a safe working level, and the flowmeter shows the rate as a ball in a tapered tube, read at the top edge of the ball and not its middle. The hazard is a cylinder that is only half full, and the warning is a gauge falling faster than the flow should allow or a low-pressure whistle. The commonest error is to transport a patient on a cylinder never weighed or checked, and a spare cylinder must always be secured upright as part of the equipment.

Portable suction provides the same pressure difference as a wall vacuum line, but makes it with its own pump or with a human hand. A battery unit uses a diaphragm or rotary pump to draw air from a canister, and a manual unit uses a piston or bellows squeezed by the operator, so the suction lasts as long as the operator's arm. The vacuum is measured in millimeters of mercury, and a typical adult setting is between 80 and 120 millimeters of mercury, a value that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A float valve rises as the jar fills and shuts the suction path to protect the pump, and the characteristic alarm is the sudden loss of vacuum when it rises. The hazard is a canister filling during transport with no one watching, and the commonest error is to keep suctioning through a full jar and drive fluid into the pump. The catheter must be measured against the patient and the vacuum released between passes, and the battery, canister level, and tubing must be checked before every shift.

An infusion pump in a moving vehicle fights gravity, and its physics differs between a syringe pump and a volumetric pump. A syringe pump drives a plunger at a controlled rate with a leadscrew, accurate and compact but limited to one syringe, while a volumetric pump squeezes tubing with a rotating cam or fingers and can run for hours from a bag. Both depend on a battery and both must be secured so that the pump and bag move with the patient rather than across the compartment. The specific hazard of a syringe pump is free flow, because when the syringe is removed the plunger can be pushed by gravity and the drug runs uncontrolled, so the line must be clamped before changing the syringe. The characteristic alarms are occlusion from a kinked tube or positional cannula and air in line, which demands that the line be cleared. The rate, alarm thresholds, and battery duration must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the commonest error is to hang the pump on a hook never designed to carry it.

The transport defibrillator is the same capacitor device used in resuscitation, but it must work on a patient lying on a moving stretcher. The physics is charge stored on two plates separated by an insulator and released across the chest in thousandths of a second, with energy measured in joules and a typical adult biphasic shock between 120 and 200 joules, an energy level that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The hazard is that the operator or stretcher may be in contact with the patient when the shock is delivered, so the command to clear must be spoken and obeyed even in a cramped space. The characteristic alarms are the rising charge tone and the low-battery tone, and a self-test failure when the unit is switched on means that the device must be withdrawn from service. The commonest error is a pad whose gel dried in a hot vehicle, and the device, pads, and battery must be checked at the start of every shift.

A cervical collar is foam and plastic that limits neck movement, and its principle is to stiffen a joint by wrapping it in a shell. It reduces flexion and extension reasonably well and rotation less well, so it aids immobilization but does not replace manual in-line stabilization while it is applied. Collars are sized by the height from shoulder to jaw, and a collar of the wrong height either fails to support the head or pushes it into a harmful position, so size and fitting must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The hazard is a collar applied too tightly, which presses on the neck veins and can raise pressure inside the skull, and the second is pressure injury where the rim presses on the chin, the shoulders, or the back of the head. There is no alarm, which is why the collar must be reassessed and the skin under it inspected. The commonest error is to fit the collar first and stabilize the neck afterwards.

A traction splint pulls a broken thigh into line, and its principle is a balance of forces across the fracture. A fractured femur is surrounded by powerful muscles that shorten the limb, and that shortening increases pain, bleeding, and damage to nearby vessels and nerves, so the splint pulls at the ankle and takes counter-traction against the pelvis. Counter-traction is taken on an ischial pad or half-ring, and tension is applied by a ratchet or windlass with a strap around the ankle. The hazards are pressure on the perineum or the foot and a limb that is over-pulled, so the pulse, color, and sensation in the foot must be checked before and after application. There is no alarm, and the check is the operator's hand and eye, which is why this device fails silently in unpracticed hands. The traction force, pad position, and reassessment interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A pelvic binder is a wide band wrapped around the hips to close a fractured pelvis, and its physics is circumferential compression. A broken pelvis can open like a book, and the widening increases the pelvic volume so that blood pools there in liters, while a binder across the greater trochanters reduces that volume and can limit the bleeding. The correct level is the greater trochanters, the bony points at the widest part of the hips, and a binder placed too high does nothing and may press on the abdomen. The hazards are pressure damage to the skin and a binder so tight that it obstructs breathing or hides the abdomen from examination. There is no alarm on fabric, so position and tension must be checked by hand and the binder released once the patient is in hospital. The application position, tension, and duration must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The bags are where the hospital becomes portable, and they are organized by the same spatial logic as a crash cart. The airway bag holds the laryngoscope with spare batteries, blades in several sizes, tracheal tubes, a supraglottic airway, stylets, a bag-valve-mask, suction catheters, and a carbon dioxide detector. The trauma bag holds dressings, tourniquets, chest seals, bandages, splints, shears, and a thermal blanket, while the drug bag holds the small pharmacy chosen for pre-hospital use with its ampoules, vials, syringes, and a sharps container. A tourniquet stops arterial bleeding, a chest seal keeps air from entering the chest through a hole, and blankets and warmed intravenous fluid protect a cold patient who clots badly. Each bag should have a contents list on the outside and a numbered seal so that a missing item is visible without opening it, and the seal numbers and the list must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The commonest error is the unrecorded replacement, in which a used item is swapped from another bag so that the quantity is right but the location is wrong, and every bag must be restocked from a written list at the end of every shift.

Communication equipment is the part of the kit that lets the vehicle ask for help, and its physics is radio. A transmitter creates an oscillating current in an antenna, the antenna radiates electromagnetic waves, and a receiver tuned to the same frequency turns them back into sound, while higher frequencies travel less well around obstacles but carry more data. A vehicle may carry a very high frequency radio, an ultra high frequency radio, a cellular telephone, and a satellite telephone, and the choice depends on terrain and on the local network. The hazard is the dead zone, a valley or tunnel where the radio cannot reach the base, and the commonest error is to leave the handover until the vehicle is moving. The channels, call signs, and failure procedure when the network is absent must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and every battery must be charged at the end of the shift.

Tracking keeps a moving patient visible to the system that will receive them. The pre-hospital record carries the time of the call, the time of arrival, the vital signs, the treatments, the drugs and doses, and the change in the patient's condition. The handover passes all of that knowledge to the receiving team, and its equipment is a structured form and a spoken format listing the situation, background, assessment, and recommendation in a fixed order so that nothing is forgotten under pressure. An electronic health record can attach the pre-hospital note to the hospital record, but only if the two systems exchange it, and the formats that make that exchange possible must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The commonest error is a handover delivered while the receiving team is still connecting monitors, so that the information is spoken into a room that is not listening. A good handover names the patient, mechanism, time, findings, treatment, and the question the team must answer next.

The neonatal transport incubator is a small intensive care unit on wheels, and its first principle is that a newborn loses heat far faster than an adult because the surface area is large relative to the mass. A premature infant cannot shiver effectively and has little insulating fat, so the incubator provides a warmed microenvironment with controlled temperature and humidity, usually with a forced-air heater, a skin temperature probe, and a transparent hood. It carries its own oxygen, its own monitor with the electrocardiogram, oxygen saturation, and temperature, and often its own small ventilator or a continuous positive airway pressure generator, and every temperature, oxygen concentration, and pressure setting must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The incubator locks into the ambulance floor, and the infant is strapped inside in a way that allows treatment without lifting the baby out. The hazard is overheating or underheating, because a loose skin probe reads the air rather than the baby, and the characteristic alarm is a temperature alarm that must never be silenced without inspecting the probe. The commonest error is opening the hood repeatedly in a cold vehicle, and the warming battery also runs the monitor. The team must be able to ventilate, warm, and monitor the infant at once, and the incubator must be checked against its own checklist before every transfer.

Maintenance of transport equipment is organized around the battery, the cylinder, and the seal, because those three fail quietly. A battery loses capacity with age and heat, so a device that ran for hours when new may run for minutes after a year in a hot vehicle, and the state of charge and age of every battery must be recorded rather than assumed. Cylinders are weighed or their pressure read, exchanged below the reserve level, and stored upright, and the reserve level and exchange procedure must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Cleaning follows the rules of any patient-contact equipment, with a detergent wipe for surfaces, a disinfectant at the correct contact time for soiled parts, and attention to crevices, straps, and cuffs where blood and vomit collect. The vehicle is decontaminated after every patient who may carry an infection, including the mattress, rails, and suction canister, and consumables rotate by expiry date with the oldest at the front. The commonest maintenance failure is the spare borrowed and never returned, because the kit looks complete until the item is needed.

The field variant begins where the road ends, and the honest comparison starts with what disappears. A motorcycle, a boat, a bicycle, a donkey or another pack animal, or a pickup truck used as an ambulance gives up the walk-through compartment, piped oxygen, the inverter, the suction line, the seated workspace, and the secure brackets that make a crash survivable. What it demands instead is that every item be carried in a bag one person can lift, that the oxygen be a cylinder the carrier can bear, that the monitor run on a battery recharged from a solar panel or a vehicle, and that the patient be held by straps rather than a stretcher lock. A motorcycle trailer carries a stretcher and one clinician, a boat crosses a flooded road, and a pickup with a canopy carries a mattress on its bed, and in each case the ride is harder and the restraint more improvised. The load limit, strap arrangement, and oxygen quantity for the journey must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the field clinician must decide early that a patient needing a hospital ventilator, blood, or surgery must travel toward the hospital rather than wait.

What the field kit loses in capability it must regain in discipline, and the first discipline is packaging. Every item is packed by function into a bag that is waterproof, numbered, and light enough to hand up a slope or across water, with contents listed on a card that can be read in the dark. The ventilator of the field is usually a self-inflating bag with a valve and mask, or a small gas-driven ventilator that consumes oxygen, and the monitor is often a handheld pulse oximeter and a manual blood pressure cuff rather than a full screen. Suction becomes a syringe, a bulb, or a foot pump, and infusion becomes a bag squeezed by hand or a spring-driven pressure infuser, so the accuracy of a pump is replaced by a count of drops. The principle of the drip count is that a known number of drops equals a known volume, so the rate is set by watching the chamber and timing the drops, and the drop factor of the giving set must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The hazard is that a hand-squeezed infusion has no occlusion alarm, and the commonest field error is to overload the carrier, because a bag too heavy to run with arrives late.

Power and communication are the two field systems that fail most often, and both must be planned before the patient is loaded. A vehicle charging system, a solar panel, and a small generator are the usual sources, and each device is charged in rotation so that no single failure leaves the team without a monitor, with the state of charge written on a tag at the end of every day because a discharged defibrillator is a case with a handle. A radio reaching the base station may be the only link for hours, and a satellite or mobile telephone with a stored local number is the backup, so the frequencies, call signs, and contact schedule must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Heat is the enemy of every battery and every drug, because a vehicle parked in the sun can reach a temperature that destroys both, so drugs travel in an insulated box opened as little as possible. The commonest organizational failure is the absence of a written plan for who is contacted when the first radio call fails.

Failure in transport is usually a chain rather than a single event, and the chain almost always begins with an unchecked item. The oxygen runs out because the cylinder was not weighed, the ventilator stops because the battery was not charged, the monitor alarms falsely because the electrode was placed over hair, and the patient deteriorates because no one was watching the chest while the team watched the screen. The equipment answer is the checklist, a written sequence read aloud at the start of the shift and again before departure, with every item touched and named. The check that matters most is function rather than presence, because an item present but not working is worse than a gap, as it creates false confidence, and a ventilator is tested with a test lung, a defibrillator with its self-test, and a suction unit by blocking its tip. The service interval, battery replacement schedule, and checklist items must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The commonest error is to sign a checklist from memory, and a kit that fails in the field almost always failed first in the station, unnoticed.

The most important piece of transport equipment is a decision, and that decision is whether the patient's needs can be met in motion. A patient who needs complex ventilator modes, a blood transfusion, an operation, or imaging that exists only in a building must move toward that building, and the transport kit exists to keep the patient stable along the way rather than to replace the destination. The principle of the decision is that every device carried into the field takes space, weight, and attention from the patient, so the smallest kit that meets the need is the safest. A short journey with a stable patient may need only oxygen, a monitor, and access, while a long journey with a sick patient may need ventilation, infusion, warming, and suction, and the difference must be recognized before the doors close. The clinical triggers for immediate transport, the maximum safe transport time, and the level of monitoring required must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The commonest error is to keep treating on scene until every possible intervention has been attempted, when the intervention the patient needs is the movement itself.

To summarize, transport and pre-hospital equipment is the hospital compressed into a vehicle and then into a bag, and its two governing principles are stored energy and restraint. The ambulance is a treatment room that falls over, so every stretcher, cylinder, monitor, and pump must be locked into a rated bracket, and the vacuum mattress, spine board, scoop stretcher, collar, traction splint, and pelvic binder all work by limiting the movement of a broken body. The transport monitor and the transport ventilator reproduce a ward's measurements and breaths from a battery and a cylinder, and their settings, gas consumption, and battery life differ from the ward in ways that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Portable oxygen, suction, infusion, and defibrillation each carry the same physics as their hospital counterparts, with a smaller reservoir and a shorter warning, and the neonatal transport incubator adds temperature control and demands its own checklist. In the field the motorcycle, the boat, the animal, and the pickup truck give up the compartment, the pipeline, and the bracket, and demand instead light bags, charged batteries, weighed cylinders, a drip count, and the judgment to move early. What never changes is the habit that makes all of it work: know what is in the bag, test the function and not the presence, secure every item before the wheels turn, and confirm every setting and threshold against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before it is used on a patient.
