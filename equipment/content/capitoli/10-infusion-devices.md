# Chapter 10 — Infusion Devices

An infusion device is any apparatus that moves fluid into a patient at a rate someone has chosen and can defend. Fluid flows from a place of higher pressure to a place of lower pressure, and the volume delivered in a given time depends on that pressure difference and on the resistance of the tubing. In a gravity set the pressure comes from height, and a column of fluid standing 1 meter above the vein produces roughly 74 millimeters of mercury, a value that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. In a pump the pressure comes from a motor that squeezes or pushes the fluid, and the machine meters volume rather than trusting height. Every device in this chapter is one of those two ideas, and the hazard is always the same, because the amount delivered can differ from the amount intended.

The gravity set is the simplest complete device in the hospital and the measure against which every other system is understood. It is clear plastic tubing, usually polyvinyl chloride, PVC for short, with a spike that pierces the fluid bag, a drip chamber below it, a filter in the line, a roller clamp as the regulator, and a connector that locks into the cannula. The drip chamber is a flow indicator and an air trap, because air from an empty bag rises and collects there instead of reaching the patient. A typical filter has a pore size of 15 micrometers, a value that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and it holds back glass, rubber, and precipitate. Injection ports allow a drug to be added without a new puncture, and each port is a place where contamination can enter. The common operator error appears at the first step, spiking the bag with the roller clamp open so that fluid and then air run down an unconnected line.

Because the drip chamber makes drops, the gravity set measures rate without electricity. The set is calibrated so that a known number of drops makes 1 milliliter, and that number is the drop factor, commonly 10, 15, 20, or 60 drops per milliliter, a calibration that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A set of 60 drops per milliliter is called a microdrip set, and its small drops allow finer control of slow infusions. The operator counts drops for 1 minute and converts the count into a rate using the drop factor and the prescribed volume and time. A misplaced decimal or a mistaken drop factor changes the dose by a factor of ten, and the mistake is invisible once the fluid has run in. The characteristic alarm of a gravity set is not a sound but the attention of the nurse, and the common error is to set the clamp by eye once and never count again.

When a small controlled volume must be given, the hospital uses a burette set or volume control set. A graduated chamber of 100 or 150 milliliters, a capacity that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, is filled from the main bag, and only the fluid inside that chamber is connected to the patient. This lets the nurse give a measured volume of a diluted drug and then stop, which is why the set is used for children, for potent drugs, and for infusions where a rapid bolus would be dangerous. The physics is unchanged, because the chamber still empties by gravity through the same roller clamp. The hazard is the clamp between the main bag and the chamber, which, if left open, turns a guarded volume into a free-running infusion, and leaving it open is the common error.

A gravity infusion has no alarm, and this absence defines how it must be watched. The hazards are air embolism, unnoticed occlusion, infiltration of fluid into tissue, phlebitis along the vein, and free flow from a clamp left open. Air embolism occurs when air enters a vein in quantity, usually through a disconnected or badly primed line or a central catheter whose hub is open to the air. Infiltration begins as swelling, coolness, or pain at the cannula site, which is why the site is inspected at a frequency that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. An occlusion is quiet, because the chamber stops, the site looks unchanged, and the patient receives nothing. The common error is to trust the bag and the clock rather than the chamber and the arm, so the ward routine becomes the only alarm the set has.

The volumetric infusion pump removes the arithmetic and the eye from the gravity set. Its mechanism is peristalsis, the principle a gut uses, because a row of small fingers or cams presses the tubing closed in sequence and each step pushes a fixed small volume along the line. The pump counts steps and therefore measures what it has delivered rather than inferring it from drops, and it uses either a dedicated cassette with valves or a channel that squeezes ordinary tubing. The rate is set in milliliters per hour, and a typical range runs from 1 to 999 milliliters per hour, all of which must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The pump is accurate only with the tubing it was calibrated to use, so loading another pump's set can change the delivered volume while the display still looks correct. The safety hazard of the device is the free-flow state, when the tubing leaves the pump and the line becomes a gravity set with no regulator.

Modern pumps protect themselves with sensors, and understanding each one explains the alarms it raises. An air-in-line sensor passes ultrasound across a small section of tubing, where fluid carries the sound and air reflects it, and the change trips the alarm. An occlusion sensor reads pressure, and it may look upstream toward the bag, where an empty bag or a closed clamp raises the alarm, or downstream toward the patient, where a kink or a clotted cannula does. The occlusion threshold is often adjustable, for example between 100 and 500 millimeters of mercury, a range that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Raising that threshold to the maximum to silence a recurring alarm is the common operator error, because it makes the pump tolerate a real obstruction. The characteristic alarms of the pump family are air in line, downstream occlusion, upstream occlusion, door open, battery low, and pump error.

The alarms of a volumetric pump must be answered in a fixed order, and it is not the order the sound invites. The line is clamped first, because a pump that has stopped may release the tubing and let gravity take over. The patient is examined second, for the cannula site, the arm, the breathing, and the color, because an alarm may be the first sign of an infiltration that has been developing for an hour. The line is checked third, for kinks, closed clamps, a full chamber, and a bag that has run dry, and only then is the pump examined. Good sets carry an anti-free-flow clamp that closes when the tubing is released, a feature that must be tested and never disabled, a requirement that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The common error is to silence the pump and walk away, which turns a brief interruption into an unmeasured infusion.

The hospital pump is only as good as the consumables that join it to the patient. Each pump family uses a dedicated administration set or cassette that carries the priming volume, the filter, the injection ports, and the connector the calibration assumes, and priming must fill the whole line before it is connected. Extension sets, stopcocks, and needleless connectors add dead space, and every added connection can separate or become contaminated. A set is changed on a schedule set by policy, commonly every 24 hours for a continuous infusion and sooner for blood, lipid, or suspected contamination, and that schedule must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The line is labeled at the pump and at the patient with the drug and the rate, because a tangle of identical tubing is a recognized cause of the wrong drug reaching the wrong lumen. The common error is to add a second infusion to a set already in use, mixing two drugs in one line and losing the ability to stop either alone.

A syringe pump is the precision instrument, and it gains accuracy by giving up volume for control. The syringe is held in a cradle, its plunger is captured by a driven screw, and a stepper motor advances that screw at a chosen linear speed, so flow equals the cross-sectional area of the barrel multiplied by the speed of the plunger. The pump must know which syringe it is pushing, and it asks the operator to select the size and the model, because the barrel's diameter is part of the arithmetic. Because the barrel is narrow, the volumes are small and the steps are fine, and rates as low as a fraction of a milliliter per hour are possible, values that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. This is why syringe pumps carry vasoactive drugs, insulin, and opioids, and the characteristic alarms are occlusion, plunger not engaged, syringe dislodged, and near end of infusion. The physical hazard is siphoning, because a syringe above the patient whose plunger is not firmly held can let fluid run by gravity at an uncontrolled rate, and the common error is to seat the syringe without confirming that its flange and plunger are captured.

Patient-controlled analgesia, PCA for short, is designed around the patient's own hand, and its safety lies in the limits locked inside it. The machine is a syringe pump or a small volumetric pump with a handset that carries a single button, and the patient presses it to request a small bolus of analgesic. The pump enforces three boundaries, the size of the bolus, the lockout interval during which no second bolus is given, and a cumulative limit over a set period, and each of these values must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. These settings sit behind a locked access, often a key, a code, or a physical cover, so the patient cannot change them. The hazard is not the machine's arithmetic but the people around the bed, because a relative who presses the button on the patient's behalf can cause sedation and respiratory depression, and that is the most common operator error in PCA care. The characteristic alarm is the occlusion alarm, and the monitoring that accompanies a PCA is the sedation score and the breathing rate.

Enteral feeding pumps look like infusion pumps and are deliberately made to be different. They move liquid nutrition, water, or medication into the stomach or small intestine through a feeding tube, and their sets are wider, differently connected, and built to tolerate a thicker fluid. The mechanism is usually peristaltic, the rate is set in milliliters per hour, and a typical range runs from 1 to 300 milliliters per hour, a range that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The hazard that defines this device is the wrong route, because an enteral set connected to an intravenous line delivers food into the bloodstream and is a recognized fatal event, which is why adapters that defeat the differently shaped connectors are dangerous. Aspiration is the second hazard, and it follows a displaced tube, a patient lying flat, or a stomach that is not emptying. The characteristic alarm is the occlusion or empty container alarm, and the common error is to hang the feed without first confirming that the tube is still in the stomach, so the operator's checks are the tube position, the residual volume, and the elevation of the head of the bed.

The elastomeric pump is the device for places where electricity is a rumor, and it contains no motor, no battery, and no keys. Its engine is a stretched elastic membrane inside a rigid housing, filled under pressure at the pharmacy or the factory, and its recoil pushes the fluid out at a nearly constant pressure. The rate is set not by a controller but by a flow restrictor, a very narrow tube of precise length and diameter at the outlet, whose resistance fixes the flow for a given fluid and temperature. Because the driving force is elasticity and the restriction is fixed, the delivered rate depends on the viscosity of the drug and on the temperature, and a rise of a few degrees Celsius thins the fluid and speeds the flow, a sensitivity that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The hazard is that silent variation, and the common error is to store a filled pump against the body or in the sun. The characteristic signal is not an alarm but the visible collapse of the balloon, which tells the observer the dose has been delivered.

A blood or fluid warmer is a heat exchanger that prevents the patient from losing temperature when a large volume enters a vein. Two fluids at different temperatures, separated by a conducting wall, move toward a common temperature, and the warmer uses a water bath, a dry heating plate, or a countercurrent path beside a warmed stream. The target is body temperature, near 37 degrees Celsius, and a warmer is set to bring fluid to that point rather than above it, a setting that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The upper limit matters because warming blood above about 41 degrees Celsius causes hemolysis, a ceiling that must also be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the damaged cells release potassium and free hemoglobin. The hazard is the overheated unit or the bag placed on a radiator, and the characteristic alarm is the over-temperature alarm. The common error is to warm a bag on a radiator or in a sink of hot water, and the field version is a basin of warm water or a warm pack that demands a thermometer rather than a thermostat.

The infusion stand or pole is the least respected piece of equipment in this chapter and the cause of a recognizable class of injury. It is a vertical pole with a telescoping section, several hooks, and a weighted base, usually with five legs and small casters, and its stability depends on the width of the base and the height of its center of gravity. Hanging a pump high on the pole raises the center of gravity, which is why every pole carries a weight limit and a maximum number of pumps, values that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A rolling pole that crosses a threshold or a wet floor can catch a caster and go over, dropping a pump onto the patient, the line, or the nurse's foot. The common error is to push a heavily loaded pole by its top or to hang extra pumps on a pole designed for one, and the daily check is short, because the casters roll and lock, the base is intact, and the hooks are not bent.

Behind every hospital pump lies an infrastructure the operator rarely sees and always depends on. The pumps draw power from the wall, and in an intensive care unit they are commonly plugged into a circuit supported by an uninterruptible power supply, UPS for short, so a brief failure of the mains does not stop a vasoactive drug. The pharmacy prepares the drugs, and the ward supplies the sterile single-use tubing, filters, connectors, and labels whose packaging must be intact and whose expiry date must be in the future. Many pumps carry a drug library, a stored list of standard concentrations with limits, and the operator must select the correct entry rather than override it, with every pressure limit and alarm threshold confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Cleaning and maintenance decide whether the pump is still the device the manufacturer calibrated, so the housing, keypad, door, and cradle are wiped with the approved disinfectant and the pump is never immersed. The parts that soil most are the parts that fail, namely the door seal, the occlusion sensor and air sensor windows, and the tubing channel, and the common error is to leave a cracked case or a swollen battery in service.

Alarm handling is a skill, and it begins with the refusal to press the silence button first. The sounds a pump makes are information, because air in line points to the bag or the priming, a downstream occlusion points to the cannula or the patient's arm, an upstream occlusion points to a closed clamp or an empty bag, a battery alarm points to a socket, and a pump error points to the device itself. The trouble-shooting sequence is therefore physical before electrical, so the operator looks at the patient, then along the whole line, then at the bag and the chamber, and only then at the screen. Alarm fatigue is the quiet hazard, because a pump that alarms often is a pump whose alarms are ignored, and the response to a nuisance alarm is to find its cause rather than raise a threshold, with every adjustable threshold confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The common error is to restart the pump to clear an alarm without correcting the condition, which resets the alarm and hides the problem. The failure modes are few, because over-infusion follows a programming error, a miscalculated drop rate, a raised threshold, a siphon, or an open clamp, while under-infusion follows an occlusion, a kink, an empty bag, or a dead battery, and most are prevented by watching the site, confirming the rate, and never leaving a line unclamped.

The daily check is the ritual that makes an infusion safe, and it takes only a few minutes when done the same way every time. The operator confirms the patient, the drug, the concentration, the route, the rate, and the volume to be infused against the written order, and reads the values back aloud when a second person checks a high-alert drug. The pump is checked for a mains connection, a charged battery, a set within its labeled use-by date, a primed and bubble-free line, and a chamber at its marked level. The site is inspected for redness, swelling, coolness, pain, or leakage, and the line is followed from the bag to the patient to confirm that every connection is tight and no clamp is closed by accident. The alarm function is tested at the start of the shift on many protocols, and every rate limit and occlusion threshold used in the check must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The common error is to perform the check from memory, which is how a decimal point and a drop factor change places.

The field variant of an infusion device begins with what the field does not have. A mobile clinic or a frontier health post usually lacks reliable mains power, dedicated pump tubing, calibrated cassettes, a pharmacy that prepares standard concentrations, and a technician who can service a peristaltic mechanism. What it has instead is a gravity set, a bag, a vein, a watch, and a person who can count, and in the field the gravity set is not a backup but the ordinary instrument. The field gives up the pump's memory, its alarms, and its precision, and it demands arithmetic, a written rate, and a person who watches. The bag is hung as high as the situation allows, because the height sets the pressure and therefore the rate, and the drop factor is read from the packaging. If the packaging is missing, the operator calibrates the set by counting how many drops fill a known volume, then converts the prescribed volume into drops per minute and counts for a full minute. The rate is recounted after every bag change and at intervals that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, because in the field the observer is the alarm.

The wide-open clamp is the field's characteristic catastrophe, and it is worth describing precisely. A roller clamp opened fully offers almost no resistance, so a full bag of 1,000 milliliters, a volume that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, can empty into an adult in a few minutes at a height of 1 meter. This happens when a clamp is knocked by a passing patient, when a clamp that no longer grips is kept in service, or when a hurried operator opens the clamp to catch up a delayed infusion. The consequences range from fluid overload and pulmonary edema to an air embolism if the bag empties and the line is not clamped, and the common error is to widen the clamp to catch up without ever remeasuring the new rate. The safeguards are physical and procedural, so the operator uses a clamp that grips, keeps it turned toward the closed position when not adjusting it, never leaves a full bag running unobserved above a patient, and prefers the narrowest tubing that will carry the required flow. Field improvisation also raises pressure, whether by raising the bag or by wrapping a blood pressure cuff around it, and any external pressure applied to a fluid bag must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

When a field clinic does have a battery pump, the battery becomes the central consumable. The device is charged whenever the sun or a generator allows, a spare battery or a charged power bank is carried, and the pump is used only for drugs whose interruption would harm the patient, because a pump that dies mid-infusion is worse than a gravity set that never needed power. The elastomeric pump is the field's answer to this problem, since it carries its own stored energy in a stretched membrane and needs nothing from the grid, while enteral feeding is usually done by gravity with a syringe and a funnel. Warming is done in a basin of water or with a warm pack, and the operator must use a thermometer to be sure the fluid does not exceed a safe temperature, a limit that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Infection control is harder, because the field may reuse tubing and connectors when supplies run out, and every reuse is a trade between scarcity and sterility, so a reused set is flushed, inspected, and stored dry, its ports are disinfected before every access, and it is replaced at the earliest opportunity. The common error is to keep a set in service because it looks new, when the part that matters is the inside of the connector.

The final safeguard in every setting is the person at the bedside, and the human factors of infusion care deserve the same attention as the mechanics. Labels must be legible and complete, tubing must be traced rather than assumed, and high-alert drugs must be checked by two people who read the values aloud and compare them with the order. Training should cover the arithmetic of drop rates, the loading of each pump used in the facility, the meaning of each alarm, and the physical signs of infiltration and overload. Documentation should record the drug, the concentration, the rate, the volume, the start time, the site, and every change, and equipment failures belong in the incident system rather than in the corridor. Every default value, alarm threshold, and concentration stored in a pump library must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The operator error that outranks all the others is silence, which is not reporting the alarm that kept sounding or the clamp that would not hold.

To summarize, an infusion device is a way of turning pressure into a measured dose, and the two sources of that pressure are height and a motor. The gravity set, its drip chamber, its drop factor, and its roller clamp remain the physical foundation on which every pump is built, which is why counting drops is still a clinical skill worth keeping. The volumetric pump, the syringe pump, the patient-controlled analgesia pump, the enteral feeding pump, the elastomeric pump, and the blood warmer each add control in exchange for consumables, power, and maintenance. The safety hazard is almost always a mismatch between the intended and the delivered amount, the common operator error is a rate that was set once and never verified, and the characteristic alarms of the pump family are air in line, occlusion, door open, and battery low. The hospital supports these devices with power, dedicated sets, a pharmacy, a drug library, and a technician, while the field replaces them with arithmetic, observation, and a clamp that is never trusted, so the discipline that unites both halves is to know the rate, watch the site, clamp the line, and never leave an infusion to look after itself.
