# Chapter 25 — Wrist and Hand Injuries

The wrist and the hand carry the work of a whole life, so an injury there is as serious as a bleeding thigh. Scene safety comes before the patient, because the machine that caused the injury may still be running, the blade may still lie in the wound, and the traffic that caused the fall has not stopped. The lethal possibility to name first is a hand or a finger that has lost its blood supply, revealed by a digit that is pale, cool, and insensate beyond a wound. The second is hemorrhage that soaks through every dressing while the patient turns pale and restless. Personal protective equipment, or PPE, guards against the patient's blood, and the same moment guards the patient against hypothermia, because an injured hand on cold ground loses heat quickly. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country where you work.

The mechanism of injury is the first clinical evidence, because it predicts structures that no eye can see. A fall onto an outstretched hand drives force through the wrist and can break the distal radius, dislocate a carpal bone, or fracture the scaphoid beneath intact skin. A punch against a wall loads the knuckles and may break a metacarpal neck or drive a tooth into a joint through a wound that looks like a scratch. A blade makes a wound whose depth cannot be judged from its length, and a crush beneath a wheel injures bone, tendon, nerve, and vessel together. These injuries are missed because attention goes to the larger wound elsewhere, so the trained responder examines both hands in the secondary survey and compares them.

Life comes before limb, and an injured hand does not change that order. A patient with a shattered wrist and a closed airway is treated for the airway, and a patient with an amputated finger and a chest filling with air is treated for the chest. The hand can wait for minutes that the brain and the heart cannot, and saving a fingertip while the patient dies saves nothing. The responder completes the primary survey in the fixed order of ABCDE before turning to the wrist, and returns to it whenever the patient changes. Only when the patient is breathing, circulating, and protected does the hand become the urgent problem, and then tendon, nerve, and vessel tolerate delay poorly.

Some anatomy must be held in the ear, because the examination depends on it. The radius on the thumb side and the ulna on the small-finger side meet the wrist, and the radius carries most of the load. The small bones of the wrist move as a unit, and the scaphoid lies at the base of the thumb with so fragile a blood supply that a break can starve it. Tendons that bend the fingers run through sheaths on the palm side and tendons that straighten them along the back, while the median, ulnar, and radial nerves cross the wrist to the hand and the radial and ulnar arteries meet in a loop within it. The position of function, with the wrist slightly extended and the fingers gently curled, is the posture in which the hand works best.

The examination begins with looking and compares the injured hand with the uninjured hand. The responder notes the position of the limb, any deformity, the color, the swelling, the wounds, and the posture of the fingers at rest. Feeling follows, with gentle touch along the bones and a question about where the pain is worst, because an injured hand does not need to be hurt further. Movement is tested last and in the smallest amount that answers the question, because a torn structure can be torn further by a vigorous test. The trained responder records the findings before any splint and repeats them afterwards, since a hand that was intact before a splint and numb after it tells a story about the splint.

The neurovascular examination decides the destination and is repeated before and after every intervention. Color and warmth are compared with the uninjured hand, and a finger that is pale, blue, or cooler than its neighbor must not wait. Capillary refill is judged by pressing the nail bed until it blanches and counting the seconds until color returns, and a refill time greater than 2 seconds is a warning that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the accepted figure shifts with room temperature, age, and lighting. A single pulse felt at the wrist does not prove that the other artery is open, so both are sought. Sensation is tested in the territory of each nerve, and the distance at which separate points of touch can still be told apart is measured where training allows, with a distance such as 5 millimeters offered only to explain the reasoning and requiring confirmation against the current guideline and the local protocol. Movement is tested by asking the patient to bend and straighten each finger against light resistance, which asks whether the tendons remain connected.

A sprain and a severed structure can look alike from outside, and the examination separates them. A sprain hurts at the joint that is moved rather than along a tendon or in the territory of a nerve. A divided tendon changes what the finger can do, so a finger that cannot bend at its far joint when the nearer joint is held straight has lost the deep flexor tendon even if the wound is small. A divided extensor tendon leaves a finger that droops and cannot be lifted against gravity, and a divided nerve leaves skin that cannot feel and muscle that cannot act. The response is not to explore the wound, not to pass an instrument along it, and not to test the finger again and again, because each act may complete a partial division. The correct act is to cover the wound, splint the hand, record exactly what the patient could and could not do, and send the patient to a surgeon.

The distal radius fracture is the most common serious injury of the wrist and usually follows a fall on the outstretched hand. The finding is pain and tenderness just above the wrist on the thumb side, with swelling, bruising over hours, and sometimes a deformity in which the far fragment tilts backward so the wrist looks like a fork. The lay first aider does not straighten that deformity, because the bone ends may rest against a vessel or a nerve and movement can turn a closed injury into an open bleeding injury. The trained responder splints the wrist and forearm, supports the joint above and the hand below, checks the fingers before and after, and keeps the arm in a sling so its weight does not pull on the break. Definitive hospital care restores the length and angle of the bone and fixes it when the break is unstable or the joint surface is involved, and that reduction belongs to the surgeon and not to the field.

Among the small bones of the wrist, the scaphoid hides for weeks, and missing it is a classic error. It lies at the base of the thumb, and a break may cause only a dull ache, with tenderness in the hollow at the base of the thumb when the thumb is drawn back. Suspicion must rise when that hollow is tender and loading the thumb along its length hurts, even when the first radiograph shows nothing. A scaphoid fracture can take 10 to 14 days to appear on a repeat radiograph, a period offered only as an illustration and to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, and the delay matters because the blood supply enters from a single direction and a break can cut it off. A missed scaphoid dies and leaves an arthritic wrist, a permanent loss for a patient who arrived with a sore thumb. The trained responder splints the wrist and thumb whenever that tenderness is present and asks for computed tomography, or CT, or magnetic resonance imaging, or MRI, where available.

The bones of the palm break in patterns named by the finger they serve. A punch against a hard surface commonly breaks the neck of the metacarpal of the small finger, and the back of the hand looks bruised with a knuckle that has lost its height. The finding that most changes the plan is rotation, in which a finger turns and crosses its neighbor when the patient curls the hand into a fist, because a rotated finger that heals rotated will not close the hand again. The lay first aider supports the hand in the position of function, keeps the fingers apart with soft padding, and does not pull on a finger to straighten it. The trained responder splints with the knuckles bent and the fingers gently curled, checks sensation and pulses afterwards, and sends the patient for surgical assessment when rotation, an open wound, or several broken bones are present.

The fingers break in ways that decide whether a fingertip will ever straighten or bend again. A blow to the tip of an outstretched finger can tear the tendon that straightens the far joint and leave the tip drooping, an injury called a mallet finger. A forceful pull on a bent finger can tear the tendon that bends the far joint from its attachment and leave a finger that cannot curl at the tip. A break through the body of a phalanx causes swelling, tenderness, and sometimes a visible bend, and a break into a joint may leave a fragment that blocks movement. The lay first aider does not pull on the finger, because a fragment already pulled from the bone may be pulled further away, and a finger splinted as found is safer than a finger that has been manipulated. The trained responder splints the finger in the position found, pads between the fingers, and refers every injury involving a joint surface or an open wound.

A dislocation of a finger joint is unmistakable when the finger bends at an angle no joint allows, and the lay first aider must resist pulling it straight. A displaced joint may rest against a nerve or a vessel, and a blind reduction can injure what is still intact, while a joint reduced without assessing the tendons may hide a torn structure. A dislocation at the base of the thumb is a different matter, because the ligament holding the thumb to the palm may tear with a fragment of bone and leave the thumb weak and painful if it is treated as a sprain. The finding is tenderness over the inner side of that joint with pain when the thumb is bent away from the palm, often after a fall on a ski pole or onto the thumb. Definitive hospital care examines and images the joint and repairs the ligament, and no field test or field reduction takes its place.

Wounds of the wrist and the hand are deeper than they look, because tendons, nerves, and vessels lie immediately beneath thin skin. A cut across the front of the wrist may divide several tendons and the median nerve at once, and a cut across the back of a finger may divide the tendon that straightens it, while the skin wound closes over the damage and looks small. The lay first aider covers the wound, controls bleeding with direct pressure around rather than over the wound, and does not pour anything into it or pack it tightly. The trained responder examines the structures, records what the patient can move and feel, and dresses the wound without a constricting bandage, because a hand that swells inside a tight dressing loses its circulation. Every wound that might involve a tendon, a nerve, a vessel, or a joint belongs to a surgeon, and repair is definitive care. Protection against tetanus is a clinical decision made under the local protocol by the provider authorized to make it, and the substance and the timing belong to the prescriber.

Nerve injuries change what the patient will be able to do and are easily overlooked while a wound is dressed. Injury to the median nerve weakens the muscles at the base of the thumb so the patient cannot bring the thumb across the palm, and it removes sensation from the palm side of the index and middle fingers. Injury to the ulnar nerve weakens the small muscles of the hand and removes sensation from the small-finger side, and the hand takes on a clawed appearance with a weak grip. Injury to the radial nerve removes the ability to lift the wrist, so the hand hangs down when the arm is held out, and it removes sensation from the back of the hand between the thumb and the index finger. The exact area of numbness and the exact weak movement must be recorded before and after every splint and dressing, and repair of these nerves is a surgical act performed in an operating room and never at the roadside.

The blood supply of the hand runs through the radial and ulnar arteries at the wrist and then along each side of every finger, so an injury to any vessel can threaten a digit. A laceration at the base of a finger may divide the vessel on that side, and the finger beyond becomes pale, cool, painful, and then insensate. A crush or a closed fracture can tear a vessel with no external wound, so a hand that is tense, cool, and painful out of proportion deserves the same alarm as a bleeding hand. The findings that circulation is failing are a finger that has changed color beside its neighbor, a slow capillary refill, a pulse that cannot be felt, and numbness reported by the patient. The lay first aider supports the limb as found, keeps it warm, and does not massage it or warm it aggressively, because heat increases the oxygen demand of tissue that is starved of blood. Definitive hospital care examines the vessels and decides whether the finger can be saved, and that decision cannot be made in the field.

An amputation of a finger or of a hand is a vascular emergency, because the remaining tissue and the separated part both lose viability from the moment of injury. The stump is controlled first with direct pressure over a dressing, and the responder does not clamp or tie vessels, because blind clamping crushes nerves that might be repaired and does not reliably stop bleeding. A tourniquet on the arm is a measure for the trained responder or the paramedic under a protocol when the stump will not stop, and once it controls the bleeding it is not loosened in the field, with the time written on the patient where the receiving team will see it. The severed part is wrapped in clean dry material and sealed in a bag, and the bag is placed in a cool container rather than directly on ice, because ice damages tissue the surgeon hopes to replant. A period such as 6 hours of warm ischemia is often quoted as the limit for replantation, and that interval is offered only as an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since tolerance differs with the level of amputation and the storage of the part. The part travels with the patient and its label and time, and the lay first aider does not place it back on the stump.

Splinting protects what remains, and the position of function is the shape in which it is done. The wrist is supported in slight extension rather than allowed to drop, the knuckles are bent as though holding a small ball, and the thumb is held away from the palm so the web does not tighten. The splint supports the joint above and the joint below the injury and must not press on a bony point or circle the limb so tightly that circulation is lost, which is why the fingers are checked after it is secured. Rings and bracelets come off early where they come off without force, because swelling turns a ring into a tourniquet within hours, and a ring that will not come off is reported rather than cut from a swollen finger by an untrained hand. The trained responder chooses the splint and the padding, while the lay first aider may support the limb on a folded magazine, a piece of board, or a sling, provided the support does not press on the wound.

Covering the wound is done with clean material rather than with anything expected to disinfect. The dressing is held with a bandage firm enough to stay and loose enough that the fingers beyond do not change color, and the fingertips are left visible so circulation can be watched. The responder does not probe the wound, remove a foreign body, or press on an embedded object, because a shard of glass or a fragment of metal may be plugging a vessel or resting against a nerve, and it is stabilized in place while the patient is moved with it in place. A bite, whether human or animal, is treated with particular suspicion, because the mouth carries organisms into a joint or a tendon sheath and infection can destroy a hand quickly. Protection against tetanus and the decision about antibiotics belong to the provider authorized by the local protocol and to the prescriber, while the lay first aider cleans the surface gently, covers the wound, and records the time and the cause.

A wound over a knuckle from a fight is the most commonly underestimated injury of the hand. When a person strikes another person's teeth with a closed fist, a small wound appears over a knuckle and may look like a scrape that needs only a dressing. The teeth have driven organisms into the joint beneath the knuckle, and the wound closes over them, so the hand becomes swollen, hot, and painful while the surface injury is already healing. The finding that reveals the depth is pain and swelling over a knuckle that grow over the following day, and every such wound is a joint injury until a surgeon says otherwise. The lay first aider covers the wound, supports the hand in the position of function, and insists that the patient be seen, because the patient who is embarrassed about the fight is the patient most likely to leave before assessment, while definitive care opens the wound, examines the joint and the tendon sheath, and makes the decisions about infection under the local protocol.

Compartment syndrome must be anticipated in the hand and the forearm rather than discovered, and its earliest sign is pain out of proportion to the injury. The finding that matters most is pain on stretching the fingers or the wrist, which is pain produced by moving a part that is not itself injured, together with a limb that is tense and numbness that appears later. A measured pressure such as 30 millimeters of mercury is sometimes quoted as a threshold for concern, and that figure must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the diagnosis rests on the whole picture and not on a single number. The lay first aider must not wait for a diagnosis, and must remove any bandage that has become tight, keep the limb as found, and send the patient to definitive care quickly. Release of the pressure, which is called fasciotomy, is a surgical procedure performed in an operating room and never a field act.

Some injuries of the wrist and the hand require surgical assessment, and the responder must recognize them without needing to name a diagnosis. An open wound over a joint, a wound that crosses a tendon, a wound that leaves part of the hand unable to feel or move, a deformity that does not improve with support, a finger that has lost its circulation, an unstable thumb base, a fracture that involves a joint surface, a fracture with rotation, an amputation, a bite, a crush with a tense swelling, and a tender hollow at the base of the thumb with a radiograph that shows nothing all belong in that group. The lay first aider does not decide which of these needs an operating room, and the lay first aider's duty is to keep the hand supported, keep the patient warm, and make certain that the patient reaches a place where the question can be answered. The trained responder chooses the destination under the local protocol, which may be a district hospital that can stabilize the limb and arrange a transfer or a trauma center with imaging, an operating room, and an intensive care unit. Every hand injury that might involve a tendon, a nerve, a vessel, or a joint is a patient for definitive care, and the field phase ends when that care begins.

Cold deserves a further word in a chapter about an exposed limb, because a hand on the ground while the responder works loses heat from a large surface. Wet clothing is replaced with dry covering where that can be done without disturbing the wound, the arm is wrapped along its whole length rather than only at the splint, and the patient is insulated from the ground and from the metal of a vehicle. A body temperature below 35 degrees Celsius is often named as the point at which cold becomes dangerous, and that figure is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the effects appear at different temperatures in different patients and the way the temperature is measured changes the reading. Cold makes blood clot poorly, deepens shock, and makes the heart more likely to develop a dangerous rhythm, so warming and bleeding control run together rather than in sequence. The receiving team should be told how long the hand was exposed, because exposure time is part of the injury and not a detail of the journey.

It is worth saying plainly what the lay first aider may do with a wrist or a hand, because the temptation to do more is strong when the injury is visible and the patient is in pain. The lay first aider may cover the wound, apply direct pressure around but not on an embedded object, support the limb as found, keep the fingers visible and the hand warm, remove a ring that comes off easily, place the arm in a sling, and send for emergency medical services without delay. The lay first aider may not reduce a dislocation, straighten a deformed wrist, pull on a finger, explore a wound, remove a foreign body, cut a ring from a swollen finger, apply a tight bandage, or give any substance by any route. The trained responder may splint, dress, reassess, and use the equipment and the procedures the service authorizes, and the paramedic may consider a tourniquet for a stump that will not stop bleeding, with the time recorded and the device left in place. Everything that restores structure, whether it is a bone, a tendon, a nerve, or a vessel, belongs to a surgeon in an operating room. Knowing the line between support and repair is the competence this chapter most wants to teach.

To summarize, the wrist and the hand are treated with the seriousness of any other limb, and the injury that matters most has lost its blood supply or its connection to tendon, nerve, or bone. Scene safety comes first, the lethal possibilities are a hand without circulation and a hemorrhage that will not stop, and the patient is protected against hypothermia while the wound is protected from probing hands. The mechanism of injury predicts damage beneath intact skin, the examination looks, feels, and moves in that order, and the neurovascular examination is repeated before and after every intervention, because the capillary refill, the pulses, and the sensation decide the destination. Distal radius fractures are splinted as found and never straightened, the scaphoid is treated as broken whenever the hollow at the base of the thumb is tender, and metacarpal, phalangeal, tendon, nerve, and vessel injuries each change what the hand will be able to do. Amputation of a digit is a vascular emergency in which the stump is controlled, the severed part is wrapped, cooled, and sent with the patient, and any tourniquet applied by the trained responder or the paramedic is not loosened and is timed on the patient. Splinting in the position of function, covering the wound, removing a ring before swelling, and moving early to definitive care are the acts that save hands, while reduction, repair, fasciotomy, and the removal of a foreign body belong to the surgeon and never to the field. Every time, pressure, temperature, depth, and figure in this chapter is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country where you work.
