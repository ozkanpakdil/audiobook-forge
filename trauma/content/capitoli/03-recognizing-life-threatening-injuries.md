# Chapter 3 — Recognizing Life-Threatening Injuries

An injury that will kill a patient within minutes seldom announces itself with the noise of an emergency, and the responder who waits to be impressed has already waited too long. The lethal injuries of trauma are recognized by a small number of findings that can be sought with the eyes and the hands, and each of those findings takes less than a minute to look for once the responder knows what it is. Before any examination begins, the scene must be made safe, because traffic that is still moving, fire, live electricity, deep water, unstable structures, animals, and violence are lethal possibilities in their own right, and a rescuer who becomes a second patient has helped no one. The fluids of the patient's body are a hazard as well, so gloves, eye protection, and a barrier for the mouth belong in place before the first hand is laid on a wound. This chapter names the injuries that kill in the first minutes and hours, names the finding that reveals each of them, and says plainly what each level of responder may do about them. It is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

The first discipline this chapter asks for is the difference between an injury that is dramatic and an injury that is lethal, because the two are not the same and the noisier one often takes the responder's attention. A patient with a broken forearm bent at an impossible angle will draw every eye on a scene and may be in no immediate danger at all, while a patient who is quietly pale, quiet, and slow to answer may be bleeding to death inside a body that shows no wound. A constructed illustration makes the trap clear, and it is offered only as a construction and never as a real patient or a real incident: at a road crash, one casualty cries out and holds a deformed arm while another sits silent and still against a wall, and the responder who runs first to the screaming patient has chosen the wrong order. The silent patient is the one to reach first when both can be reached, and the reason is not that the screaming patient does not matter but that the quiet one may have only minutes left. The responder who understands this keeps looking past the wound that is visible to the patient who is saying nothing, and asks what the mechanism of injury was and what forces the body met.

Massive external hemorrhage is the injury that can empty a patient in minutes, and the finding that reveals it is blood that is pouring, spurting, or pooling faster than a dressing can hold it. The lay first aider acts first and acts simply: firm direct pressure with both hands, or with a folded clean cloth if one is at hand, held without lifting an edge to see whether the bleeding has stopped. Lifting to look is the commonest way to lose the clot that has begun to form, so the pressure is held until a trained responder or the receiving team takes over. When bleeding from a limb continues despite correct direct pressure, the trained responder or paramedic may apply a tourniquet where protocol authorizes it, and once the bleeding has been controlled the tourniquet is not loosened in the field. The time it was applied is written on the patient in a place the receiving team will see, because the duration of limb ischemia decides what the hospital must do and how quickly. Where an object is embedded in the wound it is left exactly where it is and stabilized in place, since pulling it out may release hemorrhage that cannot be controlled and may tear vessels and nerves that are still intact. Blood loss also takes heat from the patient, so wet clothing is removed and the patient is covered while these actions are taken.

Not every lethal hemorrhage can be seen, and the finding that reveals concealed bleeding is a patient whose circulation is failing without a wound to explain it. Blood pooling in the abdomen produces a belly that becomes tender, then rigid, then visibly distended, and blood filling the chest produces a dullness on one side, breathlessness, and the same failing circulation. A fracture of the pelvis can bleed into the space behind the peritoneum, and a long bone can bleed into the thigh or the arm until the limb is tight and swollen. The lay first aider cannot see any of this and must not press hard into an injured belly, because pressing may increase the bleeding and may cause pain that hides the change. What the lay first aider can do is keep the patient still, keep the patient warm, call emergency medical services early, and report exactly what was seen: the pale skin, the confusion, the thirst, the restlessness. The trained responder watches the abdomen for distension and the chest for dullness, records repeated observations, and supports the failing circulation while the patient travels to a place where the bleeding can be stopped. Definitive hospital care opens the abdomen or the chest and controls the source, and no field measure substitutes for that operation.

Airway compromise is the first lethal possibility of the primary survey, and its findings are heard before they are seen: a snore, a gurgle, a whistle, a harsh rasping sound on inspiration, or a voice that has become hoarse. Blood, vomit, broken teeth, and swelling of the tongue or the throat can all close the passage, and a burn of the face, a blast injury, or a blow to the neck can narrow it over the following minutes without any wound at all. The lay first aider opens the mouth, removes only what is loose and visible, and if the patient is breathing and has no suspected spinal injury, turns the patient onto one side so that fluid drains rather than pools. Where a spinal injury is possible the head is kept in line with the trunk, and help is called before the patient is turned at all. The trained responder or paramedic may use suction, airway adjuncts, and oxygen according to protocol, and only a provider authorized by protocol performs the definitive airway that bypasses the obstruction. That definitive airway is never a first-aid measure, and a responder who attempts it without training and equipment can convert a partial obstruction into a complete one.

Tension pneumothorax is the chest injury that kills fastest, and it develops when air enters the space around the lung and cannot escape. The finding that reveals it is a patient who is increasingly breathless and distressed, with breath sounds that are absent on one side, a chest that sounds hollow when tapped, and neck veins that become distended as the pressure inside the chest rises. As the pressure continues to build, the heart is compressed, the pulse becomes weak and rapid, and the pressure falls, and a shift of the windpipe away from the injured side is a late sign that must not be waited for. The lay first aider cannot relieve this injury but can still save time by calling emergency medical services at once, keeping the patient sitting if that is more comfortable, and reporting that the breathing is one-sided and getting worse. Needle decompression and the chest drain that follows belong to definitive care, performed by a physician or by a paramedic where the local protocol explicitly authorizes it. The same signs appearing after such a procedure are a reason to look again, since the pressure can return.

An open chest wound, sometimes described as a sucking wound, declares itself by the sound of air moving through the opening with each breath and by bubbles appearing at the wound. The immediate danger is that air is drawn into the chest with every inspiration and cannot leave, so the wound can produce the same rising pressure as a tension pneumothorax. The trained responder covers the wound with a dressing sealed on most of its border but left free on one edge, so that air trapped inside can escape while air from outside is kept out, and the patient is watched closely for the return of breathlessness and the distension of the neck veins. A chest is never bound tightly, because tight binding prevents the chest from expanding and turns a survivable injury into a fatal one, and this is true even when the wound is small. The lay first aider covers the wound with a clean hand or cloth and does not tape it down on every side unless a trained responder directs it. Definitive hospital care places a chest drain and repairs the injured lung or chest wall, and that work belongs to the hospital and the surgeon.

Traumatic brain injury is dangerous less because of the blow than because of what happens inside the closed box of the skull afterward, where a small volume of blood can raise the pressure until the brain is pushed through the openings at its base. The findings that must be sought are a change in the level of consciousness, a headache that worsens, repeated vomiting, a seizure, weakness of one side of the body, an unequal pupil, and a period of apparent recovery that is then followed by deterioration. That interval of seeming recovery is a trap of its own, because the patient who speaks clearly and then becomes drowsy has not improved but has entered a more dangerous phase. The trained responder records the level of consciousness in a repeatable form using the Glasgow coma scale, writes the time beside each recording, and repeats it, because the direction of change carries more meaning than any single value. The lay first aider keeps the airway clear, keeps the patient still and warm, gives nothing by mouth, and reports every change in behavior to the arriving team. Computed tomography, or CT, of the head and any operation to remove blood or to relieve pressure belong to definitive hospital care, where the surgeon and the imaging suite take over.

Spinal injury does not always announce itself with paralysis, and the reason it must be assumed is that movement of an unstable spine can convert a recoverable injury into a permanent one. The mechanism of injury is the first clue: a fall from a height, a dive into shallow water, a collision at speed, a heavy object striking the head or the back, or any patient found unconscious after trauma. The findings that support it are pain or tenderness along the midline of the neck or the back, weakness or numbness in the limbs, a loss of sensation in a band around the trunk, and an inability to control the bladder or the bowel. The lay first aider keeps the head, the neck, and the trunk in line, does not move the patient unless there is immediate danger such as fire, and never asks the patient to turn the head or to sit up in order to test the neck. The trained responder applies manual in-line stabilization, uses a collar and a board where protocol provides them, and moves the patient with enough trained hands to keep the spine from bending or twisting. Straightening a deformed spine, or pulling on the head to realign it, is not a field procedure, and imaging of the spine with CT or magnetic resonance imaging, or MRI, belongs to the hospital.

Pelvic trauma is lethal in a way that is easy to underestimate, because the pelvis is a ring of bone with large vessels running against it, and a fracture that opens the ring can bleed into the space behind the abdomen without any swelling that a hand can feel. The mechanism is usually a high-energy one such as a collision between a vehicle and a pedestrian, a fall from a height, or a crush, and the findings include pain in the pelvis or the lower back, an inability to stand or to lift the legs, bruising over the perineum or the flanks, and blood at the opening of the urinary passage. The lay first aider does not spring the pelvis to test it, because repeated compression can make the bleeding worse, and instead keeps the patient flat, still, and warm. The trained responder may place a pelvic binder around the pelvis at the level the local protocol specifies, and once it is in place it is left there until the receiving team assesses it. Definitive hospital care includes early transfusion, imaging of the vessels, and stabilization of the ring in the operating theater, and none of that can be reproduced in the field.

Major fractures of the long bones are dangerous for two reasons that arrive together, because the bone itself can injure the vessels and nerves beside it, and the bleeding inside the limb can be enough to produce shock on its own. The findings of a fractured thigh are a swollen, tense thigh, shortening of the limb, and the foot turned outward, and the limb may look almost normal in the first minutes before the swelling grows. A fractured thigh can hold an amount of blood large enough to endanger the circulation, and a fractured pelvis can hold more, so a patient with either injury is treated as a patient in shock until the opposite is proven. The lay first aider does not straighten the limb to make it look normal, because forcing the ends of the bone into line may tear a vessel or a nerve that is still intact, and the limb is supported in the position in which it was found. The trained responder checks the pulse, the movement, and the feeling in the limb before and after splinting, splints the joint above and the joint below the fracture, and binds the limb to the body when no splint is available. Reduction of the fracture and the fixation that holds it belong to definitive care in the operating theater.

Traumatic shock is not a diagnosis but a state in which the delivery of oxygen to the tissues has fallen below what they need, and it begins long before the patient looks unwell. In its early phase the body defends itself: the pulse rises, the vessels of the skin and the gut narrow to preserve the brain and the heart, and the pressure is held near normal while the tissues that have lost their supply begin to suffer. The findings of this phase are a pulse that has climbed, skin that is cool and pale, a hand that feels clammy, a delay in the return of color after a fingernail or the forehead is pressed, and a patient who is anxious or unusually quiet. As the state worsens the pressure falls, the pulse becomes weak and fast, the breathing becomes rapid and shallow, and the level of consciousness declines. A pulse of 110 beats per minute, a pressure of 90 millimeters of mercury, or a capillary refill longer than 2 seconds are the sort of figures used to teach that change, but each of them must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the threshold that matters depends on the patient, on the patient's age, and on the service. The lay first aider cannot measure most of this and does not need to, since pale, cold, clammy, restless, and confused together are enough to act on: keep the patient flat and warm, control any bleeding that can be seen, and call emergency medical services without delay.

The patient who looks well and is not is one of the central traps of trauma, and it is produced by the body's own defense of the circulation. A young, fit patient can lose a large share of the blood volume and still hold a normal pressure while the pulse quietly climbs, so a reassuring pressure taken once is not evidence of safety. Children compensate in the same way and then deteriorate very suddenly, an athlete with a slow resting pulse may never show the expected increase, and a patient who takes medicine that slows the heart may not develop the rapid pulse that would otherwise give the injury away. A pregnant patient has more circulating volume than before the pregnancy, which hides loss for longer, and an older patient may have less reserve and may be taking medicine that changes the response to bleeding. The trend taken over time, with the same findings looked for in the same order and written down with the time of each set, is what reveals the patient who is compensating and beginning to fail.

The trend matters more than the single observation, and the reason is that a change over time belongs to the patient while any one figure may belong only to the moment. A pressure that was comfortable and is now lower, a pulse that was steady and is now quick and thready, a patient who was answering questions and now only nods: these movements say what the body is doing, and they say it before the final collapse. The trained responder therefore repeats the primary survey at intervals that the local protocol sets, records each set with the time, and compares rather than merely collecting. The lay first aider can contribute to this by staying with the patient and noticing what has changed, because the person who has watched from the beginning often sees the difference before anyone arriving can. Records written on paper or on the hand, with the time beside each finding, travel with the patient and shape the decisions of the receiving team.

Between the moment of injury and the arrival of help, the lay first aider holds the patient's chances in a small number of actions that require no equipment. The first is safety, for the rescuer and for the patient, because a second casualty makes the situation worse for everyone. The second is a call to emergency medical services that gives the location, the number of patients, the mechanism of injury, and the findings that worry the caller, since that information decides what help is sent and how quickly. The third is control of any bleeding that can be seen, by firm direct pressure that is not lifted to check, and the fourth is an airway kept clear and a patient kept still and warm. Nothing is given by mouth, nothing is removed from a wound, and no limb is straightened, because each of those acts has harmed patients who were otherwise surviving. The first aider who does these things, and who then watches without stopping, has done the work that this level of care owns.

The trained responder and the paramedic bring equipment, a protocol, and authorization to acts that the lay first aider must not attempt. Oxygen is given according to the protocol of the service, with the amount and the method set there and not in this book, and the decision to give it is made on findings such as breathlessness, a blue tinge of the lips, or a level of consciousness that is falling. A breathing rate that has risen above 30 breaths per minute, or a pulse that has become difficult to feel, is the kind of observation that changes the plan, though the exact figure that should trigger a given action must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol. The trained responder may apply a tourniquet, place a pelvic binder, splint a fracture, insert an airway adjunct, and suction the mouth and the throat, and each of those acts carries a finding that tells the responder whether it has worked. Analgesia is a decision rather than an instruction: the responder may recognize that pain is severe enough to threaten breathing and movement, but the substance, the amount, the route, and the interval belong to the prescriber and the local protocol, and the same is true of antibiotics and of tetanus protection. No amount, no regimen, and no route is given in this manual, because a listener must not be able to take a prescription from it.

Definitive hospital care begins where the field ends, and its first actions are a continuation of the same primary survey rather than a different subject. A patient with a suspected chest injury is assessed with ultrasound and a chest radiograph, a chest drain is placed if air or blood is compressing the lung, and a patient whose circulation is failing without an obvious cause may go directly to the operating theater. Computed tomography, or CT, answers questions about the head, the chest, the abdomen, and the pelvis, and magnetic resonance imaging, or MRI, is used for the spine and the spinal cord when the patient is stable enough to be in the scanner. The intensive care unit, or ICU, receives the patient who needs support of breathing or of circulation, and the trauma center that holds all of these resources is the destination the field team is trying to reach. Transfusion, the control of a bleeding vessel, the removal of a blood clot from the skull, the reduction of a fracture, and the repair of a torn organ all belong to this level. The first responder's task is not to perform them but to recognize the injury, do what the field permits, and deliver the patient to them in the best condition possible.

Each of these injuries has its own clock, and knowing the clock is what turns recognition into action. Airway obstruction and massive external hemorrhage act within minutes, tension pneumothorax within minutes after that, and a patient with a torn major vessel may be lost before any vehicle arrives. Internal bleeding, an expanding blood clot inside the skull, and the leakage of fluid into the tissues of a crushed limb act over a period of hours, which is why the quietly worsening patient is as dangerous as the dramatic one. The first 6 hours after a major injury are often described as decisive for the patient who needs an operation, and that interval is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the meaningful window depends on the injury, the distance to care, and the resources of the receiving service. The practical consequence is that recognition must be fast, and that transport must not be delayed by procedures which can wait.

Hypothermia is a complication of trauma rather than a detail of comfort, and it begins on the ground in the first minutes. A patient who is bleeding, burned, undressed for examination, lying on a cold surface, or receiving fluids at room temperature loses heat quickly, and cold makes blood clot poorly, worsens the state of shock, and makes the heart more likely to develop a dangerous rhythm. A body temperature below 35 degrees Celsius is the figure commonly used to describe the point at which these effects become serious, and that figure, like every other in this chapter, must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the accepted line varies between services and between editions. The lay first aider removes wet clothing, covers the patient with whatever is available, including blankets, coats, and foil, and insulates the patient from the ground. The trained responder continues that protection during transport and limits unnecessary exposure during the examination. Warmth is maintained for every patient who is bleeding, burned, or waiting, and it is not withdrawn when the patient begins to look better.

The errors that cost lives in this chapter are mostly errors of doing too much or of doing it in the wrong order. Removing a knife or a piece of glass from a wound, pulling a bullet out of the skin, pressing hard into an injured belly, springing the pelvis to see whether it is stable, straightening a deformed limb, binding the chest tightly, and loosening a tourniquet that has stopped the bleeding are each acts that can turn a survivable injury into a fatal one. Each of those acts belongs either to definitive hospital care or to no one at all, and the reason is always the same: the field cannot control what the act releases. The other family of errors is the error of attention, in which the responder treats the wound that is visible and misses the patient who is bleeding internally or the airway that is quietly closing. A third is the error of the single observation, in which a comfortable first reading is allowed to close the assessment. The antidote to all three is a fixed order of looking, a repeated examination, and a refusal to be reassured by a patient who is only briefly better.

To summarize, the injuries that kill in trauma are a short list, and each is recognized by a finding rather than by a name: hemorrhage that pours or hides, an airway that is narrowing, a chest under rising pressure, a brain whose level of consciousness is falling, a spine that must not be moved, a pelvis that can bleed without swelling, long bones that bleed enough to cause shock, and the state of shock itself, which begins quietly and ends quickly. The dramatic injury is not always the lethal one, the patient who looks well may be compensating, and the trend over repeated observations matters more than any single value. Every responder acts within a level of practice: the lay first aider gives pressure, position, warmth, and an early call; the trained responder and the paramedic add equipment and protocol; and the definitive procedures belong to the hospital and the surgeon. Every number in this chapter, whether a rate, a pressure, a temperature, or a duration, is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between services, countries, and editions. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.
