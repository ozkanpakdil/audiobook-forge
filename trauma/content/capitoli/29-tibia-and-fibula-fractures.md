# Chapter 29 — Tibia and Fibula Fractures

Two bones share the lower leg, and only one of them carries the weight of the body, so the pair can be broken in ways that look alike and behave very differently. The tibia is the thick inner bone that runs from the knee to the ankle and bears the load, while the fibula is the slender bone on the outer side that steadies the ankle. Before either is examined the scene is made safe, because traffic, fire, electricity, water, an unrestrained animal, a violent person, and a structure that may collapse are each a lethal possibility, and the patient's own blood is treated as infectious, with personal protective equipment, or PPE, worn wherever it is available. The lethal possibilities in a broken lower leg are named at the first look: an open wound that carries contamination into the bone, a leg swelling inside its skin until the pressure starves the muscle, and bleeding from a torn vessel that can fill the limb while the surface looks almost dry. The lay first aider may steady the limb, cover a wound, and keep the patient warm; the trained responder or paramedic may splint, control bleeding within a protocol, and monitor; and the cleaning of an open fracture, the reduction and fixation of the bone, and the release of a compartment belong to definitive hospital care. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

The tibia demands more caution than its size suggests because it lies directly beneath the skin along its front and inner border. In most of the body a broken bone is cushioned by a thick sleeve of muscle, but over the shin the covering is only skin, a thin layer of tissue, and bone, so a bone end that shifts can threaten the skin from the inside. A wound that seems small, a graze, or a puncture that a hurried responder might dismiss can therefore be the mouth of a track that reaches the fracture, and the break becomes open even when the bone is not visible. The fibula has more muscle around it and does not carry weight, so an isolated fracture of the fibula is usually a lesser injury, but the nerve that winds around its upper end can be injured by the same force or by a splint that presses there. The finding to seek on the outer side of the upper calf is a foot that cannot be lifted and a patch of skin on the top of the foot that has gone numb, because either one points to that nerve and changes the urgency of the journey.

The mechanism of injury is the first clinical evidence, and it predicts damage that no eye can see from the shape of the leg. A direct blow from a vehicle bumper, a motorcycle, a falling object, or a piece of machinery breaks the bone across its width and bruises the soft tissue at the same time, while a twist of the body against a planted foot produces a spiral break that may come with a torn ligament at the ankle. A fall from a height greater than 3 meters, offered only as a teaching illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, predicts compression of the spine and of the heels and should prompt an examination of the back, the pelvis, and the feet before attention settles on the leg. A crushing weight hides the most, because muscle beneath an intact skin can be damaged without an obvious fracture, and the same force that broke the tibia may have broken the thigh, the pelvis, or the ribs. The responder who reads the mechanism examines with a purpose, looking for the injury the force makes likely rather than the one that is most visible, and remembers that a patient who cannot breathe or circulate is treated before a leg is splinted.

A closed fracture of the tibia is one in which the skin over the break is intact, and closed must never be heard as harmless. The broken ends can have torn the vessels that run beside the bone or the nerves that cross it, and blood can collect inside the leg until the limb is tense and the circulation beyond the break is failing. The closed fracture is also the classic setting for compartment syndrome, because the fascia that wraps the muscle does not stretch and a swelling that has nowhere to go raises the pressure within. The findings that mark a closed fracture as serious are pain that is severe and increasing, a leg that feels hard and tight to the hand, numbness or tingling in the foot, and a foot that is pale or cool although the skin is unbroken. The lay first aider does not squeeze the leg to test it, tighten a bandage around it, or lift the patient to standing, and leaves the leg as it lies, covered, supported, and warm, while help is called.

Deformity is the sign that tells the responder most about the soft tissue, and it means any departure from the normal line of the leg, whether the bone is angled, the leg is shortened, or the foot is turned outward where it should point forward. The bone ends have come to rest in a position that may be pressing on an artery or a nerve without having torn it, and the tissue around the break is stretched or kinked in a way that straightening can reverse only by dragging the sharp end across what is still intact. The rule is therefore absolute and belongs to every level of provider: a deformed lower leg is splinted as it lies, and its shape is not corrected to make it look normal. The reason is survival of the limb rather than appearance, because a clot that has sealed a torn vessel can be released by the movement and a nerve that was only bruised can be cut, so an injury that was survivable becomes one that threatens the leg or the life. If the foot beyond the deformity is cold, pale, or pulseless, the responder says so plainly and shortens the journey rather than attempting a correction, because reduction is a decision for definitive hospital care and never a field procedure.

An open fracture of the lower leg turns a mechanical break into an infective one, and it is a surgical emergency from the moment it is recognized. The wound may be dramatic with bone visible in it, or it may be a small puncture over the shin that a responder almost misses, because a spike of bone can pierce the skin, withdraw, and leave a tiny mark above a widely contaminated track. The wound admits soil, road grit, fragments of clothing, and the material of whatever struck the leg into the same space as the broken bone, and the tissue around the break is often bruised and deprived of its blood supply, which is exactly the ground on which infection grows. The finding that establishes the fracture as open is any break in the skin of the leg near the break in the bone, and a responder who cannot tell whether the wound reaches the bone treats it as open until definitive care decides otherwise. No one pushes a protruding bone end back under the skin, puts a finger or an instrument into the wound, or pours anything into it, because each of those acts drives contamination deeper and may injure a structure that is still intact. The wound is covered with a clean or sterile dressing, the limb is supported where it lies, and the patient is moved toward a facility where the wound can be cleaned in an operating room.

Bleeding from an open fracture is controlled with firm direct pressure over a clean dressing placed around the wound rather than over the bone end, and if blood soaks through, further layers are added without lifting the first. A dressing pressed directly onto a protruding bone end achieves nothing and adds pain, so the padding is built up on either side of the bone and the force is directed at the soft tissue that is bleeding. An object embedded in the wound, whether glass, a splinter of metal, or a piece of the machinery that caused the injury, is stabilized in place with padding around it and is never removed in the field, because removal may release a hemorrhage that no dressing can control. A tourniquet on the lower leg belongs to the trained responder or the paramedic under a protocol, it is placed above the wound and never over the knee if a length of thigh is available, and once it has controlled the bleeding it is not loosened in the field. Its time of application is written on the patient where the receiving team will see it and spoken aloud at handover, because a limb deprived of blood has a limited tolerance and the receiving team must know how long the interval has been. A lay first aider without a manufactured tourniquet keeps firm pressure on the wound with both hands and does not improvise a narrow band, because such a band can tighten the limb without stopping the flow.

Care of the wound in the field is deliberately small, because the acts that clean and repair an open fracture belong to definitive hospital care. The responder covers the wound, protects the dressing from the pressure of the splint, and removes only obvious gross dirt from the skin around the wound without disturbing what lies within it. Nothing is poured into the wound, no antiseptic, no water under pressure, and no powder, because irrigation that is not performed with the right fluid, volume, and technique can push contamination deeper and damage tissue that is already fragile. The wound is not closed in the field by tape, by a suture, or by pulling the skin together, because a closed contaminated wound traps infection inside the leg and hides the tissue that the surgeon needs to see. The dressing is checked for strike-through as the patient is moved, a soaked dressing is covered rather than replaced, and the limb beneath it is examined for a swelling that is growing. The finding that should make the responder state the wound's age and contamination at handover is a wound produced by soil, by a farm machine, or by a delay of hours before help arrived, because those details shape the decisions about protection against tetanus and about antibiotics, and those decisions belong to the provider authorized to make them under the local protocol.

The decisions about protection against tetanus and about antibiotics in an open fracture are clinical decisions that belong to a prescriber working under a local protocol. The finding that makes the decision urgent is a wound that is deep, heavily contaminated, produced by soil or machinery, or seen many hours after the injury, and the responder's part is to describe those features accurately rather than to suggest a substance. The substance, the amount, the route, and the frequency are set by the local protocol and the prescriber in front of the patient, so no reader should take a prescription from these pages. The same rule governs pain relief, because a patient with a broken leg is in genuine pain, and untreated pain worsens the circulation and makes the patient move.

The neurovascular examination is performed in a fixed order at the foot, and it is the examination that decides how quickly the patient must travel. Circulation is judged by the color and the warmth of the skin of the foot, by the time it takes for color to return after the nail bed is pressed and released, and by the pulses that can be felt at the top of the foot and behind the inner ankle when the responder is trained to find them. A return of color that takes longer than 2 seconds is one figure used to describe poor flow, and that figure, like every other in this chapter, is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the acceptable time differs with the patient and with the temperature of the limb. Sensation is checked by asking the patient what can be felt, by touching the skin lightly on the top of the foot, between the first two toes, on the sole, and on each side of the leg, and by comparing the injured foot with the other one. Movement is checked by asking the patient to pull the toes up and to push them down, and where the patient cannot, the responder notes whether the failure comes from pain, from weakness, or from an obvious deformity. The lay first aider reports what the patient says and what the skin looks like, while the trained responder performs the full check and repeats it at every stage of the care.

Compartment syndrome is the complication of the lower leg that is most often missed, and it is missed because the leg can look almost undamaged from the outside while the tissue within is dying. The muscles of the lower leg are divided into four compartments by sheets of fascia, one at the front, one at the outer side, and two at the back, and each compartment is a closed space that cannot expand. When blood and fluid collect after a fracture, after a crush, or after a tight bandage, the pressure inside that space rises until it exceeds the pressure that fills the small vessels, and the muscle and the nerve within lose their blood supply. A fracture of the shaft of the tibia is the classic cause, and a splint applied too tightly, a circumferential bandage, and a limb that continues to swell can each produce the same result. The earliest and most reliable finding is pain that is severe and out of proportion to the injury, pain that is made worse when the toes are gently pulled upward or pushed downward, and a leg that feels tense and hard to the hand. The responder must not wait for the pulse to disappear, because the pressure that starves the muscle can be present while the main artery still carries blood, and waiting for a pulseless foot is waiting far too long.

The findings that follow the pain are numbness and tingling in the foot, weakness of the muscles that move the ankle and the toes, and a limb that is shiny and taut rather than soft. A patient who has been given strong pain relief, a patient who is unconscious, and a patient whose nerves are already damaged by illness may not report the pain at all, so the responder looks at the whole picture, including the swelling, the tightness, and the movement the patient can no longer make. The care that the field allows is narrow: loosen or remove anything that encircles the limb, release a bandage or splint that has become too tight where the protocol permits, keep the limb at the level of the heart rather than raised above it, keep the patient warm, and move toward the surgeon without delay. Elevation above the level of the heart is avoided when compartment syndrome is suspected, because it lowers the pressure that drives blood into the muscle and can deepen the injury even as it makes the swelling look less. The measurement of the pressure inside the compartment and the operation that opens the fascia belong to definitive hospital care, and a fasciotomy is never performed in the field by any responder, however urgent the leg appears. The lay first aider's part is to report the pain, the swelling, and the numbness honestly and to keep the leg still, since a leg that is moved and squeezed by many hands has been handled enough.

Reassessment is not one examination but a rhythm that begins at the scene and continues to the handover, and the same questions are asked in the same order each time. The circulation, the sensation, and the movement of the foot are checked before the splint is applied, immediately after it is applied, after every move of the patient, and at intervals during the journey. The purpose is not to find a new injury but to detect a change caused by the treatment, because a splint or a position that has made the foot colder, paler, more painful, or more numb than it was has become part of the problem. The findings are written down with the time of each entry and spoken aloud at handover, since the trend over time tells the receiving team more than any single reading. The findings that should send the responder back to the beginning of the primary survey are a falling level of consciousness, a changed breathing pattern, a pulse that has become fast and weak, and skin that has become cold and clammy, because those signs speak of bleeding or shock rather than of the leg alone.

Splinting the lower leg means holding both the knee and the ankle, because a bone that lies between two joints cannot be kept still by a support that stops short of either. A rigid support is placed along the back or the sides of the leg from above the knee to below the ankle, padded so that the force of the ties is spread, and secured at several points with broad strips rather than a narrow cord or a wire that concentrates pressure on one line of skin. A second support alongside the first, or the uninjured leg bound to the injured one with padding between the knees and the ankles, gives the same result when no manufactured splint is at hand, and the uninjured leg is a good splint because it is the right length and it moves with the patient. The foot is held at a right angle to the leg, neither dropped nor forced, so that the ankle does not fall forward and stretch the tissue at the back of the calf, and the heel and the tendon above it are supported rather than left hanging. The bony points that must be padded are the head of the fibula on the outer side of the upper calf, because the nerve passes there, the shin, the ankle bones on both sides, and the heel. The splint is checked as soon as it is in place, and the finding that it must be adjusted is a foot that has changed in color, warmth, or feeling.

Elevation and padding are the two details that make a lower leg splint safe to carry, and each has a limit the responder must understand. Padding under the whole length of the limb, including under the calf and the heel, spreads the weight and prevents the skin from breaking down over a bony point. Elevation of the leg above the level of the heart reduces swelling and eases pain in a closed fracture with an intact circulation, and it is a reasonable comfort measure while the patient waits or travels. The same elevation becomes harmful when compartment syndrome is suspected or when the foot is already pale and pulseless, because raising the limb lowers the pressure that pushes blood into the muscle and can worsen the very injury that is feared. For that reason the leg is kept at the level of the heart when the responder is worried about the compartment, and the decision is written down and passed on so that the next person does not raise it out of habit. The padding also protects against cold, because a patient lying still on the ground with a broken leg loses heat from the exposed limb, and warmth is a treatment rather than a comfort.

An open fracture of the tibia is a surgical emergency, and the operation it needs is called debridement, which means the removal of dead, contaminated, and foreign material from the wound together with washing of the tissues. The surgeon opens the wound fully, examines the muscle for tissue that has lost its blood supply, removes what cannot survive, and washes the wound under controlled conditions, and only then does the surgeon stabilize the bone and decide whether the wound can be closed or must be left open. A period such as 6 hours from the injury to the debridement is a common teaching figure for the window in which the risk of infection is lowest, and that figure is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the interval differs with the contamination, the state of the tissue, and the resources of the receiving hospital. The responder's contribution to that operation is made at the roadside, and it consists of covering the wound, keeping it still, controlling the bleeding, and delivering the patient quickly, because every hour of delay and every unnecessary handling adds to the work the surgeon must undo. Bleeding that continues despite pressure, tissue that has lost its color, and dirt or clothing driven deep into the wound are findings that make the journey urgent.

Definitive hospital care owns everything that aligns, repairs, and fixes a fracture of the lower leg, and knowing where that line lies is part of competence for every responder. A plain radiograph taken from the front and the side defines the pattern of the break, a computed tomography scan is used when the joint at the knee or the ankle is involved, and the images are read by the clinician who will decide the treatment. Reduction, which means bringing the broken ends back into alignment, may be performed by closed manipulation or through an operation, and fixation may be achieved with a rod placed inside the bone, with plates and screws along its surface, or with an external frame when the soft tissue will not tolerate an internal device. The surgeon also repairs a torn vessel, releases a compartment under pressure, covers exposed bone with a flap of tissue where that is needed, and closes the wound only when the tissue is clean enough to accept closure. The decisions about antibiotics, tetanus protection, fluids, and pain relief belong to the providers authorized to make them with the patient in front of them. The complications watched for over the following weeks are infection of the bone, failure of the break to unite, and union in a poor position.

The record and the handover carry the mechanism of injury, the time it happened, the state of every wound, the neurovascular findings before and after splinting, the material used, the time of every intervention, and every change observed along the way. The receiving facility is chosen for the care the patient needs, which may be a trauma center with imaging, an operating room, and an intensive care unit, and that choice is made before the patient is moved rather than after the journey has begun. Transport is urgent and gentle at once, because a jolting journey moves the broken ends, adds pain, and can convert a closed injury into an open one, so the patient is secured, the limb is supported, and the speed is the fastest that is safe. Life comes before limb when two correct actions compete for the same hands, so a patient whose airway is closed or whose circulation is failing is treated before the leg is splinted, even when the leg is the more frightening injury to look at. The two failures that run through the care of a broken lower leg are the compartment syndrome that was not suspected because the pulse could still be felt and the open fracture that was treated as a wound while the patient was bleeding somewhere else. Both are defeated by the same habits, which are to examine the leg rather than to look at it, to repeat the examination until the handover, and to say aloud what has changed.

To summarize, the tibia carries the weight and lies just beneath the skin, so its fractures are easily opened to contamination and easily hidden in their depth, while the fibula is a lesser bone whose upper end can still injure the nerve that lifts the foot. The mechanism of injury is the first evidence, a closed fracture is never a minor one, and a deformed lower leg is splinted exactly as it lies because straightening it can tear the vessels and the nerves that the position has spared. An open fracture is a surgical emergency, its wound is covered and never pushed, probed, poured into, or closed in the field, and the decisions about tetanus protection and antibiotics belong to the prescriber and the local protocol. Compartment syndrome is the complication most often missed, its earliest finding is pain out of proportion that worsens when the toes are stretched, and a pulse that can still be felt proves nothing, so anything that encircles the limb is loosened, the leg is kept at the level of the heart, and the patient moves quickly to the surgeon. Splinting holds the knee and the ankle, pads every bony point, keeps the foot at a right angle, and is checked before and after every move, and the neurovascular examination of the foot is repeated on the same rhythm until handover. Every number, time, pressure, temperature, and depth in this chapter is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between services, countries, and editions, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.
