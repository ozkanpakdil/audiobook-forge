# Chapter 13 — Gunshot Wounds to the Abdomen and Pelvis

A gunshot wound to the abdomen or the pelvis is a scene before it is a patient, and the scene must be made safe before anyone kneels down. The first questions are whether the person who fired is gone, whether the police or another authority has control of the area, whether a second shot or a moving vehicle is possible, and whether broken glass, fire, or a crowd threatens the responders. Gloves and personal protective equipment, or PPE, are worn before contact, because blood and body fluids from a firearm wound carry infection, and a responder who becomes a casualty doubles the number of people who need help. Only when the area is secure does the responder approach, and even then the approach is planned, with the patient moved as little as possible and the mind already fixed on the lethal possibility that dominates this chapter. That possibility is pelvic hemorrhage, the bleeding from torn vessels behind and within the pelvis, which can fill the space it creates and kill a patient whose skin shows almost nothing. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

The abdomen is the part of the body that hides its catastrophes best, and the reason lies in what it is, a cavity with room to spare. A wound of entry may be a small hole in the skin, sometimes no larger than a fingertip, while the track inside crosses several organs and opens a space that can hold a large volume of blood without pushing the wall outward enough to be seen. A bullet that enters the chest may pass downward through the diaphragm, and one that enters the abdomen may travel upward into the chest, so the visible wound is a poor guide to the organs at risk. A wound on the back, the flank, the buttock, or the groin may be hidden by the position of the patient or by clothing, and it will be missed by anyone who examines only the front. The abdomen also shares its neighborhood with the pelvis, where the bones, the bladder, the rectum, and the great vessels lie close together, and an injury to one of them rarely respects the boundary a textbook draws. The listener should therefore hold one rule above all others, that a gunshot wound anywhere between the nipple line and the groin is a torso wound until a hospital has proved otherwise.

To reason about the abdomen, the listener needs a simple picture of what lies inside it. A thin membrane called the peritoneum lines the cavity and wraps most of the organs, and the space it encloses is normally empty of everything but a little fluid. Behind the peritoneum lies the retroperitoneal space, where the kidneys, the pancreas, the great vessels, and part of the duodenum sit, and bleeding there is even quieter than bleeding in the open cavity because the peritoneum hides it. Above and in front are the solid organs, the liver on the right and the spleen on the left, which are rich in blood and bleed steadily when torn. The hollow organs, the stomach, the small intestine, the colon, the rectum, and the bladder, hold contents that are not meant to be free, and their injury spills those contents into spaces where infection begins at once. The pelvis is a ring of bone with vessels running along its inner surface, and the space inside it is not empty, because it contains the bladder, the rectum, and in the female patient the reproductive organs.

Several lethal possibilities should be named in the first minutes, each with the finding that reveals it. The first is intra-abdominal hemorrhage, which shows as shock that is out of proportion to the blood visible on the ground, with a rising pulse, a falling pressure, pale cool skin, thirst, anxiety, and a patient who cannot settle. The second is injury to a solid organ, the liver, the spleen, or a kidney, which may bleed slowly enough to allow a brief improvement before the bleeding resumes, so a patient who seems to steady is not a patient who is safe. The third is hollow-organ injury, which declares itself later with pain that spreads, tenderness that becomes guarding, and a quiet fever that speaks of contamination and peritonitis. The fourth is pelvic vascular injury, and this is the one to name aloud as the lethal possibility, because bleeding from the vessels beside the pelvic bones can fill the pelvis and the retroperitoneum with blood while the abdomen stays soft and the external wound stays small.

The most dangerous abdomen is the one that cannot be diagnosed by pressing on it, and the responder must understand why. Blood irritates the peritoneum far less than the contents of a torn bowel, so a patient with a cavity full of blood may have no rigidity at all and may tolerate examination without flinching. A distracted patient with a broken limb, a head injury, or a reduced level of consciousness may not report pain, and a patient whose awareness is fading may answer questions with a delay that hides the truth. The findings that should raise suspicion are therefore general rather than local, and they include a pulse that climbs while the pressure falls, breathing that becomes faster and shallower, skin that turns pale and clammy, a patient who is restless or unusually thirsty, and a belly that slowly distends or that feels dull when tapped in the flanks. Pain at the tip of the shoulder, caused by blood under the diaphragm, and pain referred to the back or the groin, are further clues that the cavity is not innocent. Any one of these findings, and especially a combination of them, is enough to treat the patient as bleeding internally and to move without delay.

A single reading can be misleading, so the responder records the observations that can be taken without instruments and watches their direction rather than their absolute value. The pulse is counted carefully, the breathing rate is counted without telling the patient, and the skin is felt for its warmth and moisture. A pulse of 110 beats per minute in a young adult at rest is a warning, and a pressure measured at 90 millimeters of mercury systolic is a warning of a different order, but both figures are teaching illustrations that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because normal values shift with age, fitness, pregnancy, and the medicines a patient already takes. The trend matters more than the snapshot, which is why the same observations are repeated and written down with the time beside them. Where no instrument exists to measure pressure, the signs of shock must carry the whole assessment, and a patient who is pale, cool, anxious, and thirsty is treated as shocked even when the pressure cannot be numbered. A patient whose measured values look acceptable but whose appearance is poor is judged by the appearance.

The pelvis deserves its own passage of reasoning because a fracture there bleeds into a space that the bone itself creates. When the ring is broken in more than one place and springs open, the volume inside it grows, and the vessels that run along the bone, the veins especially, are torn or stretched until they bleed into that enlarged space. A pelvic fracture can therefore produce a large loss of blood with no wound on the abdomen at all, and the only external sign may be a small wound of entry near the hip, the buttock, or the groin. The finding that should make the responder think of the pelvis is pain or instability when the bones are moved, though the pelvis must not be rocked or sprung repeatedly to test it, because that movement can restart or worsen bleeding that is already under way. Blood at the urinary opening, inability to pass urine, or a wound near the perineum supports the suspicion, and so does a leg that appears shortened or rotated. A patient with a gunshot wound near the pelvis and any sign of shock is a patient whose pelvis is treated as broken until imaging proves otherwise.

The first aider's work begins with the bleeding that can be seen, because that is the bleeding that can be touched. Gloved hands press directly on any wound that is pouring blood, using a dressing or the cleanest cloth available, and the pressure is held without lifting the cloth to look underneath. A wound that continues to bleed through the first layer of dressing is covered with more material and pressed harder rather than unpacked, and a limb wound that cannot be controlled by pressure may be treated with a tourniquet by a trained responder or paramedic acting under protocol. An embedded object, a blade, a fragment of glass, or a piece of metal, is never removed in the field, because pulling it free may open a vessel that nothing can then close, and it is stabilized in place with padding built up around it while the patient is moved with it. Bullets are not extracted by first responders, since removal is a surgical decision made later and only when it is clinically indicated, and it is not a field procedure. Nothing is pushed back into the abdomen, and exposed bowel or omentum is covered with a clean, moist dressing and left alone.

While the bleeding is controlled, the rest of the first aid is deliberately simple and deliberately disciplined. The airway is checked by looking, listening, and asking the patient to speak, and a patient who answers clearly has an airway that is open for the moment. The patient is kept flat, still, and warm, because cold is a complication of trauma and not a matter of comfort, since blood loss makes the body lose heat quickly and a cold patient bleeds more and tolerates the loss less well. Blankets, foil sheeting, and whatever else is at hand are placed under and over the patient, and the patient is kept off cold ground or a cold metal surface wherever that is possible. Nothing is given by mouth, no food and no drink, because the patient may need an operation and because vomiting after a belly injury can fill the airway. The emergency medical services are called early, the wound and the mechanism are described plainly, and the patient is moved only when the move carries the patient toward a hospital that can operate.

The responder watches for shock while the patient travels, and the watch is continuous rather than occasional. Shock is the state in which the circulation can no longer deliver enough oxygen to the tissues, and after an abdominal gunshot wound it is usually the result of blood loss, though contamination and later infection can deepen it. The signs are a rising pulse, a falling pressure, fast and shallow breathing, pale and clammy skin, thirst, restlessness, and a level of awareness that drifts, and the Glasgow coma scale is the tool the trained responder uses to record that drift in a way the receiving team can follow. A core temperature below 35 degrees Celsius is a threshold that adds its own harm, but the exact line used to define hypothermia differs between services and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol rather than remembered as a fixed value. Warming and hemorrhage control are therefore the same task and not two tasks, and they are carried out together from the first minute to the last. A patient who was talking and then stops talking, or who was restless and then becomes quiet in a way that is not calm, is a patient who is deteriorating.

The decision that saves the most lives in this chapter is not a procedure but a destination. A patient with a gunshot wound to the abdomen or the pelvis belongs in a trauma center where a surgeon, an operating room, blood for transfusion, and imaging are all under the same roof, and the responder who recognizes this early gives the patient the only real treatment available in the field, which is rapid and gentle transport. The receiving team is warned before arrival, with the mechanism, the number of wounds, the observations, and the trend over time passed on in plain words, because that warning lets the hospital prepare blood, a room, and a team before the patient arrives. A delay on scene for actions that do not control bleeding or open an airway costs the patient the time that surgery needs, and the responder who is tempted to do more should remember that the abdomen is not a place for improvisation. If the patient must be carried, the carrying is done with the spine kept in line and the pelvis supported, and the patient is not made to walk even when the patient insists. The shortest safe route is chosen, and the patient is not taken to a place that cannot operate unless that place is the only one within reach and can stabilize the patient and transfer.

The trained responder or paramedic builds a fuller assessment within the protocol of the service, and the purpose of each step is to decide how fast the patient must move. The primary survey follows the ABCDE order, in which airway, breathing, circulation, disability, and exposure are addressed in turn, and the exposure step is the one that finds the wound on the back, the flank, or the buttock that was hidden. The abdomen is inspected, gently felt, and listened to, but it is not pressed repeatedly, because repeated examination both hurts the patient and tells the responder little that the trend has not already told. The pelvis is assessed once, gently, with the hands on the hip bones and the knees kept together, and a pelvic binder is applied by the responder or paramedic under protocol when the mechanism and the findings support it, since the binder is not a first-aid measure for an untrained hand to improvise. The patient is kept warm, the observations are recorded with times, and the findings are handed to the receiving team in the order in which they were found.

In the hospital, the same reasoning continues but the tools change. The receiving team repeats the primary survey, secures the airway if it is threatened, supports breathing, obtains access to the circulation, and takes blood for the tests that will guide transfusion and operation. The abdomen is examined again, the rectum and the vagina may be examined where the wound pattern calls for it, and a urinary catheter may be placed after the team has confirmed that no injury makes that unsafe. The history is taken from the patient if the patient can give it and from the responders if the patient cannot, and the exact time of the injury and the time of the last observation are written down. A patient who is unstable is not sent for a long study before the team has decided what the instability means, because a patient who is bleeding to death is treated before the bleeding is mapped in detail. The physician or trauma surgeon decides the order of the tests, and that order is a clinical judgment rather than a fixed rule.

Imaging answers specific questions, and each study is chosen because its answer would change what happens next. Plain radiographs of the chest and the pelvis show the position of a bullet, the presence of free air, and the shape of a pelvic ring that may be broken and sprung. An ultrasound examination of the abdomen performed at the bedside answers whether there is free fluid, which in this setting usually means blood, and it can be repeated at the bedside as the patient changes. Computed tomography, or CT, gives the most complete picture of the track, the solid organs, the retroperitoneum, and the pelvic bones, and it is used when the patient is stable enough to travel to the scanner and when its result will direct the operation. A patient who is too unstable for CT goes to the operating room or to an intervention that stops bleeding, and the imaging is done afterward or not at all. The choice between these studies belongs to the physician or trauma surgeon, and the responder's task is to give the team the information that makes the choice possible.

Blood replacement is a hospital decision that begins with the recognition that the patient is bleeding, and it is guided by the patient's condition, the observed loss, and the results of testing rather than by any single figure. A trauma center keeps blood available, and a patient with major hemorrhage may be treated through a massive transfusion protocol that the local service defines and that the physician activates. The substances given, the amounts, the order in which they are given, and the tests that follow belong to the treating team and to the protocol of that hospital, and no amount or schedule can be taken from a manual such as this one. A patient who is still bleeding may need an operation or a procedure to stop the bleeding before transfusion can succeed, because replacing blood that continues to pour out is a losing race. The decision to give blood is therefore tied to the decision to control the source, and the two are made together by the physician or trauma surgeon.

Operation is the definitive treatment for most of the injuries in this chapter, and it belongs entirely to the hospital and the surgical team. A laparotomy opens the abdomen so that the surgeon can find and control the bleeding, repair or remove a damaged organ, close or divert a torn bowel, and wash out the contamination that has spilled. In a patient who is too unstable for a long repair, the surgeon may choose damage control, in which bleeding and contamination are controlled quickly, the abdomen is packed or left open with a temporary cover, and the patient is warmed and stabilized in the intensive care unit, or ICU, before returning to the operating room. A torn spleen may be repaired or removed, a torn liver may be packed or repaired, a kidney may be preserved or removed, and a torn vessel may be repaired or bypassed, and each of those choices is made on the findings at operation. Pelvic bleeding may be controlled by packing the pelvis, by stabilizing the ring with an external frame, or by a radiological procedure that blocks the bleeding vessel, and all of these are definitive interventions rather than field measures.

Three decisions deserve to be named even though none of them can be reduced to a prescription in this book. The first is the decision about antibiotics, which becomes urgent when a hollow organ has been torn and its contents have spilled, because contamination of the peritoneal cavity is the beginning of an infection that can kill days later, and the choice of substance belongs to the prescriber and to the local protocol while the responder's contribution is to report the contamination and the delay. The second is the decision about tetanus protection, which the treating clinician makes after considering the wound, the patient's history, and the local schedule, and which is not a decision for the roadside. The third is the decision about pain, which should be made early rather than late, because pain itself worsens breathing, raises the pulse, and adds to the work of an already strained body. Analgesia may be given by a paramedic under protocol or by the hospital team, and the substance, the amount, and the method of giving it are the business of the prescriber and the local protocol, while a lay first aider gives no medicine at all. Oxygen and fluids given into a vein follow the same rule, in that the trained responder, paramedic, or hospital team gives them when the protocol allows, and the amount and the device belong to that protocol.

Two groups of patients need a word of their own because their injury hides even better than the usual abdomen. A child has a smaller cavity and organs that sit closer together, so a single bullet can injure more of them, and a child may keep a normal pulse and pressure for longer before the circulation fails, which makes the eventual collapse more sudden. A pregnant patient has a uterus that rises out of the pelvis and pushes the bowel upward, so a wound low in the abdomen may injure the uterus and the fetus while the bowel above is spared, and a wound higher up may injure the bowel while the uterus escapes. The finding that decides the urgency is often the state of the mother, because the best treatment for the fetus is a mother whose circulation works. Both groups belong in a center with pediatric or obstetric and surgical capability, and the local protocol decides where the patient is taken.

Consider a constructed illustration, not a real patient and not a real incident, to see how the reasoning runs. A young man is found beside a road after a shooting, awake and talking, with a single small wound below the ribs on the left and no blood on the ground. His pulse is fast, his skin is cool and damp, he asks for water, and he says the pain is not bad, and a responder who judged only by the pain would call him stable. The responder instead counts the breathing, records the pulse and the pressure, inspects the back and the flank, and finds a second wound near the spine, and the picture becomes clear, that this is a torso wound with internal bleeding and a hidden exit. The scene is made safe, the wound is covered, the patient is kept warm and flat, nothing is given by mouth, and the emergency medical services are asked to take him to a trauma center with a warning that the abdomen and the pelvis are involved. The illustration is constructed to teach the order of the actions, and every figure in it would have to be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol in any real service.

Several traps recur often enough to be named plainly. The first is the patient who looks well at first, sometimes called the honeymoon period, when a young person with a strong circulation keeps a normal pulse and pressure while the abdomen fills, and then deteriorates quickly, so the responder who was reassured by the first set of observations has been misled by compensation rather than by any lie. The second is the small wound, which says nothing about the size of the cavity within it, because the skin closes behind a bullet and a tiny entrance can hide a track through several organs. The third is the distracting injury, the broken leg or the scalp wound that draws the eye and takes the attention while the abdomen bleeds quietly. The fourth is the assumption that a bullet which passed through has done no harm, when a through-and-through track can cross the liver, the bowel, or the vessels just as easily as a retained one.

Some things must be said again because the temptation to do them is strong. First responders do not explore a wound with a finger or an instrument, because probing neither finds the bleeding nor stops it, and it can dislodge a clot that was holding a vessel closed. First responders do not remove a bullet, and they do not remove a blade or any other embedded object, because removal in the field may cause hemorrhage that cannot be controlled and further damage to tissue, so the object is stabilized and the patient is moved with it. First responders do not push bowel or omentum back into the abdomen, do not tie off vessels they cannot see, and do not straighten a deformed limb to make the patient look better, because the position found is the position that protects the vessels and nerves. First responders do not give food or drink, and they do not give medicines unless their scope of practice and their protocol authorize it. A pelvic binder, a splint, or a traction device is applied only by a responder trained and authorized to use it, and a lay first aider who has no such training keeps the patient still and warm and waits for help to arrive.

To summarize, a gunshot wound to the abdomen or the pelvis is dangerous mainly because it is quiet, and the responder must treat the wound and the scene before trusting the appearance of either. The abdomen holds its blood without showing it, a solid organ may bleed steadily and then pause before bleeding again, a hollow organ spills its contents and starts an infection, and a broken pelvis can fill with blood from the vessels beside the bone, so pelvic hemorrhage is the lethal possibility that must be named early. The findings that matter are general rather than local, and a rising pulse, a falling pressure, pale clammy skin, thirst, restlessness, a changing level of awareness, and a slowly distending belly are enough to act upon even when the belly is soft. First aid is hemorrhage control, warmth, and transport, the trained responder assesses within protocol, applies a pelvic binder when authorized, records the trend, and warns the receiving team, and hospital imaging, transfusion, and operation are the definitive care. Every figure in this chapter, whether a pressure, a pulse, a temperature, a time, or a depth, is an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such numbers differ between services, countries, and editions. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, or the protocol of the service in which you work, and any example here is constructed rather than drawn from a real patient. A patient whose story and appearance do not match the wound deserves another set of observations, a clearer plan, and a faster journey, and the discipline of that judgment is the skill this chapter teaches.
