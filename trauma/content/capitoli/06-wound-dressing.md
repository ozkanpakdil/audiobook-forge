# Chapter 6 — Wound Dressing

A dressing is a covering placed on a wound, and it is the most common act in trauma care and the most easily misunderstood. Its purposes are four and must be held together: to help control bleeding, to protect exposed tissue, to keep the wound clean, and to allow the patient to be moved. A dressing does none of these things permanently, and it does not clean a wound, close a wound, remove contamination, or repair anything that has been divided. It is a temporary shelter that buys time for the acts that do belong to treatment, and it is never a substitute for definitive hospital care. A dressing lies against the wound and is chosen for what it does there, while a bandage only holds it in place and may also exert pressure. The lay first aider covers the wound with whatever clean material is at hand and without probing it, the trained responder or paramedic opens the sealed dressing and applies it within training and protocol, and definitive hospital care decides what lies beneath the dressing and what must be repaired or removed. This chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country where you work.

Before any dressing is opened, the scene must be made safe, because a responder who kneels in traffic or beside a fire becomes a second patient and helps no one. Traffic, unstable vehicles, fire, electricity, water, animals, violence, structural collapse, and the hazards of the patient's own body fluids all take precedence over the wound, and personal protective equipment, or PPE, with gloves at the least, protects both the responder and the patient. The lethal possibility that must be named first is hemorrhage that cannot be controlled, and the finding that reveals it is blood that spurts with each beat of the heart, blood that soaks through one dressing and then another, or a swelling that grows while the responder watches. A dressing applied to such a wound without a plan is a gesture rather than a treatment, and the trained responder or paramedic must move to the methods the protocol authorizes for catastrophic bleeding. The second lethal possibility is a wound that has taken the airway, and the finding is a patient coughing blood whose voice has changed or who is struggling to breathe after an injury to the face or the neck. The third is a wound that has opened the chest, and the finding is air that moves in and out of the opening with each breath. A patient who is bleeding, burned, or lying exposed also loses heat, so protection against hypothermia begins at the same moment as the dressing and is not postponed until transport.

A sterile dressing is one that has been prepared and packaged so that it carries no living organisms when it is opened, and it is the right choice for any wound whose surface is open to the air. An abrasion, a laceration, a burn, a puncture, or a wound with exposed deep tissue all qualify, because every one of them has lost the barrier that keeps contamination out. Sterility is a method as much as a supply, and it is preserved only by handling, so the responder touches the edges of the wrapper and never the surface that will lie on the wound. A dressing that touches the floor, the clothing, or the skin around the wound is no longer sterile, and a dressing that has been dropped or removed is never returned to a wound. The lay first aider may use the cleanest material available when no sterile dressing exists, because a clean covering is better than an exposed wound, and must not delay care while searching for a perfect package. The trained responder carries sealed dressings in a kit and follows the protocol for the size and the number to be applied. A sterile dressing placed on a heavily contaminated wound does not sterilize it, because contamination already in the tissue stays there until it is removed, and that removal belongs to the trained responder or to definitive hospital care rather than to the roadside.

A pressure dressing is built on top of direct pressure, and it is the dressing that is asked to stop bleeding rather than merely to cover. The material is placed over the bleeding point, a pad is stacked on it, and the bandage is wrapped firmly enough to press the pad into the wound while the circulation beyond remains. The situation that calls for it is bleeding that continues under a simple covering, and the finding that says it is working is that the flow stops and the dressing stays dry at its edges. The lay first aider may hold direct pressure with a gloved hand or a clean cloth and may add layers without removing the first one, because lifting the dressing to look can pull away the clot. The trained responder or paramedic applies a formal pressure dressing, checks the pulse, the feeling, and the movement beyond it, and reinforces rather than removes when blood appears at the margin. A pressure dressing that is too tight can shut off the circulation, and the findings are a limb that becomes pale, cold, and numb with pain that grows worse. The distinction between pressure that controls bleeding and pressure that strangles a limb is a clinical judgment the trained responder reviews repeatedly while moving toward definitive hospital care.

An occlusive dressing is one that does not allow air or fluid to pass, and its place is narrow and must be stated as a decision rather than as a habit. The wound that calls for it is an open wound of the chest, where air entering the pleural space with each breath can collapse a lung, and the finding that reveals the problem is the sound or the feel of air moving through the opening. It is also used to cover exposed bowel or other abdominal contents and to protect a wound that must not dry, such as an open joint or a burn surface that is being moved a short distance. The trained responder or paramedic places it within the protocol, and the lay first aider covers a chest wound with the cleanest available material and lets the arriving emergency medical services decide. An occlusive dressing applied to a wound that is already infected, or to a wound with dead tissue and heavy contamination, traps organisms and fluid and can turn a dirty wound into a worse one, so it is not a default covering. A plastic sheet pressed flat over a chest wound may seal the opening completely, and the finding that this has become a problem is a patient who becomes more breathless as the dressing is applied. The decision about how the occlusive dressing is made, how it is secured, and whether it is vented belongs to the trained responder and to the protocol, never to improvisation.

Packing is the act of placing material deep into a wound to press on a bleeding surface that cannot be reached from the outside, and it belongs in a short list of places. The wounds that should be packed are those in the junctional zones, the groin, the armpit, the base of the neck, and the stump of an amputation, where a surface dressing cannot press on the artery that is bleeding. Packing is not first aid with bare hands, and it belongs to the trained responder or paramedic who works under a protocol, or to a lay first aider only where a dispatcher or a training course has explicitly authorized the technique. The material is introduced firmly and continuously until the wound is filled and the bleeding is controlled, and the finding that packing has succeeded is that blood stops welling from the wound and the patient's pulse begins to steady. The wound is then covered with a pressure dressing so that the packing is held in place, and the patient is moved without delay toward definitive hospital care. Just as some wounds should be packed, others must never be, because packing a space that communicates with a body cavity pushes contamination inward and hides bleeding that the hospital needs to see. A wound of the chest, a wound of the abdomen with exposed bowel, and a wound of the eye are not packed, and an embedded object is never pushed deeper or removed in the field, because removal may cause hemorrhage that cannot be controlled. A wound whose depth is unknown, and a wound of the neck near the airway, are also not packed blindly, because the material may enter a vessel or the airway.

Contamination control is the part of dressing that is invisible when it is done well, and its logic is simple enough to state. Every wound carries organisms in its depths, and the dressing must keep new contamination out while not driving the contamination already there further in. The finding that makes contamination control urgent is visible dirt, grit, clothing fibers, or dead tissue, because each carries organisms and gives them a place to grow. The lay first aider covers the wound and does not clean it, does not scrub it, does not pour anything into it, and does not touch the wound surface with ungloved fingers. The trained responder may clean around a wound within training and protocol, and definitive hospital care performs the irrigation and the removal of dead tissue, and the decisions about antibiotics belong to the physician. A dressing that has been used once is never returned to a wound, and a dressing that has become soaked is reinforced from outside rather than lifted away. The responder who changes a dressing simply to see the wound has contaminated it again and has also disturbed whatever clot was forming.

Exposed tissue is the situation that most often frightens a responder into doing too much, and the correct response is restraint plus protection. Exposed bone looks pale or white and may be the end of a fractured bone that has come through the skin, and the finding that accompanies an open fracture is a wound that communicates with the bone and often a limb that is deformed. Exposed tendon appears as a white or silvery cord that may be under tension within the wound, and its presence means that the wound has crossed a structure whose repair belongs to the surgeon. Exposed bowel appears as loops of pink or dusky bowel that have left the abdomen through a wound, and the finding that demands immediate action is a wound that keeps the bowel outside and prevents its return. The protection of exposed tissue begins with covering it, and a moist dressing is used for exposed bowel and for other surfaces that must not dry, held with a clean outer layer that does not press on the tissue. Nothing is pushed back into the body, and no organ or bone is straightened, because forcing tissue back can strangulate it or introduce contamination the body had kept out. The trained responder or paramedic may apply a moist sterile covering and an occlusive layer within the protocol, and the patient is moved to definitive hospital care where the wound can be explored and repaired.

Certain materials must never be placed in a wound, and the reason is not cleanliness alone but the harm that the material itself does to tissue. Loose cotton, tissue paper, and any fabric that sheds fibers leave strands in the wound that act as a foreign body, keep organisms in place, and provoke inflammation that delays healing. Soil, sand, gravel, animal dung, and the ash of a fire are never used to pack or to cover a wound, because they carry organisms and particles that cannot be removed by the responder who put them there. Household remedies such as toothpaste, engine oil, butter, or a poultice of leaves have no place in a wound, because they injure the tissue, hide the appearance of the wound, and mislead the clinician. Adhesive tape is not laid directly on broken skin, because removing it tears the wound surface, and a dressing is never secured with a band so narrow that it cuts into the limb like a cord. A dressing is also never taken from another patient, never reused after it has been removed, and never applied from a package that is torn, damp, or already open. The material that belongs on a wound is a sterile or clean dressing of a kind the protocol recognizes, and the decision about what a particular wound needs belongs to the trained responder, the nurse, or the physician.

A dressing is a temporary act, and the moment it is in place the attention of the lay first aider, the trained responder or paramedic shifts from applying it to checking it. The first check is whether the bleeding has stopped, and the finding is a dressing that stays dry at its margins rather than one that is quietly filling underneath. The second check is the circulation beyond the dressing, which is judged by the pulse, the color, the warmth, the feeling, and the movement of the limb, and the finding that the dressing is too tight is a limb that is pale, cold, or numb with pain that increases. The third check is the position and the security of the dressing, and the finding that it has failed is a dressing that has slipped, that has been displaced by movement, or that no longer covers the wound. The fourth check is the patient as a whole, because a dressing that looks adequate on a limb means little if the patient is becoming restless, confused, or drowsy, and those findings belong to the assessment of the level of consciousness and to the search for shock. The signs that a dressing has failed include blood striking through the material, an expanding swelling around the wound, a limb that grows tense and hard, and air that is felt escaping near a chest dressing. A dressing soaked with fluid that lies cold and wet against the patient also steals heat, and cold worsens bleeding because it interferes with clotting.

The temptation to make a wound look normal is the error this chapter exists to prevent. A dressing that is neat and dry is satisfying to the eye, and it can persuade a responder that a patient who is still bleeding internally is stable, which is why the patient and not the dressing is the object of the assessment. A dressing is never lifted to look at a wound that is not bleeding through it, because every lifting disturbs the clot and introduces organisms, and the information gained is rarely worth the harm. An embedded object is stabilized in place by the trained responder or paramedic and the patient is moved with it, because the object may be tamponading a vessel whose bleeding cannot be controlled once the object is gone. A deformed limb is not straightened to make it look normal, because the movement can injure the vessels and the nerves that survived the original injury, and the limb is splinted in the position found. A burn is not dressed with a material that will have to be peeled from the surface, and a wound is not closed in the field when the protocol places closure in definitive hospital care. The responder who leaves a wound looking imperfect but keeps the patient alive has done the work, and the responder who makes it look tidy at the cost of a clot or a pulse has done harm.

Burns ask for dressings that do not stick, because the surface of a burn is tissue that will be lost and an adhering dressing will remove it when it is taken away. A burn is covered with a clean, non-adherent sheet, and in the field a clean plastic film may serve for a short time to keep the surface moist and reduce pain from the air. The finding that changes the plan is a burn that circles a limb or the chest, because such a burn can tighten as it swells and can interfere with the circulation or with breathing, and that patient belongs to definitive hospital care urgently. The stump of an amputation may bleed heavily, and the trained responder or paramedic controls the bleeding with the methods the protocol authorizes and applies a sterile pressure dressing. An open fracture is covered with a sterile dressing by the trained responder or paramedic, the limb is splinted in the position found, and the bone is not pushed back through the wound, because the wound is contaminated and the movement is a surgical decision. A wound that crosses a joint may have opened the joint space, and the finding is a wound that gapes with movement or that leaks a clear or blood-stained fluid, so it belongs to definitive hospital care because an open joint becomes an infected joint. A scalp wound bleeds heavily because the scalp is richly supplied with blood, and the finding that matters is not the blood on the outside but a change in the level of consciousness or a pupil that differs between the two eyes.

The dressing is also a message, and how it is described to the next clinician determines what that clinician does first. The handover carries the time the injury happened, the time the patient was first seen, the time the dressing or the tourniquet was applied, what was placed on the wound, and whether the bleeding has stopped. The tourniquet is not loosened in the field once it has controlled the bleeding, and the trained responder or paramedic writes the time it was applied on the patient where the receiving team will see it, because the duration of its use changes the decisions made in the hospital. The handover also carries what was not done, including the object that was left in place and the wound that was not packed, so that the next team does not have to guess what happened before arrival. The findings of the last check, including the pulse, the feeling, the movement, the color, and the warmth beyond the dressing, are more useful than a summary of how the wound looked.

Warmth is not a comfort but a treatment, because a bleeding patient loses heat with the blood, a burned patient loses it through the wound surface, and an immobilized patient loses it to cold ground. Wet clothing and soaked dressings conduct heat away far faster than dry ones, so a soaked dressing that has served its purpose is covered with dry layers where the protocol allows. A temperature of 35 degrees Celsius is sometimes named as a line for hypothermia, and that figure is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the accepted line differs with the patient and with the way the temperature is taken. Cold interferes with clotting and worsens shock, so a warm patient is easier to keep alive, and the lay first aider insulates the patient from the ground and covers the head without delaying transport. A patient who has lost blood loses the ability to keep warm as well as the blood itself, and that is why warmth is part of every dressing and not an afterthought.

Protection against tetanus and the decision about antibiotics are clinical decisions, and the dressing is the moment at which they are first considered. The finding that makes the tetanus question urgent is a wound that is deep, that is contaminated with soil or with animal or human material, that contains dead tissue, or that was produced by a puncture, and the question is asked at the first contact rather than at the next visit. The decision about whether protection is needed, and about what is given, belongs to the trained responder or paramedic under the local protocol, to the nurse, or to the physician, and this manual names the decision and never the amount. The same rule applies to antibiotics, which are considered when a wound is heavily contaminated, when it involves a joint or a body cavity, when tissue is dead, or when the patient has a condition that makes infection more dangerous. A patient with a fever, a wound that is becoming more painful, spreading redness, or a discharge after a dressing has been in place needs reassessment rather than a new dressing alone. Pain is information as well as suffering, and the decision about pain relief belongs to the trained responder or paramedic under the local protocol and to the prescribing clinician, so the choice of substance, the amount, and the way it is given are theirs and not this manual's. The lay first aider does not give any medicine, does not apply any substance to the wound, and reports what was seen and when the injury happened so that the clinician can make the decision.

Observation after the dressing is continuous rather than a single act, and it is the trend of the observations that guides the next decision. The trained responder records the pulse, the blood pressure, the breathing rate, the temperature, the level of consciousness, and the findings of the Glasgow coma scale where it is used, and repeats them so the direction of travel is visible. A pulse of 110 beats per minute in a bleeding patient is a finding to report and to follow, and that figure is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the rate that signals danger depends on the patient's age, fitness, and usual state. A blood pressure of 90 millimeters of mercury is the same kind of illustration, and the number alone never replaces the examination of the patient, because a young patient may hold a pressure until the loss of blood is severe. A patient whose pulse rises while the pressure falls, whose breathing becomes faster, whose skin becomes pale and clammy, and whose level of consciousness declines is a patient who is bleeding or in shock, and the dressing is only one part of that picture. The trained responder or paramedic acts on that pattern at once and moves the patient toward definitive hospital care, while the lay first aider reports the change in plain words rather than waiting for a measurement. Nothing in this chapter can be learned from listening alone, because the skills it describes are learned in a practical course with supervision and repetition, and when stores are short the substitute must imitate the dressing rather than the treatment. The finding that a responder has reached the limits of the role is a wound that cannot be controlled by pressure, an exposed structure that cannot be protected with what is at hand, or a patient whose condition is changing in a way the responder does not understand.

A constructed illustration may make the sequence easier to hold, and it is invented for teaching and describes no real patient and no real incident. Two trained responders reach a driver who is sitting in a car after a collision, and one of them makes the scene safe while the other puts on gloves and looks at the patient. The first finding is blood soaking through the clothing over the thigh, and the responder holds direct pressure while the second checks the pulse, the feeling, and the movement in the foot below. The bleeding continues under the first covering, so the responder packs the wound as the protocol allows and adds a pressure dressing above the packing, and the flow slows. The patient is covered with a blanket, insulated from the metal floor, and moved toward a trauma center with the time of the packing written where the receiving team will see it. At the hospital the dressing is removed by the surgical team, the wound is explored under proper conditions, and the decisions about repair, antibiotics, and protection against tetanus are made by those who are trained and authorized to make them.

To summarize, a dressing exists for four purposes, which are to help control bleeding, to protect exposed tissue, to keep the wound clean, and to allow a patient to be moved, and it is a temporary act rather than a treatment. The sterile dressing covers an open wound, the pressure dressing presses on a bleeding point, and the occlusive dressing excludes air or keeps a surface moist, and each is chosen for a situation rather than applied by habit. Packing belongs in the junctional zones when the protocol authorizes it, and it never belongs in a wound of the chest, the abdomen, or the eye, around an embedded object, or in a depth that cannot be seen. Exposed bone, tendon, and bowel are covered and protected and never pushed back, and no material that sheds fibers, carries contamination, or injures tissue is ever placed in a wound. After the dressing is applied the responder asks whether the bleeding has stopped, whether the circulation beyond it is intact, whether the dressing is secure, and whether the patient as a whole is changing, and then repeats those checks because the trend is what matters. The signs of failure are blood striking through, an expanding swelling, a limb that is pale, cold, or numb, air escaping near a chest wound, and a patient who becomes restless or drowsy, and any of them sends the patient faster toward definitive hospital care. Warmth, continuous observation, an honest record, and a clear handover are part of the dressing, and the lay first aider, the trained responder or paramedic, and definitive hospital care each own a different part of the act. Every figure mentioned in this chapter is an illustration whose value must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country where you work.
