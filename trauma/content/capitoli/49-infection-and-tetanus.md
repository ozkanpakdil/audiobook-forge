# Chapter 49 — Infection and Tetanus

Infection is the complication of trauma that arrives after the sirens have stopped, and it is the one that most often turns a survivable injury into a death that no one expected. Before any wound is examined, the scene is made safe, because traffic, fire, electricity, water, animals, violence, structural collapse, and the patient's own body fluids threaten the responder as much as the patient, and personal protective equipment is whatever barrier is actually available. The lethal possibility this chapter addresses is an infection that spreads faster than the patient can be moved, and the finding that reveals it is a wound that is far more painful than it looks, in a patient who is far more ill than the wound suggests. The chapter follows the tetanus question, the antibiotic question, and the infections that follow trauma from the moment of injury to the intensive care unit, and it names the provider level that owns every decision. It is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

Every wound is a breach, and the breach is contaminated at the instant it is made, because the skin that kept bacteria out has been opened and the instrument that opened it carried the world with it. Soil, dust, manure, water, clothing, the patient's own skin, the mouth of an animal that bit, and the bowel that has been perforated all deliver organisms into tissue that has lost its defenses. What follows depends on a balance that the responder cannot see, which is the number and the kind of organisms delivered, the amount of dead tissue and foreign material left behind, the blood supply to the injured part, and the ability of the patient's own defenses to contain what has entered. Dead muscle, a hematoma, a retained fragment of cloth, and a bone end stripped of its covering form a medium in which bacteria multiply without resistance. A wound that is cleaned and protected early is not sterile, but it is given the best chance to heal, while a wound left dirty beneath a soaked dressing is given the opposite. This is why the first responder's contribution to the prevention of infection is not a substance but a clean, protected, and promptly delivered wound.

The wounds that produce the worst infections are frequently the ones that no one took seriously. A puncture from a nail, a splinter of wood, a thorn, a small bite, a scraped shin, or a wound made by a piece of wire can close over at the surface while the track beneath holds soil and dead tissue, and the surface then conceals the danger. A constructed illustration, offered as a construction and never as a real patient, is the gardener who steps on a nail, sees a speck of blood, washes the foot, and returns to work, only to develop a stiff jaw and a spasm of the back some days later. The reason this happens is that the opening is too small to drain, the tissue along the track has been crushed, and the organisms have been carried to a depth where the blood supply is poor and the body's defenses arrive late. The responder must therefore treat every wound as contaminated until cleaning and inspection prove otherwise, and must never let a small opening be read as a small injury. The finding that should end any reassurance is a wound whose depth cannot be seen, or a wound made by an object that was lying in soil or manure.

Tetanus is the disease that gives this chapter its name, and it is caused by a poison produced by a bacterium that lives in soil, in dust, and in manure, and that survives for years as a dormant form until it is placed in the conditions of a wound. The bacterium does not spread from person to person, so a patient with tetanus is not a danger to the responder in the way that a patient with a respiratory infection is, and the patient cannot pass the disease to anyone else. Once the poison is produced it travels along the nerves to the spinal cord and the brain, where it removes the normal inhibition of muscle activity, and the result is a body that contracts without the patient's will. The earliest sign is often a stiffness of the jaw that makes opening the mouth difficult, followed by a fixed smile, a stiff neck, an arched back, and spasms of the limbs and the trunk. The patient usually remains fully conscious throughout and the pain is severe, while light, noise, touch, and movement can each trigger a spasm. The spasm that matters most closes the muscles of the throat and the chest, so difficulty in swallowing and a tightening of the chest wall are the two findings that make tetanus an emergency of the airway and of breathing.

The time between the wound and the first symptom is the incubation period, and it is commonly given as 3 to 21 days, a range offered here only as an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the interval differs with the size of the inoculum and the depth of the wound. The shorter the incubation period, the more severe the disease tends to be, and a wound that was forgotten weeks earlier may be the one that is responsible. Tetanus is rare where routine vaccination is universal and common where it is not, and it remains a disease of the unvaccinated, the incompletely vaccinated, and the patient whose vaccination history is unknown. It is also a disease of the newborn in places where mothers are not protected, and its elimination is one of the successes of public health where programs have reached every mother and every child. Because no treatment can undo the poison once it has bound to the nervous system, the whole weight of the disease falls on prevention and on the decision made at the time of the wound. That decision is the subject of the next paragraphs, and it is a decision about the patient, the wound, and the record, never a prescription to be copied from a page.

The question of protection against tetanus is asked at the first contact with a wounded patient, and it is answered by a clinician or by a provider acting under a protocol that authorizes that decision. The decision rests on two histories taken together, which are the nature of the wound and the patient's vaccination history, including whether the course was completed and when the last protection was received. A wound that is deep, that contains dead tissue or a retained foreign body, that is a puncture, that is a bite, that is contaminated with soil or manure, that follows a burn or a crush, that is an open fracture, or that presents late is treated as a wound at higher risk, while a clean superficial wound in a fully vaccinated patient usually requires nothing further. The vaccination history decides the other half of the answer, and the possibilities are that protection is already sufficient, that active protection needs to be completed, or that immediate passive protection is also required while the patient's own response develops. Which preparation is used, how much is given, by which route, and at what interval are not matters that can be taken from this manual, because the substance and the schedule belong to the prescriber and the local guideline. The lay first aider asks the question and reports the wound, the trained responder may act only within the protocol that covers the service, and definitive hospital care makes the final decision. What no reader may do is decide the tetanus question from a chapter, or borrow another person's medicine to answer it.

Two errors are made so regularly that they deserve to be named. The first is to judge the tetanus risk by the appearance of the wound at the roadside, because the surface of a puncture can look clean while the track is full of soil, and because the wound that seemed trivial is the classic wound of tetanus. The second is to assume that a wound which was not reported at the time cannot be the cause, because patients forget small injuries, because a wound beneath clothing is missed, and because the patient may be unable to give any history at all. Certain patients carry a higher risk from the same wound, among them the older patient, the patient with diabetes, the patient who drinks heavily, the patient whose nutrition is poor, the patient without a spleen, the patient receiving medicines that suppress the immune response, and the patient with poor circulation in the legs. The finding that makes the tetanus question urgent is not a laboratory result but a wound that is deep, soiled, devitalized, or old, in a patient whose vaccination state is unclear. When that combination is present, the responder does not wait to see whether the wound becomes infected before raising the question.

Antibiotic prophylaxis follows the same discipline, and it is a clinical decision rather than a field ritual. The finding that makes the decision urgent is a wound that is deep or heavily contaminated, a bite, a crush injury, a wound with dead tissue, an open fracture, a wound that has been closed over contamination, a wound involving the bowel, a burn of significant extent, or a wound in a patient whose defenses are impaired. The decision belongs to the prescriber and to the local protocol, and it is weighed against the patient's allergies, the patient's other medicines, the organisms likely to be present, the state of the patient's kidneys and liver, and the growing problem of resistance to antimicrobials. The lay first aider never gives or shares an antibiotic, because the wrong substance delays the right treatment and can cause a reaction that no one at the scene can manage. The trained responder may carry and give an antimicrobial only where a protocol authorizes it, and the choice of substance, the amount, the route, and the duration remain outside this manual. Antibiotics do not sterilize a wound, they do not remove dead tissue, they do not drain pus, and they do not replace the operation that an infection may require. The record of any antimicrobial already given is part of the handover, because the receiving clinician must know what the patient has already received.

Because antibiotics are so easily imagined as the answer to infection, the limits of what they can do must be stated plainly. An antimicrobial can reach organisms only where the blood supply delivers it, so dead tissue, a collection of pus, a foreign body, and a bone end without a covering are places where it cannot work, and those places must be removed or opened by a surgeon. Irrigation of a wound, removal of dead and foreign material, drainage of a collection, and the decision to leave a contaminated wound open rather than close it are all acts of definitive care, and none is a procedure for the roadside. The field's contribution is to cover the wound with a clean dressing, to avoid pushing contamination deeper, to avoid probing, to keep the patient warm, and to shorten the time to the place where the wound can be properly cleaned and assessed. A wound that has been packed with soil or with a traditional preparation must be described honestly at handover, because the receiving team needs to know what is in the wound in order to clean it. Definitive hospital care decides when to explore, when to debride, when to close, and when to leave open, and that sequence is decided by the state of the tissue rather than by the calendar alone.

Prevention begins with cleaning, and cleaning begins with the removal of visible contamination and the use of clean water under the pressure that the service authorizes. The decision about what solution to use, and whether to irrigate a deep wound at all, belongs to the protocol and to the clinician, because the wrong fluid or the wrong pressure can drive contamination deeper or damage tissue that is trying to survive. A wound that is contaminated is often left open for a time and closed later, and a period such as 6 hours after injury is sometimes quoted as the point beyond which primary closure becomes less safe, though that figure is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the decision depends on the wound, the tissue, and the patient. The responder's part is to protect the wound from further contamination, to change a soaked dressing without disturbing the wound bed, and to avoid the repeated lifting of a dressing that breaks the fragile work of healing. A clean dressing, clean hands, and clean water accomplish more at the scene than any substance applied to a wound, and each of them is available to the lay first aider.

The infections that follow trauma can be understood by where they live and how far they have spread, and the names are worth learning because each one means a different urgency. Cellulitis is an infection of the skin and the tissue just beneath it, and it spreads along the planes of the tissue rather than collecting in one place. An abscess is a collection of pus that has been walled off, and it needs to be drained rather than only treated with a substance. A necrotizing infection is one that travels through the deep tissue and destroys the blood supply as it goes, and it is the most rapidly lethal of the group. Osteomyelitis is an infection within bone, and it follows an open fracture or a contaminated wound more often than it follows a clean one. Sepsis is the final common path, in which the body's response to an infection becomes a disease of its own and begins to injure the organs it was meant to protect. The responder who hears the name of a category should also hear its urgency, because the categories are not equal.

Cellulitis announces itself with a redness that spreads, a warmth over the area, a swelling, and a tenderness that is greater than the wound alone would explain. The border of the redness may be indistinct and it advances over time, and a red streak running toward the lymph nodes in the groin or the armpit, together with tender nodes, tells the responder that the infection is traveling. A period such as 24 to 72 hours after the injury is a common time for cellulitis to appear, but that interval is only an illustration and must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, because a wound can become infected sooner in a patient whose defenses are poor and later in a wound that was small. The patient may feel well at first, and the appearance of fever, shivering, or a general feeling of illness marks the point at which the infection is no longer merely local. The lay first aider can see spreading redness and can ask whether the patient feels unwell, and the correct action is to seek medical assessment rather than to wait for the redness to fade on its own. The trained responder records the extent of the redness, the temperature, and the pulse, and marks the border so that the next observer can tell whether it is advancing. The treatment itself is decided by the clinician and the local protocol, and it may include an antimicrobial that this manual does not name or measure.

An abscess is a cavity of pus that the body has walled off, and it feels different from cellulitis because the swelling is more defined and more tense. The signs are a tender, warm, raised swelling, pain that is out of proportion to the size of the wound, and often a fever, while the skin over it may become shiny and red. An abscess does not resolve with cleaning alone, and it does not respond to an antimicrobial that cannot reach the inside of the cavity, so the treatment is drainage performed by a clinician. The lay first aider must never squeeze, cut, or pierce a suspected abscess, because that act can push infection into the deeper tissue and into the blood, and it can injure structures that the responder cannot see. The trained responder recognizes the tense swelling, reports it, and arranges prompt assessment, while the drainage itself belongs to definitive care. The finding that makes an abscess urgent is a collection in a dangerous place, such as the hand, the neck, the groin, or the deep tissue of the thigh, or a patient who is becoming systematically unwell.

A necrotizing infection is the emergency of this chapter, and its signature is pain that is far greater than the visible wound suggests. The early skin may look deceptively mild, with only a little redness or a dusky patch, while the infection is already traveling along the deep planes and destroying the vessels that feed the tissue. Over a period measured in hours, an interval that is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol rather than a fixed rule, the skin may become blistered, the color may turn a dark blue or a gray, and the area may lose sensation because the nerves have been destroyed. The tissue may crackle under the fingers when gas is produced within it, and the patient may become confused, feverish, and faint while the wound still looks unimpressive. This is a surgical emergency, and antimicrobials alone do not cure it, because the dead tissue must be removed and the infection must be followed until it has been controlled. The decisive act is the operation, and the time from recognition to the operating room is the measurement that matters most. The finding that must send the patient to definitive care at once is a pain that does not fit the wound, a skin that is changing color, or a patient who is more ill than the wound can explain.

Because the early appearance of a necrotizing infection is so easy to dismiss, the diagnosis rests on the mismatch between the patient and the wound rather than on a single sign. A clinician who feels that something is wrong, even when the wound looks mild, is right often enough that the impression must be acted upon, and the same is true of the trained responder who observes a patient deteriorating without an explanation. Imaging may support the assessment, and computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging may show gas or deep involvement, but a normal image does not exclude the disease, because the diagnosis is made by looking at the tissue itself. Surgical exploration is often the step that settles the question, and it belongs entirely to the operating room and never to the field. The responder's task is to describe the pain, the color, the speed of the change, and the patient's general state, and to make the referral without waiting for certainty. Delay in this condition is measured in tissue and in life, and a false alarm that leads to an operation is a far smaller harm than a missed diagnosis that leads to a death.

Osteomyelitis is an infection within bone, and it develops when organisms reach the bone through an open wound, through an open fracture, or by traveling in the blood from an infection elsewhere. It is a slower infection than the ones above, and it may appear weeks after the injury, an interval offered as an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the timing depends on the organism, the bone, and the patient. The signs are a deep pain in the bone that persists, tenderness over the bone, warmth and swelling of the surrounding tissue, a wound that drains continuously or repeatedly, a fever that comes and goes, and a failure of the wound to heal or of the fracture to unite. A patient with an open fracture who was improving and then begins to have pain, discharge, or loosening of the fixation is showing the pattern that suggests infection in the bone. The diagnosis belongs to the hospital, where blood tests, imaging with a plain radiograph, a computed tomography scan, or a magnetic resonance imaging study, and sampling of the bone are used to confirm it. The treatment is surgical cleaning of the bone together with a prolonged course of antimicrobials chosen by the prescriber, and the field has no part in either. The field's part is to recognize the wound that drains, to protect it, and to arrange referral rather than to wait.

An open fracture deserves particular attention, because a broken bone that has communicated with the outside world is a deep infection waiting to happen. The wound may be small, and a bone end may be visible only for a moment or not at all, so the responder must suspect an open fracture in every wound near a fracture and must describe what was seen. The field care is to control bleeding with pressure around the wound and never over the bone, to cover the wound with a clean dressing, to splint the limb in the position found, to keep the patient warm, and to record the time of injury and the time of the dressing. No one in the field pushes a bone end back into the wound, probes the wound with a finger or an instrument, or pours a substance into it, because each of those acts drives contamination deeper and destroys tissue that the surgeon will need. The definitive care is surgical cleaning of the bone and the tissue, removal of dead material, stabilization of the fracture, and the decision about closure, together with antimicrobials chosen by the prescriber. The single most valuable thing the field can give the patient is a shorter time to that operation.

Sepsis is the final common path of infection after trauma, and it is a state in which the body's response to infection injures its own organs. The signs are a temperature that is high or abnormally low, a breathing rate that has become fast, a pulse that has become rapid, a level of consciousness that has become dull or confused, a blood pressure that has fallen, skin that is mottled or cool, and a reduction in the passing of urine. A temperature such as 38 degrees Celsius is often used as a warning line, and that figure is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol rather than a threshold to be applied from memory. A pressure such as 90 millimeters of mercury is often used as another warning line, and it too must be checked against the guideline and the protocol in force where the patient is being treated. The lay first aider cannot measure all of these, but the lay first aider can see a patient who is becoming drowsy, breathing quickly, or behaving strangely, and that observation is enough to call for help at once. The trained responder measures what the service allows, records the trend rather than a single set of values, keeps the patient warm, and moves the patient toward definitive care without delay. Definitive care finds the source of the infection, controls it, gives the antimicrobials that the prescriber chooses, and supports the failing organs in an intensive care unit.

Sepsis after trauma does not always come from the wound that the responder can see, and this is the reason the diagnosis must be made by looking at the patient and not only at the injury. A patient with a chest injury may develop pneumonia, a patient with a tube placed to drain the bladder may develop an infection of the urinary tract, a patient with a tube placed in a large blood vessel may develop an infection of the bloodstream, a patient after abdominal surgery may develop a collection deep in the abdomen, and a patient with a fracture of the skull may develop infection around the brain. A patient whose immune defenses are impaired, whether by illness or by medicine, may develop a serious infection with fewer signs than a healthy patient, and a patient at the extremes of age may do the same. The responder who searches only the wound for the explanation will miss the source, and the search must include the whole patient and the story of everything that has been done. The finding that should widen the search is a patient who is clearly unwell while the wound looks clean.

The timeline of infection after trauma is a teaching aid rather than a schedule, and every interval in this chapter must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol before it is used to decide anything. Cellulitis commonly appears within a day or a few days, an abscess may form over a few days, a necrotizing infection can advance within hours, osteomyelitis may declare itself weeks later, and sepsis can develop at any point and can move quickly once it begins. The reason the timeline varies is that infection is a relationship between the organisms, the wound, and the patient, and each of those three changes the others. A wound in a patient with diabetes and poor circulation may become serious in less time than the same wound in a healthy young patient. A wound that was cleaned and closed early may never become infected, while a wound that was left open and dirty may become infected despite good care. The responder therefore watches the patient over time instead of judging a single moment, because the trend is the message.

The criteria for referral can be spoken as a sequence that any responder can follow. The first finding is a wound that is spreading redness, becoming more painful, or producing pus or a foul discharge, and any one of those means the patient needs assessment. The next is pain that is out of proportion to the wound, because that finding raises the possibility of a necrotizing infection and it demands immediate referral rather than a period of watching. The next is a patient who is feverish, shivering, confused, drowsy, breathing quickly, or faint, because those findings suggest that the infection has become general. The next is a wound that will not heal, that drains repeatedly, or that exposes bone, because those findings suggest infection in the bone. The next is a wound in a patient whose defenses are impaired, or in a patient whose vaccination state is unknown, because the risk is higher and the margin for delay is smaller. When any of these findings is present, the responder refers early rather than waiting for certainty, because the cost of a false alarm is small and the cost of a delay can be a life.

The examination that finds infection begins with the whole patient and then moves to the wound. The responder looks first at the level of consciousness, the breathing, the pulse, the temperature, and the color and warmth of the skin, because these tell whether the infection is local or general. The responder then looks at the wound for redness, swelling, warmth, tenderness, discharge, smell, and the state of the edges, and marks the border of any redness so that a later observer can compare. The responder looks at the tissue beyond the wound for pulses, sensation, and movement, because a spreading infection can compromise the blood supply and the nerves of a limb. The responder asks about the mechanism, the time of injury, the material that entered the wound, the treatment already applied, and the patient's vaccination history, because each answer changes the risk. What the responder does not do is probe the wound deeply, remove a dressing that is controlling bleeding, or squeeze a swelling to see what comes out, because each of those acts can spread the infection and worsen the injury. The conclusion of the examination is a decision about urgency, and infection after trauma is a diagnosis made by looking at the patient and not only at the wound.

Prevention of infection is a shared task, and the responder's own hands are the first instrument in it. Hands are washed or cleaned before and after contact with every patient, gloves are used for every wound, and a glove that is torn or soiled is replaced before the responder touches anything else. The responder keeps the wound covered with a clean dressing, avoids touching the wound with the fingers, avoids coughing or breathing directly over an open wound, and keeps soiled dressings away from the patient and from the vehicle. The same gloves are never used for two patients, and equipment that has touched one wound is not used on another without being cleaned as the protocol requires. When the responder has a break in the skin, it is covered before the glove is applied, and a splash to the eye or the mouth is washed at once while another person takes over the patient. These acts are simple, and they prevent more infection than any substance the responder could apply.

The same wound carries a different risk in different patients, and the responder who knows the host factors will watch the right patient more closely. Increasing age, diabetes, poor nutrition, obesity, smoking, heavy use of alcohol, disease of the blood vessels, an illness or a medicine that suppresses the immune response, the absence of a spleen, and pregnancy all change the balance between the organisms and the patient. A constructed illustration, described as a construction rather than as a real case, is the older patient with diabetes who feels a pebble in the shoe, develops a small wound on the sole, and loses sensation so gradually that the wound is not noticed until the foot is swollen and the bone is involved. The reason such a wound is dangerous is that the blood supply is poor, the sensation that would have warned the patient is gone, and the infection has time to reach deep structures before anyone looks. The finding that should raise concern in any patient is a wound that is not healing as expected, together with a limb that is warm, swollen, or painful. The responder asks about these conditions in every history and reports them at handover, because the patient's own risk is part of the wound.

The circumstances of the wound decide much of its danger, and the responder should ask where and how it happened. A wound made in a field, a barn, a garden, or any place where animals live is contaminated with soil and manure, which is the environment in which the tetanus bacterium persists, and such a wound must always raise the tetanus question. A bite carries the organisms of the mouth of the animal or the person who bit, and a human bite can be more dangerous than it appears because the wound is often small and deep. A wound made by machinery carries foreign material and dead tissue, a wound made in water carries the organisms of that water, and a wound made by a projectile carries whatever the projectile brought with it. A wound that has been treated at home with soil, leaves, dung, or another traditional preparation has been given additional contamination, and the responder must ask about this without judging the patient. The finding that should always be reported is the material that entered the wound, because the receiving team bases part of its cleaning and its antimicrobial decision on it.

Protection against tetanus is achieved before the wound rather than after it, and this is the message that belongs to the whole community rather than to the emergency alone. A complete course of vaccination in childhood, maintained through life as the local schedule provides, is what keeps tetanus rare, and the protection of mothers is what keeps it away from newborns. The responder who works in a community can reinforce that message at every contact, because a patient who is not protected today is a patient who is at risk from the next small wound. It must be said clearly that vaccination does not sterilize a wound, does not remove dead tissue, and does not prevent the other infections described in this chapter, so a wound still needs cleaning, protection, and assessment even in a fully vaccinated patient. The local protocol decides how vaccination status is recorded and how it is checked when the patient cannot answer, and the responder should never assume protection because a patient cannot recall a wound or a date. The absence of a scar is not evidence of protection, and the presence of a scar is not proof of it, so the history and the protocol decide.

Several beliefs about wounds cause harm, and each can be corrected with a sentence of reason. It is not true that a wound which looks clean cannot be infected, because the surface says nothing about the track beneath it. It is not true that an antimicrobial prevents every infection, because it cannot reach dead tissue and it cannot replace cleaning. It is not true that a wound which drains pus is healing, because pus is a collection that needs to be assessed and often drained. It is not true that a tetanus vaccination alone makes a wound safe, because the wound must still be cleaned and the decision about protection still belongs to the clinician. It is not true that a substance bought without a prescription, or left over from another illness, can treat an infection, because the wrong substance delays the right one and can cause harm. It is not true that opening a swelling at home is a small act, because the incision can spread the infection and injure what lies beneath. The responder who explains these points calmly gives the patient something more useful than reassurance.

The record is part of the treatment of infection, because the receiving team continues a story that the field began. The handover carries the mechanism of injury, the time of the injury, the material that contaminated the wound, the time the wound was covered, the dressings and substances applied, the vaccination history if it is known, and whether the tetanus question has already been raised by someone authorized to raise it. It carries the observations in the order in which they were taken, so that the receiving team can see a trend rather than a single set of values. It carries the patient's other illnesses, the medicines the patient takes, and any allergy, because each of those changes the treatment that will be chosen. It carries the patient's condition at the moment of handover, together with any change that occurred during transport. A gap in that story forces the receiving team to start again, and the time lost in starting again is time lost to an infection that may be advancing.

Cold is an enemy of the patient with an infection, just as it is an enemy of the patient who is bleeding. A low body temperature reduces the blood flow to the tissues, impairs the work of the immune cells that would contain the organisms, and makes the patient less able to respond to treatment, so a patient who is infected and cold is worse than either condition alone. The responder therefore removes wet clothing, covers the patient, insulates the patient from the ground, and keeps the vehicle warm, and this protection continues during every examination and every transfer. A temperature below 35 degrees Celsius is often used to describe the point at which cold itself becomes dangerous, and that figure is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the threshold depends on the patient and on how the temperature is measured. A fever is not a reason to remove covering from a patient who is shivering, because the patient who is shivering is losing heat and using energy that the body needs for defense. The two tasks of keeping the patient warm and treating the infection run together rather than one after the other.

Every act in this chapter belongs to a named level of provider, and the level is decided by training, equipment, authorization, and protocol. The lay first aider may cover a wound with a clean dressing, keep the patient warm, ask about the wound and the vaccination history, watch the patient for change, and call for help, and may not decide the tetanus question, give an antimicrobial, drain a collection, or open a wound. The trained responder and the paramedic may assess the wound, clean and dress it as the protocol allows, record the observations, arrange the transport, and raise the tetanus question within the protocol that covers the service. The nurse, the physician, and the trauma surgeon in definitive care decide the tetanus protection, choose and give the antimicrobials, drain the abscess, debride the dead tissue, image the bone, and support the failing organs. The finding that changes the level of care is not the size of the wound but the state of the patient, and the responder who recognizes a general deterioration has already done the most important part of the work.

The infections that kill after trauma are often the ones that were not watched, and the reason is that they begin quietly and declare themselves late. A patient who improves for a time and then worsens is telling the responder that something new has happened, and the new event may be an infection rather than the injury itself. A wound that was small and clean at the first look may be the source of a fever on the second day, and a patient who was walking may be drowsy on the third. The responder therefore repeats the examination in the same order and compares each set of findings with the one before, instead of relying on the impression of the first contact. The finding that should return the responder to the beginning of the assessment is any change for the worse, at any time, in any patient. Watching is not a passive act, and in the prevention of infection it is one of the most active things a responder does.

To summarize, infection is the complication of trauma that arrives after the injury, and its prevention begins with scene safety, clean hands, a clean dressing, warmth, and the shortest safe time to definitive care. Tetanus is the disease of the wound that seemed trivial, and the decision about protection belongs to the clinician or to the provider acting under a protocol, and it rests on the nature of the wound and the patient's vaccination history rather than on the appearance of the surface. The substance, the amount, the route, and the schedule of any protection or antimicrobial belong to the prescriber and the local protocol, and no reader may take a prescription from this chapter. The infections that follow trauma are cellulitis, abscess, necrotizing infection with its disproportionate pain and its speed, osteomyelitis in the open fracture and the contaminated wound, and sepsis as the final common path, and each has signs, a timeline, and a threshold for referral that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. Infection after trauma is a diagnosis made by looking at the patient and not only at the wound, because the patient who is more ill than the wound suggests is the patient who needs the most urgent assessment. The lay first aider covers, warms, watches, and calls, the trained responder assesses, records, and transfers, and definitive hospital care decides, drains, debrides, and treats. Every number, interval, temperature, and pressure named in this chapter is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between services, countries, and editions, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country where you work.
