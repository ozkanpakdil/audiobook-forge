# Chapter 32 — Shock

Shock is not a number but the state in which the circulation can no longer deliver enough oxygen to the tissues, and it can be present while every measurement available to the responder still looks acceptable. Before anything is assessed, the scene is made safe, because traffic, fire, electricity, water, violence, animals, structural collapse, and the patient's own body fluids can injure a rescuer who has not looked first. Personal protective equipment, or PPE, with gloves at the least, protects the responder and the patient together, and a patient who is confused from a shortage of oxygen is a hazard as well as a casualty. The lethal possibility is named early, and the finding that reveals it is skin that is pale, cool, and damp, a pulse that is fast and thin, and attention that is drifting from the conversation. This chapter explains what shock is, how four mechanisms produce it, how it is recognized with the hands and the eyes alone, and what each level of provider may do, and it is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country where you work.

Perfusion is the delivery of oxygen and fuel to every cell and the removal of the waste the cell produces, and it depends on a volume of blood, a working pump, vessels with enough tone, and a passage for air. Shock begins at the level of the cell, where delivery falls below what the tissue needs, so the cell turns to a wasteful metabolism, produces acid, and dies if the shortage continues. The body defends itself by narrowing the vessels of the skin and the gut to keep the brain and the heart supplied, by speeding the heart, and by holding fluid inside the circulation. The pressure is therefore the last measurement to fall and the least useful one to wait for, because a patient can be short of oxygen while the pressure is still held up by a body spending its reserve. What the responder reads instead is the direction in which the patient is moving, and the finding that matters is the same observation made again and found worse.

The four mechanisms that produce shock are named hypovolemic, obstructive, cardiogenic, and distributive, and the value of naming them is that the treatment of one can harm a patient with another. Hypovolemic shock is the loss of circulating volume, and in trauma it is almost always the loss of blood. Obstructive shock is a physical block to the flow of blood into or out of the heart, so the pump is sound but cannot fill. Cardiogenic shock is the failure of the pump itself, from an injury or a disease of the heart muscle or its rhythm. Distributive shock is the loss of vessel tone, or a circulation poisoned by infection or by an allergic reaction, so the container becomes too large for the volume inside it. No responder can measure the mechanism at the roadside, but the injury, the skin, and the pulse suggest it, and the substance and the amount of any treatment belong to the local protocol and the prescriber.

Hypovolemic shock in trauma is hemorrhage until proved otherwise, and the bleeding is often concealed where no dressing can reach it. Blood can collect in the chest until the lung is compressed, in the abdomen until the belly is firm and distended, in the pelvis behind the peritoneum, in the thigh around a broken femur, and in the tissues around any fracture. The findings that reveal concealed bleeding are a patient who is pale, cool, thirsty, and restless while the visible injury is minor, a pulse that climbs while the pressure falls, and a breathing rate that rises without a wound of the chest. The lay first aider calls emergency medical services early, controls visible bleeding with direct pressure, keeps the patient flat and warm, and does not press into a tender belly or spring the pelvis to test it. The trained responder repeats the observations, splints a deformed limb in the position found rather than straightening it, applies a pelvic binder where the protocol authorizes one, and shortens the journey, and definitive hospital care opens the cavity and controls the source.

Obstructive shock is produced by a block that a trained provider or a surgeon can sometimes relieve, and the two injuries to suspect in trauma are a chest filled with air under pressure and a heart compressed by blood in the sac around it. A tension pneumothorax occurs when air enters the chest and cannot escape, so the lung collapses, the vessels in the chest are kinked, and the return of blood to the heart fails. The findings are a patient who worsens quickly after a chest injury, a windpipe pushed to one side, absent breath sounds on the injured side, distended neck veins, and a pulse that weakens as the patient breathes in. Cardiac tamponade produces a similar collapse, with muffled heart sounds, distended neck veins, and a falling pressure, and it may follow a chest wound that looked small. Needle decompression of the chest belongs to the trained responder or paramedic who carries the equipment and works under a protocol and an authorization, and the definitive treatment of both conditions is an operation, while the chest is never bound tightly at any stage.

Cardiogenic shock is the failure of the pump, and in trauma it follows a blunt blow to the chest that bruises or tears the heart, while outside trauma it follows a heart attack or a dangerous rhythm. The patient may complain of chest pain, may be unable to lie flat because breathing becomes hard, may have a slow or irregular pulse, and may show cool skin together with congestion in the lungs. The distinction matters because the treatment that helps a patient who has lost blood can worsen a patient whose heart is failing, so the decision about fluid in this patient belongs to the clinician and not to the responder. The lay first aider keeps the patient still, warm, and in the position that breathing allows, calls emergency medical services, and stays with the patient. The trained responder supports the airway and the breathing within the protocol and moves the patient to a hospital where the heart can be examined and supported.

Distributive shock is the loss of vessel tone, and it appears in three forms that a responder may meet. A spinal cord injury removes the signals that keep the vessels narrowed, so the container widens, and the findings are warm dry skin, a pulse that is slow rather than fast, and a pressure that falls without the usual compensation. An allergic reaction to a sting, a food, or a medicine produces swelling of the airway, wheezing, hives, and a sudden collapse, and the decision about the substance that reverses it belongs to the protocol and to the provider authorized to give it. An infection that has entered the blood produces a patient who is feverish and confused, with warm skin at the beginning and cold skin later, and this form develops over hours rather than minutes. The finding that traps a responder is warm skin in a patient who otherwise looks desperately ill, because warmth is wrongly taken for safety, and the trained responder treats the airway, supports the breathing, and moves the patient without delay.

Recognition follows the primary survey in the fixed order of airway, breathing, circulation, disability, and exposure, and the skin is the largest surface a responder can read, because its color, its temperature, and its moisture tell more about the circulation than any single number. A patient in shock is pale, then gray, and later mottled, with cool hands and feet, and the coolness climbs the limb toward the knee and the thigh as the body shuts down the periphery to protect the center. The skin may be damp with a cold sweat, and the lips, the gums, the inner eyelid, and the palms are the places to look in a patient whose skin is dark, because a change in color is easier to see there than on the forearm. Capillary refill, which is the return of color to the nailbed after it is pressed and released, is delayed when the periphery is shut down, and that delay is a finding that the circulation is already compensating.

The pulse is felt at the neck, in the groin, at the wrist, and on the top of the foot, and the character of the pulse matters more than its presence alone. A pulse that is fast and thin is one of the earliest signs of a falling circulation, because the heart is trying to move a smaller volume faster, and a pulse that is slow may belong to a patient with a spinal injury, a patient whose medicines slow the heart, or a patient whose heart is failing. The loss of the pulse at the wrist while a pulse can still be felt at the neck is a grave finding, because the body has closed the periphery completely, and it means that the remaining time is short. A rate above 100 beats per minute in an adult is a figure often taught as a warning, and that figure, like every threshold in this chapter, is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the line shifts with age, with pregnancy, with fitness, and with the patient's usual state.

The brain is the organ most sensitive to a fall in perfusion, so the level of consciousness is the most honest witness the responder has. The patient who is anxious, restless, argumentative, or thirsty is often at an earlier stage than the patient who has become quiet, because restlessness is the brain asking for oxygen while it can still speak. Confusion, drowsiness, and a failure to answer the same question twice in the same way are later findings, and a patient who has become silent after talking is deteriorating rather than settling. The responder asks the patient's name, the place, and the time, and repeats the questions later so that two answers can be compared, and the Glasgow coma scale is used where the responder has been trained in it because it turns an impression into something two people can compare. Alcohol, drugs, a head injury, and fear can each change the level of consciousness, and none of them may be allowed to explain a deterioration that could be shock.

The breathing rate is a quiet and early messenger, and it is counted while the patient is unaware of being watched. A patient in shock breathes faster and more shallowly as the body tries to take in more oxygen, and the patient may sigh, yawn, or be unable to finish a sentence without stopping for air. A breathing rate that climbs while the chest shows no wound suggests that oxygen is not reaching the tissues, and it belongs with the pulse and the skin as signs that move together. Monitoring without equipment is a skill of repetition, and it needs nothing more than a watch, a light, a pen, and the responder's own hands.

The most dangerous moment in shock is the moment when the patient still looks stable, because the body is defending the pressure by narrowing the vessels, speeding the heart, and holding fluid inside the circulation. A pressure that remains normal in a patient with a fast pulse, cool hands, delayed capillary refill, and thirst is not reassurance, and a narrowing pulse pressure is one of the earliest changes. The young patient and the fit patient compensate well and then deteriorate within a short time, and the finding that marks the turn is a pressure that begins to fall while the pulse continues to climb. The responder who waits for a low pressure before calling for help has waited too long, and the correct response is to treat the cause, keep the patient warm, and reduce the time to definitive care.

Positioning is a treatment, and its purpose is to help what volume remains reach the heart and the brain without making the breathing harder. The patient who has lost blood is laid flat and kept still, and the legs are raised only when there is no injury to the head, the chest, or the spine, no difficulty in breathing, and no pregnancy, because a raised leg in a patient who cannot breathe causes harm. A patient who is struggling for air is allowed to sit up, because breathing comes before the return of blood in that patient, and a patient who is unconscious but breathing is turned onto the side unless a spinal injury forbids it. Every movement is made slowly and with the neck and the trunk held in line, because movement costs pressure and pain, and the responder explains each movement before it is made. The finding that requires a change of position is any worsening of breathing or of the level of consciousness, and that change is reported at once.

Cold is a complication of trauma and not a detail of comfort, and a patient in shock becomes cold quickly because the volume that carried heat has been lost. A cold patient clots poorly, so the bleeding continues, and the continued bleeding removes more heat, so the two problems make each other worse, and a low body temperature also makes the heart more likely to develop a dangerous rhythm. A temperature below 35 degrees Celsius is often named as the point at which those effects become serious, and that figure is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the accepted line differs between services and editions. The lay first aider removes wet clothing by cutting it away, covers the patient with blankets, coats, or foil, insulates the patient from the ground, and keeps the vehicle or the room warm. The finding that warns the responder that cooling has already begun is a patient who has stopped shivering while still cold, because shivering is the body's attempt to make heat.

A patient in shock is usually thirsty, and thirst is a sign of the loss rather than a request that must be granted. Food and drink are withheld because an operation may be needed soon and a full stomach can be vomited into the airway when consciousness fades, and because the bowel tolerates little when the circulation is failing. The lips and the mouth may be moistened, and a note of the last time the patient ate or drank is carried to the receiving team, because that detail changes what the anesthesia team may do. The responder explains each act before it is performed, tells the patient what will happen next, answers questions honestly, and keeps talking to a patient whose eyes are closing.

The lay first aider has a defined and sufficient part in the care of a patient in shock, and it needs no equipment beyond the hands and whatever covering is at hand. The lay first aider calls emergency medical services early, describes the mechanism of injury and the condition of the patient, controls visible bleeding with firm uninterrupted pressure, and keeps that pressure in place without lifting the dressing to look. The patient is kept flat and warm, wet clothing is removed or covered, the patient is insulated from the ground, and the patient is given nothing to eat or drink. The lay first aider watches the skin, the pulse, the breathing, and the level of consciousness, and reports every change to the arriving team. The lay first aider does not press into a painful belly, does not straighten a deformed limb, does not remove an object embedded in a wound, does not loosen a tourniquet that has stopped the bleeding, and does not leave the patient alone.

The trained responder, the emergency medical technician, and the paramedic work within a protocol, a set of equipment, and an authorization that the lay first aider does not have. They repeat the primary survey, control bleeding by packing a wound that is suitable for packing, by a hemostatic dressing where the service carries one, and by a tourniquet where a limb continues to bleed, and each act is performed only as the protocol allows. They splint fractures in the position found, apply a pelvic binder where that device and that authorization exist, protect the spine, and give oxygen only as the protocol and the prescriber decide, because oxygen is a treatment with a decision behind it. They obtain access into a vein and make decisions about fluid and blood within the same protocol, and they monitor the patient continuously on the journey rather than at a single moment. Every one of those interventions is named with the provider level that owns it, and none becomes a lay skill because the patient looks desperate.

Fluid and blood resuscitation are advanced and hospital care, and the decisions are what a manual may describe while the amounts belong elsewhere. The substance that is given, the amount, the route, and the timing are set by the local protocol and by the prescriber, and the reason a book must not carry them is that the right answer changes with the mechanism, with the patient's age and illness, and with the distance to the hospital. Too little fluid leaves the tissues without oxygen, and too much dilutes the clotting factors, cools the patient, and may raise the pressure enough to restart bleeding that had stopped. The choice between a crystalloid solution and blood products, the use of blood that has been matched and blood that has not, and the decision to activate a massive transfusion belong to the hospital and to the trauma team. The finding that tells the team the bleeding is still active is a patient who responds to the first fluid and then deteriorates again, and that finding sends the patient to the operating room rather than to more fluid.

Definitive hospital care owns everything that repairs rather than contains, and the only true treatment of shock is the treatment of its cause. Stopping the bleeding inside the chest or the abdomen, repairing a torn vessel, relieving the pressure around the heart, decompressing a chest filled with air under pressure, supporting a failing heart, and treating an infection all belong to the hospital, and none is a field procedure no matter how urgent the patient appears. The surgeon may open the abdomen, open the chest, repair a vessel, remove a damaged organ, or place a drain, and the team watches the pressure, the pulse, the breathing, the urine output, the temperature, and the level of consciousness while those decisions are made. Needle decompression of the chest, an airway adjunct, oxygen, access into a vein, a pelvic binder, a traction splint, wound packing, a hemostatic dressing, a tourniquet, and spinal immobilization are advanced procedures decided by the protocol and the provider's training, while exploration of a wound, debridement, repair of a vessel or a tendon or a nerve, reduction of a fracture, fixation, fasciotomy, and transfusion are definitive procedures that no responder performs in the field.

A tourniquet that has controlled the bleeding of a limb is not loosened in the field, and the time it was applied is written on the patient where the receiving team will see it. The reason for the rule is that the clot that formed behind the tourniquet may not hold, so releasing it may produce hemorrhage that no one at the roadside can control, and the reason for the time is that the receiving team must know how long the limb has been without blood. A period such as 2 hours is often quoted as the point at which the risk to muscle and nerve becomes serious, and that interval is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because tolerance differs with temperature, with the patient, and with the injury. The record is written on the skin near the tourniquet, on a tape fixed to the limb, or in the place the local protocol agrees, and it is spoken aloud at handover. Reassessment after a tourniquet means confirming that the bleeding has stopped, watching for the signs of shock, checking the limb beyond the device, and searching for a second wound that was missed.

Some patients break the usual rules of recognition, and the responder who knows them is harder to mislead. A child compensates for a long time and then deteriorates within minutes, the child's usual pulse and breathing are faster than an adult's, and the caregiver who knows the child is often the first to see that something is wrong. An older patient may take medicines that slow the heart, so the pulse does not rise as expected, and may live with a pressure that is high at baseline, so a pressure that looks normal may already be too low for that patient. A pregnant patient can lose a large volume before the mother looks ill, the fetus is at risk before the mother is, and the pregnant patient who must lie down is tilted so that the weight of the womb does not press on the vessels that return blood to the heart. A patient with a spinal injury may have a slow pulse and warm skin rather than the fast pulse and cold skin of blood loss, so the pulse and the skin must be read together.

Shock may hide inside another injury, and the responder who fixes attention on the visible wound will miss it. A patient with a head injury may be confused from the brain injury, from alcohol, or from shock, and the three may exist together, so the level of consciousness cannot be used to dismiss the circulation. A patient with a burn loses plasma from the burned surface over the hours that follow, and a patient who has been crushed loses fluid into the muscle that was compressed, so both may be stable at the first contact and deteriorating later. A patient with an injury to the chest may be losing blood into the chest or developing a block to the return of blood to the heart while the outside of the chest looks untouched. A constructed illustration, offered as a construction and never as a real patient, is the rider who stands up after a collision with a grazed arm, whose pulse is fast and whose skin is cool while the arm explains none of it.

The traps that undo the care of a patient in shock are few and they repeat, and each has a finding that warns the responder in time. The first is to treat pain or fear as the whole explanation, when restlessness and a fast pulse in an injured patient belong to shock until proved otherwise. The second is to give fluid as though it were the treatment, when the treatment is to stop the bleeding and to reach the place where it can be stopped, and the third is to wait for a low pressure before calling for help. The fourth is to warm the patient after the journey rather than before it, and the fifth is to attribute a dulling level of consciousness to alcohol or to age rather than to the circulation. The sixth is to stop watching once the patient looks better, because a patient who improves after the first treatment can deteriorate again, and the seventh is to forget the record, since a tourniquet time that was never written is a time that was never made.

The record that travels with the patient carries the mechanism of injury, the time of the injury, the observations in the order they were made, the bleeding that was controlled, the tourniquet and its time, the warmth that was provided, and every change that was seen. A value written without a time cannot be compared with the next one, so the time belongs beside every number, and the trend across several sets of observations carries more meaning than any single set. Pain relief is a clinical decision, and the finding that makes it urgent is severe pain together with a circulation that is failing, while the substance, the amount, the route, and the timing belong to the provider authorized to prescribe and to the local protocol. The handover is spoken aloud in the same order, and the receiving team continues a story that the field began, so a gap in that story forces them to start again while the patient waits.

To summarize, shock is the failure of the circulation to deliver enough oxygen to the tissues, and it is recognized by the patient rather than by a number, because the pressure is defended until the reserve is spent. The four mechanisms are hypovolemic, obstructive, cardiogenic, and distributive, and naming the mechanism matters because the treatment of one can harm a patient with another. The skin, the pulse, the level of consciousness, and the breathing are read with the hands and the eyes, repeated in the same order, and compared so that the trend becomes the diagnosis. The lay first aider calls for help early, controls visible bleeding, keeps the patient flat and warm, withholds food and drink, and watches without stopping, while the trained responder works within a protocol and a defined authorization, and the surgeon owns everything that repairs. Fluids and blood are decisions of advanced and hospital care whose substance, amount, route, and timing belong to the local protocol and the prescriber, a tourniquet is never loosened in the field and its time is written on the patient, and every figure in this chapter is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.
