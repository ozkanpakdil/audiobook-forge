# Chapter 23 — Elbow Injuries

The elbow is a joint where a small injury can destroy the whole limb, because the bones, the artery, and the three nerves that cross it lie in a tight envelope with no room to swell. Scene safety comes before the patient, so traffic, fire, electricity, water, animals, violence, and the patient's own blood are dealt with before a hand is laid on the arm. The lethal possibility to name first is an injury that has interrupted the blood supply to the hand, and the finding that reveals it is a hand that is pale, cool, pulseless at the wrist, and painful while the fingers no longer move. This chapter names the provider level for every intervention: the sling of the lay first aider, the splint of the trained responder or the paramedic, and the surgery of definitive hospital care. It is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country where you work. A small injury here can leave a stiff and useless arm, so the responder moves the joint less and the patient sooner.

The elbow is not one joint but a meeting of three bones, the humerus above and the radius and the ulna below, held in one tight capsule. The brachial artery passes in front of the joint and divides below it into the arteries of the forearm, while the median, the ulnar, and the radial nerves pass around the joint to supply the hand. The ulnar nerve lies in a shallow groove behind the inner side of the elbow, and the radial nerve winds around the bone above the joint, where a displaced fracture can stretch it. Because the capsule is tight and the space inside it is small, swelling after a fracture or a dislocation raises the pressure quickly, and that pressure squeezes the vessels and nerves within the same envelope. There is almost no muscle over the prominences of the elbow, only skin and a thin layer of fat, so a bone end can open the skin from inside and turn a closed fracture into an open one.

The mechanism of injury is the first evidence at the elbow, because the direction of the force predicts which structure has been damaged. A fall onto the outstretched hand drives force up the forearm into the joint and is the classic cause of a fracture of the radial head, of a break just above the joint, and of a dislocation. A direct blow to the point of the elbow breaks the olecranon, the bony tip that the triceps muscle pulls on, and that fracture may open the skin over it. A fall from a height or a vehicle collision delivers far more energy and produces fractures that are comminuted, meaning broken into several pieces, with damage to the artery and the nerves. A mechanism such as a fall from 3 meters is a teaching illustration whose height and meaning must be checked against the guideline in force and the local protocol, because the energy absorbed depends on the surface and on the body's position. The responder who hears the mechanism is already looking for a specific injury rather than examining at random.

The primary survey in its fixed order of airway, breathing, circulation, disability, and exposure is not abandoned because the arm looks terrible. Life comes before limb when two correct actions compete, so a patient whose airway is obstructed or whose circulation is failing is treated before the elbow is examined, and the responder says this order aloud so that no helper pulls on the arm. Catastrophic bleeding from an open elbow wound is controlled by firm direct pressure with a clean dressing and, where the trained responder or the paramedic works under a protocol, by packing the wound or by a tourniquet above the injury. A foreign body embedded in the elbow is stabilized in place and never removed in the field, because removal may release hemorrhage that cannot be controlled and may cut a vessel or a nerve that is still intact. The finding that tells the responder that pressure has failed is blood that soaks through every layer, a swelling that grows while the hand is held in place, or a level of consciousness that is dulling. An open fracture of the elbow is a surgical emergency, and the field dressing protects the wound but does not clean it, does not reduce it, and does not close it.

The neurovascular examination of the elbow is performed before the arm is splinted and again after every intervention, because a record made after the splint cannot tell anyone what the splint changed. The pulse is felt at the wrist on the thumb side, which is the radial artery, and the responder compares it with the other arm. The color and warmth of the hand are compared with the other hand, and the time it takes for color to return after a fingertip is pressed and released is observed. Sensation is tested in the areas each nerve owns, on the thumb side of the palm for the median nerve, on the little finger for the ulnar nerve, and between the thumb and the index finger for the radial nerve. Movement is tested by asking the patient to bend the fingers, to spread them, to oppose the thumb to the little finger, and to bend the wrist upward. The classic signs of compromise are pain, pallor, pulselessness, paresthesia, paralysis, and coldness, and pain out of proportion to the wound and increased by passively straightening the fingers is the earliest and most reliable. The finding that makes this an emergency is any loss of pulse, new numbness or weakness, or a hand that is colder and paler than its partner, and a pulse that can still be felt never proves that the limb is safe.

Fractures of the distal humerus, the part of the upper arm bone that forms the upper half of the joint, most often threaten the limb, because the bone splits between the two columns that support the joint. A fall on the elbow or on the outstretched hand in an older patient with thin bone commonly produces this fracture, and the arm is swollen, deformed, and painful, with movement refused. In a young child the fracture occurs just above the joint, where the bone is still growing, and it is called a supracondylar fracture; the child cries when the arm is moved, and the pulse at the wrist may be lost later as swelling increases. The brachial artery and the median nerve pass in front of this bone, so a displaced fracture can injure either, and the finding is a pale, cool hand with a weak or absent pulse and numbness on the palm side. A fracture that has opened the skin, or one whose pieces are far apart, cannot be treated with a splint alone, and definitive hospital care owns the decision about how it will be held. The responder does not reduce the fracture, does not pull on the forearm to make the arm straight, and does not test the joint through its range, because each act can convert a repairable injury into a lost limb.

Fractures of the radial head, the disc of bone at the top of the radius that turns when the palm is turned, are often the mildest-looking injuries at the elbow and the easiest to underestimate. They follow a fall onto the outstretched hand, and the patient reports pain on the outer side of the elbow that becomes sharp when the forearm is turned, with the joint held slightly bent. The swelling may be modest and the deformity absent, so a responder who looks only for a crooked arm will miss the fracture, and the tenderness is found by pressing gently over the outer side of the joint. Fractures of the olecranon, the bony point into which the triceps inserts, follow a direct blow, and the muscle pulls the broken piece upward so the patient cannot straighten the elbow against resistance, while the thin skin over the point is frequently torn. Definitive hospital care owns the imaging, which may include a computed tomography scan, or CT, and the decision about fixing or replacing the broken bone, because an untreated fracture that blocks the joint leaves a stiff elbow. The lay first aider covers an open wound with a clean dressing, supports the arm in the position found, and sends the patient for surgical care without exploring the wound.

A dislocation of the elbow means that the bones have lost their normal relationship, and the posterior dislocation, in which the forearm bones move behind the humerus, is by far the most common. It follows a fall onto the outstretched hand with the elbow slightly bent, and the arm is held in a fixed partial bend that the patient will not allow to be changed. The findings are a shortened forearm, a bony prominence that gives the elbow a squared-off shape, a hollow where the tip of the elbow normally sits, and severe pain with a refusal to move. The vessels and nerves are stretched across the displaced bones, so the pulse at the wrist may be weak or absent and the fingers may tingle or feel numb. Reduction, which is the act of putting the joint back into its place, belongs to definitive hospital care and is performed by a physician with sedation, analgesia, and preparation for what may be found. It is never a field procedure for a lay first aider, a trained responder, or a paramedic, because a dislocation may carry a fracture, a torn artery, or a trapped nerve, and an unskilled pull can lose the limb.

A dislocation with an associated fracture is the injury that most deserves the respect of every responder, because the combination is greater than the sum of its parts. The most familiar form is a posterior dislocation in which the head of the radius and a piece of the ulna are also broken, a pattern that leaves the joint unstable and the blood supply to the bone at risk. Another form is a dislocation with a fracture of the humerus at the joint, and another is the open dislocation in which the bone has torn the skin, and each requires imaging, anesthesia, and often an operation. The finding that suggests an associated fracture is a dislocation without the usual shape, a bony fragment that can be felt, a wound that communicates with the joint, or a deficit that does not improve when the arm is supported. Definitive hospital care owns the reduction and the repair, and images taken before and after the reduction decide the operation that follows. A first responder who tries to reduce such an elbow because the deformity looks like a simple dislocation may push a bone fragment into a vessel and may be the reason the limb is lost.

The position of comfort is the position in which the patient holds the injured arm when it is left alone. The lay first aider supports that position rather than changing it, with a folded blanket or a pillow beneath the arm, and then a sling to take the weight of the forearm. The position that must never be forced is any position the patient resists, and above all the fully straight arm that a responder might try to produce to make the limb look normal. Forcing the elbow straight may drive bone through the skin, tear the artery, and stretch a nerve beyond recovery, and pulling the forearm to reduce a dislocation does the same damage. A child who will not use the arm after being lifted by the hand may have a slipped radial head rather than a fracture, and no lay first aider may attempt to put it back, because that maneuver is a clinical act. Immobilization for transport is performed in the position found, with padding over the bony points and a rigid support from the upper arm to the wrist. The device is not placed over an open wound and is not tightened so much that the fingers become pale or numb, because a splint that stops the circulation has done the work of a tourniquet. The pulse, color, warmth, and sensation are rechecked after the splint is secured, and a pale or pulseless hand means the splint must be loosened at once.

The assessment of the elbow is not an act performed once at the roadside but a rhythm repeated from the first contact to the handover, because the injury can worsen while the responder watches. The examination is repeated in the same order every time, with the pulse, the color and warmth of the hand, the sensation in the three nerve areas, the movement of the fingers, and the pain reported by the patient. It is repeated after a dressing, a sling, a splint, a move onto a stretcher, and after any change in the patient's condition, because an intervention that helps one patient can harm another. The level of consciousness, the breathing, and the pulse of the patient are recorded with the same discipline, since a patient whose hand remains well but whose circulation is failing elsewhere has a new priority. The finding that returns the responder to the beginning of the primary survey is any deterioration at all, and the finding that marks a worsening limb is a weaker pulse, a colder hand, or sharper and more diffuse pain. A time such as 30 minutes between the first and the second examination is a teaching illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the interval suited to one service may not suit another.

Compartment syndrome turns a treatable elbow injury into a destroyed forearm, and it happens when pressure inside the closed envelopes of tissue around the muscles rises until blood can no longer enter. The forearm is divided into compartments by sheets of tough tissue, and swelling after a fracture, a dislocation, or a crush injury raises the pressure within them, so muscle and nerve begin to die while the skin remains intact and the pulse remains palpable. The earliest and most reliable finding is pain out of proportion to the injury, increased by passively straightening the fingers and not relieved by the support the responder has applied, with a forearm that feels tense and wood-like. Numbness, weakness, and a hand that becomes pale and cold follow, but they follow late, and a responder who waits for them has waited too long, while the presence of a pulse never rules the condition out. The responder removes any bandage or splint that constricts the limb, keeps the arm at the level of the heart rather than raised above it, keeps the patient warm, and moves the patient to definitive care with the history of the pain. Definitive hospital care owns the measurement of pressure within the compartments and the operation called a fasciotomy, in which the tight sheets are opened to let the muscle expand.

A wound over the elbow is treated with firm and uninterrupted pressure, a clean covering, and no probing of the track with a finger or an instrument. The trained responder may irrigate a contaminated wound where the local protocol authorizes it, using the technique and the volume the protocol sets. An open fracture is covered with a clean or sterile dressing and splinted in the position found, and the exposed bone is not pushed back beneath the skin, because pushing it in drives contamination deeper. The decision about protection against tetanus is made at the first contact by the provider authorized to make it, and it depends on the vaccination history of the patient and the nature of the wound, while the substance and amount belong to the prescriber. The decision about antibiotics is made in the same way, and the finding that makes it urgent is a wound that is deep, contaminated, or connected to a broken bone. The finding that marks the wound as a surgical problem is bone visible in the wound, a wound that communicates with the joint, or bleeding that continues despite correct pressure.

A torn artery at the elbow can kill the patient as well as lose the limb, and its findings are blood that spurts or soaks through every dressing, a swelling that expands while the responder watches, and a hand that is pale, cold, and insensate. A tourniquet above the elbow is the field answer to bleeding from the arm that cannot be controlled by direct pressure and packing, and it belongs to the trained responder or paramedic working under a protocol. Once a tourniquet has controlled the bleeding it is not loosened in the field, and the time it was applied is written on the patient where the receiving team cannot miss it, because that time decides how the circulation is restored. An interval such as 2 hours is often named as the point at which the risk to muscle and nerve becomes serious, and that interval must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because tolerance differs with temperature and with the patient. The finding that tells the responder a tourniquet is not enough is bleeding that continues below a correctly tightened device, and the finding that the patient is losing the battle is a level of consciousness that dulls while the pulse climbs. A tourniquet is never a reason to delay transport, and it is never relaxed to see whether the bleeding has stopped, because the clot behind it may not hold.

Damage to a nerve at the elbow may be present from the moment of injury and may be discovered only by an examination performed deliberately, and the record of that examination is as important as any treatment given in the field. Injury to the median nerve weakens the muscles that bend the thumb and fingers, so the patient may be unable to oppose the thumb to the little finger, while sensation is lost over the palm side of the thumb and index finger. Injury to the ulnar nerve weakens the small muscles of the hand, so the fingers cannot be spread with force, the little finger drifts away, and sensation is lost along the little finger and the edge of the hand. Injury to the radial nerve produces a wrist drop, in which the hand hangs at the wrist and cannot be lifted, with numbness between the thumb and the index finger on the back of the hand. The examination is recorded before the arm is splinted and repeated after, because a deficit that appears only after immobilization points to the splint, while a deficit present from the beginning must be known to the receiving team. Nothing done in the field restores a torn nerve, and the exploration and repair of a nerve belong to definitive hospital care, where the decision is made with the operating microscope.

A young child with a supracondylar fracture may hold the arm still and cry only when it is moved, and a child who refuses to use an arm after a fall must be treated as seriously injured until a clinician decides otherwise, because swelling can close the blood supply to the hand. A child who has been lifted or swung by the hand may have a slipped radial head rather than a fracture, and that child needs clinical assessment rather than a lay attempt at manipulation. An older patient with thin bone may sustain a distal humerus fracture broken into many pieces from a fall at standing height, and the same patient may have a dislocated elbow with little deformity and a great deal of pain. A patient who has been drinking alcohol, or whose level of consciousness is reduced, cannot be relied on to report pain or numbness, so the physical findings of pulse, color, warmth, and swelling carry the whole assessment. An injury at the elbow is rarely the only injury in a patient who has fallen from a height or been in a collision, so the patient is examined from head to toe when the primary survey allows. A constructed illustration, offered as a construction and never as a real patient or incident, is a worker who falls from a ladder onto an outstretched hand and complains only of the elbow, while tenderness over the wrist is found because the mechanism demanded the search.

Pain is real, and poorly treated pain worsens the patient's condition and makes every examination harder, so the relief of pain is a clinical objective rather than a courtesy. The decision to give analgesia, the choice of substance, the amount, the route, and the frequency belong to the provider authorized to prescribe under the local protocol, and this manual names only the decision. Protection against tetanus, the choice of antibiotics, the decision to give oxygen, and fluids given into a vein are decisions of the same kind, made by the clinician authorized to make them under the local protocol. Hypothermia travels with every serious limb injury and must be prevented from the first minute, because a cold patient bleeds more, feels more pain, and tolerates the journey less well. Protection begins by moving the patient onto a dry insulating layer, removing wet clothing without exposing the wound, and covering the patient, including the injured arm. A body temperature such as 35 degrees Celsius is often described as the point at which cold itself becomes dangerous, and that figure must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the accepted line differs with age and with the way the temperature is measured. A patient who has stopped shivering while still cold is a patient in whom the warming has failed, and that finding is reported rather than noted.

The record is part of the treatment, and at the elbow it carries information that no receiving team can reconstruct from the arm alone. The handover states the mechanism and the time of injury, the position in which the limb was found, the nerve and pulse findings before and after each dressing, sling, and splint, and every tourniquet with its time. Definitive hospital care owns everything that repairs rather than supports, and the physician orders the radiographs and, where the fracture is complex, a computed tomography scan, or CT, while magnetic resonance imaging, or MRI, answers the remaining questions. In hospital a dislocation is reduced with analgesia, sedation, or anesthesia and with imaging before and after, and unstable pieces are fixed internally with plates and screws. A damaged artery or nerve is repaired by a surgeon with the appropriate training, a compartment syndrome is treated by fasciotomy, and the intensive care unit, or ICU, receives the patient whose injuries reach beyond the limb. The surgeon decides when the elbow may begin to move and what rehabilitation follows, because a joint kept still for too long becomes stiff and a joint moved too early loses the repair.

Urgent hospital evaluation is required for any deformity, any wound over the elbow, any inability to move the joint, any tense or increasing swelling, any loss of pulse, sensation, or strength in the hand, and any severe pain. It is required for every dislocation and every suspected fracture, because the joint cannot be examined properly without images and because a missed injury leaves a permanently stiff arm. It is required for the child who will not use the arm and for the older patient whose bone has broken with little force, because both injuries are more serious than they appear. The destination is chosen before the patient is moved, and it is the nearest facility that can image the joint and provide surgical care, which may be a trauma center or a district hospital that stabilizes and transfers. The mistakes that cost limbs are few and repeating: forcing the joint straight, pulling the forearm to reduce a dislocation, binding a splint so tightly that the fingers lose their color and pulse, probing a wound, and failing to repeat the examination. The finding that makes evaluation an emergency rather than an urgency is any neurovascular deficit, any open wound, or a growing swelling, because the interval in which the limb can still be saved is measured in hours.

To summarize, the elbow is a joint where a small injury can destroy the limb, because the artery, the nerves, and the bones that cross it lie within a tight envelope, and the first duty at the scene is safety and the control of hemorrhage. The mechanism of injury predicts the damage, and the fractures that matter are the distal humerus, the radial head, and the olecranon, together with dislocations and the dislocation with an associated fracture. The neurovascular examination is performed before and after every intervention, recorded with the time, and repeated until handover, because a pulse that is present does not prove that the limb is safe and the earliest sign of disaster is pain out of proportion. The position of comfort is supported and the position the patient resists is never forced, the arm is splinted in the position found, no dislocation or fracture is reduced in the field, and no exposed bone is pushed back into a wound. Pain relief, protection against tetanus, antibiotics, oxygen, and fluids are decisions of the provider authorized to prescribe under the local protocol, and no substance, amount, route, or frequency may be taken from this chapter. Hypothermia is prevented from the first minute, the record travels with the patient, and every figure named here must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. Definitive hospital care owns the imaging, the reduction, the repair of vessels and nerves, the fixation of bones, and the operation for compartment syndrome, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, or the law where you work.
