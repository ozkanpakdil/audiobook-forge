# Chapter 17 — Flail Chest

A chest that has lost the ability to move as one piece is among the quietest catastrophes in trauma, because the patient may speak, sit upright, and look almost well while the breathing is already failing from within. Before any hand is laid on that patient the scene must be made safe, since the same energy that broke the ribs may have left traffic moving, fuel escaping, a structure unstable, a wire live, or a person still violent, and the patient's own blood and body fluids are a hazard that a barrier of gloves and eye protection is meant to answer. The lethal possibility to name first is respiratory failure, and the finding that reveals it is a section of the chest wall that sinks as the patient breathes in and rises as the patient breathes out, moving against the rest of the rib cage instead of with it. This chapter describes what that movement means, why the bruised lung beneath it matters more than the broken bones, what each level of provider may do, and where definitive hospital care begins, and it is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country where you work.

Flail chest is the condition in which a portion of the chest wall loses its bony continuity with the rest of the rib cage and can no longer carry the pull of the muscles that normally lift and spread the ribs. The usual description speaks of several adjacent ribs, each broken in more than one place, so that the piece between the breaks is attached to the spine and to nothing else, and the exact count of ribs and of breaks that defines the segment is a teaching figure to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because services and editions do not all draw that line in the same spot. The loose piece is sometimes called a floating segment, although the name misleads, because the piece does not move freely; it moves with the pressure inside the chest rather than with the muscles outside it. That distinction is the whole of the injury, because the chest wall is a pump, and a pump whose wall no longer follows the muscle cannot move air with the same efficiency.

The mechanism that produces this injury transfers a great deal of energy into a broad area of the chest, and the mechanism of injury is therefore the first clue available before any examination begins. A vehicle collision in which the chest strikes the steering wheel or the door, a fall from a height onto the side, a heavy object that crushes the trunk, a blast wave, and a blow delivered by a horse or a machine are the familiar stories, and each predicts injury to the lung beneath the break as surely as to the bone. A constructed illustration, offered as a construction and never as a real patient or a real incident, is the restrained driver whose chest met the wheel, who climbed out of the vehicle with a bruised side, and who before long was breathing quickly and could not finish a sentence, because the ribs were not the part of the injury that was worsening. The responder who hears such a story should stop searching for a wound that explains the patient and start listening to the breathing, because the account has already named the organ at risk.

The reason the loose segment travels the wrong way lies in the pressure that surrounds it on both sides of the chest wall. During inspiration the muscles enlarge the chest and the pressure inside falls below the pressure of the air outside, so the loose piece is pushed inward while the intact rib cage moves outward, and during expiration the pressure inside rises and the piece is pushed outward while the rest of the chest falls. The result is the paradoxical movement that gives the injury its name, a section of wall that travels in the opposite direction to its neighbors with every breath. That sign is easy to miss, because it can be hidden by clothing, by a blanket, or by the position in which the patient is sitting, so the chest must be exposed for the examination while the patient is kept warm. The movement may also be delayed, since in the first minutes the patient splints the chest by tensing the muscles around it, and the paradox becomes plain only when those muscles tire. The finding that tells the responder the injury is present is that opposition of movement, seen with the chest exposed and in good light, and the finding that tells the responder it is worsening is a breathing pattern that becomes faster, shallower, and more effortful.

Beneath the broken ribs, and sometimes beneath a chest wall that still moves almost normally, lies the injury that actually kills, which is the bruise of the lung itself. A pulmonary contusion is bleeding and fluid collecting in the tissue of the lung, and it does not respect the borders of the broken bones, because the energy that fractured the ribs passed through the lung and tore the small vessels and the air sacs within it. The bruised area becomes stiff and filled with fluid, so it cannot expand, cannot clear its secretions, and cannot pass oxygen into the blood even though air may be reaching it. The injury is progressive rather than static, which means the lung is usually less damaged at the first examination than it will be hours later, and a patient who seems comfortable at the roadside may be approaching respiratory failure by the time the ambulance reaches the hospital. The conclusion is that the ribs are the visible part of the injury and the lung is the dangerous part, and treatment aimed only at the ribs will not save the patient.

Respiratory failure is the destination toward which all of this travels, and it arrives by a path that the responder can recognize in stages. Pain limits the depth of every breath, the bruised lung cannot take part in the exchange, and the small airways begin to close where secretions collect, so the patient compensates by breathing faster and faster. That compensation is exhausting, and a patient who has been breathing at a rapid rate for hours begins to tire, at which point the rate may fall and the breathing becomes shallow and ineffective. Carbon dioxide then accumulates while oxygen falls, and the patient becomes restless, then drowsy, then confused, and the color of the lips and the skin may change toward blue. The finding that should frighten the responder is not a single measurement but a trend, because a patient who is breathing faster than before, working harder than before, and answering more slowly than before is moving toward failure whatever the first impression suggested. The endpoint is a patient who cannot maintain the work of breathing at all, and that patient needs a hospital team and a ventilator rather than a dressing and a longer wait.

Recognition at the scene rests on the mechanism, the breathing, and the whole appearance of the patient rather than on any instrument. A breathing rate above 30 breaths per minute in an adult is often quoted as a warning figure, and that number, like every other in this chapter, is an illustration to be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the threshold differs with age and with the patient's usual state. The findings that accompany it are visible use of the neck and shoulder muscles, a patient who cannot speak a full sentence without stopping for air, a chest that does not rise equally on both sides, tenderness over several ribs, and a crackling sensation under the fingers where air has escaped into the tissue. The patient may also be anxious, restless, or difficult to keep still, and that behavior is a sign of oxygen shortage rather than of poor cooperation. The responder who finds these signs should not wait for the paradox to become obvious, because the judgment is made from the whole picture and not from one sign alone, and that picture includes the head, the abdomen, the pelvis, and the limbs, since a patient with a flail chest has usually been subjected to enough energy to injure more than the chest.

Flail chest seldom travels alone, and the reason is the amount of energy required to produce it. The same blow that breaks a series of ribs may tear the pleura and allow air into the space around the lung, may fill that space with blood, may bruise the heart, may tear the diaphragm, and may injure the liver or the spleen beneath the lower rib cage. A patient who becomes suddenly worse, with a rising breathing rate, a falling level of consciousness, distended neck veins, and an absent breath sound on one side, may have a tension pneumothorax, which is air trapped under pressure around a lung and is a lethal state that a trained responder may relieve under a protocol while a surgeon treats its cause. A hemothorax is blood collecting in that same space, and it is suspected when the chest is dull to percussion and the circulation is failing without an external wound to explain the loss. A needle introduced into the chest, a tube left in the chest, and the opening of the chest in an operating room are all definitive procedures and not field measures, and this manual names them so that a listener recognizes the urgency without being tempted to imitate it. The finding that must prompt immediate escalation is any deterioration the responder cannot explain by the ribs alone.

The lay first aider's part in this injury is small, specific, and more valuable than any improvised procedure. The scene is made safe first, and then emergency medical services are called at once and told that a patient has been struck in the chest, is breathing quickly, and cannot speak without pausing, because that description moves the right resources toward the scene. The patient is kept in the position that eases the breathing, which is usually sitting up and leaning slightly forward with the arms supported, and the patient is not made to lie flat, because lying flat worsens the work of breathing in most chest injuries. A conscious patient usually finds that position without instruction, and the responder should let the patient choose it while protecting the neck and the spine if the mechanism makes spinal injury possible. Nothing is bound around the chest, nothing is pressed onto the broken ribs to test them, and nothing is given by mouth, because a patient who may need an operation should arrive with an empty stomach. The patient is covered and insulated from the ground, because a chest exposed for examination and a patient who lies still for a long time both lose heat quickly, and the lay first aider then stays with the patient and reports any change in the breathing or the level of consciousness to the team on arrival, because that continuous observation is itself a treatment.

Oxygen is one of the measures that can change the course of this injury before the hospital, and its use is a decision that belongs to the trained responder and the paramedic under a protocol, never to the lay first aider with bare hands. The trained responder carries oxygen, a mask or a cannula, and an authorization that names when and how it is given, so the amount, the flow, and the delivery method are set by the local protocol and the prescriber rather than by any figure in this book. The finding that makes the decision urgent is a patient who is short of breath, confused, restless, or blue about the lips, together with a breathing pattern that is failing to keep up with the demand. Oxygen does not repair the bruised lung, and it must never become a reason to delay transport, because the definitive treatment for a contused lung is hospital care, monitoring, and often a ventilator. The patient receiving oxygen is watched continuously, because the appearance of the patient matters more than the device, and a patient who becomes drowsy while receiving oxygen is a patient whose breathing is failing. Nothing in this paragraph is a prescription, and no reader should carry away a flow, a concentration, or a duration from this manual rather than from the protocol that governs their practice.

The trap that has killed more patients with this injury than any other error in first aid is the tightly bound chest. For much of the last century the treatment taught for broken ribs was to strap the chest, to bind it with wide tape, or to hold it still between sandbags, and the reasoning seemed sensible because the patient was in pain and movement hurt. The result was the opposite of help, because a chest that cannot expand cannot clear its secretions and cannot keep the small airways open, so the lung beneath the fracture collapsed, infection followed, and the patient died of the treatment rather than of the injury. No provider at any level in this manual binds a chest tightly, straps a chest, or places weight upon it, and that rule holds for the lay first aider, for the trained responder, and for the paramedic alike. Splinting the chest is not the same as supporting a limb, because the chest wall is a moving pump and must be allowed to move, and the pain it causes is treated by other means and by professional decisions. The finding that should make the responder stop any binding at once is a patient who is breathing less deeply and making less noise after the chest has been wrapped, because a quiet chest in an injured patient is not a comfort but a warning.

Once the patient is positioned, covered, and moving toward care, the work becomes a rhythm of watching rather than a series of new acts. The trained responder repeats the observations of the primary survey in the same order, because a breathing rate that has climbed or fallen, a breathing pattern that has become noisy, a pulse that has become rapid and weak, skin that has become pale and cool, or a level of consciousness that has dulled all mean the patient is moving in the wrong direction. The record carries the position that helped, the oxygen that was given where the protocol allowed it, and the time of every change, because the receiving team continues a story that began in the field. The finding that ends the watching is the handover, and even then the account of the trend is the most useful thing the responder can pass on.

Cold is a complication of chest trauma rather than a matter of comfort, and a patient with a flail chest is unusually easy to cool. The chest is often undressed for examination, the patient lies still because movement hurts, and the work of breathing already consumes a great deal of energy, so heat is lost from an exposed surface by a body that cannot spare it. Cold makes the blood clot poorly, deepens shock, and makes the heart prone to dangerous rhythms, and in a patient whose oxygen supply is already fragile those effects can be decisive. A body temperature below 35 degrees Celsius is often named as the point at which cold itself becomes dangerous, and that figure must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the accepted line shifts with age, with the site of measurement, and with the way the temperature is taken. The lay first aider covers the patient with whatever is available, insulates the patient from the ground, and removes wet clothing without exposing the chest longer than the examination requires. The trained responder keeps the vehicle or the room warm during transport, limits the number of times the patient is uncovered, and remembers that a patient who has stopped shivering while still cold has already lost the ability to make heat.

Pain is not a side issue in this injury, because pain is the reason the patient cannot breathe deeply enough to keep the lung open. A patient who is hurting guards the chest, takes small quick breaths, and cannot cough, and that pattern is what allows secretions to collect and the lung to collapse, so the relief of pain is part of the treatment of the breathing and not a kindness added afterward. The decision about pain relief belongs to the provider authorized to make it under the local protocol, which in most systems means the paramedic and the physician, and the substance, the amount, the route, and the frequency are set by that protocol and that prescriber rather than by this manual. The lay first aider gives no medicine for this injury, offers nothing by mouth, and does not move the patient to a more comfortable place if movement makes the breathing worse. The finding that makes the decision urgent is a patient whose breathing is shallow and whose coughing is absent because of pain, and the finding that must be reported before any medicine is considered is a falling level of consciousness or a breathing pattern that is already failing. Definitive hospital care may use methods that block the sensation from a group of ribs, and those methods require a trained operator and monitoring that the field does not have.

Movement is a treatment decision in a patient with a flail chest, and it must be as gentle as the urgency allows, because a patient with broken ribs who is rolled roughly or carried in an awkward position splints the chest further and arrives worse than they left. If the mechanism makes a spinal injury possible, the head and the neck are held and the patient is moved as a unit, and the chest injury is not used as a reason to abandon that precaution. The destination is chosen before the patient is lifted, and it is the nearest facility that can provide imaging, breathing support, and surgical care, which is usually a trauma center rather than the closest building with a bed. A patient with a failing breathing pattern should not wait for a distant specialist if a nearer hospital can stabilize the breathing and arrange the transfer. The finding that decides the destination is not the size of the injury but the stability of the breathing, and a patient who is tiring needs the shortest safe route to a team that can support ventilation.

In the hospital, the diagnosis is confirmed by examination and by imaging, and the imaging serves the whole patient rather than the ribs alone. A chest radiograph may show the fractures, the blood or air around the lung, and the separation of the ribs, while computed tomography, or CT, shows the contused lung far more clearly and reveals injuries that a plain film can miss. Ultrasound performed by a trained clinician can find blood or air around the lung at the bedside, and it is used where the service has the equipment and the training. The examination also weighs the patient's age, the state of the lungs before the injury, and the presence of other injuries, because those factors decide the course more than the count of broken ribs does. A contused volume of about one fifth of the lung is sometimes used in teaching to describe the extent of the bruise, and any such figure must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the measure differs with the method and with the observer. The diagnosis is therefore not a count of fractures but a judgment about a patient whose breathing is at risk.

Much of the hospital treatment of a flail chest is devoted to the lung rather than to the bones, and it begins with the same priorities with which the field began. Pain control is provided in a way that allows deep breathing and coughing, and the team watches the level of consciousness and the breathing while that is achieved. Breathing exercises, devices that encourage a deep breath, clearance of secretions, and movement out of bed are used to keep the small airways open and to prevent pneumonia, because infection in a lung that is already bruised is a common cause of a poor outcome. The patient is monitored with repeated examination and with measurements of oxygen and carbon dioxide, and the trend of those measurements decides whether the breathing is being supported adequately. When the work of breathing cannot be sustained, the patient is supported with a ventilator in an intensive care unit, where the machine takes over the work of the muscles, holds the lung open, and allows the contused areas time to heal. Ventilation is a clinical decision made on the trend of the breathing and the blood gases rather than on a single reading, and no figure in this paragraph should be taken as a rule, because every threshold must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. The finding that tells the team a patient is ready to breathe alone is a stable breathing pattern with adequate gas exchange while the support is reduced, and that trial is conducted by the intensive care team with continuous observation.

Surgical fixation of the broken ribs is a definitive procedure that belongs to the trauma surgeon and the operating room, and it is not a field measure under any circumstance. Plates, screws, and pins may be used to rebuild the chest wall in selected patients, usually when the wall is so unstable that the patient cannot be separated from the ventilator, when a deformity threatens the lung, or when the chest is already open for another reason. The purpose is to restore a wall that can move with the muscles and to reduce pain enough that the patient can breathe and cough, and the decision rests on the whole patient rather than on the appearance of the ribs on an image. Even a skilled paramedic does not reduce, stabilize, or reposition the ribs of a flail chest in the field, and no responder should press on the loose segment to test it or attempt to push it back into place. A chest tube placed to drain blood or air, and an operation on the heart or the great vessels, are further definitive procedures under the same rule, and the field responder's task is to recognize the need and to deliver the patient quickly. The listener should hear in this paragraph the boundary of practice, because an unauthorized attempt at the roadside may turn an injury that could be repaired into one that cannot.

Flail chest is best understood as a diagnosis of the whole patient rather than of the ribs, because the ribs will heal whether or not anyone touches them and the patient may not survive to see it. The outcome is decided by the extent of the pulmonary contusion, by the patient's reserve of lung and heart, by the presence of other injuries, and by the speed with which breathing support and pain control are provided. An older patient with chronic lung disease and a modest fracture may fare worse than a young patient with a more dramatic injury, because reserve matters more than the visible damage, and a patient older than 65 years is often described as being at higher risk, a figure to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. A child's chest wall is soft and yielding, so a great deal of energy can pass into the lung with few or no ribs broken, and a pregnant patient adds further considerations because the growing uterus changes the position of the diaphragm. Certain failures recur, and each is preventable, beginning with the dismissal of a patient who looks well at first contact, continuing with the bound or strapped chest that an older teaching still encourages, and ending with the missed companion injury such as air trapped under pressure around the lung. The defense against all of them is the same, which is to treat the mechanism as a prediction, to repeat the examination in the same order, and to move the patient toward definitive care without waiting for certainty.

To summarize, a flail chest is a segment of the chest wall that has lost its continuity and moves with the pressure of breathing rather than with the muscles, and paradoxical movement is the sign that reveals it. The bruise of the lung beneath the break is the injury that kills, and respiratory failure is the destination toward which pain, collapse, and infection all lead, so the patient is treated as a whole person and not as a set of broken ribs. The scene is made safe first, emergency medical services are called early, the patient is positioned upright and supported and kept warm, and the lay first aider binds nothing, presses nothing, and gives nothing by mouth. Oxygen and pain relief are decisions owned by the trained responder, the paramedic, and the prescriber under the local protocol, and no dose, route, or frequency may be taken from these pages. Definitive hospital care owns analgesia, ventilation, drainage of the chest, and surgical fixation of the chest wall, and none of those is a field procedure, while the field contribution is recognition, support, warmth, continuous observation, and the shortest safe journey to a trauma center. Every figure in this chapter, whether a breathing rate, a temperature, an age, or an extent of contusion, is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such numbers differ between services, countries, and editions.
