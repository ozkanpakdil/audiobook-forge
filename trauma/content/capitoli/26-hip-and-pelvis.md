# Chapter 26 — Hip and Pelvis

An injury to the hip or the pelvis announces itself in a way that rarely matches the danger beneath it, and the first thing a responder must understand is that a broken pelvis is a hemorrhage before it is a fracture. The pelvis is a ring of bone wrapped around a network of veins and arteries that lie in a space the examining hand cannot reach, so a fracture of that ring can pour blood into the abdomen, the buttocks, and the thighs while the skin remains unbroken and the patient looks, for a moment, almost intact. The lethal possibility to be named first is pelvic hemorrhage, and the finding that reveals it is a patient who is pale, restless, and thirsty after a high-energy mechanism, with a pulse that is climbing while the pressure falls, in whom no external wound explains the loss. Blood loss of this kind can kill within minutes to a few hours, and a patient who reaches a hospital alive may still die of it, which is why every responder at every level must treat a suspected pelvic injury as a time-critical emergency. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work, and every intervention it describes is named with the provider level that owns it.

No clinical act may begin before the scene has been made safe, because a responder who is injured while approaching a patient with a pelvic injury has added a second casualty to a scene that is already short of hands. The hazards are the ones that accompany the mechanisms which break a pelvis: moving traffic at a road crash, a vehicle that may still be burning or may roll, a load that has shifted, a structure that has collapsed onto the patient, and the violence that may have caused the injury in the first place. The quieter hazard is the patient's own blood, which may be present in quantity beneath the body and around the hips, so gloves and eye protection, which is the personal protective equipment the responder actually has, are put on before the first hand is laid on anyone. The finding that tells the responder to stop and withdraw is a scene that has not yet been held: traffic that has not been warned, a wire that has not been isolated, a surface that has not been made stable, or a person who is still dangerous. Only when the scene is safe does the responder approach, and the approach itself is part of the treatment, because dragging or rolling a patient with an unstable pelvis can turn a contained bleed into a fatal one.

The reason the pelvis bleeds so badly is that it is not a solid block but a ring of bone with a rich and fragile vascular bed suspended inside it. The veins that drain the pelvic organs and the walls of the pelvis form a plexus that has little support from surrounding tissue, so a fracture that tears it does not seal itself with the pressure that a torn vessel in a limb would feel. Behind the peritoneum, in the space that lies against the back wall of the abdomen, there is room for a large volume of blood to collect without any visible swelling, and the bony surfaces of a fractured pelvis themselves bleed because the bone is spongy and rich in blood. The volume that can be lost into that space is measured in liters, and a figure such as 2 liters is often quoted as the capacity of the space before the swelling becomes obvious, a number that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol because it differs with the size of the patient and with the way the loss is measured. The clinical consequence is simple, in that a patient can be exsanguinating into the pelvis while the abdomen is soft, the thighs look normal, and the only clue is the behavior of the circulation.

The mechanism of injury is the first predictor of pelvic damage, and it is read from the story rather than from the wound. A high-energy vehicle collision, a pedestrian struck by a vehicle, a fall from a height, a crush between two heavy objects, and a blast are the events that most often break the ring, and each of them delivers energy through the pelvis in a different direction. A lateral force compresses the ring from the side, an anteroposterior force opens it like a book, and a vertical force shears one half of the pelvis against the other, and the direction of the force decides which structures are torn. A fall from a height greater than 3 meters, a figure offered as a teaching illustration that must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, predicts pelvic injury together with injury to the spine, the heels, and the organs of the abdomen. The responder who knows the mechanism therefore examines the pelvis deliberately rather than waiting for the patient to complain about it, because a patient distracted by pain elsewhere may never mention the hip.

Instability of the pelvic ring is the finding that predicts major hemorrhage, and it is recognized by the position and the behavior of the patient rather than by any test the hands may perform. A patient whose pelvis is unstable often lies with one leg shorter than the other or rotated outward, with pain in the groin, the hip, or the lower back, and with an inability to lift the leg or to bear weight on it. Tenderness over the pubis, the sacrum, or the buttock, bruising over the perineum, and a visible deformity of the pelvic outline are further signs that the ring has been disrupted. The lay first aider and the trained responder must not spring the pelvis, that is, must not press the two halves of the ring together to see whether they move, because that act can tear a clot that is holding and start a hemorrhage that no field measure can stop. The examination is therefore performed with the eyes and with gentle pressure on the bony points, and any patient with a mechanism that could break the ring is treated as though the pelvis were unstable until definitive hospital care proves otherwise.

A fracture of the hip is a different injury from a fracture of the pelvic ring, and its danger lies in the days that follow rather than in the first minutes. The typical patient is older, often with thin bone, and the typical mechanism is a fall from standing rather than a high-energy crash, so the responder must not dismiss a hip fracture because the accident seemed minor. The findings are pain in the groin or the knee, an inability to bear weight, and a leg that lies shortened and rotated outward, and in some patients the only complaint is knee pain, which is why the knee must never be examined in isolation from the hip. The fracture may be in the neck of the femur, between the prominences at the upper end of the femur, or through the bone just below them, and each of those sites can bleed into the thigh and can injure the vessels that supply the head of the femur. The lay first aider keeps the patient still and warm, does not attempt to straighten the leg, and calls emergency medical services, while definitive hospital care decides whether the fracture needs an operation and how soon, because early surgery reduces the complications of immobility.

A dislocation of the hip is a true emergency of the joint, and it belongs to the same high-energy mechanisms that break the pelvis. The head of the femur is driven out of its socket, most often backward after a blow to the knee in a vehicle collision, and the limb then lies flexed, turned inward, and drawn toward the middle of the body. The danger is not the position of the bone but the structures trapped beside it, because the sciatic nerve and the vessels that supply the head of the femur can be stretched or torn, and a dislocation left unreduced threatens both the function of the leg and the survival of the bone. Reduction of a dislocated hip is a procedure of definitive hospital care performed with imaging and often with anesthesia, and it is not a field procedure, so no responder at any level should attempt to pull the joint back into place. The same rule applies to a severely deformed limb of any kind, in that it is splinted in the position found and never straightened to make it look normal, because the movement can injure a vessel or a nerve that is still intact.

Care of the patient with a suspected pelvic injury follows the same order as the care of every trauma patient, and that order is the primary survey remembered as ABCDE. The airway is opened and kept open, breathing is assessed and supported, and circulation is assessed with the hand on the pulse and the eyes on the skin, because a patient who is bleeding into the pelvis will show the failure of the circulation before any wound is found. The presence of catastrophic external bleeding is controlled where it is found, and the pelvic injury is treated as a source of concealed loss rather than as a limb to be splinted first. Disability is assessed through the level of consciousness and the movement of the limbs, and exposure means undressing the patient enough to see the pelvis and the perineum while covering the rest of the body at once to prevent cooling. The finding that returns the responder to the beginning of the sequence is any deterioration, and the pelvic injury is never allowed to hold attention so completely that the airway or the chest is neglected.

The neurovascular assessment of the limb is performed before and after every intervention, and it is the part of the pelvic examination that the responder can actually own. The trained responder feels the pulse at the groin and at the foot, compares the two sides, and notes the color and the temperature of the skin below the injury. Sensation is tested by asking the patient whether the leg feels normal and whether any part of it is numb, and movement is tested by asking the patient to move the toes and the ankle if the injury allows, because a loss of feeling or a loss of movement may be the first sign that a nerve or a vessel has been injured. The lay first aider performs the part that requires no equipment, which is to ask the patient about numbness, to look at the color of the foot, and to report any change at once, without moving the limb to test it. Any deterioration in the pulse, the sensation, or the movement is reported to the receiving team and recorded, because a change found late is a change that has already cost tissue.

The recognition of hemorrhage in the patient with a pelvic injury rests on the behavior of the circulation and on the trend of the observations rather than on a single measurement. A pulse that rises above 110 beats per minute, a figure given as an illustration which must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, and a blood pressure that falls below 90 millimeters of mercury, another figure that must be checked against the guideline and the protocol in force, together describe a patient whose loss is already serious. The earlier and more dangerous stage is the one in which the numbers still look acceptable while the patient is pale, cool, anxious, and thirsty, because the body is compensating and the loss is masked. The responder watches the level of consciousness, the breathing rate, the pulse, the skin, and the amount of urine where that can be observed, and compares each set of findings with the set before it. Fluid replacement and the decision to give blood belong to the paramedic under a protocol and to definitive hospital care, and nothing in this chapter names a substance, an amount, a route, or a frequency, because those decisions are made for a particular patient by an authorized provider under the local system.

Hypothermia is a complication of pelvic injury and not a detail of comfort, and it begins as soon as the patient is lying on cold ground with clothing cut away. A patient who is bleeding loses the volume that carries heat, and a patient who has been exposed loses heat from the surface, and cold then makes the clotting system work less well, so the bleeding worsens and the patient cools further in a circle that is difficult to break. The finding that warns the responder is a patient who has stopped shivering while still cold, because shivering is the body's attempt to make heat and its disappearance is a sign that the effort has failed. A temperature below 35 degrees Celsius is often named as the point at which cold itself becomes dangerous, and that figure must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the accepted line differs with age and with the way the temperature is taken. The lay first aider removes wet clothing, covers the patient including the pelvis and the legs, and insulates the patient from the ground, while the trained responder continues that protection in the vehicle and limits the time the patient is exposed for examination. Warming is never delayed until the bleeding is controlled, because the two tasks run together, and the patient is kept warm on the way to definitive care.

A pelvic binder is a device that encircles the pelvis and squeezes the two halves of the ring together, and it is the one field intervention that can reduce bleeding from an unstable pelvic fracture before the patient reaches hospital. It belongs to the trained responder or the paramedic working under a protocol, and in some services a trained first responder who has been taught and authorized to use it may apply one, so the level of training that owns the act is decided by the local system rather than by this chapter. The evidence on which the decision rests is a mechanism that could have broken the ring together with the findings of instability, and the binder is applied before the patient is moved rather than after bleeding has declared itself. A binder is not a treatment for a hip fracture or for a dislocated hip, and it is not a substitute for the operation that controls a torn vessel, so it is described here as a temporary measure that buys time rather than as a definitive repair. The lay first aider who has not been trained and authorized to apply a binder does not improvise one, and instead keeps the patient still, keeps the patient warm, and calls emergency medical services, because a binder applied wrongly can do harm without controlling the bleeding.

The position of a pelvic binder decides whether it helps or harms, and the correct level is across the greater trochanters at the widest part of the hips rather than across the waist or the upper thighs. A binder placed too high sits over the abdomen, where it compresses the belly, limits the movement of the diaphragm, and can worsen breathing while failing to close the pelvic ring, and a binder placed too low sits over the thighs and pulls the two halves of the pelvis apart at the top instead of bringing them together. A binder that is applied too tightly can injure the skin beneath it, can press on the nerves that cross the pelvis, and can cause pressure damage that is discovered only after hours, so the tension is set by the design of the device and by the training rather than by the strength of the responder. The device is checked for folds and for twisting, because a crease concentrates pressure on a small area of skin, and the patient's clothing is smoothed beneath it where that can be done without moving the pelvis. The finding that the binder has been placed correctly is a patient whose pain and instability appear reduced while the circulation is stable, and the finding that it is wrong is a patient whose breathing has worsened or whose skin is being damaged at the edge of the device.

Once a pelvic binder is in place it is monitored rather than forgotten, because a device that is left unexamined can hide the deterioration it was meant to prevent. The trained responder reassesses the breathing, the abdomen, the circulation, and the neurovascular state of both legs at regular intervals, checks the skin at the edges of the binder, and records the time the device was applied in a place the receiving team will see. The binder is not removed in the field once it has been placed, even when the patient improves, because the improvement may depend on the compression and because releasing it can restart a hemorrhage that field measures cannot control. Removal is a decision for definitive hospital care, made with imaging and with preparation for what may follow, and the receiving team must be told that a binder is present and when it was applied. The finding that requires the responder to escalate immediately is a patient whose circulation continues to fail despite the binder, because that patient needs blood and an operating room rather than a tighter device.

Immobilization for transport has one purpose, which is to prevent movement of the pelvis between the scene and the place where the bleeding can be controlled. The patient is kept lying flat on a firm surface, the legs are kept still, and the knees and the ankles may be bound together with a padded band or a folded sheet so that the limbs move as a single unit, which is a measure that belongs to the trained responder and to the paramedic. A scoop stretcher or a firm board is used to lift the patient without rolling the pelvis, and where a log roll is required to examine the back it is performed by a team that moves the patient as one piece, keeping the pelvis and the spine in line. The lay first aider keeps the patient in the position found, does not lift the legs, does not straighten a shortened leg, and does not pull on the limb, because each of those acts can move the fracture and restart the bleeding. The destination is chosen before the patient is moved, and it is the nearest trauma center or the nearest facility that can provide imaging, blood, and an operating room, with the receiving team warned in advance that a pelvic injury is coming.

An open pelvic fracture is the most dangerous form of the injury, and it is recognized by a wound that communicates with the broken bone or by bleeding from the perineum, the rectum, or the vagina. Blood at the opening of the urethra, an inability to pass urine, a bruise over the perineum, and a wound near the buttock are all findings that raise the possibility of injury to the bladder, the urethra, or the bowel, and each of them changes the care the patient will need. A fracture that has torn the skin of the perineum or the rectum carries contamination directly into the pelvis, so the tetanus question and the antibiotic decision are raised early, with the substance and the amount belonging to the prescriber and to the local protocol rather than to this manual. A fragment of bone or a foreign object embedded in the wound is stabilized in place and never removed in the field, because removal may release hemorrhage that cannot be controlled or damage a structure that is still intact. The lay first aider covers the wound with a clean dressing and does not pack it, and the trained responder protects the area, keeps the patient warm, and moves the patient toward definitive care without delay.

Two groups of patients deserve a separate word because their anatomy changes the risk. A child has a pelvis that is more elastic than an adult's, so the ring can absorb energy and spring back without breaking, while the vessels inside it are torn, and the child may therefore have a serious pelvic injury with an apparently normal radiograph and a normal examination. A pregnant patient carries two lives, and a pelvic fracture threatens the uterus, the placenta, and the fetus at the same time, so the responder treats the mother first because the best treatment for the fetus is a mother whose circulation is supported, and the receiving team is told that the patient is pregnant. An older patient may break the pelvis in a fall from standing, and the fracture may be a single break in one of the pubic bones rather than a disrupted ring, yet even that injury can bleed and can leave the patient unable to walk. In each of these patients the same principles apply, which are to keep the patient still, warm, and monitored, and to reach definitive care as quickly as is safe.

Definitive hospital care owns everything that repairs rather than contains, and the line between the field and the hospital must be clear to every responder. The hospital confirms the injury with imaging, which may include a computed tomography scan, called CT after the first mention, and it restores the circulating volume with blood and blood products given by the team that is authorized to prescribe them. Bleeding from a torn pelvic vessel may be controlled by external fixation of the ring, by packing of the pelvis in the operating room, or by a procedure that blocks the bleeding vessel from inside using imaging, and each of those is a decision of the trauma surgeon rather than a field procedure. Internal fixation, the repair of a torn vessel, the repair of the bladder or the urethra, and the removal of a fragment are operations performed in an operating room with anesthesia, and no responder should attempt any of them outside it. The patient who has lost a large volume of blood is watched in an intensive care unit, called ICU after the first mention, for the complications that follow, which include continued bleeding, infection, and the effects of a large transfusion.

Reassessment is not a single act but a rhythm that continues from the first contact to the handover, and it follows the same order as the primary survey. The trained responder repeats the airway, the breathing, the circulation, the level of consciousness, and the neurovascular state of the limbs, and compares each set of findings with the one before, because the trend carries more meaning than any single observation. The findings that mark a patient who is still bleeding are a pulse that has risen and softened, a breathing rate that has climbed, skin that has become cold and clammy, a level of consciousness that is dulling, and a binder or a dressing that has become soaked. What was done is recorded as carefully as the treatment is given, and the time at which a binder was applied, the time at which a tourniquet was placed, and every change in the patient are written where the receiving team will see them. The handover carries the mechanism of injury, the injuries found, the interventions performed with their times, and the observations that have changed, because a story with a gap forces the receiving team to begin again.

A constructed illustration, offered as a construction and never as a real patient or a real incident, may make the sequence concrete. A driver is struck on the side by another vehicle and is found awake and anxious at the wheel, with pain in the hip, a pulse that is fast and thin, and no blood on the ground. The responder makes the road safe, protects the hands and the eyes, opens the airway, and assesses the breathing, and then treats the mechanism as a prediction of a pelvic injury rather than waiting for a deformity that may never appear. The patient is kept flat, kept warm, and moved as one piece onto a firm surface, and a binder is applied by a trained responder only where the protocol authorizes it, at the level of the greater trochanters, with the time recorded. The patient's circulation continues to fail on the way, and the receiving team is warned before arrival so that blood and an operating room are ready when the vehicle doors open, which is the only definitive answer to a bleeding pelvis.

To summarize, a fracture of the hip or the pelvis is a hemorrhage before it is a fracture, and the pelvis is a bleeding space because its ring of bone surrounds a fragile vascular bed and a cavity that can hold liters of blood without a visible wound. The lethal possibility is pelvic hemorrhage, which can kill within minutes to a few hours, and the finding that reveals it is a patient whose circulation is failing after a high-energy mechanism with no external injury to explain the loss. Scene safety comes before the patient, the primary survey is performed in the order of ABCDE, the pelvis is never sprung, a deformed limb is never straightened, and an embedded object is never removed in the field. Immobilization, warmth, and the shortest safe journey to a trauma center are the field tasks, a pelvic binder belongs to the trained responder or paramedic working under a protocol and is placed at the level of the greater trochanters and monitored once in place, and transfusion, fixation, and the control of a torn vessel belong to definitive hospital care. Every time, pressure, temperature, depth, distance, and figure in this chapter, including every threshold and every interval, is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such numbers differ between services, countries, and editions.
