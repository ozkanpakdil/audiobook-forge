# Chapter 31 — Foot Injuries

A crushed foot, a broken heel, or a puncture through the sole of a shoe is easy to underestimate and hard to repair, so the scene is made safe before the foot is touched. The hazards named here are the machine still powered, the vehicle still in the roadway, the load still shifting above the patient, the cable dragged into water, and the animal or the person still dangerous. Blood, dirty water, and manure on the skin are the quieter hazards, and personal protective equipment, or PPE, with gloves and eye protection at the least is used for every foot wound. The lethal possibilities are hemorrhage from a mangled foot, the compartment syndrome that a swollen foot can hide, and the crush syndrome that may follow a limb released after a long compression. The foot rarely kills by itself, but the mechanism that broke it can, because a fall that shattered a heel may also have broken the spine. Every act below is named with the provider level that owns it, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country where you work.

The foot is a platform of small bones bound by ligaments and moved by tendons that turn the corner at the ankle, and its shape can be described in words without a picture. The heel is the calcaneus, the large bone that receives the body's weight at landing, and above it sits the talus, which carries the load of the leg down into the foot. In front of these lies the midfoot, a cluster of small bones that builds the arches, and beyond it run the metatarsals, the long bones of the forefoot, ending in the phalanges of the toes. Two arches give the foot its spring, one along the inner border and one crossing the foot, and the soft tissue over the bones is thin, so swelling has little room to spread. The foot carries the whole weight of the body and it is wrapped in footwear, and those two features explain why a serious foot injury is so often called minor. A shoe hides swelling, holds a wound against dirt, and can become a band that strangles a foot whose circulation is failing, so the responder examines the bare foot and compares it with its neighbor.

A fall from a height lands the patient on the heels, and the force travels upward through the calcaneus into the ankle, the leg, the pelvis, and the spine. The finding is a heel that is swollen and bruised on the sole and around the ankle, widened and shortened, and painful beyond anything the skin suggests. A patient who cannot bear weight on either heel is describing a fracture rather than a sprain, and when both heels are injured the likelihood of a spinal and pelvic injury rises sharply. A fall from a height greater than 3 meters is often quoted as the point at which those secondary injuries become likely, and that distance must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. The injury depends on the surface, the position of landing, and the patient as much as on the height, so the figure guides suspicion rather than proving damage. Compression of the spine can exist without pain at first, because the patient is distracted by the heel and the rescue, so the spine is protected from the first minute.

Machinery and vehicles produce the other great family of foot injuries, and they behave differently from a fall. A roller, a press, a conveyor, an agricultural machine, or the wheel of a car can crush the foot between two surfaces, and the skin may be intact while the bone and soft tissue beneath are destroyed. The finding that matters is a foot that is swollen, tense, and deformed even when the skin is whole, because a closed crush can tear vessels and muscle inside a compartment that does not stretch. A run-over injury often strips the skin from the sole, and those wounds are heavily contaminated by road surface, grit, and shoe material before the patient reaches help. A heavy object dropped onto the toes, a motorcycle that falls on the foot, and a bicycle chain that catches the ankle cause the same categories of damage on a smaller scale. A twisted foot may pull a fragment from the base of the fifth metatarsal, and a puncture, a bite, or a small wound from a nail or glass can become a deep infection in a foot with poor blood supply.

Scene safety in this chapter has features peculiar to machinery and industry. A machine that has caught a foot is isolated by a person authorized to isolate it, and until that isolation is confirmed the machine is treated as able to start. A guard removed and a switch left on have killed responders who trusted them, so the machine is not handled until it is locked out by someone with the authority and the key. A vehicle that has run over a patient is held by warning other drivers and positioning a vehicle to protect the space, and the patient is not moved from beneath a load until the load is stable. Water, whether a fast stream, a flooded ditch, or a wet floor beneath an electrical machine, is treated as a conductor and as a drowning hazard at once, and an animal or a violent person is met by withdrawal to a defensible position. A patient lying on cold, wet ground loses heat while the rescue is arranged, so warmth is part of the scene work and not an afterthought.

The foot injury is placed within the primary survey rather than treated on its own, because the mechanism that damaged the foot may have damaged the patient. Airway and breathing come first, then the control of life-threatening bleeding, then the level of consciousness and the movement of the limbs. The principle of life before limb decides the order, and a mangled foot beside a closed airway or a chest filling with air waits while the airway and chest are treated. The findings that send the responder back to the beginning are a falling level of consciousness, a changed breathing pattern, a pulse that is fast and thin, and skin that is pale and cool. A patient whose foot is crushed but whose circulation is otherwise intact is moved toward definitive care with the foot protected, and the journey is shortened rather than delayed by tidying the injury.

Exposure of the foot is the first diagnostic act, and it is done on both feet at once so that the injured one has a comparison. The shoe and sock are removed or cut away, the sole is inspected as carefully as the top, and the skin between the toes is examined because a wound there is easily missed. The size, shape, and color of the feet are compared, and a foot that is swollen, turned, shortened, or sitting at an angle is described before it is touched. Footwear is removed or opened when it threatens the circulation, and the decision is made by what the foot is doing rather than by a rule about every boot. The signs are a foot swelling while the responder watches, a boot tight across the instep, a toe that is pale, blue, or cold, and pain described as pressure rather than soreness. The trained responder cuts the laces and opens the boot along its seams and lifts it away without pulling or twisting, while the lay first aider may cut the laces and steady the limb. A foot trapped in a machine keeps its boot until the machine is released, and the foot is then supported exactly as it lies.

The neurovascular examination compares the injured foot with the uninjured one and asks four questions in a fixed order. Color and warmth are compared, and a foot that is pale, blue, mottled, or cooler than its neighbor is a foot whose blood is not arriving. The pulse on the top of the foot and the pulse behind the inner ankle bone are both sought, and a pulse that was present and has disappeared must be reported at once. Capillary refill is judged by pressing the skin or nail until it blanches and counting the seconds until color returns. A refill time greater than 2 seconds is a warning that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the accepted figure shifts with room temperature, age, and lighting. Sensation is tested over the top of the foot, the sole, and both borders, and movement is tested by asking the patient to bend and straighten the toes against light resistance. A foot that is dusky or white, cold, numb in the sole, or unable to move its toes requires immediate definitive care, and the responder does not massage it, apply heat to it, or elevate it above the heart when the arterial supply is uncertain.

Bleeding from a foot wound is controlled in the same ladder as bleeding anywhere else, but the anatomy changes the technique. Direct pressure with a gloved hand over a clean dressing is the first act, and pressure is held without lifting the dressing, with further layers added on top if blood shows through. The trained responder may pack a deep wound of the foot or ankle firmly down to the bleeding point, and may use a hemostatic dressing where the service carries one, because the sole and heel can conceal a torn vessel. A tourniquet is a measure for the trained responder or paramedic under a protocol, and it is placed above the ankle on the leg rather than around the foot, because a band around the foot cannot compress the vessels between the small bones. Once a tourniquet has controlled the bleeding it is not loosened in the field, and the time it was applied is written on the patient where the receiving team will see it. A partly amputated foot is managed as it lies, and the amputation is never completed by pulling on the tissue, because that pull may release hemorrhage and tear what is still intact. A separated toe is wrapped in clean dry material, sealed in a bag, placed in a cool container rather than on ice, and sent with the patient, because reattachment is a decision for a trauma center.

Open wounds of the foot are among the most contaminated wounds in trauma, because of the environment in which the foot lives. The sole of a shoe carries soil, grit, fragments of glass, and the residue of whatever the patient has walked through, and a wound that passes through that layer carries all of it into the tissue. A puncture from a nail, a splinter, a thorn, or a bite leaves a narrow track that closes at the surface and traps organisms where there is little blood supply and no drainage. A crush or degloving injury presses dirt into a wide surface, and a wound from an agricultural machine adds manure and water to the mixture. Infection in the foot is more dangerous than the same infection elsewhere, because its blood supply is at the end of a long chain of vessels, the tissue over the bone is thin, and the foot sits below the heart. A patient with diabetes, narrowed arteries, or a neuropathy that has removed the feeling from the feet may not notice a wound until the infection is deep, and the decisions about tetanus protection and antibiotics belong to the authorized provider and the prescriber under the local protocol.

The field care of a contaminated foot wound is deliberately limited, and the limit is what keeps the patient safe. Gross dirt and pieces of shoe material on the surface may be lifted away by the trained responder with clean hands, but the wound is not probed, squeezed, or explored, because probing pushes contamination deeper. The trained responder may irrigate a dirty wound with clean water or saline under a protocol, and the lay first aider keeps the surface covered, because irrigation without training can drive fluid into a track that should have been left alone. The wound is covered with a clean dressing that does not constrict the foot, and a wound that is deep, gaping, or contaminated is not closed in the field, because closing it traps infection inside. An object embedded in the foot is stabilized in place and never removed, because a nail, a piece of glass, or a fragment of metal may be plugging the vessel beneath it, and pulling it out can release hemorrhage that no dressing will control. The responder builds padding around the object so pressure falls on the skin beside it, splints the foot with the object in place, and checks the toes after every layer for tightness.

Metatarsal fractures are common in the forefoot and follow a direct blow, a dropped object, a vehicle wheel, or a twisting injury to the front of the foot. The finding is swelling over the top of the foot, tenderness along the bone, bruising under the toes or across the sole, and pain when the patient tries to bear weight. A displaced fracture may show a step in the contour of the foot or an unnatural angle of a toe, and the responder describes that shape without attempting to correct it. The lay first aider supports the foot on a pillow or rolled clothing and keeps the patient from walking on it, and the trained responder applies a padded splint and records the neurovascular findings before and after. The fifth metatarsal deserves its own word, because a break at its base on the outer border heals slowly when the blood supply enters from one direction. Tenderness in the hollow at that base is treated as a fracture even when the patient can still walk, and definitive care decides from a radiograph whether the injury needs support, rest, or an operation.

Fractures of the toes usually follow a stubbed foot, a dropped object, or a crush, and they matter most when the great toe or the joint at its base is involved. The findings are swelling, bruising, pain on movement, and a toe that is bent or overlaps its neighbor. The great toe carries a large part of the push-off in walking, so a break at its base is more disabling than a break in a small toe and may be part of a larger midfoot injury. The trained responder may support an injured toe against its neighbor with soft padding between them, and the padding must be loose enough that the toes beyond it keep their color and warmth. The responder does not pull on the toe to straighten it, because the shape is set by the fracture and the pull can tear the soft tissue around it.

A calcaneal fracture is one of the most serious injuries in this chapter, and it begins with a fall onto the heel. The talus is driven down into the calcaneus and splits it, and the finding is a heel that is swollen, bruised on the sole and around the ankle, widened and shortened, and painful beyond the appearance of the skin. The patient cannot bear weight on the heel, and the responder must not press the heel to test it, because the pressure adds pain and can move the fragments. Because the same force travels into the spine and pelvis, the injury is treated as part of a whole-body injury until the spine is cleared, and the patient is kept flat and still with the head and neck supported. The trained responder immobilizes the foot and leg in the position found, keeps the patient warm, and moves the patient to a trauma center rather than a clinic. Definitive care uses computed tomography, or CT, to show the shape of the break, and an operation may be needed to rebuild the heel, because a calcaneus that heals badly leaves a patient who cannot walk without pain.

A crush injury of the foot is the mechanism in this chapter that most often hides a compartment syndrome, because the foot contains several tight compartments between its bones. A machine, a wheel, a heavy load, or a prolonged entrapment squeezes the muscle and small vessels inside those compartments, and when the pressure inside rises above the pressure that fills the vessels, the tissue is starved even though the skin is intact. The findings are a foot that is tense and swollen, pain that is severe and out of proportion, pain that increases when the toes are bent or straightened, and numbness that begins in the toes and moves inward. The responder removes anything that constricts the foot, including a boot, a sock, a bandage, or a splint that has become tight, and keeps the foot in the position found. The limb is not massaged, not warmed, and not elevated above the heart when the arterial supply is uncertain, and the patient is moved to a hospital where the pressure can be assessed. The diagnosis is clinical and is made from the story and the examination rather than from a single number, so a measured pressure such as 30 millimeters of mercury is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, and the responder who waits for that number has waited too long. The act that relieves the pressure is called a fasciotomy, and it is a surgical procedure performed in an operating room and never a field procedure, so the field responder simply removes every constricting layer and states at handover that a compartment syndrome is suspected and why. The finding that demands the fastest possible transfer is pain on stretching the toes together with a foot that is becoming numb, because the window for saving the muscle is closing.

When a foot has been compressed for a long time, the danger is not only inside the foot but in the whole patient, and that is the crush syndrome described in its own chapter of this manual. Muscle without blood for a long period breaks down, and when the load is released the products of that breakdown enter the circulation and can poison the kidneys, disturb the rhythm of the heart, and deepen shock. The findings are a patient trapped for a long time, a limb that is swollen and tense when uncovered, urine that is dark, brown, or the color of cola, and a pulse that becomes fast and weak as the patient is freed. The release of a prolonged crush is therefore a rescue for a trained team with medical direction, and the lay first aider does not lift the load or pull the patient free, because the release itself can cause a sudden deterioration. The responder keeps the patient warm and still, watches the level of consciousness and the pulse, and reports the length of the entrapment and the weight involved to the receiving team. Definitive care gives the treatment this condition requires, and the substance, amount, and route of that treatment belong to the protocol and the clinician rather than to this chapter.

Splinting of the injured foot holds it in the position found, and the position found is never corrected to make the foot look normal. A deformed foot is splinted as it lies, because straightening it can move a fractured bone into a vessel or a nerve and turn a survivable injury into a lost foot. The trained responder uses a padded splint that supports the foot and ankle and extends up the leg, with the ankle held as close to a right angle as the injury allows. Padding is placed over the heel, the bumps on either side of the ankle, the top of the foot, and the toes, because pressure on those points can injure skin already short of blood. The toes are left uncovered so their color, warmth, and movement can be watched, and the splint is secured firmly enough to keep the foot still and loosely enough that the toes do not swell. A dressing on the foot must protect the wound and must not become a band, so the wound is covered with a clean dressing held with a bandage wound without stretching the skin, and blood that soaks through is answered by adding layers rather than by lifting the dressing. The lay first aider supports the foot on a pillow or rolled clothing and waits for trained help rather than applying a splint.

Hypothermia is a complication of a foot injury rather than a detail of comfort, because a patient lying on cold ground, a patient whose wet boot and sock are removed, and a patient waiting for a rescue in the rain all lose heat quickly. Cold makes blood clot poorly, deepens shock, and makes the heart more likely to develop a dangerous rhythm, so the patient is covered before the journey rather than after it. The responder removes wet clothing and replaces it with dry coverings where the wound allows, insulates the patient from the ground, and keeps the vehicle or the room warm on the way. A body temperature below 35 degrees Celsius is often named as the point at which cold itself becomes dangerous, and that figure must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the threshold differs with age and with the way the temperature is measured. Heat is never applied directly to the injured foot, because a foot that has lost its feeling can be burned by a hot pack or a hot water bottle that the patient cannot feel. The finding that warns the responder that cooling has already begun is a patient who has stopped shivering while still cold, and that finding makes warmth an urgent treatment.

Definitive hospital care owns everything that repairs the foot rather than protects it, and the boundary is worth stating clearly. A radiograph is taken of the foot, and computed tomography, or CT, is used where the shape of a fracture or an injury of the midfoot needs to be seen in more detail. An operation may be needed to put the bones back into place and hold them with metal, which is called open reduction and internal fixation, and that procedure belongs to the surgeon and the operating room. An open wound is cleaned and dead tissue is removed in a procedure called debridement, and a heavily contaminated wound is often left open and closed later when the infection risk has fallen. A compartment syndrome is treated with a fasciotomy, a foot beyond saving may be amputated, and protection against tetanus and the use of antibiotics are decisions of the prescriber and the local protocol. The responder's part ends with an accurate handover, and that handover is what allows the surgeon to begin with the right question.

Reassessment continues from the scene to the handover and follows the same four questions in the same order, which are color and warmth, pulses, sensation, and movement. The comparison that matters is not a single set of findings but the difference between one set and the next, because a foot that is losing its blood supply tells the responder through change. The findings that send the responder back to the beginning are toes that have become pale or blue, a pulse that has disappeared, a foot that is more painful or more numb, a swelling inside a splint, and a patient whose pulse has risen. The receiving team is told the mechanism, the time, whether the patient was trapped and for how long, what was removed from the foot, what was left in it, and the time of every intervention. Two mistakes recur, which are the injury called a sprain while the skin is whole and the bandage or splint that becomes a tourniquet by accident, and both are prevented by comparing the feet, testing what the patient can do, and checking the toes after every layer. A constructed illustration, offered as a construction and never as a real patient, is a worker whose foot is caught between the rollers of a machine and whose boot is left in place until the machine is isolated, and the lesson is that the rescue itself can be the second injury.

To summarize, the foot is a weight-bearing platform of small bones with thin coverings, and the two mechanisms that dominate its injuries are the fall that drives the body's weight through the heel and the machine or vehicle that crushes it between hard surfaces. The scene is made safe before the patient is touched, with machinery isolated, traffic held, water and electricity treated as hazards, and PPE used for every wound, and the foot is then placed within the primary survey rather than examined on its own. The bare foot is compared with its neighbor, the footwear is removed or opened whenever it threatens the circulation, and the neurovascular examination asks in order about color and warmth, the two pulses, sensation, and the movement of the toes. Bleeding is controlled by direct pressure, by packing where the trained responder is authorized, and by a tourniquet above the ankle under a protocol, and the tourniquet is not loosened in the field and its time is written on the patient. A swollen foot inside a boot, a foot that is tense and painful out of proportion, and a foot that is numb all point toward a compartment syndrome, while a foot released after a long compression may produce the crush syndrome in the whole patient, and both require immediate definitive care. Fractures of the metatarsals, the toes, the fifth metatarsal, and the calcaneus are splinted in the position found, a deformed foot is never straightened, an embedded object is never removed, and an open or contaminated wound is covered and left for surgical cleaning. Hypothermia is treated as a complication, the special feet of children, older patients, and patients with diabetes are examined with the same suspicion as any other, and every time, pressure, temperature, distance, and threshold in this chapter is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.
