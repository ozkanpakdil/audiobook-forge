# Chapter 9 — Knife and Stabbing Injuries

A knife is the most deceptive of the objects that penetrate the body, because a blade is judged by what it has crossed rather than by what can be seen. A blade divides tissue along a narrow line, and the skin closes over that line almost at once. A wound a few millimeters across can lie above a track that has opened the heart, the aorta, the liver, or the great vein behind the collarbone. The responder who arrives first must therefore treat the visible injury as the least reliable clue on the scene. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of your country.

Before anything is done for the patient, the scene must be made safe, because a stabbing is an act of violence. The person who held the knife may still be present, so the first action is to look at the whole scene rather than at the bleeding. Where the assailant may still be near, the responder stays behind cover, calls emergency medical services, and waits for the police to secure the area. Personal protective equipment is worn before contact, and the blade is never picked up with bare hands. Only when the scene is calm does the patient become the object of care.

Several lethal possibilities must be named early, because each announces itself with a finding that can be sought in the first minutes. A patient who becomes breathless, whose breath sounds are absent on one side, and whose neck veins are distended may have a tension pneumothorax. A patient whose heart sounds are muffled and whose pulse weakens with each breath may have blood collecting in the sac around the heart. A patient who is pale, restless, and sweating with a rapid, thin pulse may be bleeding from a major vessel or a solid organ, even when the wound is tiny. A patient who cannot be roused is treated as having an airway injury until hospital care proves otherwise.

The central lesson of penetrating trauma is the mismatch between the size of the wound and the depth of the track. A blade that is 15 centimeters long can cross a whole cavity and reach the spine while the entry remains a slit a dressing covers completely. That length is only an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since services describe blades and wounds differently. The track is not always straight, because a patient who twists or falls with the blade inside can deepen a shallow wound. The search for a second wound matters as much as the first, because a knife used more than once leaves wounds the patient cannot show, and a blade that passes completely through a limb or the neck leaves an exit wound that is often smaller and more ragged than the entry. A stab wound is classified by where it goes and not by how it looks, and any wound whose track can enter the chest, the abdomen, the pelvis, the neck, or a limb is treated as having entered it until hospital care says otherwise.

The mechanism of injury turns a wound into a prediction, and the responder should ask about it while the patient can still answer. Was the blade long or short, held overhand or underhand, and was the patient standing, sitting, or lying down. A blade driven downward into the upper abdomen can reach the heart through the diaphragm, and the same blade driven upward can reach it by another path. A knife that broke, a blade left in the wound, and blows struck afterward all change the picture. The account of the patient is valuable but never a substitute for examination, since a stabbed person may not have felt the wound at the time.

A wound to the chest threatens more structures than a wound anywhere else, and the structures at risk are the heart, the great vessels, the lungs, and the diaphragm. The heart lies behind the lower breastbone and the left ribs, but a blade entering the upper abdomen or the right chest can reach it when the track crosses the midline. The diaphragm is a thin sheet that is crossed easily, so a wound below the nipple line may also have entered the abdomen. The findings to seek are breathlessness, pain on breathing, a bubbling wound, reduced breath sounds on one side, and a fast, weak pulse. The trap is the patient who talks comfortably and complains only of soreness at the wound.

Air in the pleural space, or pneumothorax, and blood in the pleural space, or hemothorax, are the two complications of chest stabbing that must be recognized without imaging. A pneumothorax turns lethal when air can enter but cannot escape, so the pressure rises, the lung collapses, and the heart and windpipe are pushed to the other side. The findings of that tension are worsening breathlessness, a silent side of the chest, distended neck veins, and a windpipe no longer in the middle of the neck. A hemothorax is quieter and may be large before signs appear. Tight binding of the chest is forbidden, because it stops the chest from expanding and hides the deterioration. Covering a bubbling chest wound is first aid, but relieving tension by needle or finger and inserting a chest drain belong to trained hospital staff.

A wound to the abdomen is dangerous because the cavity tolerates injury silently and then declares it without warning. The liver on the right and the spleen on the left are solid organs that bleed heavily, and both can bleed while the patient still looks well. The stomach and the bowel leak their contents, producing a chemical peritonitis that begins as a deep ache and becomes a board-like rigidity. The findings to seek are pain, tenderness, guarding, a rigid abdomen, distension, and a rising pulse, and the absence of pain early proves nothing. The wound is never probed, because probing introduces infection, opens a clot that was holding, and tells the responder nothing the hospital cannot learn better.

The pelvis and the back are regions of large vessels and deep organs, and a wound there combines hemorrhage with contamination. The iliac arteries and veins, the bladder, the rectum, the prostate, the uterus, and the vagina lie within or behind the pelvic ring. The retroperitoneal space holds the kidneys, the pancreas, the duodenum, the great vessels, and the colon, and it is not protected by the abdominal wall. The findings to seek are bleeding from the wound, blood at the meatus, blood in the rectum or urine, lower abdominal pain, and a fast, weak pulse. A broken pelvis can bleed behind the peritoneum until the patient is close to death, and a wound reaching the spinal cord can cause weakness, numbness, or loss of bladder control.

The neck is the most crowded and least forgiving region of the body, holding the airway, the esophagus, the carotid arteries, the jugular veins, and the spinal cord within the width of a hand. A wound there can bleed into the tissues rather than out of them, so an expanding hematoma may compress the airway while the external wound stays small. The findings to seek are a bubbling wound, a hoarse or absent voice, blood in the mouth, a growing swelling, and any change in the level of consciousness. The head and neck are held in the position found while the airway is managed. No responder probes or explores a neck wound, and none removes a blade sitting in it, because a clot may be the only thing closing a torn vessel. Pressure in the neck is gentle, since firm pressure on both sides can stop the blood to the brain, and repair of a torn vessel belongs to the vascular surgeon.

A wound to an arm or a leg is often treated as the least frightening kind of stabbing, and that judgment is wrong. The brachial artery in the upper arm, the femoral artery in the groin and thigh, and the popliteal artery behind the knee are all within reach of a short blade. The nerves to the hand and the foot lie beside those vessels, and tendons crossing the wrist and the ankle are divided by wounds that look trivial. The findings of a vascular injury are bleeding that pressure cannot stop, a pulsating or expanding swelling, a pale and cold limb, a pulse absent beyond the wound, and lost sensation or movement below it. A tourniquet that has controlled bleeding is not loosened in the field, and the time it was applied is recorded on the patient where the receiving team will see it.

Some patients carry a higher risk from the same wound, and the responder who recognizes them moves faster. A child has a smaller blood volume and a greater surface area, so a wound that appears minor can produce shock more quickly than in an adult. A pregnant patient has a uterus that rises out of the pelvis after the early months, so a wound to the lower abdomen may injure the uterus and the fetus. An older patient may take a medicine that prevents clotting, so a wound that would seal in another patient may bleed for a long time. A patient whose level of consciousness is reduced by alcohol, drugs, or a head injury may not report pain, so the examination must be complete rather than guided by complaint.

Hemorrhage is the complication that kills most stabbed patients who die before reaching hospital, and it kills by taking away the volume the heart needs to fill. Blood may leave through the wound, which can be seen and treated, or it may collect in the chest, the abdomen, the pelvis, or the tissues of a limb, which is missed because the skin is intact. The body compensates by narrowing the vessels of the skin and gut, then by raising the heart rate, and only then by failing. The transition is marked by a rapid, thin pulse, cold clammy skin, delayed return of color to a pressed fingernail, restlessness that turns into drowsiness, and a thirst the patient cannot satisfy. The responder treats those findings as hemorrhage until proven otherwise, stops what can be stopped, keeps the patient warm, and reaches definitive care without delay.

The measurement of the pulse and the pressure is part of every assessment, and the figures taught as the line between stability and shock are illustrations rather than rules. A pulse above 110 beats per minute in an adult who is not frightened and not in pain may be the first sign of blood loss, and that figure must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. A systolic pressure below 90 millimeters of mercury is often described as the threshold of shock, and that threshold must be checked against the guideline and the protocol that govern your service. A temperature below 36 degrees Celsius and a falling level of consciousness point the same way, and each of those figures must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol before it drives a decision.

Injury to a major vessel is the complication that turns a limb or neck wound into a fatal one, and it is recognized by findings rather than by imaging in the field. The hard findings are blood that spits or pulses from the wound, a swelling that expands while it is watched, a wound over the path of a named vessel, a pulse absent beyond the wound, and a limb that is pale, cold, and without sensation. Bleeding from a major vessel in a limb is controlled first by direct pressure, and where pressure fails, a tourniquet applied by a trained responder or paramedic under protocol is the next step. A tourniquet is not loosened in the field once it has worked, and the time of application is written on the patient and spoken in the handover. The repair of a divided vessel belongs to the vascular surgeon in the operating room.

The responder does not try to decide which abdominal organ has been injured, but records the position of the wound, notes the pain and the tenderness, and passes the patient to hospital care quickly. An abdomen that becomes rigid and that is tender when pressed and released has peritonitis, and tenderness on release turns a soft abdomen into a surgical emergency. Where bowel has come out through the wound, a condition called evisceration, the bowel is not pushed back, because pushing it back drives contamination into the cavity. The eviscerated bowel is covered with a dressing moistened as the local protocol directs, the patient is kept warm, and the patient is transported with the knees slightly bent. The definitive care of an injured organ belongs to the operating room and never to the roadside.

Hemorrhage control at the scene follows an order that must not be rearranged, and the first action is always the simplest. The lay first aider applies firm direct pressure over a dressing, adds further dressings without removing the first, and keeps the pressure on while help travels. The trained responder may pack a wound bleeding from a deep track, pushing gauze firmly into the wound to its full depth, and may apply a tourniquet to a limb when pressure and packing have failed. The paramedic may use a hemostatic dressing where the local protocol authorizes it, and may consider a junctional tourniquet for bleeding at the groin, the armpit, or the base of the neck. No lay first aider applies a tourniquet, packs a deep wound, or uses a hemostatic agent without training and authorization. The finding that tells the responder to move on is that the bleeding has stopped and the dressing is holding.

An object that remains in the wound, whether a blade, a fragment of it, or a piece of clothing, is never removed in the field, and this rule has a reason that must be spoken aloud. The object may be pressing on a torn vessel and acting as the only plug holding the bleeding back, so removing it may release a hemorrhage that no pressure can control. The object may also be the only thing showing the direction of the track, and the surgeon learns more from it in place than from a wound emptied at the roadside. The object is stabilized where it was found, with dressings packed around it and secured so that it cannot move, and the patient is moved with it still in place. An object in the chest, the neck, or the abdomen is stabilized and left entirely alone.

Protecting the wound is the next task after bleeding is controlled, and it begins with clean hands and clean dressings. The wound is covered with a sterile or clean dressing, and the dressing is left in place rather than changed at the scene, because every change disturbs the clot and adds contamination. A dressing that becomes soaked is covered with another rather than removed, and the number of dressings used is recorded. A wound that bubbles at the chest is covered in a way that lets air leave but not enter, and a responder not trained to do this covers it simply and watches closely for the signs of a tension pneumothorax. The responder does not wash out a deep stab wound, does not pour antiseptic into it, and does not close it with tape or clips, since closing a wound that may have injured deep structures belongs to hospital care.

After hemorrhage control, the responder returns to the beginning of the primary survey, which is the ABCDE approach, and assesses the airway, the breathing, the circulation, the level of consciousness, and the exposure of the whole body. The airway is assessed by listening for air movement and by looking into the mouth for blood or vomit, and a patient who answers clearly has a patent airway for the moment. The breathing is assessed by looking for equal chest movement, by listening for breath sounds on both sides, and by feeling for air at the mouth and nose. The circulation is assessed by feeling the pulse and by watching the color of the skin. The level of consciousness is recorded with the Glasgow coma scale, a score that runs from 3 to 15, and that range must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol where you work. A patient who stops breathing and has no pulse needs cardiopulmonary resuscitation, or CPR, at once, and the exposure step is what finds the second wound.

Cold is a complication of trauma and not a detail of comfort, and a patient who is bleeding or exposed loses heat faster than a healthy person can replace it. The patient is placed on a barrier against the ground, wet clothing is removed only when it can be replaced with dry covering, and blankets are placed over and under the patient. Warm coverings are applied to the head and neck, and the patient is shielded from wind and rain. A low body temperature impairs the clotting of blood and makes shock deeper. Pain is also real, and the lay first aider answers it with position, reassurance, quiet, warmth, and the presence of someone calm. The trained responder or paramedic may give an analgesic when the local protocol authorizes it, and the physician in hospital makes the further decisions, none of which is reproduced here.

Every stab wound carries the risk of tetanus and of infection, and the questions are the same for every patient even though the answers are local. Tetanus is caused by a bacterium that lives in soil and in manure and enters through a wound, and the wound at greatest risk is deep, contains dead tissue or soil, or was made by an object that was not clean. The first task for the responder is to ask about the immunization history and record the answer, because a patient who cannot remember is treated as a patient who is not protected. The decision to provide protection against tetanus is made by a clinician who knows the history and the local protocol. Antibiotics are likewise not a first-aid measure, and the decision to use them belongs to a prescriber who has examined the wound, so the responder describes the wound, says what contaminated it, and reports any fever or spreading redness. The deep cleaning of a wound, the removal of dead tissue, and the drainage of pus belong to definitive hospital care.

Movement of a stabbed patient is a clinical decision and not a matter of convenience, and the destination is the nearest facility that can provide definitive care. The responder calls emergency medical services early, states that the patient has a penetrating wound, names the regions of the body involved, and gives the number of patients so that the receiving team can prepare. A patient with a wound to the chest, the abdomen, the pelvis, or the neck is transported to a trauma center where a surgeon and imaging are available, and the local protocol defines which facility that is and how far it lies. The target time from injury to definitive care is a figure that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

The search for other wounds continues during transport and again at the hospital, because the clothing is cut away, the patient is examined from the scalp to the soles, and the back is examined by rolling the patient as a unit. The mouth, the ears, the nose, the armpits, the groin, the buttocks, and the spaces between the fingers and toes are all examined, because these are the places where a wound hides. Any wound found is described by its position in relation to a fixed landmark and by whether it is bleeding, bubbling, or discharging anything. In hospital, the assessment continues with imaging chosen to answer a specific question that the examination has already raised. A plain radiograph shows air outside the lung, the level of blood in the chest, the position of a retained fragment, and sometimes the track of a blade that has marked bone. An ultrasound at the bedside answers the question of fluid in the chest, the abdomen, or around the heart, and it is repeated because a negative first look does not exclude an injury that is still bleeding.

A computed tomography, or CT, scan answers the question of which structures lie along the track, and it is used in stable patients whose wound has entered a dangerous region. A magnetic resonance imaging, or MRI, scan has a limited role in the acute phase because it takes time and because metallic fragments may make it unsafe, and it is used later for nerves and the spinal cord. Every one of these tests is ordered by the clinician who can act on the result. Surgical consultation is requested as soon as a stab wound has entered a dangerous region, and it is requested before the imaging is complete rather than after. The decision to explore a wound is a surgical decision, made on the combination of the track, the findings, and the stability of the patient. The abdomen may be explored when there is peritonitis, when there is evisceration, or when imaging shows free fluid or free air, and that exploration is the definitive treatment of an injured organ.

The chest may be explored when there is continuing hemorrhage, when a great vessel is injured, or when the heart is bleeding into its sac, and that repair is performed by the thoracic or trauma surgeon. The neck may be explored when there is an expanding hematoma, when a vessel is bleeding, or when the airway or the esophagus has been injured. None of these procedures belongs to the field, and a patient who survives the operation may still need the intensive care unit, or ICU, for support of breathing and circulation. The assessment of the blood supply to a limb is performed by a clinician and it decides whether the limb can wait. The pulses beyond the wound are felt and compared with the other side, the color and temperature of the limb are recorded, and movement and sensation below the wound are tested.

The repair of an injured artery by suture, by patch, or by graft is performed by the vascular surgeon in the operating room, and the pressures that decide whether the fascia must be opened are measured in hospital. A divided tendon is repaired by the hand or orthopedic surgeon under anesthesia in an operating room, with the wound cleaned and explored first. A divided nerve is repaired in the same setting or is left for a later procedure, and the decision depends on the contamination of the wound and on the condition of the patient. A wound that has injured the bowel is repaired or resected and joined again, and a wound that has injured the liver or spleen may require packing, repair, or removal. The closure of the skin is sometimes deliberately delayed, because a contaminated wound closed too early becomes an abscess.

Not every stab wound requires an operation, and the patients who do not are the ones who require the closest watching. A patient whose wound has not entered a cavity, whose pulses are intact, whose abdomen is soft, and whose observations are stable may be observed with repeated examination rather than taken to the operating room. The observation is performed by nursing staff and medical staff at intervals set by the local protocol, and each examination is compared with the one before. The findings that end the period of observation are a rising pulse, a falling pressure, increasing pain, a rigid abdomen, a falling urine output, a new breathlessness, or a change in the level of consciousness. The interval of observation is a figure that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

A constructed illustration may make the reasoning clearer, and it is explicitly a construction rather than the record of any real patient or incident. Consider a person who arrives at a clinic with a small slit below the left nipple, walking without help, speaking normally, and complaining only of soreness when breathing deeply. The pulse is a little fast, the breath sounds are equal, and the pressure is normal, so the temptation is to dress the wound and send the patient home. The reasoning of this chapter says otherwise, because the wound lies in a region whose track can reach the heart, the lung, and the diaphragm, and the absence of a dramatic finding proves nothing. The correct decision, made by the clinician who examines the patient, is to keep the patient under observation with repeated examination and imaging if it is available, and to transfer the patient if the condition changes.

To summarize, a knife wound is judged by the track it may have made and not by the size of the opening in the skin, and the regions that make a stabbing lethal are the chest, the abdomen, the pelvis, the neck, the back and flank, and the limbs. The lethal possibilities are named early with the finding that reveals each one, including a silent side of the chest, muffled heart sounds with distended neck veins, a rigid abdomen, and a cold clammy patient with a racing pulse. The first responder makes the scene safe, controls bleeding within the limits of that responder's training, stabilizes any embedded object, protects the wound, assesses the airway and the breathing, protects the patient from cold, and moves the patient to definitive care without delay. Exploration, vessel repair, tendon and nerve repair, fasciotomy, and the removal of an embedded object belong to hospital care and never to the field. Every figure in this chapter is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.
