# Chapter 12 — Gunshot Wounds to the Chest

A gunshot wound to the chest begins, for the responder, before the patient is touched, because the scene decides whether anyone can be helped at all. The first question is whether the shooter is gone, whether a second shot is possible, and whether the police have made the area safe, since a responder who becomes a casualty removes two people from help. Traffic, fire, a crowd that may contain the person who fired, and the hazards of the patient's own blood all belong to the same assessment, and gloves and personal protective equipment, or PPE, go on before contact. Only when the scene is safe does the responder approach, count the breathing, and search for the wound on the chest, the back, the armpits, and the flanks. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, or the protocol of the service in which the patient is treated.

Four lethal possibilities must be named before any comfortable explanation is accepted, because each announces itself with a finding that can be sought in the opening assessment. A tension pneumothorax is air trapped in the chest under pressure, and it reveals itself by desperate breathing, distended neck veins, and absent breath sounds on one side. A pericardial tamponade is blood around the heart that prevents it from filling, and it reveals itself as shock with quiet breath sounds and a falling pressure while the neck veins stay full. A massive hemothorax is a large volume of blood in the chest, and it shows as shock, dullness on tapping, and diminished breath sounds on the injured side. Injury to a major vessel may show nothing outside at all, so a patient who fades with no wound to explain it is treated as though such an injury is present.

The chest is a cage of ribs and muscle containing two lungs, each separated from the chest wall by a space that normally holds no air, while between them sit the heart, the great vessels, the esophagus, and the trachea. A wound near that crowded corridor is more dangerous than one at the edge, and a bullet entering the upper abdomen can pass upward through the diaphragm and injure the chest without leaving a wound on the chest. The path of a bullet is never predictable, and a wound that looks small on the skin may conceal a large cavity within, so the responder must never judge the injury by the entrance alone. A wound on the back may be missed if the patient lies on it, so the patient is rolled with the spine kept in line while the back, the armpits, and the flanks are examined. Where a figure such as 2 centimeters is used for an entrance wound in a teaching example, it is only an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

The first aider's order of work begins with the bleeding that can kill in minutes, and the chest is covered and pressed where it bleeds but never bound tightly, because a tight band stops the chest from expanding and turns a survivable injury into a fatal one. The airway is checked by looking in the mouth, by listening for air movement, and by asking the patient to speak, since a patient who answers clearly has an open airway. Breathing is judged by four things that need no instrument, which are the rate, the effort, the symmetry, and the sound, and the two sides are compared from the armpits, where air movement is heard most cleanly. A patient breathing at 30 breaths per minute, a figure offered here as an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, is working hard, and one who can no longer speak a full sentence is working harder still. While this is done, another person calls the emergency medical services, and the patient is kept still, warm, and covered, because cold worsens bleeding and shock.

A pneumothorax is air in the space between the lung and the chest wall, and it is the most common chest injury a bullet produces. The lung on that side collapses partly or completely, because the air outside it removes the pressure difference that holds it open, and breathlessness grows with the amount of lung lost. The findings are a fast breathing rate, diminished breath sounds on the injured side, and sometimes pain that worsens with a deep breath. A small pneumothorax may cause almost no symptoms, and that quiet appearance is exactly why it is dangerous, since a small leak can enlarge with every breath. The first aider's task is not to treat it but to recognize it, to keep the patient warm, and to reach a place where a chest radiograph and a trained team can decide what it means.

A tension pneumothorax is the same air under a rising pressure, and it kills quickly because it pushes the heart and the great veins aside rather than merely collapsing one lung. Air enters the chest with each breath and cannot leave, so the pressure grows until the vena cava is compressed and no blood can return to the heart. When the heart receives no blood, it has nothing to pump, and the pressure falls until the brain and the heart itself are no longer supplied. The sequence may take only a few minutes, and it can follow an open wound, a closed wound, or a wound already covered with a dressing. This is why the responder must keep watching every chest wound, and why a patient who was talking a moment ago and is now drowsy and gasping has a new and lethal problem. A pressure such as 90 millimeters of mercury is used in some teaching as the point at which shock becomes obvious, but that figure must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the line depends on the patient and on the way it is measured.

Tension pneumothorax is separated from a simple pneumothorax by the direction of the patient's condition rather than by any single sign. In a simple pneumothorax the patient is breathless but stable, the neck veins are flat, and the pressure holds, while in a tension pneumothorax the breathlessness worsens steadily, the neck veins become visibly full, and the pressure falls. The trachea, felt at the notch above the breastbone, may be pushed away from the injured side, but that finding appears late and never excludes the diagnosis. The injured side is quiet to the ear and dull or drum-like to the tapping finger depending on whether blood or air predominates, and the responder records what was found and when, because comparison over time is the most useful information the hospital will receive. Any table that sets these signs side by side is of no use in the ear, so the responder carries the single question that matters, which is whether this patient is getting worse.

There is a hard line between what may be done for a tension pneumothorax outside a hospital and what may not. The lay first aider may recognize the pattern, keep the patient sitting up if that helps and the pressure allows, keep the patient warm, and call for help without delay, and that is all. The first aider must not insert a needle, a pen, a tube, or any object into the chest, because the structures beneath cannot be identified by touch, and an untrained puncture can injure the lung, a vessel, or the heart. The trained responder or paramedic may relieve the pressure with a needle only when the service authorizes that act, the equipment is carried for it, and the protocol sets the site and the decision. Definitive hospital care owns the chest drain, the surgery, and the repair, and even a needle placed in the field is a bridge to that care rather than a treatment that finishes the job.

A hemothorax is blood in the space between the lung and the chest wall, and a bullet produces it by tearing the lung, an intercostal vessel along the inside of the ribs, or a vessel of the mediastinum. A small hemothorax behaves much like a small pneumothorax, with breathlessness and diminished breath sounds on one side, and it may be found only when the chest is imaged. A large hemothorax is a different disease, because the chest can hold several liters of blood and a patient can bleed to death into it while the outside of the body shows almost nothing. The findings are shock with a fast, thin pulse, breathlessness, dullness when the chest is tapped on the injured side, and diminished breath sounds, while the neck veins may be flat because the problem is lost volume rather than trapped pressure. The responder who finds shock with a quiet, dull chest and no external bleeding must think of bleeding into the chest, not of bleeding that has stopped.

An open pneumothorax is a wound of the chest wall that stays open, so that air moves in and out of the chest through the hole instead of through the trachea, and the classic finding is a wound that sucks and bubbles with each breath. The danger is not only the collapsed lung but the loss of every breath, because the air the patient draws follows the path of least resistance and moves through the wound rather than into the lungs, so the patient suffocates while the chest is moving. Heat and moisture are lost through the same opening, and a patient with a large open chest wound becomes cold quickly, which worsens shock and makes the blood less able to clot. A wound of 2 centimeters in a teaching example may look modest, yet the same figure must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the effect depends on the site and the tissue around the wound as much as on its size. The responder covers the wound and then watches the patient, since the cover can create the very pressure that follows.

The occlusive dressing is the central field act for an open chest wound, and it belongs to the trained responder, while the lay first aider uses whatever clean, airtight material is at hand. The aim is to stop air entering through the wound while allowing air that is already trapped to escape, which is why a commercial seal or a dressing taped on three sides has been the traditional teaching. The dressing must cover the wound completely and extend well beyond its edges, and it must be fixed so that it does not shift during transport, because a seal that slides off leaves the wound open again. The lay first aider who has plastic sheeting and tape may place it and must then say clearly what was placed and when. The responder records the time of the dressing, the condition before and after, and any change in the breathing, because those comparisons are what the receiving team uses. No dressing and no seal is a substitute for moving the patient to a hospital where the chest can be examined and drained.

Once an occlusive dressing is in place, the patient must be monitored for the build-up of pressure behind it, because a seal that works too well can produce a tension pneumothorax. The signs to watch are worsening breathlessness, a rising breathing rate, a falling level of consciousness, distended neck veins, and a pressure that cannot be maintained. If a trained responder finds those signs, the protocol may direct that a corner of the dressing be lifted briefly to let the trapped air escape and then resealed, and that decision belongs to the service protocol and not to improvisation. The lay first aider does not lift the dressing on a hunch but reports the change to the emergency medical services and keeps the patient warm and still. The final judgment about whether the seal should stay, be changed, or be removed belongs to the team that can see the chest and image it.

A bullet that passes near the heart can injure it in several ways, and not all of them involve a hole in the muscle. The heart may be bruised by the passage of a projectile nearby, which weakens the pumping and can produce an abnormal rhythm hours later. A chamber may be perforated so that blood leaks into the pericardial sac or into the chest, and a valve, the septum between the chambers, or a coronary artery on the surface of the heart may be torn. The patient may present with chest pain, a fast or irregular pulse, breathlessness, or a falling pressure, and in the worst case the patient collapses and needs cardiopulmonary resuscitation, or CPR, at once. A wound between the nipple line and the lower end of the breastbone, sometimes called the cardiac box in teaching, raises the probability of cardiac injury, but a bullet can reach the heart from the abdomen or the back as well. Because the surface wound cannot settle the question, any patient with a gunshot wound near the heart is treated as though the heart is injured.

Pericardial tamponade is the consequence that makes cardiac injury so deceptive, and it presents as shock with quiet breath sounds. The pericardial sac is a tough envelope that cannot stretch quickly, so a small amount of blood inside it can raise the pressure around the heart until the chambers can no longer fill. The result is a heart that beats but moves almost no blood, and the patient slides into shock while the chest looks and sounds deceptively calm. The findings are a falling pressure, distended neck veins, muffled heart sounds, a fast and weak pulse, and a level of consciousness that drifts, and breath sounds may be quiet simply because the patient is not moving enough air. The lay first aider cannot separate this from other causes of shock on scene and does not need to, because the action is the same, which is to treat the shock, keep the patient warm, and transport without delay. The needle that drains the sac and the surgery that repairs the heart belong to definitive hospital care.

Tamponade and tension pneumothorax can look alike at the roadside, and separating them is worth the effort because the two demand different help. Both produce shock, distended neck veins, and a struggling patient, and both can follow a wound to the chest. In tension pneumothorax the breath sounds are absent on one side, that side does not move, and the tapping finger finds drum-like air or dull blood, while in tamponade the breath sounds may be quiet but equal on both sides and the chest moves more evenly. The trachea may be pushed away from the injured side in tension and stays central in tamponade, though this sign is late and unreliable. The pressure in tamponade often falls in a way that does not respond to the measures used for other shocks, and the neck veins do not empty when the patient sits up. When the two cannot be separated, the responder reports both possibilities and moves, because the receiving team can settle the question with an ultrasound of the heart and a chest radiograph.

Injury to a major vessel inside the chest is the injury that most often leaves no sign at all, and it must stay in mind. The aorta, the pulmonary artery and its branches, the vena cava, and the vessels beneath the collarbone all lie within reach of a bullet, and each can bleed into the chest or into the tissues of the neck and shoulder. A patient with such an injury may have a small wound, may be awake and talking, and may then lose consciousness and die within minutes as the blood volume falls. The findings that suggest it are shock with no external bleeding, a difference in the pulses or the pressures between the two arms, a swelling at the base of the neck, and a wound whose track points toward the center of the chest. The responder's role is to recognize the possibility, to control what external bleeding can be controlled, to keep the patient flat and warm, and to move quickly to a trauma center. Nothing outside a hospital repairs a torn vessel, and the attempt belongs to the surgeon and the surgical team.

Support of the airway and the breathing follows the patient's condition rather than a fixed script, and each act has an owner. The lay first aider opens the airway by the method taught in training, clears what can be seen, and places the patient in the position that keeps the airway open while protecting the spine. The trained responder or paramedic may use a bag and mask, an airway device, and oxygen according to the protocol of the service, and the decision to give oxygen is a clinical one that the protocol and the prescriber govern rather than this manual. A patient who stops breathing needs cardiopulmonary resuscitation, or CPR, started at once by whoever is present, with compressions and breaths given as the training and the protocol direct. A patient who is breathing but tiring needs help before the breathing stops, so the responder watches the effort, the rate, and the level of consciousness and calls for advanced help early. Positive pressure given by an untrained hand can drive air into the stomach and worsen the injury.

Circulation in a chest wound is managed by controlling what can be seen and by restoring what has been lost, and the second belongs to hospital care. A bleeding wound of the chest wall is pressed with a dressing, and a wound of the armpit, the shoulder, or the base of the neck may need a pressure dressing applied by a trained responder, because those junctional areas cannot be compressed against a bone. A patient in shock is kept flat and warm, and the legs may be raised, though this measure buys time and does not replace lost blood. Fluid and blood replacement belong to the hospital and to the prescriber, and the choice of what to give, how much, and by which route is made by the receiving team under the current guideline and the local protocol. The responder's true contribution to circulation is the fastest safe journey, because the only definitive treatment for hemorrhage inside the chest is surgical.

Hypothermia is a complication of trauma rather than a detail of comfort, and a patient with a chest wound is at high risk of it. The patient is often lying on cold ground, undressed for examination, losing blood, and losing heat and moisture through an open chest wound, and each of those paths adds to the cooling. A temperature below 35 degrees Celsius is the teaching figure for significant hypothermia, and that value must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the threshold at which harm begins differs with the patient and the measurement. Cold makes the blood clot poorly, makes the heart irritable, worsens shock, and blurs the level of consciousness, so warmth is part of the resuscitation and not an afterthought. The patient is covered with blankets or any insulating material, the back is protected from the ground, and the ambulance is warmed before the patient is placed in it. Every movement is kept gentle, because cold and rough handling together can precipitate a rhythm that stops the heart.

The decision whether to stay and stabilize or to leave for the hospital is made on the mechanism of injury, the distance, the patient's condition, and the responder's scope of practice. A patient with a chest wound who is short of breath, whose pressure is falling, or whose level of consciousness is changing needs a trauma center quickly, and time spent on scene is time lost before surgery. The trained responder may perform the few field acts that the protocol permits, such as covering an open wound and supporting the breathing, and then travels, because a dressing is not a substitute for a surgeon. Where the distance is long, the decision may be to meet an air service or to move toward the nearest facility that can place a chest drain, and that judgment is made with medical direction. A period such as 10 to 15 minutes on scene is sometimes quoted as a goal for the fastest patients, but any such interval is an illustration and must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, because services differ in what they expect.

The information that travels with the patient is as important as the treatment given, and it must be spoken and written in a form the receiving team can use at once. The mechanism of injury, the time of the shooting, the number and site of the wounds, and whether an exit wound was found belong at the top of the account. The observations recorded on scene, with their times, must be handed over, including the breathing rate, the level of consciousness, the pulse character, and any pressure that was measured. Every intervention is named with its time, including the occlusive dressing, the position the patient was kept in, the warmth provided, and any tourniquet or pressure dressing placed elsewhere, and the time a tourniquet was applied must be written on the patient where the next team will see it. The account also names what was not done and why, because a decision not to attempt a procedure is part of the record and protects the patient from a repeated and useless attempt.

Hospital assessment begins with the same primary survey that was begun in the field, now with equipment, and it follows the ABCDE order until each problem is found and addressed. The airway is secured by the team that owns that skill, breathing is assessed with the chest exposed and both sides examined, and circulation is judged by the pulse, the pressure, the skin, and the level of consciousness, with the pelvic and abdominal examination done at the same time. A chest radiograph is taken early, and an ultrasound of the chest and the heart answers the questions of blood around the heart, air or blood in the pleural space, and the movement of the heart muscle. A computed tomography, or CT, scan gives the detailed picture of the track, the vessels, the mediastinum, and the retained fragments, and it is used when the patient is stable enough to travel to the scanner. The examination is repeated, because a chest that was quiet on arrival can become a tension pneumothorax as the observation continues, and the trend of the observations is the most sensitive test in the building.

The interventions that belong to definitive hospital care are named plainly here so that no listener mistakes them for field procedures. A chest drain placed through the chest wall lets air and blood out of the pleural space and allows the lung to re-expand, and it belongs to the physician, the surgeon, or the trained team working under protocol. Blood and fluid replacement, the correction of the clotting, and the warming of the patient are managed by the hospital team, and the substances, the amounts, and the routes are decisions of the prescriber under the current guideline and the local protocol. Pericardial drainage and the repair of a wounded heart belong to the surgeon and the surgical team, and so does the repair of a torn vessel or the control of bleeding that cannot be reached by a drain. A large hemothorax that keeps bleeding may need an operation through the chest wall, and that decision is made on the imaging, the drain output, and the patient's response. A trauma surgeon decides which of these is needed, and the earlier that decision is made, the better the patient's chance.

Not every chest wound needs an operation, and the choice between watching and operating is one of the central judgments of hospital care. A patient with a small pneumothorax, stable observations, and no ongoing bleeding may be watched with repeated examination and imaging, and a drain may still be placed to be safe. A patient whose drain returns a large volume of blood, whose pressure will not hold, or whose imaging shows a vessel injury goes to surgery, and that decision is made by the surgical team and not by the responder. A retained bullet is not removed for its own sake, because removal is a surgical decision made when it is clinically indicated, such as when it presses on a structure, lies in a joint, or causes infection that can be traced to it. A bullet that sits quietly in the chest of a stable patient may be watched for years, and no responder may attempt to extract it, because the attempt risks bleeding and tissue damage that cannot be controlled outside a surgical setting.

To summarize, a gunshot wound to the chest is managed by naming the lethal possibilities early, by making the scene safe before the patient is touched, and by following the ABCDE order with the chest examined on both sides and the back never forgotten. Pneumothorax, tension pneumothorax, hemothorax, open pneumothorax, cardiac injury, and major-vessel injury each announce themselves through the breathing, the neck veins, the tapping finger, and the breath sounds. Tension pneumothorax kills by pressure that prevents the heart from filling, open pneumothorax suffocates the patient through the wound itself, and tamponade presents as shock with quiet breath sounds, so all three must be kept in mind together. Only needle decompression within protocol, an occlusive dressing with monitoring, and warmth, oxygen or positioning within protocol belong to the field, while chest drainage, pericardial drainage, blood replacement, and the repair of the heart or a vessel belong to definitive hospital care. Every pressure, rate, temperature, depth, and interval named in this chapter is an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such numbers differ between countries, between services, and between editions.
