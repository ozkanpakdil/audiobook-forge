# Appendix A — Practical Emergency Algorithms

An algorithm in this manual is not a chart to be consulted but a path to be walked, and at every turn one action is finished before the next becomes possible, so a listener who follows the words in order is following the care in the only order that protects the patient. Six of these paths are gathered here because they are the ones a responder is most likely to need within the first minutes, and because each crosses the boundary between what may be done in the field and what belongs only to definitive hospital care. The six are the unconscious trauma patient, massive external hemorrhage, the suspected fracture, the suspected spinal injury, penetrating trauma, and the gunshot wound. None of them replaces the primary survey, because each is that survey applied to a particular patient, and each must be restarted whenever the patient changes for the worse. Every step names the provider level that owns it, which is the lay first aider, the trained first responder, the emergency medical technician, the paramedic, the nurse, the physician, or the trauma surgeon, and every step states what finding allows the responder to move on. The figures offered along the way are teaching illustrations rather than rules, and each must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such values differ between services, countries, and editions. This appendix is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

The first algorithm is for the patient who does not respond to voice or to touch, because that patient cannot protect an airway, cannot report pain, and cannot be asked what happened. No hand is laid on that patient until the scene is safe, so traffic is warned, fire and smoke are avoided, cables are treated as live until a person with authority has isolated the supply, water is not entered, unstable structures are not entered, loose animals are handled by those equipped to do it, and violence is answered by withdrawal rather than by approach. The lay first aider calls for emergency medical services, describes the scene and the number of patients, and keeps bystanders from crowding, while the trained responder or the paramedic takes charge of the hazards that require equipment and authority. Personal protective equipment, or PPE, goes on before contact, with gloves at the least and eye and mouth protection wherever the service provides it, because an unconscious patient cannot warn anyone of blood or vomit. The lethal possibility to be named first is catastrophic external hemorrhage, and the finding that reveals it is blood that soaks through every dressing, blood that pools under the body, or an amputation stump that pours without pause. That bleeding is controlled before the airway is examined, under the rule that life comes before limb, and the lay first aider presses hard with a clean cloth without lifting it to look, while the trained responder or the paramedic moves to packing or to a tourniquet within protocol when pressure fails.

With the scene safe and catastrophic bleeding controlled, airway, breathing, circulation, disability, and exposure, remembered as ABCDE, are performed in that fixed order, and each part decides whether the responder moves on or stops to treat. For the airway, the trained responder or the paramedic opens the mouth, looks for blood, vomit, a loose tooth, or a fallen tongue, and lifts the chin or the jaw to open the passage, while the lay first aider may perform the same chin lift if trained and calls at once for those who can do more. The finding that permits the next step is air that moves without noise or obstruction, and the finding that forbids it is snoring, gurgling, or silence. For breathing, the responder watches the chest rise, listens at the mouth, and feels the breath on a cheek, and the finding that moves the algorithm forward is a chest that moves on both sides at a rate and depth the patient can sustain, while a chest that does not move, moves on one side only, or bubbles at a wound sends the responder to the treatment of that injury. For circulation, the trained responder feels a pulse and watches the skin for color, warmth, and moisture, the lay first aider watches for restlessness, thirst, and fading awareness, and the appearance of any of those signs returns the responder to the control of bleeding and to the earliest transport. For disability the responder notes the level of consciousness, the size and reaction of the pupils, and the movement of each limb, because a patient who was speaking and has fallen silent has told the responder that the brain is losing its supply. For exposure the clothing is cut away only as far as the examination requires and the patient is covered again at once, because the search for hidden injury must not become the cause of cooling.

Prevention of hypothermia belongs inside this algorithm and not after it, because a patient who is cold bleeds more, clots poorly, and tolerates shock less well. The lay first aider removes wet clothing, replaces it with dry covering, insulates the patient from the ground, and covers the head, while the trained responder or the paramedic continues that protection during every examination and inside the vehicle. A body temperature below 35 degrees Celsius is often described as the point at which cold becomes dangerous in itself, and that figure is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the line shifts with age, with pregnancy, and with the way the temperature is taken. Transport is prepared as the patient is warmed, and the destination is the nearest facility that can give the care the patient needs, which may be a trauma center with imaging, an operating room, and an intensive care unit, or a district hospital that stabilizes and transfers. The field algorithm stops at the door of definitive care, where a physician may secure the airway with equipment the field does not carry, where a trauma surgeon may open the chest or the abdomen, and where imaging finds bleeding that no examiner could see, and the handover that ends the algorithm carries the mechanism of injury, the time of every intervention, and every change observed.

The second algorithm is massive external hemorrhage, and it starts with recognition rather than with a technique, because a responder who has not understood the rate of loss will choose the wrong rung of the ladder. The findings that establish the emergency are bleeding that continues through a dressing already applied, blood that pools beneath the patient so that a hand placed under the back comes away wet, a swelling that grows while the responder watches, and a stump or a junction that pours without pause. The patient speaks the same warning in the body, with a pulse that is fast and thin, skin that is pale and cool, breathing that is climbing, restlessness, thirst, and a level of consciousness that is dulling. The lay first aider reads these signs with the eyes and the hand rather than with instruments and reports them at once, because a trend is more useful to the receiving team than a single impression. A pressure such as 90 millimeters of mercury is sometimes quoted as a threshold, but it is an illustration of the reasoning that must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the pressure that matters differs with age and with the patient's usual state.

The first action is direct pressure, which belongs to the lay first aider as much as to the paramedic, and it means a protected hand pressing firmly on a clean cloth over the wound without lifting the edge to look. If blood shows through, further layers are laid on top and the hand returns to the same place with the same force, because each look breaks the clot that is forming, and the finding that means the step has failed is a dressing that keeps soaking while the pulse rises. The next action, once direct pressure has been held long enough to judge, is a pressure dressing applied by the trained responder or the paramedic, wound tightly enough to keep the pressure and loosely enough that the tissue beyond it is not starved. The check after a pressure dressing looks at the color and warmth of the skin beyond it, feels the pulse at the wrist or the foot, and asks the patient to move the fingers or the toes, and a limb that is pale, cool, or pulseless means the bandage has become a tourniquet by accident and must be released and reapplied with less tension. A period such as 10 minutes of uninterrupted pressure is often given as the time needed to judge whether a wound will stop, and that interval is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the time differs with the size of the vessel and with the patient's clotting. Packing is the next action, and it belongs to the trained responder or the paramedic under a protocol, because it requires a wound that is appropriate, which is a deep wound of the groin, the armpit, or the neck where a tourniquet cannot reach and a dressing across the surface cannot press on the vessel. The gauze is pushed to the bleeding point and packed firmly back along the track until the cavity is filled, and then pressure is held over it with the flat of the hand, while packing is not used on a chest wound or on an abdomen with bowel exposed and is not a technique for the lay first aider, who covers and presses from the outside and waits.

A hemostatic dressing, where the service carries one, is used by the trained responder or the paramedic at the same stage, and it never replaces direct pressure, packing, or the tourniquet, because it works only where it touches the bleeding vessel and only while pressure is still applied. The tourniquet is the last rung of the field ladder, its place is a limb that continues to bleed despite pressure and packing, and it is applied by the trained responder or the paramedic between the wound and the heart, as close to the wound as the anatomy allows and never over a joint when a length of limb above the joint is available. A tourniquet is not placed around the neck, the chest, the abdomen, or the groin, because it cannot control bleeding there and causes harm, and those sites belong to packing and to immediate surgery. The lay first aider does not apply a manufactured tourniquet unless emergency medical services have instructed it and trained the responder to do so, because a makeshift band can tighten a limb without stopping the flow and add pain and tissue damage while the patient continues to bleed. Once a tourniquet has controlled the bleeding it is not loosened in the field, since the clot behind it may not hold and the patient may bleed again where no one can restore control, and the time of application is written on the patient where the receiving team cannot miss it. The reason the time matters is that a limb without flow begins to suffer, and the team that receives the patient must know how long it has been without circulation in order to plan the release, while a period such as 2 hours is quoted as the point at which that risk becomes serious and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol because tolerance differs with temperature and with the patient.

Reassessment continues in the order of the primary survey, with the airway, the breathing, the circulation, the level of consciousness, the pupils, and the limbs examined again, and the finding that a dressing has soaked through or that the pulse has risen sends the responder back to the beginning and forward to definitive care at the same time. Hypothermia is prevented throughout, wet clothing is replaced with dry covering, the patient is insulated from the ground, and the journey begins as soon as the bleeding is controlled rather than when the patient looks comfortable. The algorithm stops and becomes hospital care where a surgeon explores the wound, repairs the torn vessel, and controls bleeding that pressure could not reach, and no field measure takes the place of that operation. The record that travels with the patient names the control measures used, the time of each, and the trend of the observations, because the receiving team continues a story that the field began.

The third algorithm is the suspected fracture, and it begins with the mechanism of injury rather than with the limb, because a fall from a height, a collision, a direct blow, or a twisting force predicts damage that the eye cannot confirm. A fall from greater than 3 meters is one teaching illustration of a mechanism that predicts injury far beyond the obvious, and the figure is offered only as an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since such a distance means different things for a child and for an adult. The look is performed before the limb is moved, and the findings that matter are a deformity, a shortening, a wound over the bone, bleeding, a change in the color of the skin, and a swelling that is growing. A wound over a fracture is an open fracture until proved otherwise, and it is a different emergency from a closed one because contamination has entered the bone and the infection risk changes the destination and the urgency. A severely deformed limb is splinted in the position found and is never straightened to make it look normal, because the ends of the bone may lie against a vessel or a nerve and movement can convert a closed injury into a hemorrhage or a permanent loss of function. The neurovascular assessment belongs to the trained responder or the paramedic and is performed before and after every splint, while the lay first aider contributes by reporting what the patient can feel and move and by keeping the limb still.

The neurovascular assessment compares one side with the other and records the pulse beyond the injury, the color and warmth of the skin, the ability to feel a light touch, and the ability to move the fingers or the toes. The findings that make it urgent are a pulse that cannot be felt, skin that has turned pale or blue, numbness or tingling that was not there before, and pain that grows out of proportion to the injury, because those findings point to a vessel or a nerve that is compressed and to compartment syndrome. Immobilization comes next, and the trained responder or the paramedic applies a splint that includes the joint above and the joint below the fracture, supports the limb in the position found, and pads it so that the skin is not pressed against a hard edge. The lay first aider immobilizes by keeping the limb still, by supporting it with the hands or with a sling made from clothing where the arm is injured, and by refusing to test the limb by moving it. A fracture is not reduced in the field, because reduction is a decision made in definitive care with imaging and with preparation for what the bone ends may have torn. Reassessment repeats the neurovascular examination once the splint is secure, and any loss of pulse, feeling, or movement sends the patient onward without delay to the physician or the trauma surgeon, who obtains a radiograph and decides about reduction, fixation, or an operation, which is where this algorithm stops.

The fourth algorithm is the suspected spinal injury, and its trigger is the mechanism rather than the symptom, because a patient with a broken neck may feel nothing at all while a patient with a minor strain may feel everything. The mechanisms that raise the suspicion are a fall from a height, a dive into shallow water, a collision in which the head was struck or the body thrown, a heavy object that fell on the back or the head, a blast, and any injury in which the patient was found unconscious. The first action is the same as in every algorithm, which is to make the scene safe, and the second is to treat catastrophic hemorrhage before anything else, because a patient who bleeds to death is not saved by a collar. The airway is then opened with the head and neck held in the neutral position found rather than tilted backward, and this belongs to the trained responder or the paramedic, who can keep that position while opening the mouth and clearing it. The lay first aider's part is to hold the head and neck still with the hands, to discourage the patient from turning or sitting up, and to call for those who carry the equipment, because manual support of the head is the one spinal measure available without a kit. The neurological assessment follows, and it asks what the patient can feel and move in each limb, whether sensation is the same on both sides, and whether the level of consciousness is changing, because a difference between the sides or a loss of movement must be reported at once.

Unnecessary movement is minimized rather than forbidden, because a patient who must be moved to escape fire, traffic, or a collapsing structure is moved, and the reason for the move is recorded. Where a move is required, the trained responder or the paramedic uses a technique that keeps the head, neck, and trunk in one line, with enough helpers for the size of the patient, and the lay first aider helps only by following the instructions of the person leading. Immobilization is applied according to the local protocol by the trained responder or the paramedic, and it may include manual support of the head, a rigid collar, side supports, and a board or a vacuum mattress, while the lay first aider is not asked to decide which device is used or how it is fitted. The device keeps the alignment that has been found and allows the patient to be moved, and it does not heal the spine, so the search for other injuries continues while the patient is held still. Breathing is watched throughout, because an injury high in the neck can weaken the muscles of breathing and the chest may move less well than the patient's awareness suggests. Transport goes to a facility that can assess the spine with imaging, which may be a trauma center with computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging, and an intensive care unit, and the algorithm stops there, where the physician or the trauma surgeon clears the spine or plans its treatment.

The fifth algorithm is penetrating trauma, whether the agent was a knife, a fragment, a piece of glass, or a tool, and it begins with scene safety because the weapon may still be present, an assailant may still be near, and the ground may be covered with blood that carries infection. The findings that must be sought are the wounds themselves, and the rule is to look for more than one, to undress the patient enough to see the front and the back, and to examine the places that are easy to forget, which are the scalp, the armpits, the groin, the buttocks, and the back. A penetrating wound is not a wound but a track, and the opening says nothing about the depth, so the responder must not probe it with a finger or an instrument, must not introduce anything into it, and must not explore it, because probing can open a vessel that has sealed itself and injure a structure that is still intact. The number of wounds matters for the same reason, since a knife withdrawn leaves an entrance that may be small while the track has crossed the chest, the abdomen, or a large vessel. The lay first aider looks without touching the inside of the wound, covers it, presses around any object that remains in place, and sends for help, while the trained responder or the paramedic performs the fuller examination and the control of bleeding.

Catastrophic bleeding comes first, and its control follows the same ladder as the hemorrhage algorithm, with direct pressure for the lay first aider, packing for the trained responder or the paramedic where the wound is appropriate, and a tourniquet for a limb that will not stop. A penetrating wound of the chest that bubbles or sucks, or one whose dressing is lifted by air, is covered with an occlusive dressing by the trained responder or the paramedic, and the chest is never bound tightly, because binding prevents the chest from expanding and can turn a wound that leaks into a lung that cannot fill. The ABCDE sequence follows in the fixed order, with the airway opened and cleared, the breathing watched and listened to, the circulation assessed, the level of consciousness and the pupils noted, and the patient exposed and covered again at once. After the primary survey the secondary survey looks for the second wound and for the injury the track has made, and the areas that change the destination are the chest, the abdomen, the pelvis, and the limbs. An object embedded in the wound is never removed in the field, and the reason is that the object may be plugging the vessel beneath it and may be the only thing preventing a hemorrhage that cannot be controlled, so the object is stabilized in place, padding is built around it, and the patient is moved with it still in the body. The algorithm stops at the door of the operating room, where a surgeon explores the track, removes the object when that is indicated, repairs what has been torn, and controls bleeding from within.

Shock and hypothermia travel with every penetrating wound, so the patient is kept warm from the first minute, wet clothing is replaced, the patient is insulated from the ground, and the vehicle is warmed before the journey. Fluids, blood products, and analgesia are decisions made by the paramedic under a protocol and by the physician in definitive care, and this manual names neither a substance nor an amount nor a route nor a frequency, because those choices belong to the local guideline and to the prescriber. Transport is rapid and gentle, because speed that throws the patient around adds injury and pain, and because the destination that matters is the one that can open a chest or an abdomen. The receiving team is told the mechanism, the number and site of the wounds, the interventions performed, the tourniquet time if one was placed, and every change observed, since the story is part of the treatment.

The sixth algorithm is the gunshot wound, and it begins with scene safety as well, because a firearm may still be present, a second person may still be armed, and a responder who becomes a patient has made the scene worse. The wounds are found by looking at the whole patient, and the rule is the same as for any penetrating injury, which is that the opening seen is only the beginning and there may be an exit wound and further wounds on the back or the limbs. Hemorrhage control comes before the rest of the examination, with direct pressure for the lay first aider, packing for the trained responder or the paramedic where the wound is deep and the site is appropriate, and a tourniquet for a limb that continues to bleed, and the tourniquet is not loosened once it has controlled the bleeding and its time is written on the patient. The finding that sends the responder onward is bleeding that has stopped while the pulse and the level of consciousness hold, and continued soaking, a growing swelling, or a falling awareness means the control has failed and the journey must shorten. The ABCDE sequence follows, and in the gunshot patient the chest, the abdomen, the pelvis, and the limbs each deserve a deliberate question after the primary survey.

The chest is examined for a wound, for breathlessness, for a side that moves less than the other, for dullness on one side, and for distended neck veins, and a wound that bubbles means an occlusive dressing applied by the trained responder or the paramedic, with the chest never bound tightly. The abdomen is examined by the physician or the trauma surgeon for tenderness, rigidity, and distension, and the trained responder reports what is seen without pressing into a painful belly, because pressing can worsen bleeding that is already concealed. The pelvis is not sprung by hand in the field, because a fractured pelvis can bleed into the space behind the peritoneum, and the findings that suggest it are pain, instability reported by the patient, or blood at the opening of the urethra. The limbs are examined for a wound, a fracture, a pulse that has been lost, and a compartment that is tense, and those findings are recorded for the team that will take over. A bullet is not removed by a first responder, and the reason is that removal is a surgical decision made when it is clinically indicated, because a retained bullet may lie against a vessel or a nerve and the operation to take it out may cause more harm than the bullet itself.

Treatment in the field stays inside the scope of practice, so the lay first aider presses, covers, keeps the patient still, keeps the patient warm, and calls, while the trained responder or the paramedic uses the equipment and the protocol that the service authorizes, and the nurse and the physician continue that care on arrival. The algorithm stops and becomes hospital care at the point where imaging is needed, where a chest or an abdomen must be opened, where a vessel must be repaired, and where the operating room and the intensive care unit take over. Shock is anticipated in every gunshot patient, and its early signs, which are a fast thin pulse, cool pale skin, restlessness, and thirst, are watched for and reported rather than waited for. Hypothermia is prevented at the same time as the bleeding is controlled, and the patient is covered, insulated from the ground, and kept warm on the way. Transport goes to a trauma center when the injuries require it and to the nearest facility that can stabilize when the distance is long, and the choice follows the local protocol rather than the preference of the responder. The handover carries the time of the injury, the sites of the wounds, the control measures used, and the trend of the patient's condition, because the receiving team continues a story that the field began.

To summarize, the six algorithms in this appendix are the same few acts arranged in the order that keeps a patient alive, and each begins with scene safety and with the control of catastrophic bleeding. The unconscious patient is carried through airway, breathing, circulation, disability, and exposure, with hemorrhage controlled first when a limb is pouring, and with warmth and rapid transport running alongside the survey. Massive external hemorrhage is met with direct pressure, then a pressure dressing, then packing of an appropriate wound, then a hemostatic dressing where the service carries one, and finally a tourniquet for a limb that will not stop, with reassessment after every rung and with the tourniquet left in place and its time written on the patient. The suspected fracture is splinted in the position found and never straightened to make it look normal, the neurovascular state is recorded before and after the splint, and the suspected spinal injury is managed by minimizing movement and by immobilizing according to protocol. Penetrating trauma and the gunshot wound are searched for more than one opening, are treated for bleeding before everything else, are carried through the primary survey, and are moved with any embedded object still in place, because removal in the field may release a hemorrhage that cannot be controlled. Every algorithm names the provider level that owns each step, from the lay first aider who presses and covers, through the trained responder and the paramedic who pack, splint, and apply the tourniquet, to the nurse, the physician, and the trauma surgeon in definitive care, and every algorithm states the point at which the field ends and the hospital begins. No figure in this appendix is a rule, whether it is a distance, a time, a temperature, or a pressure, and each must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such values differ between services, countries, and editions. This appendix is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.
