# Chapter 1 — Principles of Emergency Trauma Care

The person who arrives first at a scene of injury holds the whole course of the patient's survival in a few unglamorous acts, and almost none of them look like the emergency that people imagine. The goals of first aid are not to make the injury look normal, to name the damage, or to finish a treatment, because none of those things saves a life in the field. They are to keep the airway open, to stop the bleeding that can be stopped by hand, to keep the patient warm, and to move the patient toward definitive care as quickly and as safely as the scene allows. A list of that kind is easy to remember and easy to dismiss, because it is boring, and the boredom is precisely why it works, since a frightened person in the rain can repeat it without hesitation. What kills patients in the first hours is rarely a lack of sophistication; it is a failure to do one of those simple things, or to do them in the wrong order. This chapter sets out the principles that govern every other chapter of this manual, and it is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

The list is boring because each item asks the responder to do less than instinct demands rather than more. Instinct says to lift the dressing and see whether the bleeding has stopped, to move the patient to a place that looks tidier, and to straighten the limb that looks wrong, and each of those instincts can undo work that has already been done. The discipline of first aid is therefore a discipline of holding still: pressure is held without lifting an edge, the patient is kept in the position found, wet clothing is replaced with covering rather than with exposure, and the journey begins as soon as it is safe rather than as soon as the patient looks comfortable. The uncomfortable truth is that such restraint feels like doing nothing, and a bystander may judge the responder as inactive while the responder is in fact keeping the patient alive. Restraint also protects the patient from the second injury, which is the injury caused by the rescue itself.

No clinical act may begin before the scene has been made safe, because a responder who is injured adds a patient to a scene that already has more than it can manage. The hazards that kill rescuers form a short and repeating list, and every one of them can be recognized before it is entered. Moving traffic on a road, fire and its smoke, live electrical cables and the current in a wire believed to be dead, deep or fast water, structures that may collapse, an animal that is still loose, and a person who is still violent are the dangers of the scene itself, and each is a lethal possibility rather than a nuisance. Contaminated material is the quieter hazard, because blood, vomit, and tissue carry infection that enters through a break in the skin or a splash into the eye. The finding that reveals the hazard is often simply looking before moving: the smell of fuel, the hum of a generator, a wire lying across the water, and a crowd that has not yet stopped shouting. The lay first aider who cannot make the scene safe does not enter it, and the willingness to stop at the edge is one of the most valuable skills taught in this manual.

Making a scene safe is a sequence, and it begins with removing the threat rather than reaching the patient. Traffic is answered by warning approaching drivers, by placing a vehicle to protect the space, and by waiting until the road is held before stepping into it. Fire is answered by moving upwind and by refusing to enter a burning vehicle, because a fuel tank can burst and a structure can fall without warning. Electricity is answered by having the supply isolated by a person authorized to do it, and until that isolation is confirmed the wire is treated as live and the patient touching it is not touched. Water is answered by reaching with an object from the bank rather than entering the current, and violence is answered by withdrawing to a position that can be defended and by waiting for those trained and authorized to control it. The trained responder or paramedic works within the same order but has the equipment and the authority to hold the road, to cut the power, and to enter a structure that has been declared stable.

Protection of the rescuer's own body belongs before the first hand is laid on a patient, and it is measured by what is actually available rather than by what is ideal. The trained responder carries personal protective equipment that is issued and replenished, which includes gloves, eye protection, a gown or apron, and a barrier for the mouth if resuscitation is needed, and it is used for every patient rather than only for the ones who look infectious. The lay first aider has whatever is in the car or the house, and ordinary gloves, a plastic bag over the hands, glasses or a scarf over the eyes, and a folded cloth over the mouth are each better than bare hands. The purpose is not a sterile field, which the roadside cannot provide, but a barrier between the patient's blood and the responder's skin and eyes. A responder with a cut on the hand covers it before the glove goes on, and a responder who is splashed washes the area with soap and water as soon as another person has taken over the patient. The finding that tells the responder to replace the barrier is a glove that has torn, a barrier that has become soaked, or bleeding heavy enough to strike the face.

Once the scene is safe, the first piece of clinical evidence is not the wound but the mechanism of injury, which is the story of the energy that acted on the body. A mechanism is a prediction about damage that no eye can see from the outside. A fall from a height greater than 3 meters, a figure offered only as a teaching illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, predicts compression of the spine and of the heels, damage inside the abdomen, and fractures of the pelvis and the long bones. A vehicle collision predicts deceleration injuries in which organs move within the body and tear where they are attached, and the direction of the impact decides which organs are at risk. A blast predicts a pressure wave that injures the lungs and the eardrums, and fragments that travel far beyond the place where the device exploded. A knife or a bullet predicts a track whose depth cannot be judged from its opening, and a crushing weight predicts muscle damage that may poison the patient hours later.

The reason mechanism predicts hidden damage is that injury is a transfer of energy, and the body absorbs that energy according to the tissue it meets. Skin may close over a cavity that has been torn inside, a bone may break without piercing the skin while the vessels beside it are torn, and a blow that leaves no mark on the head may still have shaken the brain within the skull. The responder who understands this stops searching for a wound that explains the patient's condition and begins searching for the injury that the mechanism makes likely. A constructed illustration, offered as a construction and never as a real patient or a real incident, is the rider thrown over the handlebars who stands up with a grazed knee, where the knee explains nothing about the pain in the abdomen, the tenderness over the spleen, or the blood collecting where no one can see it. The same reasoning applies to the patient who fell down a short flight of stairs, whose alcohol and whose age may distract the responder from the injury the fall produced. Mechanism is not a diagnosis, but it is the question that makes the examination purposeful instead of random.

The examination is divided into two surveys, and the difference between them is the difference between saving a life and missing an injury. The primary survey is the search for the conditions that will kill the patient within minutes, and it is performed in a fixed order remembered as ABCDE: airway, breathing, circulation, disability, and exposure. Each letter is a question, and the answer to each decides whether the responder moves to the next or stops to treat what has just been found. The secondary survey is the full head-to-toe examination that follows, and it looks for every injury not yet found, together with the history of what happened and the patient's own account where the patient can give one. The primary survey is repeated whenever the patient's condition changes, because a patient who was stable when first seen may not be stable after a move, a dressing, or part of a journey. The order of the primary survey is not a preference but a rule, and the reason is that treating a broken limb before opening an airway will not help a patient who is not breathing.

The order is not negotiable because each step protects the one that follows, and because a later step performed first can make an earlier one impossible. Airway comes first because no work on the chest or on a bleeding limb can revive a patient whose passage for air is closed, and breathing comes next because oxygen that cannot enter the lungs cannot reach the brain. Circulation comes after that because a patient who has lost the blood to carry the oxygen is dying for a different reason, and in many services the control of catastrophic external hemorrhage is placed ahead of everything else, so that a limb pouring blood is controlled before the airway is examined. Disability follows, which means the level of consciousness together with the pupils and the movement of the limbs, because a patient who was talking and has become silent is telling the responder that the brain is losing its supply. Exposure comes last, in which the patient is undressed enough to be examined and then covered at once, because the search for injuries must not become a cause of cooling. The finding that returns the responder to the beginning of the sequence is any change for the worse, at any point, in any patient.

The principle of life before limb decides the order when two correct actions compete for the same pair of hands. It means that an action which preserves the patient's survival is chosen before one that preserves a limb, even when the limb is the more urgent-looking problem and even when it could still be saved by prompt care. The situations where it decides are specific and repeated: a patient with a mangled leg and a closed airway, a patient with an amputated hand and a chest filling with air, and a patient with a deformed limb and a circulation failing from bleeding somewhere else. In each of those pairs both actions are correct, and the principle chooses the one performed first rather than the one performed instead. The reason is that a limb can wait for minutes that a brain and a heart cannot, and that the attempt to save a limb while the patient is dying of something else usually loses both. The finding that settles the order is not the size of the wound but whether the patient is currently able to breathe and to circulate.

The first of the two failures that most often undo a rescue is the hemorrhage that is not seen, and it is missed because the responder is looking at the injury that is visible. Blood can collect in the chest until the lung is compressed, in the abdomen until the belly is distended and rigid, in the pelvis behind the peritoneum, and in the thigh around a broken femur, and none of those collections is obvious at the roadside. The findings that reveal concealed bleeding are a patient who is pale, cool, restless, thirsty, or increasingly confused while the visible wound is small, and a pulse that is climbing while the pressure is falling. The lay first aider cannot measure these and must not press into a tender belly or spring the pelvis to test it, because both acts may worsen the bleeding, so what the lay first aider does is call emergency medical services early, keep the patient still and warm, and report every change observed. The trained responder repeats the observations, watches the abdomen for distension and the chest for dullness, and supports the circulation while moving toward the place where the bleeding can be stopped. Definitive hospital care opens the cavity, finds the bleeding vessel or organ, and controls it, and no field measure takes the place of that operation.

Emergency medical services are activated as soon as the scene has been made safe and the patient has been assessed, and they are activated early rather than at the moment the responder runs out of ideas. The call carries the same information in the same order every time, so that the dispatcher can send the right resource without asking twice. The location is given first, with a landmark, a road name, and a direction of travel, because a team that cannot find the patient is no help at all. The number of patients is given next, with a brief statement of what is wrong with each, so that the response is scaled to the scene rather than to the first patient found. The mechanism of injury is reported, because it tells the receiving team what to expect and what equipment to bring, and the hazards that remain are named so that nobody else is injured on arrival. What has already been done is stated plainly, including pressure applied, a tourniquet placed and the time it was placed, and whether the patient has been moved.

When there are more patients than there are hands, the responder stops treating the nearest patient and begins to sort them, and that act has a name, which is triage. Triage is the deliberate decision about who is treated first, and it is made by the trained responder or by a clinician under the authority of the local protocol, because it involves withholding care from some patients in order to give it to others. The sorting asks a small number of questions in a fixed order: who can walk, who is breathing, who is breathing after the airway is opened, who has a pulse that can be felt, and who responds to voice or to pain. Those questions are answered with the eyes and the hands within seconds, and the answers place each patient into a group that carries a priority rather than a diagnosis. A patient who can walk is moved to a safe place and given a task, because that patient is not the most urgent and may be able to help. A patient who is not breathing after the airway has been opened is placed in the group treated last when the scene is overwhelmed, and this is the counterintuitive part of triage that must be stated plainly.

The decision to leave a patient who is already dying, in order to reach patients who can still be saved, is the hardest decision in this chapter and the one most likely to be called inhuman. It is taken because a rescue that spends all its hands on one patient who cannot be saved may lose several who could have been saved, and because the number of survivors is the measure the scene must answer to. The responder who makes that decision does not make it alone where a medical controller or a senior clinician can be reached, and the local protocol decides how such patients are identified and how they are kept as comfortable as the scene allows. Comfort in this setting means warmth, shelter from the weather, a position that eases breathing where that is possible, and the presence of a person who stays with the patient if one can be spared. Nobody in this manual is authorized to end a life or to hasten a death, and nothing in triage is a substitute for the ethical and legal framework of the country in which the responder works. The lay first aider does not perform triage and is not asked to choose between patients, because that decision belongs to the trained responder and to the system that supports them.

The secondary survey begins only when the primary survey has been completed and the patient has been stabilized enough to tolerate it, and it belongs to the trained responder and to definitive hospital care rather than to the lay first aider. It is a head-to-toe examination performed in a fixed order, beginning at the scalp and the face and moving to the neck, the chest, the abdomen, the pelvis, the back, and then each limb, with the front and the back of the patient both examined. The history that accompanies it includes the mechanism of injury, the time of the injury, the patient's illnesses and medicines, the last time the patient ate, and the patient's allergies and tetanus status where those are known. The secondary survey is where the injury that the primary survey did not need to find is discovered, which is the fractured wrist, the dislocated shoulder, the bruise over the kidney, and the small wound that will need cleaning and protection against tetanus. The finding that ends the secondary survey and returns the responder to the beginning is any deterioration in the patient, because a patient who is worsening needs the primary survey again before the examination continues. The lay first aider's part in this survey is to keep the patient warm, to keep the patient still, and to answer the questions of the receiving team as accurately as possible.

Cold is a complication of trauma rather than a detail of comfort, and protecting the patient against it is part of the same work as stopping the bleeding. A patient who is bleeding, burned, undressed for examination, lying on cold ground, or receiving fluids at room temperature loses heat quickly, and cold makes blood clot poorly, deepens shock, and makes the heart more likely to develop a dangerous rhythm. A body temperature below 35 degrees Celsius is the figure often used to describe the point at which those effects become serious, and that figure, like every other in this chapter, is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the accepted line differs between services and between editions. The lay first aider removes wet clothing, covers the patient with whatever is at hand, including blankets, coats, and foil, and insulates the patient from the ground, because much of the heat is lost downward. The trained responder continues that protection during transport and limits the time the patient is exposed during repeated examinations. The finding that warns the responder that cooling has already begun is a patient who has stopped shivering while still cold, because shivering is the body's attempt to make heat.

Every action in this manual belongs to a named level of provider, and the level is decided by training, equipment, authorization, and protocol rather than by the urgency of the moment. The lay first aider may press on a wound, open an airway with the hands, keep a patient warm, and send for help, and may not perform any act that requires a kit or a decision about a substance. The trained responder and the paramedic may use the equipment they carry and the procedures their service authorizes, which may include a tourniquet, a dressing that controls bleeding, splinting, airway adjuncts, and oxygen, with the decision about whether oxygen is given belonging to the protocol and the prescriber rather than to this book. The decisions about pain relief, about antibiotics, about protection against tetanus, and about fluids are clinical decisions, and the substance, the amount, the route, and the timing are set by the local protocol and the prescriber, so a listener cannot take a prescription from these pages. Definitive hospital care owns the removal of an embedded object, the exploration of a wound, the reduction of a fracture, the repair of a vessel or a tendon or a nerve, the opening of a cavity, and the operation that controls internal bleeding, and none of those is a field procedure. Knowing what one must not attempt is as much a part of competence as knowing what to do, because an unauthorized attempt often converts a survivable injury into a fatal one.

The two causes of a rescue that fails are the scene hazard that was not recognized and the hemorrhage that was not seen, and both are failures of attention rather than failures of skill. The first is prevented by looking before moving, by naming the hazard aloud, and by refusing to enter a scene that has not been made safe by someone with the authority to make it safe. The second is prevented by treating the mechanism of injury as a prediction, by examining the patient rather than the wound, and by repeating the observations until the patient reaches a place where the bleeding can be controlled. A third and quieter failure belongs with them, which is the failure to record what was done, whether it is a tourniquet whose time was not written where the receiving team will see it or a change in the patient that was never passed on. The record is part of the treatment, because the receiving team continues a story that the field began, and a story with a gap in it forces them to start again. The finding that should prompt a written note is any intervention, any change, and the time at which either occurred.

The patient is watched continuously from the first contact until the handover, because the condition of a trauma patient can change within a single journey. Watching means repeating the same observations in the same order and comparing them with what was found before, rather than trusting the impression of the moment. The findings that should send the responder back to the beginning are a falling level of consciousness, a breathing pattern that has become faster or slower or noisier, a pulse that has become weak or rapid, skin that has become colder or paler, and bleeding that has restarted beneath a dressing. The destination is chosen before the patient is moved, and it is the nearest facility that can provide the care the patient needs, which may be a trauma center with imaging, an operating room, and an intensive care unit, or a district hospital that can stabilize the patient and arrange a transfer. Transport is both urgent and gentle, because speed that throws the patient around adds injuries and pain, and because a patient who arrives in worse condition has gained nothing from the hurry.

A manual can describe principles, but it cannot stand in the place of training, and the difference matters most at the moment when a reader becomes a responder. The skills in this book are learned with a teacher, practiced on models and on each other, and certified by an authority that also defines what may be done without supervision. Local protocol decides what equipment is carried, what procedures are authorized, whom the responder may call for advice, and which hospital receives the patient, and that protocol takes precedence over anything written here. Where this manual describes a figure or a threshold, it does so to explain the reasoning and never to set a rule, because such numbers differ between services, countries, and editions of the same guideline. The reader is asked to bring the principles to the training, to keep the training current, and to work within the system that supervises the care.

To summarize, the first responder's task is not to make the injury look normal but to keep the patient alive, and the acts that accomplish this are few and unglamorous. Scene safety comes before the patient, so traffic, fire, electricity, water, unstable structures, violence, animals, and contaminated material are dealt with before any hand is laid on a wound, and personal protective equipment is whatever barrier is actually available. The mechanism of injury is the first clinical evidence, because it predicts damage that no eye can see, and the primary survey is performed in the fixed order of ABCDE before the secondary survey begins. Life comes before limb when two correct actions compete, concealed hemorrhage is sought in every patient whose circulation is failing without a wound to explain it, emergency medical services are activated early with a report that is complete and calm, and triage decides who is treated first when there are more patients than hands. Cold is treated as a complication, the provider level is named for every intervention, the record is written as carefully as the treatment is given, and the patient is watched from the first contact until the handover. The two failures to guard against are the scene hazard that was not recognized and the hemorrhage that was not seen, and both are defeated by looking, by repeating the examination, and by refusing to be reassured too early. Every number in this chapter, whether a time, a pressure, a temperature, a depth, or a distance, is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between services, countries, and editions. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, or the law of the country in which you work.
