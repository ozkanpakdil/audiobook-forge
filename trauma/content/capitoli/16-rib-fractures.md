# Chapter 16 — Rib Fractures

A fractured rib is common and underestimated, because the bone that breaks is rarely what endangers the patient. The scene is made safe first, because a patient with a chest injury usually lies on a road, at a worksite, or beside a fire. The lethal possibility is a chest that no longer moves air, revealed by fast shallow breathing, speech that cannot be finished, or a level of consciousness that is dulling. Personal protective equipment, or PPE, is whatever barrier is available, and it is used for every patient rather than only the ones who look infectious. The fracture is not the danger; the breathing and the lung beneath it are, and this material does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country where you work.

The ribs are paired curved bones joining the spine behind and the sternum in front, forming a cage that serves both protection and movement. The upper ribs shield the great vessels entering and leaving the heart, while the lower ribs shield the liver, the spleen, and the kidneys. The cage is also the mechanism of breathing, because the ribs are levers that rise and fall with every breath. The lower ribs are the most mobile and the most exposed to a blow from the side, so a break there can injure an organ beneath the bone. A constructed illustration, never a real patient or incident, is the driver whose chest met the steering wheel and whose only mark was a bruise over the lower left ribs, with the spleen beneath taking the force.

The mechanism of injury is the first clinical evidence, because it predicts what the bone and the tissue beneath have already suffered. A direct blow concentrates force at a single place, while a crush squeezes the whole cage and may break several ribs at more than a single point. A fall from a height or a deceleration tears structures where they are attached, so a torn vessel and a bruised lung can exist with no fracture visible. The responder therefore asks what happened before asking where it hurts, and uses the answer to decide how closely the patient is watched. An impact strong enough to break a rib was strong enough to bruise the lung beneath it, and that bruise can worsen over the following hours.

The simple rib fracture, a break of a single rib or a few ribs with an intact chest wall, is the most common pattern. Its findings are pain at a single point that worsens with a deep breath, a cough, or any movement of the trunk, with tenderness over the bone. The patient then breathes shallowly to avoid pain, and that shallow breathing begins the trouble, because air that does not move lets secretions collect and small areas of lung collapse. That collapse, called atelectasis, is the first step toward pneumonia in a patient who is otherwise not seriously injured. The lay first aider keeps the patient still, warm, and in the position that eases breathing, calls emergency medical services, and never straps the chest or presses the tender rib.

When several ribs are broken, the cage stops behaving as a unit and the ability to move air begins to fail. The finding is fast shallow breathing, with visible effort of the neck and shoulder muscles, speech in short phrases, and less movement on the injured side. Pain is a reason for that failure, because every movement costs the patient, and the loss of mechanical integrity is the other. A count of 3 or more broken ribs is often used as a warning line, and that number must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because age and lung disease change what any count means. The responder does not need to count ribs, because a patient working hard to breathe has already shown the danger.

A flail chest is the extreme form, in which a segment of the chest wall is broken at more than a single point on several ribs. The revealing finding is paradoxical movement, where the injured segment sinks inward as the patient breathes in and rises outward as the patient breathes out. That movement is easiest to see during spontaneous breathing and hardest when the patient breathes rapidly or the chest is covered, so the responder looks again once the patient is exposed. The lung beneath a flail segment is usually bruised as well, which is why breathing fails more than the mechanics alone would suggest. Definitive hospital care owns the decisions about supporting ventilation, about pain control that lets the chest move, and about any operation to stabilize the wall, and none of those is a field procedure. The lay first aider covers the patient, keeps the patient still and warm, and calls for help without pressing on the segment.

A pulmonary contusion is a bruise of the lung itself, and it often decides whether a patient with broken ribs recovers without complication. Blood and fluid leak into the air spaces, so a region that was exchanging oxygen stops doing so, and the breathing becomes fast and labored while the chest wall looks almost unmarked. Its timing is cruel, because it can be small when the patient is first seen and can grow over the following hours. That is why a patient with a significant mechanism is watched and re-examined rather than reassured by a first set of acceptable observations. A measured oxygen level below a number such as 90 per cent is often named as a warning, and that number is an illustration to be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol. Definitive hospital care follows a contusion with imaging, support of breathing, and an intensive care unit, or ICU, when the injury demands it.

A pneumothorax is air that escapes into the space between the lung and the chest wall, where it compresses the lung. It can follow a broken rib that has torn the lung surface, and it can occur without any fracture when a deceleration tears the tissue, so a normal image does not rule it out. The findings are sudden worsening of breathlessness, sharp pain on a single side, reduced movement on the injured side, and a hollow sound when the chest is tapped by a trained responder. A tension pneumothorax is the lethal form, in which air enters with each breath and cannot leave, so the pressure rises, the lung collapses, and the heart is compressed. Rapid deterioration, a rising breathing rate, a falling level of consciousness, distended neck veins, and a windpipe shifted away from the injured side demand immediate movement rather than more examination. Relief of that pressure belongs to the paramedic under a protocol or to definitive hospital care, and chest tube insertion, the definitive drainage of air or blood, is a hospital procedure and never a field measure.

A hemothorax is blood in the same space, and it is dangerous because it compresses the lung and removes blood from the circulation. A fractured rib can tear a vessel between the ribs, and a torn vessel can bleed steadily into a space that holds a large volume before anything is seen outside. The finding that reveals concealed bleeding is the language this manual uses for hemorrhage: pallor, cool clammy skin, restlessness or thirst, a fast soft pulse, and a level of consciousness that dulls while the visible injury looks modest. A dull sound when the chest is tapped on the injured side is the sign that blood rather than air has collected, and it is a finding for the trained responder. A measured pressure such as 90 millimeters of mercury is quoted only as an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the line differs with age and with the patient's usual state. The responder recognizes the pattern, keeps the patient warm and still, and reaches definitive care quickly, because draining the blood and controlling the torn vessel are hospital work.

The first examination of a rib fracture can be reassuring while the patient is still in the early stage of a serious injury. A patient with a bruised lung, an undrained collection of blood, or a slowly enlarging pneumothorax may walk, talk, and give a clear history. The responder must therefore treat the mechanism and the trend rather than the moment, repeating the same observations in the same order and comparing them with the earlier set. The findings that should send the responder back to the beginning of the primary survey are a rising breathing rate, increasing effort, a falling level of consciousness, a fast thin pulse, and cold pale skin. A patient who was comfortable and is now quiet is not necessarily resting, because quietness in a trauma patient can be the sound of a patient losing the ability to respond.

Assessment of breathing is the center of this chapter, and it comes before the ribs are examined. The responder looks at the rate, the depth, the symmetry, and the effort of the chest, and listens for speech that has become short and a voice that has become weak. The hands are used gently, because pressing hard on a fractured rib causes pain and discourages the very breathing that must be encouraged. A breathing rate above 20 breaths per minute in an adult is a commonly taught warning sign, and that number must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because normal rates differ with age. A child is watched with particular care, because a child's chest wall is flexible and can absorb force without breaking, so severe bruising of the lung can occur with no fracture to explain it. The sequence of the primary survey is remembered as ABCDE, which stands for airway, breathing, circulation, disability, and exposure.

The lay first aider's work in a rib fracture is small, and each part of it protects the breathing. The scene is made safe before the patient is approached, and emergency medical services are called early with a report of what happened, how many patients there are, and whether breathing is difficult. The patient is kept in the position that is easiest for breathing, usually sitting up or leaning slightly forward, and is not forced to lie flat. Loose clothing is opened so the chest can be seen, the patient is covered to stay warm, and wet clothing is replaced with dry covering. The lay first aider does not bind the chest, does not press on the tender ribs, and does not give anything by mouth to a patient who may need an operation. What the lay first aider gives is calm presence and an accurate description of what is seen, because a responder who reports a change has already helped.

The trained responder and the paramedic add measured observations, and those numbers make a trend visible. Breathing is counted over a full minute rather than estimated, the color and warmth of the skin are recorded, and the level of consciousness is compared with the earlier finding where the responder is trained in the Glasgow coma scale. Where the service carries the equipment, oxygen saturation is measured and the chest is listened to with a stethoscope, and the findings on both sides are compared. Oxygen is given when the patient's breathing or measured oxygen level indicates it, and the decision, the amount, and the way it is delivered belong to the provider authorized by the local protocol and to the prescriber rather than to this book. The trained responder may support breathing with the equipment the service authorizes, and none of that replaces the fastest safe journey to a facility that can image the chest and drain it if needed. Every pressure, rate, and oxygen number is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

Pain control in a patient with broken ribs is a treatment of breathing, because a patient who cannot take a deep breath cannot clear the lung. The decision to treat pain belongs to the provider authorized to make it, which means the paramedic under a protocol and the clinician in definitive hospital care. The substance, the amount, the route, and the timing are set by the local protocol and the prescriber, so no listener may take a prescription from this chapter. The lay first aider may offer position, quiet, warmth, and reassurance, and may not offer any medicine. The finding that makes the decision urgent is a patient who is splinting so strongly that breathing is shallow, whose cough is suppressed, or who is exhausted by pain, because that patient is on the way to retained secretions and pneumonia. In definitive care the options are wider and include methods that block the pain of several ribs at once, and those are hospital procedures rather than field techniques.

Position is a treatment in a patient with a chest injury, because it changes the work of breathing without any equipment at all. A conscious patient who is short of breath is usually most comfortable sitting up, sometimes leaning forward with the arms supported. A patient who is drowsy, who is in shock, or who has a suspected injury of the spine is not sat up, because the airway must be protected and the spine kept in the position found. The responding team decides position by watching the patient rather than by applying a rule, and it moves the patient gently because a twisted trunk adds pain and can worsen a fracture. A patient who becomes more breathless after being laid flat is returned to the position that helped, and a patient who cannot be made comfortable in any position is treated as a patient who is deteriorating.

Monitoring is a rhythm rather than a single act, and it begins at the first contact and continues through the handover. The responder repeats the sequence of airway, breathing, circulation, disability, and exposure, compares each set with the one before, and writes down what changed and when. The findings that mark a patient who is worsening are a rising breathing rate, increasing effort, shortening speech, a fast weak pulse, cold clammy skin, and pain no longer controlled by the measures in use. The responder also watches the injured side for swelling, for movement that is not symmetrical, and for a wound that begins to bubble or suck. A patient who has been drinking alcohol or who has taken a substance is watched more closely rather than less, because drowsiness has more than a single possible cause. Every interval and measured value used to judge the trend is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

The first thing never to do is to bind the chest tightly, because the bandage treats the appearance of the injury while it worsens the injury itself. A tight binding limits the movement of the whole cage, so the patient breathes with a smaller volume, secretions collect, areas of lung collapse, and pneumonia follows. It also hides the chest from the examiner, so a swelling on a single side, a segment moving the wrong way, or a wound that has begun to suck may go unseen. Every service that once taught strapping has abandoned it, and any request to bind a chest should be refused and explained. The correct alternative is position, warmth, calm, pain control by the provider authorized to give it, and the shortest safe journey to definitive care.

The second thing never to do is to dismiss an apparently uncomplicated rib fracture in an older patient, because age changes what a broken rib means. An older patient has less reserve in the lung, a weaker cough, and a chest wall that breaks more easily because the bone is thinner. An age of 65 years or more is often used as a warning line in chest injury, and that threshold is an illustration to be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, since services and editions differ on where the line is drawn. The danger is not the bone but the pneumonia that follows, because a patient who cannot cough clears nothing, and an older patient who cannot clear the lung can die of an infection that began as a small break. An older patient may also report less pain than the injury deserves, or may be unable to describe it because of confusion, so the responder trusts the mechanism and the examination. The finding that should keep the responder concerned is a breathing rate faster than expected, a cough that has become weak, or a new confusion.

Children and other vulnerable patients need the same suspicion for the opposite reason. A child's chest wall bends rather than breaks, so a child may have a bruised lung, a pneumothorax, or bleeding inside the chest with no fracture visible on an image, and the child is judged by the work of breathing and by the mechanism. A child also loses heat quickly and compensates for blood loss until the compensation fails suddenly, so observations are repeated more often and the journey to a trauma center is planned earlier. A pregnant patient is watched with the same care, because the delivery of oxygen to the fetus depends on the mother's breathing and circulation. A patient with a long-standing lung disease, a chest deformity, or frailty from another illness has less room to absorb a chest injury than a healthy adult of the same age. What these groups share is that a small fracture and a large consequence can arrive together.

The criteria for transport are written in the patient's breathing rather than in the appearance of the fracture, and the destination is chosen before the patient is moved. A patient with difficulty in breathing, a breathing rate outside the accepted range, a falling level of consciousness, more than a small number of broken ribs, or an injury to both sides of the chest goes to a facility that can provide imaging, monitoring, and a chest drain, usually a trauma center. A patient whose pain cannot be controlled, whose oxygen level remains low despite treatment, or who is older or frail is transported on the same reasoning. A patient who appears comfortable but has a violent mechanism, a break of the first rib near the great vessels, or a fracture of the lower ribs over the liver or the spleen is watched during the journey. Where the nearest facility cannot provide the needed care, the patient is stabilized there and transferred, and the receiving team is told what has been observed. A count of ribs, a pressure, a rate, or an age used in these decisions is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

Protection against hypothermia belongs to every patient with a chest injury, because a chest being examined is a chest being exposed. A patient who is undressed, who is lying on cold ground, or who is bleeding inside the chest loses heat quickly, and cold makes blood clot poorly, deepens shock, and makes a dangerous heart rhythm more likely. The responder uncovers only the part being examined, covers it again at once, insulates the patient from the ground, replaces wet clothing with dry covering, and keeps the vehicle warm. A temperature below 35 degrees Celsius is often named as the point at which cold itself becomes dangerous, and that number must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the threshold shifts with age and with the way the measurement is taken. The finding that warns the responder that cooling has begun is a patient who has stopped shivering while still cold, because shivering is the body's attempt to make heat.

Some complications of a rib fracture arrive hours or days later, which is why the patient sent home or admitted is given clear instructions about what to watch for. Pneumonia and the collapse of small areas of lung are the most common, and they begin with the shallow breathing that pain encourages. A pneumothorax can appear late, after a rib has moved or after a patient has been walking and talking comfortably, and a collection of blood can organize and cause infection if it is not drained. A fracture of the lower ribs may be followed by pain and tenderness in the abdomen, which raises the question of injury to the liver or the spleen. The patient and the family are told to seek care again for worsening breathlessness, fever, a cough that brings up blood, increasing pain, or a change in the level of consciousness. The finding that should never be explained away is a patient who was improving and has begun to worsen.

Definitive hospital care owns the diagnosis and the repair, and it begins with imaging that shows the bones, the lung, and the space around it. A chest radiograph is usually the first study, and computed tomography, after which CT is allowed, gives a fuller picture of the lung, the ribs, and any collection of air or blood. Ultrasound at the bedside is used in many centers to look for fluid around the lung, and magnetic resonance imaging, after which MRI is allowed, is not usually needed for this injury. The clinician controls pain by methods a paramedic cannot use in the field, drains air or blood from the chest, supports the breathing when the effort becomes too great, and operates when a torn vessel continues to bleed or the chest wall must be stabilized. An operation to fix the ribs with plates and screws belongs to the trauma surgeon in an operating room and is never a field procedure, while a patient who needs support of breathing is cared for in an ICU.

A constructed illustration, described as a construction and never as a real patient or incident, shows how these pieces fit together. A middle-aged driver is pulled from a car after a low-speed collision and complains of pain over the lower left ribs, and at first the breathing rate is normal and the patient talks in full sentences. Then the breathing becomes faster and shallower, the skin becomes pale, and the patient stops finishing sentences, while the chest shows only a bruise. The responder does not press the ribs, does not bind the chest, and does not wait for the patient to complain more, but calls early, keeps the patient sitting up and warm, and reports the trend. In hospital the imaging shows a small pneumothorax and a bruised lung, and the patient is monitored and treated for pain before being admitted for observation.

The record and the handover are part of the treatment, because the receiving team continues a story that the field began. What is reported includes the mechanism of injury and its time, the number of patients, the breathing rate and effort, the level of consciousness and any change in it, the color and warmth of the skin, and the treatment given with the patient's response. The times matter as much as the acts, and a change that was observed is reported even when it has resolved, because a patient who improved and then worsened is not the same patient as one who never changed. The responder states plainly what was not done and why, so the receiving team does not assume a measure was attempted or omit one the field could not give. A handover that names the mechanism, the trend, and the provider level of each act allows the hospital to plan rather than to start the assessment from nothing.

A chapter can explain the reasoning, but it cannot certify a reader, and the difference matters when a listener becomes a responder. The skills described here are learned with a teacher, practiced on models, and authorized by a service that defines what may be done without supervision. Where this chapter names a number or a threshold, it does so to explain a decision and not to set a rule, because such numbers differ between services, countries, and editions of the same guideline. No one should take from these pages a substance, an amount, a route, or a frequency, and no one should read a hospital procedure as something a first aider may attempt. This is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country where you work.

To summarize, a rib fracture is judged by the breathing and by the lung beneath it rather than by the bone. The scene is made safe and the lethal possibility is named early, and the finding that reveals it is a patient who is breathing fast and shallow, who cannot finish a sentence, or whose level of consciousness is dulling. The anatomy explains the risk, since the upper ribs lie over the great vessels and the lower ribs over the liver, the spleen, and the kidneys, while the whole cage is the mechanism of breathing itself. Simple fractures, multiple fractures, flail chest, pulmonary contusion, pneumothorax, and hemothorax arrive in that order of severity, and any of them can worsen over the hours after the injury, so the trend is watched rather than the moment. Management follows the primary survey, so breathing is assessed in the order of ABCDE, pain and oxygen are decided by the provider authorized to decide them, position is chosen by what eases the patient's breathing, monitoring continues to the handover, and transport goes to a facility that can image, drain, and support the chest. The chest is never bound tightly, and an apparently simple fracture in an older patient is never dismissed, because age removes the reserve that hides the complication. Hypothermia is prevented whenever the chest is exposed, the provider level is named for every act, and no dose, route, or frequency is taken from this book. Every number and threshold here is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country where you work.
