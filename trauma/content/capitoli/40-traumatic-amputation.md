# Chapter 40 — Traumatic Amputation

Traumatic amputation is the injury that removes a part of the body in an instant and, with it, a volume of blood that no wound of the same size would ever lose. The part may be completely severed and lying on the ground, or it may hang by a bridge of skin, muscle, tendon, or nerve, and the second form is often the more dangerous because the responder sees a limb that still looks attached and underestimates the hemorrhage beneath it. The threat that kills is not the loss of the limb but the loss of blood, and the loss of blood is the first thing controlled and the last thing forgotten. This chapter follows the patient from the moment of injury through the control of bleeding, the protection of the wound, the recognition of shock, the preservation of the amputated tissue, and the journey to the hospital where the decision about replantation is made. Every act is named with the provider level that owns it, because a lay first aider, a trained responder, and a trauma surgeon each have a different and limited part to play. This is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country where you work.

Scene safety comes before the patient, and the scene of an amputation is usually a scene of machinery, traffic, or violence rather than a quiet room. A machine that has taken a limb may still be running or may still be holding the patient, and it is stopped and made safe by a person authorized to control it before anyone reaches inside, because a rescuer who is pulled into the same machine becomes a second casualty. Traffic on a road is held before the patient is approached, live electrical equipment is isolated before it is touched, and a structure that has fallen on a limb is lifted only by those with the equipment and the authority to do it. The patient's own blood is a hazard as well, because it carries infection, so gloves, eye protection, and a barrier for the mouth are worn for every patient rather than only for the ones who look infectious. A responder who is splashed washes the skin at once, and a responder whose glove tears replaces it before continuing. The finding that tells the responder that the scene is not yet safe is any hazard that cannot be named, controlled, or handed over to someone who owns it, and until that moment the correct act is to wait at the edge and to keep others back.

The mechanism of injury shapes the bleeding, the contamination, and the chance that the amputated part can be rejoined, so it is gathered before the wound is judged. A clean cut, as from a sharp tool or a sheet of metal, divides the vessels and the nerves in one plane, and such a part has the best prospect of replantation because the tissue between the cut ends is intact. A crushing amputation, as from a heavy wheel, a press, or a closing door, tears the vessels and bruises a length of tissue above the cut, and the part may be so damaged that rejoining is impossible even though the bleeding is no less dangerous. An avulsion, in which the part is pulled away and hangs by a bridge of skin or tendon, is often accompanied by a stripping of vessels and nerves far above the visible injury, and this hidden damage is one reason the part must be handled gently. A blast may remove several parts at once, contaminate them with foreign material, and injure the chest, the abdomen, and the other limbs, so the amputation becomes one problem among many. The mechanism also predicts the injuries that accompany the amputation, and the responder who reads it looks for the fracture above the stump, the wound of the opposite limb, and the head injury that the patient cannot report. The name of the mechanism, the time it happened, and the way the part was handled are all part of the history that the receiving surgeon will need.

The lethal possibility of a traumatic amputation is exsanguinating hemorrhage, and the finding that reveals it is bleeding that continues despite a dressing, blood that soaks pad after pad, or a swelling of the stump that grows while the responder watches. A major amputation of an arm or a leg can empty the circulation in minutes, and the danger is greatest when the division is close to the trunk, because the vessels there are large and cannot be compressed against a single bone. A torn artery often retracts into the tissue and spasms, so the stump may bleed less than expected at first, and that pause is deceptive because the vessel can open again when the pressure of the circulation rises or when a dressing is lifted. Blood also collects inside the soft tissue of the stump and in the compartments above it, where the amount lost is invisible and the swelling is the only sign. The patient tells the same story in the language of the body, with a level of consciousness that is dulling, a pulse that is fast and thready, breathing that is rapid, skin that is pale and cool, and a restlessness or a thirst that appears before the collapse. The responder does not wait for the wound to declare itself, because a stump that is not bleeding at this moment may be bleeding inside the thigh or under the dressing, and the safest assumption is that an amputation is a hemorrhage until it is controlled and observed.

The first act after scene safety is the control of hemorrhage, and it follows an order that a lay first aider can begin without any equipment. A clean dressing, a folded cloth, or any clean fabric is placed over the bleeding point on the stump, and the flat of the hand presses firmly and without interruption while help is summoned. The pad is never lifted to see whether the bleeding has stopped, because each look breaks the clot that is forming, and soaked pads are left in place and covered with fresh layers. Pressure on a stump is directed at the bleeding point rather than spread across the whole end, and fingertips or the heel of the hand can be concentrated where the blood is coming from when a broad pad does not stop it. For an amputation at the groin, the shoulder, or the base of the neck, where a tourniquet cannot be placed, the trained responder packs the wound firmly to its depth and holds pressure over the packing, because a dressing across the surface cannot press on a vessel that lies deep. The lay first aider does not probe the wound, does not pour anything into it, and does not try to find the vessel, because those acts add contamination and may release a clot. A period such as 10 minutes of uninterrupted pressure is a common teaching for judging whether a wound will stop, but that figure is an illustration which must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the time depends on the size of the vessel and on the patient's clotting.

When pressure and packing do not stop the bleeding from a limb, the tourniquet is the next step, and it is applied promptly rather than as a last resort after the patient has already lost the blood that a tourniquet could have saved. The tourniquet is a device of the trained responder or the paramedic, carried under a protocol and used within the training that authorizes it, and the lay first aider applies a manufactured tourniquet only when emergency medical services instruct it and the responder has been taught to use it. It is placed between the wound and the heart, as close to the wound as the remaining anatomy allows and away from a joint where a length of limb permits, and it is tightened until the bleeding stops rather than until a set number of turns has been made. A device tightened over a limb without stopping the flow adds pain and tissue damage while the patient continues to bleed, so the finding that decides the tightness is the cessation of bleeding and not the appearance of the band. Once the tourniquet has controlled the bleeding, it is not loosened in the field, because the clot that has formed behind it may not hold and hemorrhage may restart where no one is prepared to control it. The time of application is written on the patient near the tourniquet, on a tape fixed to the limb, or wherever the local protocol requires, and it is spoken aloud at handover, because the receiving team must know how long the limb has been without flow. An interval such as 2 hours is often quoted as the point at which the risk to the limb becomes serious, and that number is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since tolerance differs with the temperature, the patient, and the injury.

A partial amputation, in which the part is held by a bridge of skin, muscle, tendon, or nerve, must never be completed in the field, and the reason is that the bridge may contain vessels and nerves that keep the part alive and that a surgeon may be able to repair. The instinct to pull the part free, to straighten it, or to tidy the wound is wrong, and it can convert a limb that might be saved into a limb that cannot. The bridge is supported in the position found, with padding beneath it and a bandage that holds it without tension, and the part is never used as a handle for moving the patient. A deformed segment above the amputation is not straightened to make the limb look better, because movement can release a clot, tear a vessel that is still intact, or injure a nerve, and the limb is splinted in the position found. An object embedded in the stump or in the remaining limb is left where it lies and is stabilized with padding built up around it, because removal may cause hemorrhage that cannot be controlled and may damage a structure that is still whole. The finding that matters after a partial amputation is whether the part is warm, has a color close to the rest of the limb, and has any feeling or movement, and that finding is recorded and passed on rather than acted upon in the field. Definitive hospital care owns the decision to complete the amputation, to repair the vessels, or to attempt replantation, and no field technique replaces that assessment.

The stump is a wound like any other and is protected from further contamination while bleeding is controlled, but the protection is simple and does not become a procedure. The cleanest material available is laid over the wound, and where the service carries it, a sterile dressing moistened with sterile saline is used for exposed bone, tendon, or nerve so that the tissue does not dry, and this choice follows the local protocol and the equipment actually present. The dressing is not packed with soil, leaves, or cloth that has been on the ground, and no antiseptic, alcohol, or powder is poured into the wound, because those agents damage tissue and belong to no field decision. The responder wears gloves and eye protection, avoids touching the wound with bare hands, and does not breathe or cough over it, since contamination at this moment is measured in organisms that no field cleaning will remove. A dressing that becomes soaked through is covered with a fresh layer rather than replaced, and a dressing that has been placed is left until bleeding is controlled and the patient is ready to move. The decision about cleaning the wound, about removing dead tissue, and about protection against tetanus belongs to definitive hospital care and to the provider authorized to make it under the local protocol, because the substance and the timing are not decisions that can be taken from a manual. The finding that makes the tetanus question urgent is a wound that is deep, contaminated, or caused by machinery or soil, and that finding is reported rather than acted upon by the first responder.

The preservation of the amputated part is a short sequence whose purpose is to slow the death of tissue without freezing or poisoning it, and the correct order is wrap, seal, cool, and label. The part is recovered as soon as the patient's bleeding is controlled and the scene is safe, and the search never delays the transport of a patient who is bleeding or in shock, because the life comes before the limb. It is first wrapped in clean or sterile gauze, and the gauze is slightly moistened with sterile saline where that is available, because a dry dressing draws moisture from the tissue while a soaking wet one softens it. The wrapped part is then placed inside a clean plastic bag or a sealed container, and that sealed package is placed inside a second container holding ice and water, so that the cold reaches the tissue through the bag and never touches it directly. The part is never placed directly on ice, never buried in ice water, never packed in dry ice, and never immersed in water alone, because direct freezing forms crystals that destroy cells, while plain water swells and softens the tissue until it cannot be used. It is never placed in alcohol, in formalin, in bleach, or in any fixative or disinfectant, because those substances destroy the very cells that a surgeon would need to rejoin, and a part preserved in them must be discarded. A temperature near 4 degrees Celsius is often described as suitable for transport, and that figure is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the aim is cool and not frozen and the safe range is set by the receiving service. The finding that tells the responder the cooling is right is a part that feels cold but is not hard, and a part that has frozen is reported to the receiving team rather than warmed by the responder.

Labeling is what keeps the part with its patient, and it matters because a part that loses its identity cannot be used however well it was preserved. The bag or container is marked with the patient's name or identifying number, the time of the injury, the time the part was wrapped and cooled, and any treatment that was applied to it, and the same details are written in the patient's record and spoken at handover. The part travels with the patient in the same vehicle whenever that is possible, and it is handed directly to the receiving team rather than left in the ambulance, placed in a locker, or given to a relative. It is never sent ahead on a separate journey unless the local protocol and the receiving facility have arranged it, because a part that arrives before the patient is easily separated from the patient's identity and a part that arrives after a delay loses the time that matters. When the part cannot accompany the patient, the reason is documented and the receiving team is told where it is and who has it. The written record is part of the treatment rather than an administrative afterthought, and the finding that makes it urgent is any part that has been cooled, because the clock on the tissue is running and the receiving team must know when it started.

Shock is the state in which the circulation can no longer deliver enough oxygen to the tissues, and after a major amputation it is the expected companion of the blood that has been lost rather than a complication that might happen. The early patient compensates with a fast pulse, cool hands, and a blood pressure that still looks acceptable, and that apparent stability is the most dangerous moment because the loss is already large. The patient who can no longer compensate becomes restless, then confused, then drowsy, with rapid shallow breathing and a pulse that becomes weak, and by that stage the remaining time is short. The responder's own contribution to shock is to stop the bleeding, to keep the patient warm, to keep the patient flat and still unless a head or chest injury forbids it, and to shorten the journey to the place where blood and surgery are available. Fluid replacement, the choice of what is given, and the decision to give blood products belong to the paramedic under a protocol and to definitive hospital care, and they are decisions about a patient and a system rather than instructions that can be carried away from a manual, so nothing here names a substance, an amount, a route, or a frequency. The tourniquet is not loosened to find out whether the bleeding has stopped or to let the limb breathe, because that act can restart a hemorrhage in a patient who is already short of blood. The findings that are recorded at every reassessment are the level of consciousness, the pulse, the breathing, the color and warmth of the skin, and the amount of blood on the dressings, and the trend over time is worth more than any single observation.

Hypothermia travels with amputation as it travels with every severe injury, because the patient has bled, has often been undressed to expose the wound, and is lying on the ground while the responder works. Cold makes the clotting system work less well, deepens shock, and makes the heart more likely to develop a dangerous rhythm, so the patient who is cold bleeds more and the patient who bleeds more becomes colder. The protection begins at the same moment as the bleeding control rather than after it, and it means removing wet clothing, covering the patient with blankets, coats, or foil, insulating the patient from the cold ground, and keeping the vehicle or the room warm. The stump itself is covered and the rest of the patient is covered with it, and the amputated part is cooled while the patient is warmed, because the need of the part and the need of the patient are opposite, and each belongs to its own object. A body temperature below 35 degrees Celsius is the figure often used to describe the point at which cold becomes dangerous, and that number is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the accepted line differs between services and editions. The finding that warns the responder that cooling has already begun is a patient who has stopped shivering while still cold, because shivering is the body's attempt to make heat. The patient who is warm on arrival at the hospital is a patient whose blood clots and whose heart is more stable, and that advantage is bought in the field with coverings and insulation.

Packaging and transport are decided together, because the destination must be chosen for the injury and the journey must be made without adding to it. The stump is covered with a secure dressing, the limb is supported and immobilized so that the unsupported weight does not pull on the wound, and the patient is moved in a way that keeps the stump still and does not worsen the bleeding or the breathing. The amputated part is packaged, cooled, labeled, and kept in reach of the person who will hand the patient over, because the part and the patient must arrive together, and a part left in the footwell is a part that has been lost. The receiving facility is chosen for its ability to assess replantation and to provide surgery, which may be a trauma center with microsurgery rather than the nearest hospital, and the choice follows the local protocol and the advice of the medical controller. Early notification lets the receiving team call in the surgical and nursing staff, prepare the operating room, and arrange the tests that the decision about replantation requires, and that notice costs nothing and changes the outcome. Transport is urgent and gentle at the same time, because a patient thrown about on the way adds injuries and pain, and because a patient who arrives worse has gained nothing from the speed. A part that is not found, a tourniquet whose time is uncertain, or a stump whose bleeding has restarted are all spoken aloud at the beginning of the handover rather than left for the receiving team to discover.

The decision about replantation, which is the rejoining of the amputated part, belongs to the hospital, to the surgeon, and to the microsurgical team, and it is made with information that the field can only gather and preserve. The factors that enter the decision include the level of the amputation, whether the division was sharp or crushing, the amount of warm time the part has spent without blood, the age and general condition of the patient, the presence of other injuries, and the contamination of the tissue. A sharply divided thumb or finger in a young and otherwise healthy patient is the classic candidate, while a crushed and contaminated limb in a patient who is in shock from other injuries may be better served by completion of the amputation, and only the surgical team can weigh those matters. The field provider's task is to keep every option open by controlling the bleeding, protecting the wound, preserving the part, labeling it, and recording the times, and not to decide which option will be chosen. Warm ischemia, which is the time the part spends at body temperature before cooling, matters as much as the cooling itself, and a period such as 6 hours of warm ischemia is often discussed, though that number is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol because tolerance differs with the tissue and the patient. The responder must not promise the patient or the family that the part will be rejoined, because that promise is not the responder's to make and the disappointment of a broken promise falls on people who are already frightened. Definitive hospital care also owns the exploration of the stump, the removal of dead tissue, the repair of vessels and nerves, the management of the pressure inside the limb, and the operations that may follow, and none of those is a field procedure no matter how urgent the patient appears.

The scope of practice for this injury is unusually clear, and each level owns a different part of the work. The lay first aider may press firmly on the bleeding point, cover the stump with the cleanest material available, keep the patient warm and still, recover the amputated part when it is safe to do so, and send for help, and may not place a tourniquet without instruction and training, may not probe or clean the wound, and may not remove an embedded object. The trained responder and the paramedic may apply a tourniquet under a protocol, pack a junctional wound, dress the stump, splint and immobilize the limb, monitor the patient, and preserve and transport the part, all within the training and the authorization of the service. Definitive hospital care owns the control of bleeding that no field method can reach, the cleaning and exploration of the wound, the removal of dead tissue, the repair of vessels, tendons, and nerves, the relief of pressure inside a swollen limb, the completion of an amputation, and the attempt at replantation. No responder should attempt to reopen the wound to find a vessel, to straighten a deformed limb, to complete a partial amputation, or to warm a frozen part, because each of those acts can destroy the possibility that the patient was carried to the hospital to preserve. The rule about the tourniquet is repeated because it is the rule most often broken: once it has controlled the bleeding, it is not loosened in the field, and its time is recorded where the receiving team cannot miss it. The reader is asked to bring these principles to training and to work within the protocol that governs the service, because a manual can describe decisions but cannot certify competence.

To summarize, a traumatic amputation kills by hemorrhage before it disables by the loss of the part, so the first act after the scene is made safe is the control of bleeding, and the tourniquet is applied promptly, tightened until the bleeding stops, never loosened in the field, and recorded with its time where the receiving team will see it. The stump is covered and protected from contamination without probing, without pouring anything into it, and without removing an embedded object, and the wound belongs to definitive care for cleaning, exploration, and repair. Shock and hypothermia travel with the injury, so warmth, insulation, flat positioning, and continuous reassessment are part of hemorrhage control and not additions to it, while fluids, blood products, and pain relief are decisions of the authorized provider and the local protocol. The amputated part is recovered when it is safe, wrapped in clean or slightly moistened gauze, sealed, cooled indirectly in ice and water, never placed directly on ice and never put in water, alcohol, formalin, or any fixative, and it is labeled with the patient's identity and the times and kept with the patient. Partial amputations are never completed in the field, deformed limbs are never straightened to look normal, and the decision about replantation is made by the surgical team with the information the field preserved. Every number, time, temperature, and interval in this chapter is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country where you work.
