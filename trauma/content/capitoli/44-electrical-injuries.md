# Chapter 44 — Electrical Injuries

Electrical injury is a systemic event that enters through a wound too small to explain it, and the responder who judges the damage by the skin will underestimate it every time. The current follows the tissues that conduct best, which are nerve, muscle, and blood vessel, so a small charred mark may sit above muscle that has been cooked and killed, and the path from the entry point to the exit point decides which organs are at risk. Scene safety is therefore the first clinical act of this chapter, because the wire, the rail, or the appliance that injured the patient may still be energized and may injure the next person who touches it. The lethal possibilities are named early, which are an ineffective rhythm of the heart, a breathing drive that has been paralyzed, deep muscle destruction that poisons the body hours later, and the injuries of a fall from height. Every act is named with the provider level that owns it, whether the lay first aider, the trained responder or paramedic, or definitive hospital care, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

The first act at any scene where electricity may be involved is to stop the current, and that act belongs to a person authorized by the utility or by the emergency service to operate the isolation point. Until the isolation is confirmed by the person who performed it, every wire, rail, puddle, and appliance is treated as live, and no hand is laid on a patient who is still in contact with the source. A downed conductor kills without being touched, because the ground near its broken end carries a difference in potential across each step, so the safe approach keeps the feet together and does not lift the soles. A separation such as 10 meters is a figure that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the safe distance grows with the voltage. A vehicle resting against a live wire is a trap in which the patient may be safest while seated inside, and the rescuer who pulls the patient out may complete a circuit that the tires had insulated. The trained responder or paramedic may hold the scene, request the isolation, and use insulated tools where the service authorizes them, while the lay first aider keeps others back, calls for help from a safe place, and covers the hands, the eyes, and the mouth with whatever barrier is at hand. The finding that permits the rescue to begin is a clear statement that the supply is isolated, and anything less leaves the scene unsafe.

Electricity injures by passing through the body, and what it damages depends on the path it takes, the current it carries, the time it lasts, and the resistance of the tissue it meets. Dry skin resists the current and may be burned where it enters, while wet skin conducts far more readily and can leave a smaller mark for a larger internal injury, which is why a patient found in water may be more damaged than the wound suggests. Alternating current at household frequency can hold a victim against the source with a sustained muscular contraction, so the exposure lasts longer than the patient intended and the hand cannot open. Direct current, as from a battery bank or a lightning strike, tends to throw the patient clear, and that separation is why a lightning victim may have no burn at all and still be in cardiac arrest. Nerve, muscle, and blood vessel carry current well and are heated by it, while bone and fat resist and may be spared while the structures beside them are destroyed, so the visible wound is a poor measure of the injury.

Electrical injuries differ in kind, and the kind decides the urgency. A low-voltage injury in the home, from a socket, a cord, or a small appliance, may cause only a tingling sensation and a small burn, and yet it can also produce a rhythm disturbance that appears later, so a minor wound does not make the patient safe. A high-voltage injury from an overhead line, a substation, a railway supply, or industrial equipment destroys tissue along the whole path of the current, and the destruction is often deep and progressive rather than confined to the skin. An arc injury occurs when current jumps an air gap without the patient becoming part of the circuit, and it delivers an intense flash of heat that burns exposed skin and ignites clothing, so the patient may suffer a flash burn, a flame burn, and the blunt injury of a blast at once. A lightning strike is a brief and enormous current that may stop the heart and the breathing at once, and the patient who is revived may remain confused, weak, or unable to move the limbs for a time, so every mechanism must be identified from the scene and reported to the receiving team.

The heart fails first and most quietly, and the rhythm it develops is the reason this chapter insists on monitoring rather than on the wound. Current through the chest can throw the heart into an ineffective rhythm in which it quivers instead of pumping, into beats that arise from the wrong part of the heart, or into a complete standstill, and any of these may occur at the moment of the shock or hours afterwards. The finding at the scene may be no more than a patient who felt faint and sat down, and the danger is that such a patient is allowed to walk, to drive, or to wait unobserved. A patient who is unresponsive and not breathing normally is treated as being in cardiac arrest, and cardiopulmonary resuscitation, or CPR, begins as soon as the scene is safe and the patient is separated from the source. The trained responder or paramedic applies a cardiac monitor and keeps it attached during transport, because a rhythm that is normal on arrival may deteriorate later and a monitored patient can be treated before the deterioration becomes irreversible. Definitive hospital care decides how long the patient must be observed and whether an intensive care unit is required, and that decision rests on the rhythm, on the path of the current, and on the patient's history rather than on the size of the burn.

Breathing fails for two immediate reasons, and both are answered by the primary survey. The current can paralyze the muscles of respiration so that the chest does not move, and it can also cause a sustained contraction of the hand or the arm that holds the patient on the source, so the exposure continues until the current is stopped or a muscle tears. A patient who is not breathing but whose heart is still beating will suffer a cardiac arrest within minutes unless the breathing is supported, so the airway and the breathing hold their place at the front of the primary survey. The trained responder opens the airway, gives oxygen where the protocol provides it, and assists the breathing with the equipment the service authorizes, while the lay first aider supports the head, checks that the chest is rising, and calls for help without delay. A patient who has been thrown clear may have injured the neck and the spine, so the head and the neck are held in the position found and the patient is not sat up to make breathing easier, while the finding that tells the responder the breathing is failing is a chest that does not rise or lips and fingertips that have turned blue.

Nerves conduct current better than any other tissue and are often injured without any external sign. The patient may be confused, agitated, or drowsy, and the level of consciousness is a more useful measurement than the wound because it registers the effect of the current on the brain. A seizure may occur at the moment of the shock, and the patient who has seized is protected from injuring the head and the limbs while the seizure runs its course, since nothing is placed in the mouth. Weakness, numbness, or an inability to move a limb may recover over hours or may persist, and the finding must be recorded and handed over rather than interpreted in the field, while a lightning strike may leave a temporary paralysis of the limbs with consciousness preserved that is mistakenly treated as harmless. Definitive hospital care examines the nerves and the spinal cord, and imaging such as computed tomography, or CT, or magnetic resonance imaging, or MRI, may be used where the clinician judges it necessary, and none of that assessment can be performed at the roadside.

The electricity is only one of the mechanisms acting on the patient, and the fall or the blast that follows the shock may be more immediately lethal than the current itself. In a constructed illustration, a worker thrown from a ladder or a person blown from a transformer platform may have a fractured spine, a fractured pelvis, a broken thigh, a head injury, or bleeding inside the abdomen, and none of those injuries is visible in the burn mark. The spinal column is presumed to be injured in any patient who has fallen from a height, so the head and the neck are maintained in the position found and the patient is moved only with the technique the responder has been trained to use. A fractured pelvis may bleed into the space behind the peritoneum and kill without any external wound, and the responder must not press on the pelvis or spring it in order to test for pain, because pelvic pain, an inability to move a leg, or a difference in the length of the legs already moves the patient toward definitive care. Every figure in this chapter, whether a voltage, a distance, a time, or a temperature, is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such numbers differ between services, countries, and editions.

The burn at the point of contact is the mark that most responders see and the one that most misleads them. An entry wound appears where the current enters the body, and it is often small, round, and charred, with a pale rim and a depressed center, and it may be painless because the nerves in the skin have been destroyed. An exit wound appears where the current leaves the body, and it may be larger and more ragged than the entry because the current has spread before leaving, and the tissue around it may be cooked and dry. The current does not travel in a straight line, so the skin between the two marks may look entirely normal while the muscle, the nerve, and the vessel beneath are destroyed, and a patient may have more than one exit point because current can leave through both feet or through a hand and a foot. The burn is covered with a clean dry dressing, the patient is kept warm, and the wound is handed over as one fact among many rather than as the whole injury.

Muscle is injured in almost every electrical injury of consequence, and the damage may lie far from the burn. Muscle that has been heated by current breaks down and releases its contents into the bloodstream, and the pigment that carries oxygen within the muscle, myoglobin, travels to the kidneys and can damage them, a condition called rhabdomyolysis. The findings that suggest this deep destruction are muscle pain and tenderness out of proportion to the wound, a limb that is swollen and tight, weakness, and urine that has become dark, brown, or the color of tea. The trained responder watches for those findings, keeps the patient warm and still, and moves the patient toward a hospital that can measure the products of muscle breakdown in the blood and support the kidneys, because the poisoning of the body by its own injured muscle is a hospital problem rather than a field one. Fluid replacement belongs to the paramedic under a protocol and to definitive hospital care, and the substance, the amount, the route, and the timing are decisions made by the prescriber and the local guideline, so nothing in this chapter names them. The swelling of a limb can also raise the pressure within a closed compartment of muscle and produce compartment syndrome, and the finding that warns of it is pain that grows when the muscle is stretched, a limb that is tense, and numbness beyond the injury.

A low-voltage injury deserves attention even when the patient looks well, because household current can disturb the heart and can injure a small body out of proportion to its size. A child who has bitten or sucked on the end of an extension cord may have a burn at the corner of the mouth, and the danger is not only the burn but the delayed bleeding that can occur days later when the scab separates from a vessel in the lip, which is one reason every such child must be assessed in hospital rather than at home. A child has a smaller body and a lower resistance than an adult, so a current that would only startle a grown person can produce a serious injury in a child, and the same reasoning applies to a patient whose skin is wet. An older patient with an existing heart condition may develop a dangerous rhythm from a shock that leaves no mark at all, and the finding that makes that patient urgent is any racing or irregular heartbeat, any fainting, or any chest discomfort. The lay first aider does not decide who may stay at home and who must be seen, because that decision belongs to the clinician who can examine and monitor the patient, and every patient with an electrical contact, however minor the burn, is offered assessment on the basis of the mechanism rather than the appearance.

The most difficult patient in this chapter is the one who is awake, talking, and apparently unharmed. Electrical current can produce a delayed rhythm disturbance, a delayed loss of consciousness, a delayed swelling of a limb, or a delayed failure of the kidneys, and none of those can be excluded by a normal examination at the scene. The safe practice is to treat any patient with a documented electrical contact as a patient who requires monitoring, and the trained responder or paramedic applies a cardiac monitor and arranges transport to a facility that can continue that monitoring. The lay first aider keeps the patient still, warm, and observed, and prevents the patient from walking away or driving, since exertion can worsen a heart that has been disturbed, while a normal pulse at a single moment is never accepted as reassuring because the heart can change within minutes. The decision about how long the patient must be watched belongs to definitive hospital care and to the local protocol, and a patient who refuses assessment after a significant exposure is a patient for whom the receiving physician and that protocol decide what is safe.

Resuscitation of the electrical patient follows the same order as for any other cause of cardiac arrest, but two ideas belong especially to this chapter. The first is that the patient must be separated from the source and the scene made safe before any hand touches the body, because a rescuer who is electrocuted cannot help anyone and becomes a second patient. The second is that a patient struck by lightning may have a heart that can restart with prompt support while the breathing remains paralyzed, so effective CPR may be rewarded with survival even when the arrest appears prolonged, and the decision to continue belongs to the responder's protocol and to the clinician directing the resuscitation. The trained responder or paramedic follows the current resuscitation guideline of the service, and the lay first aider provides compressions when trained to do so and follows the dispatcher's instructions where the system provides them. When several patients are struck by the same lightning flash, the usual order of triage is reversed, and the patients who appear dead are treated first because they may be revived, while those who are awake and calling out are moved to safety and reassessed afterwards, since a brief loss of consciousness and the ability to speak do not exclude a later rhythm disturbance. The finding that ends resuscitation is set by the local protocol and by the clinician in charge rather than by the responder alone, and the reversal belongs to lightning incidents and must not be transferred to other multiple casualty scenes.

Cold is a complication of electrical injury rather than a detail of comfort, and it can be the factor that turns a disturbed heart into a stopped one. The patient may have wet clothing from rain or from immersion, may have had clothing burned or cut away, may be lying on cold ground, and may be unable to shiver because the current has injured the muscles or the nerves that produce shivering. Cold makes the heart more irritable, reduces the effectiveness of resuscitation, and worsens the tissue damage of any associated injury, so warmth is provided from the first moment of care. The responder removes wet clothing where that can be done without exposing the patient for longer than necessary, covers the patient with dry blankets, foil, or clothing, insulates the patient from the ground, and keeps the vehicle or the room warm during transport. A body temperature such as 35 degrees Celsius is often named as the line below which cold itself becomes dangerous, and that threshold must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the accepted figure varies with the patient and with the way the temperature is measured.

The handover of an electrically injured patient carries facts that the receiving team cannot reconstruct from the wound. The responder reports what the source was, whether a household supply, an overhead line, a railway conductor, an arc, or lightning, and whether the patient was in contact with it or was thrown by it. The duration of the contact is reported where it is known, since a sustained grip on a live conductor produces a far greater injury than a brief brush, and the entry and exit points are described by their position on the body because the path between them predicts which organs were exposed. The time of any loss of consciousness, any seizure, any arrest, and the start of CPR are recorded, together with the rhythm seen on the monitor and the interventions performed, because the receiving team continues a story that the field began. Any fall from height is described with the height and the surface, even approximately, and the record is part of the treatment, because a mechanism that is not passed on will be investigated from the beginning by a team that has lost time.

Definitive hospital care owns everything that an electrical injury requires beyond support, and knowing where that line lies is part of competence for every responder. The hospital examines the path of the current with imaging, measures the products of muscle breakdown in the blood, watches the rhythm on a monitor, and supports the kidneys when they are threatened, and none of that can be done in the field. Surgical care removes dead muscle and dead skin, releases the pressure inside a swollen compartment, repairs or diverts the vessels that the current has destroyed, and sometimes amputates a limb that cannot be saved, and each of those is an operation rather than a dressing. The burn at the entry and exit points is cleaned, protected, and dressed, and the question of protection against tetanus, like the question of antibiotics, is answered by the provider authorized to make that decision under the local protocol, because the substance and the amount belong to the prescriber and not to this manual. A patient with a burn that is deep, contaminated, or extensive is assessed by a burns service, and the transfer to such a service is arranged by the hospital, while the responder's contribution is to protect the wound, to keep the patient warm, to monitor, and to deliver the patient to the right place with the mechanism clearly stated.

Two groups deserve a word of their own because their care differs from the usual. A pregnant patient who suffers an electrical injury is assessed as two patients, since the current can affect the fetus as well as the mother, and the decision about monitoring and about the well-being of the fetus belongs to definitive hospital care with the obstetric team involved. A patient with a pacemaker or an implanted cardiac defibrillator is at risk that a current passing through the chest will damage the device or the leads that connect it to the heart, and the finding that should be sought is any change in the patient's usual rhythm or a device that has begun to alarm, though the responder does not attempt to test the device. The responder's task in both cases is unchanged, which is to make the scene safe, to support the airway and the breathing, to monitor the heart, and to reach a hospital that can examine the patient properly. The device is described at handover, including what the patient says it is and where it was implanted, and where the patient cannot describe it, a relative or a medical alert item may carry the information.

The care of the electrical burn itself is similar to the care of any other burn, and the exception is the injury beneath it. The wound is covered with a clean, dry, non-adherent dressing where one is available, because a dressing that sticks tears the tissue when it is removed and opens a route for infection. Ice is not placed on the burn, oily preparations are not applied, and blisters are not opened, because each of those acts damages tissue that is already compromised. The limb is kept in the position found, the patient is kept warm, and the wound is not packed in a way that adds pressure, since the tissue within is already swollen, while the lay first aider covers the burn with whatever clean material is at hand and does not attempt to clean or inspect the deep wound. The trained responder follows the protocol of the local burns service for the covering, and the finding that should be reported is a limb that has become more swollen, more tense, or more painful during the journey, because that suggests the pressure inside the limb is rising and that the surgeon must be told before the limb is lost.

Reassessment after an electrical injury is not a single examination performed at the scene but a rhythm that continues to the handover. The responder returns to the airway, the breathing, and the circulation, and then to the level of consciousness, the pupils, and the movement of the limbs, because a patient who was alert at the scene and is drowsy in the ambulance has told the responder more than the burn can. The findings that mark deterioration are a falling level of consciousness, a breathing pattern that has become faster, slower, or noisy, a pulse that has become rapid and weak, skin that has become cold or clammy, and a limb that has become tense and painful, and these are compared with the previous set rather than judged alone. The monitor is watched continuously where it is attached, and any change in the rhythm is reported at once, while the patient is also watched for the signs of shock, which may come from the deep tissue injury, from an associated fracture, or from bleeding that the electricity concealed. The destination is chosen before the patient is moved, and for this injury it is the nearest facility that can monitor the heart and measure the products of muscle breakdown, because transport must be urgent and gentle when the patient may have a spinal injury, a fracture, or an unstable rhythm.

To summarize, electrical injury is a systemic event whose visible wound is a poor measure of its damage, and the first act is always to stop the current and to treat the source as live until an authorized person confirms that it is isolated. The current follows the tissues that conduct best, so the burn at the entry and the exit may be small while the muscle, the nerve, and the vessel between them are destroyed, and the pathway predicts the organs at risk. The heart is monitored because an arrhythmia may appear at once or hours later, the breathing is supported because the current can paralyze it, and the level of consciousness, the spine, and the pelvis are assessed because the fall that follows the shock may be the more lethal injury. Deep muscle destruction releases myoglobin that threatens the kidneys, and the findings of pain, swelling, weakness, and dark urine move the patient toward a hospital that can measure and treat it, while fluids, analgesia, and every substance remain the decisions of the prescriber and the protocol. The lay first aider keeps others from the source, covers the burn, keeps the patient warm and still, and calls for help, and the trained responder or paramedic monitors the heart, supports the airway and breathing, and transports, while definitive hospital care images, operates, and supports the organs that fail. Hypothermia travels with electrical injury, the mechanism and the times are recorded and handed over, the patient who looks well is still a patient who must be watched, and every figure in this chapter is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.
