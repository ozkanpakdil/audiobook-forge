# Chapter 22 — Humerus Fractures

The responder who reaches a patient with a suspected broken upper arm arrives at a scene first and at a fracture second. Traffic is held, power is isolated by an authorized person, and fire, water, unstable structures, animals, and violence are left to those trained to control them. Personal protective equipment, or PPE, is worn for every patient, because blood carries infection through broken skin or an eye splash. The lethal possibility to name first is the injury that came with the break, since the same force can tear the artery, open the skin, and injure the chest and neck. Warmth belongs to the treatment from this first minute, and this chapter does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country where you work.

Why the same bone behaves differently in each region begins with what lies beside it. The upper end carries the head in the shoulder socket, the tuberosities, and the narrow surgical neck, and it lies close to the axillary nerve and artery. The lower end widens into the condyles that form the elbow, where the brachial artery and the median nerve pass into the forearm. The provider level matters throughout, because the person who recognizes the pattern and the person who repairs it are not the same.

The mechanism of injury is the first evidence, and it is a prediction rather than a diagnosis. A fall onto the outstretched hand drives force through the wrist and elbow into the upper or lower end of the bone. A direct blow breaks the shaft where it strikes, and a twisting force produces a long spiral break, the pattern most often found with a nerve injury. A distance such as 3 meters is only a teaching illustration and must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol.

The injuries that travel with the break are what make the first minutes decisive. The neck, chest, abdomen, and pelvis are examined as carefully as the arm, because the same force may have injured an organ that bleeds where no one can see. An open fracture carries heavy bleeding and deep infection, and a fragment that presses the skin white is an open fracture about to happen. A torn brachial artery may bleed into the tissues so that the arm swells and tightens while the surface wound looks small. The nerves may be bruised, stretched, or divided, and lost movement or feeling is often the only sign that the injury is more than a bone.

No examination of the arm begins until the primary survey is complete, because the airway, breathing, and circulation decide whether the patient lives long enough for the arm to matter. The survey follows the fixed order remembered as ABCDE, which stands for airway, breathing, circulation, disability, and exposure. The principle of life before limb decides the order when two correct actions compete, and a deformed arm never precedes a closing airway or a filling chest. Exposure means undressing the patient enough to find every injury and then covering the patient at once, because the search for damage must not become a cause of cooling.

Once the patient is stable, the arm is assessed in a fixed order that begins with the story and ends with the nerves and vessels. The examiner looks before touching, noting the position in which the arm is held, because a patient supporting the forearm protects a break that hurts to move. The skin is inspected for a wound, a bruise, a pale area, or a place where a bone end presses the skin outward. The examiner feels gently along the bone rather than grinding it, because repeated testing of crepitation adds pain and may move a fragment against a vessel. The finding that ends the inspection is a deformity, an open wound, or skin pushed white from within, because each means the limb must be supported as it lies.

When the skin over the break is broken, the wound is covered with a clean dressing and the bone is not pushed back, because pressing a contaminated fragment inward drives infection deep and may release bleeding that was contained. A fragment that tents the skin without breaking it is treated as an open fracture about to happen, and the limb is supported as it lies. The lay first aider does not wash the wound, probe it, remove any fragment, or line the bone up, but covers it and keeps the patient warm while emergency medical services are called. A fragment or foreign object embedded in the wound is stabilized in place and the patient is moved with it, because removal may cause hemorrhage that cannot be controlled.

The wish to straighten the arm must be named plainly, because the deformity is frightening and correction looks like help. The arm is never straightened in the field, because a bone end lying against the brachial artery or a nerve may be pulled away or driven through it. A fracture is splinted in the position found, and a joint that will not move is not forced, since force can convert a closed injury into an open one or a simple break into a vascular emergency. The responder does not spring the shoulder, does not test crepitation again and again, and does not lift the arm by the hand to see how far it travels.

The neurovascular examination must not be skipped, and it asks what lies beyond the break rather than what the bone looks like. The color of the hand is compared with the other side, because a hand that has turned pale, white, or blue is a hand whose blood supply is failing. The pulse is felt at the wrist and the elbow, and capillary refill is tested by pressing the nail bed, with both recorded rather than assumed present. Sensation is tested in each territory of the hand and movement at the wrist and fingers, and the results are compared with the uninjured side and written down at once.

Each nerve that crosses the arm has a territory that can be tested with a simple request. The radial nerve straightens the wrist and fingers and supplies feeling over the back of the hand and the web between the thumb and the index finger. The median nerve turns the thumb across the palm and bends the index finger, and the ulnar nerve spreads the fingers apart and supplies feeling over the little finger. What matters is not the name of the nerve but whether a deficit exists, where it is, and the time it was found.

The radial nerve is the nerve most often injured with a break of the upper arm, and the sign it produces is unmistakable once it has been seen. It winds around the back of the shaft in a groove against the bone, so a fragment that displaces backward can bruise, stretch, or divide it without any wound. The hand that results hangs at the wrist and cannot be lifted, which is called wrist drop, and the fingers cannot be straightened at the knuckles. Feeling is lost or blunted over the back of the hand and in the web between the thumb and the index finger. A wrist drop is a finding and not a diagnosis, because it may also follow pressure of the arm against a hard edge during unconsciousness.

The nerve examination is performed and recorded before the arm is splinted, and the record protects the patient and the clinician at the same time. If a deficit is discovered only after a splint is applied, no one can tell whether the nerve was injured by the fracture or by the splint. If the deficit is written before the splint goes on, the receiving team knows the injury produced it, and the clinician who applied the splint is protected from a suspicion that is easy to raise. The examination is repeated after the splint is applied, and both findings are written on the patient, on the splint, or on tape fixed to the limb, with the time of each. Any new weakness or numbness after handling, splinting, or transport requires the examination at once, because a new deficit is a new emergency.

A break of the upper end is common in older patients after a fall from standing height, and it ranges from a crack to a head split and pushed out of place. The axillary nerve passes close to the surgical neck, so the examiner tests feeling over the outer shoulder and the power to lift the arm sideways. The blood supply to the rounded head runs through the bone just below it, so a badly displaced break may leave the head without a supply, and the surgeon may replace the head rather than fix it. Many of these breaks are treated without an operation, while a displaced break in a younger patient, an open break, or one that threatens the nerves and vessels belongs to definitive hospital care.

Because the shoulder and the upper arm share a small space, a break of the upper end is often accompanied by a dislocated shoulder or a broken collarbone. The axillary artery lies close to the surgical neck, and although it is torn less often than the nerves are injured, a tear is a limb-threatening event whose signs are a rapidly swelling shoulder, a pale cold hand, and a pulse that cannot be felt. The lay first aider supports the arm in the position found, covers any wound, keeps the patient warm, and does not pull on the arm to test the shoulder. The trained responder reassesses the hand before and after every move and reports the direction of the force.

A break of the shaft is the injury that most often produces a visible deformity, with the arm shortened, bent, and rotated. The radial nerve runs in its groove against the middle and lower shaft, and it is the structure most at risk, so the wrist and fingers are examined before anything else is done. The brachial artery runs along the inner side and is at greater risk in a break of the lower shaft, and its injury shows as a pale, cool, pulseless hand with a swelling that keeps enlarging. The shaft may also be broken by a gunshot wound, and the wounds of entry and exit tell nothing about the nerve and the artery. The trained responder immobilizes the arm as it lies, and the surgeon decides which breaks can be held in a brace.

Most radial nerve palsies that accompany a closed break of the shaft are bruises rather than divisions, and most of them recover, though the course must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. A wrist allowed to hang unsupported may become stiff and swollen, so the wrist is supported during transport by the trained responder under the local protocol. The surgeon decides whether the nerve is watched, studied with imaging, or explored during the operation that fixes the bone. The finding that makes the nerve question urgent is a pulse that has disappeared or a hand that is cold, because that combination suggests that the artery as well as the nerve is in danger.

A break of the lower end of the bone involves the elbow, and it is the pattern that most often threatens the hand, because the artery and the median nerve pass directly beside the joint. The signs that make this injury urgent are a pale, cool, pulseless hand, a hand that has lost feeling, and an elbow that grows tense while the responder watches. A break of the lower end may also be open, and the wound is covered without probing and without forcing the joint straight. The trained responder immobilizes the arm and elbow in the position found and moves the patient without delay, because a hand that is not perfused loses muscle and nerve within hours, and that interval is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

In children, the lower end of the bone is the site of a break that is common, difficult to see on a radiograph, and dangerous out of proportion to its size. The growing end of the bone is softer than the shaft, and the same force that would sprain an adult elbow may separate a fragment and injure the artery. The findings that demand immediate movement toward definitive care are a pale or cool hand, a pulse that cannot be felt, pain when the fingers are straightened, and a tense forearm. The decision about imaging, reduction, and fixation belongs to the pediatric orthopedic surgeon, and a lay first aider does not attempt to straighten a child's swollen elbow.

Compartment syndrome is the complication that turns a broken arm into a lost arm, and the responder is the person most likely to see it first. The earliest and most reliable finding is pain out of proportion to the injury and growing worse, with pain produced by gently stretching the fingers. The responder must not loosen a splint at random, must not massage the limb, and must not elevate the arm above the heart. The correct act is to remove any band that is clearly too tight, to keep the arm at heart level, to record the findings, and to move the patient to a place where the tissue can be released by fasciotomy. A pressure such as 30 millimeters of mercury is sometimes quoted as a threshold, and that figure must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

Immobilization is the field treatment that matters most for a broken upper arm, and its purpose is to stop movement rather than to correct the bone. The arm is supported in the position found, with the joints above and below the fracture held still, because every movement produces pain, bleeding, and risk to a nerve or vessel. The trained responder chooses the splint and applies it under the local protocol, while the lay first aider supports the arm with the body, a sling, or whatever is at hand. Padding is placed over the bony points and in the armpit before the bandage is applied, because pressure harmless over muscle can injure skin over bone. The hand is examined again after the splint is applied and at intervals during transport, and the record is updated each time.

A sling and a swathe are the usual way to hold a broken upper arm, and their two jobs are different. The sling supports the weight of the forearm and hand and takes the pull of gravity off the fracture, and it is placed so that the hand is higher than the elbow. The swathe is a broad band passed around the chest and over the injured arm, and its job is to stop the arm from swinging away and rotating. The chest band is applied without constricting the ribs, because a band tight enough to stop the arm can make breathing difficult, and a patient with a chest injury must never have the chest bound. The finding that the sling is too tight is a hand that becomes swollen, blue, or numb below the wrist, and the correction is to loosen it rather than hope.

When the break is in the shaft or near the elbow, a splint spans the shoulder and the elbow or the elbow and the wrist, so the joints above and below the break are controlled together. A rigid support, a padded board, or a folded magazine is placed along the outer side of the arm and secured with broad bands rather than narrow cord. A break near the elbow is immobilized with the elbow at the angle at which it was found, and the elbow is not forced open or closed to make the splint easier. The trained responder checks the hand after every band is tied, because each band is a chance to make the splint too tight. The finding that requires the splint to be released at once is a hand that has lost its warmth, its color, or its feeling.

The position for transport depends on the patient's other injuries, and comfort is a consideration rather than the rule. A patient who is awake, breathing well, and free of injury to the chest, abdomen, or spine may sit up, because sitting reduces the pull of the arm on the shoulder. A patient who is pale, restless, confused, or whose circulation is failing is laid flat with the legs raised where the protocol allows, and the arm is kept supported across the body. The hand is examined repeatedly during transport, and color, warmth, pulse, movement, and feeling are compared with the findings before the journey. The finding that requires the receiving team to be warned in advance is a hand that has lost its pulse or a falling level of consciousness.

A patient with a broken arm is a patient at risk of cold, and the risk is greater when the patient is bleeding, undressed, lying on the ground, or waiting in a vehicle. Cold makes the blood clot poorly, deepens shock, and makes the heart more likely to develop a dangerous rhythm, so warming is part of the treatment of the fracture. The responder removes wet clothing, covers the patient with blankets, coats, or foil, and insulates the patient from the ground, because much of the heat is lost downward. A patient who has stopped shivering while still cold is a patient whose warming has already failed. A body temperature below 35 degrees Celsius is often named as the line at which cold becomes dangerous, and that figure is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

Pain from a broken upper arm is severe, and the responder's first and most effective treatment for it is immobilization, because a limb that does not move hurts far less. The decision to give a medicine for pain belongs to the provider authorized to make it and to the local protocol, and the substance, the amount, the route, and the timing are set there rather than here. The trained responder may give a medicine only where the service authorizes it, and the lay first aider offers comfort, position, warmth, and reassurance instead. The finding that makes relief urgent is a patient whose pain is increasing despite a splint that has not moved, because that pattern suggests that swelling inside the limb is rising. The record must carry what was given, when it was given, and by whom.

An open fracture of the upper arm raises two decisions that belong to the prescriber and to the protocol, and the responder's part is to recognize the wound and to move the patient. Protection against tetanus is considered for every open fracture, and the decision depends on the vaccination history and the nature of the wound, so the substance and the schedule belong to the local protocol. Antibiotics are considered in the same way, and the finding that makes the decision urgent is a wound that is deep, contaminated, or produced by a bite or a projectile. The responder does not irrigate the wound, does not apply a substance to it, and does not close it, because those acts belong to definitive care. The wound is covered with a clean dressing, the limb is splinted as it lies, and the patient is kept warm and moved without delay.

Bleeding from a wound near the shoulder or in the armpit is difficult to control, because the vessel lies where a tourniquet cannot reach and where a dressing cannot press evenly. The lay first aider presses directly on the wound with a clean dressing and the flat of the hand, adds layers rather than lifting the first one, and keeps the pressure on. The trained responder may pack the wound firmly to the bleeding point where the protocol teaches the technique, because packing along the track can succeed where a dressing over the surface fails. A tourniquet may be applied to the arm only where the local protocol authorizes it, because the upper arm is a short and muscular segment in which a tourniquet easily becomes ineffective. Once a tourniquet has controlled the bleeding it is not loosened in the field, and the time it was applied is written on the patient where the receiving team cannot miss it.

Definitive hospital care owns everything that repairs rather than contains, and knowing where that line lies is part of competence for every responder. Imaging with radiographs and, where it is needed, computed tomography shows the pattern of the break and the position of the fragments. Reduction, fixation with plates and screws or with a nail inside the bone, fixation with an external frame, replacement of the shoulder joint, repair of a torn artery, exploration of a nerve, debridement of dead tissue, and fasciotomy are all surgical acts. The field responder's contribution is the shortest safe journey, the warm patient, the controlled bleeding, the splinted limb, and the record of the nerve examination. The finding that should change the destination is a hand without a pulse or a wound that keeps bleeding, because those mean the patient needs a facility with a surgeon.

The fractures that require an operation are recognized by their behavior rather than by their name. An open fracture requires surgical cleaning and stabilization, and a fracture with a lost pulse or a cold hand requires an urgent examination of the artery. A break that cannot be held in line by a splint, a break with a fragment between the ends, and a break of the shaft with a break of the forearm on the same side all point toward fixation. A displaced break of the upper end in a younger patient, a break that has split the joint surface, and a break that has left the head without a blood supply may be fixed or replaced, and the choice belongs to the surgeon. A break that injures the radial nerve, that is accompanied by a torn artery, or that fails to unite after a period of observation also belongs to the operating room.

The complications that follow a broken upper arm are the reason the record matters as much as the first splint. A radial nerve palsy recorded before splinting can be followed honestly, because the receiving team knows where it began and can judge whether it is improving. The responder's part is to keep the limb still in the position found, keep the patient warm, protect the wound, record the nerve examination and the time, and hand over with a story that has no gaps. The handover names the mechanism of injury, the time of the injury, the position of the arm, the nerve and vessel findings before and after splinting, and every change observed. The finding that must never be omitted is any deterioration in the hand or in the patient, because the receiving team can act on a change that is spoken.

A constructed illustration, offered as a construction and not as a real patient, shows how the order of this chapter works. A rider is thrown from a bicycle onto the outstretched arm, and the scene is held against traffic before anyone kneels beside the patient. The pulse at the wrist is weak, the fingers are cool, and the responder writes on the tape that the wrist drop was present before any splint was applied and the time it was found. The arm is supported in the position found, a sling and a swathe are applied without binding the chest, and the receiving team is told the mechanism and the nerve finding. The point of the illustration is that the responder never made the arm look normal and never guessed at the nerve.

Nothing in this chapter authorizes a lay first aider to do more than support, cover, warm, and send, because every repair of this bone demands training, imaging, and an operating room. The trained responder and the paramedic act within a protocol that names the splints they may use, the medicines they may give, and the tourniquets they may apply. The nurse and the physician in a district hospital stabilize the patient, control the pain within their authority, protect the wound, and arrange the transfer to a trauma center. To summarize, a fracture of the upper arm is treated by protecting the limb and the patient rather than by correcting the bone, and the scene is made safe before any hand is laid on either. The bone breaks at its upper end, along its shaft, and at its lower end, and each region carries its own risks, with the axillary nerve at the shoulder, the radial nerve along the shaft, and the artery and the median nerve beside the elbow. The lethal possibilities are the hemorrhage that will not stop, the open wound over the bone, the torn artery that leaves a cold and pulseless hand, and the chest, neck, and spinal injuries that arrived with the same force. The radial nerve is examined and the finding of wrist drop is written down before the splint is applied, because that record protects the patient from a missed injury and the clinician from a false accusation. The arm is splinted in the position found, never straightened and never forced, with the joints above and below the break supported and the hand examined after every band.
