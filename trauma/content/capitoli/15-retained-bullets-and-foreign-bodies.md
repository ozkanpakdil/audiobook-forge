# Chapter 15 — Retained Bullets and Foreign Bodies

The retained projectile is the injury that most often tempts a responder to act, and it is the one where acting is most likely to kill the patient. Scene safety comes before the patient here as in every other chapter, because a place where shots were fired may still hold a shooter, a weapon nearby may still be loaded, and the patient's own blood carries infection that enters through a break in the responder's skin, so the area is made safe by those authorized to hold it and personal protective equipment, or PPE, is worn before a dressing is opened. The lethal possibility to be named first is hemorrhage that is being held back by the object itself, and the finding that reveals it is a wound that has stopped bleeding around a projectile with a pulse beside it or a swelling that grows while the responder watches. The rule that governs everything that follows can be spoken in one sentence, which is that a first responder never pulls a bullet or an embedded object out of a wound. This chapter explains why that is so, how to tell a retained fragment that can safely be left from one that must come out, how the hospital decides, and what surgical removal costs. It is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country where you work.

The reason a bullet is not routinely removed is not that surgeons are timid but that the wound left by removal is a second injury, often worse than the wound the projectile made. A bullet that has come to rest has already spent its energy, and the tissue around it has closed down and sealed the track, so the object may be plugging the very vessel that would pour blood the moment it is drawn out. Removing it also means passing instruments along a track whose course is known only from imaging, and the structures beside that track, which include nerves, tendons, and named vessels, can be divided by the instrument hunting for the metal. A further reason belongs to the operating room itself, because an operation has its own risks of infection, of anesthesia, and of bleeding, and a fragment causing no harm does not earn those risks. The principle is the same one that governs an embedded knife at the roadside, in that what can be contained is contained and what must be repaired is repaired, and the two acts are separated by the finding that the object is already causing harm. This is the distinction the listener must carry away, because the popular picture of a bullet that must be chased through the body is a picture that costs lives.

The retained fragment that can be left is the one at rest in a place where it does nothing, and the hospital recognizes it by a set of findings rather than by a single test. It lies in muscle or in the soft tissue beneath the skin, it is away from a joint, away from a major vessel, and away from a nerve, and it is producing no symptom the patient can describe and no sign the examiner can find. The wound around it has stopped bleeding, the limb beyond it has normal color and warmth and a pulse that can be felt, and the movement and the feeling of the limb are intact. A patient with such a fragment is watched rather than operated on, and the watch continues after discharge, because a piece of metal that is quiet today can announce itself months later. A constructed illustration, offered as a construction and never as a real patient or a real incident, is the fragment that comes to rest in the outer muscle of the thigh, where the patient has full strength, full feeling, and no pain, and where the surgical decision is to leave it and record exactly where it sits. The finding that tells the receiving team which fragment this is, is the combination of its location, of an intact examination of the vessels and nerves, and of the absence of any symptom the fragment explains.

The fragment that must come out is the one already producing harm or positioned where harm is expected, and the brief names the three situations that decide it. The first is a fragment near a joint, because a joint is a closed space with a delicate lining and a surface that must glide, and metal inside or beside it grinds, inflames, and releases lead into the fluid that bathes the cartilage. The second is a fragment near a major vessel, because a vessel wall pressed on or nicked by metal can give way days or weeks later, and the hemorrhage that follows may be the first symptom the patient has. The third is a fragment producing neurological symptoms, because an object touching the spinal cord or a nerve is telling the examiner that the tissue it touches is already losing function, and delay may make that loss permanent. Each of those three is a reason for the hospital to plan an operation rather than a reason for a responder to touch the wound, and that distinction is the whole of the field rule. Other fragments are also removed when they lie in the eye, the airway, the heart, or a vessel they have entered, but these are the three that a listener must be able to repeat.

A joint deserves its own explanation because the harm a fragment does there is mechanical and chemical at the same time. The lining of a joint produces fluid that lubricates the surfaces, and a piece of metal sitting in that fluid is bathed continuously rather than fixed in place, so it can migrate within the joint and be carried into the space where the bones meet. Every movement then becomes a grinding movement against the fragment, and the cartilage wears at a rate no healthy joint would see, while the patient reports a click, a catch, or a pain that appears only at the end of a movement. Lead that dissolves from the fragment into the joint fluid is absorbed by the lining and can produce a delayed inflammation that looks like an arthritis rather than an injury. The finding that should raise the question is a wound near a joint with a painful arc of movement, a joint that swells after use, or metal seen on imaging within the capsule, and that question belongs to the orthopedic surgeon rather than to the responder. Removal in that setting is a planned operation performed with imaging available in the operating room, because the fragment must be found precisely and the joint protected while it is retrieved, and a fragment deep in a joint is never a target for a hand in the field.

A fragment near a major vessel is a delayed emergency, and the delay is what makes it dangerous, because the patient may be discharged well and return in collapse. The wall of an artery or a large vein is elastic and can be pressed on by metal for a long time without tearing, and the pressure of the fragment against that wall can thin it until a false aneurysm forms, which is a sac of blood held together by the tissue around the vessel. That sac can enlarge quietly and then rupture, and the finding that announces it is a pulsatile swelling near the wound, a new sound the examiner can hear with a stethoscope, or a limb that becomes cool and pulseless without any new injury. A fragment that has entered the wall of a vein can also be carried by the bloodstream, so the imaging that follows must look along the whole vessel and not only at the place where the projectile entered. The decision to operate is made with the vascular team and with preparation for the blood loss removal may release, and the operation is planned with the ability to control the vessel above and below the fragment. Nothing in that plan is available at the roadside, and a responder who pulls the fragment out to see what happens has taken the decision away from the people who could have controlled it.

A fragment that produces neurological symptoms is the third situation, and the symptoms may be as loud as a paralyzed limb or as quiet as a numbness the patient mentions in passing. An object lying in the spinal canal can press on the cord, and the finding that reveals it is a change in the strength, the feeling, or the control of the bowel and the bladder below the level of the wound, together with a level of consciousness that is otherwise normal. An object lying against a peripheral nerve can produce a shooting pain along the limb, a weakness of a single muscle group, or a patch of skin that has lost its feeling, and the pattern of that loss tells the examiner which nerve the fragment is touching. Movement of the patient can push a fragment that was merely touching a nerve into the nerve, so the patient with a suspected fragment near the spine is kept still and moved with the spine protected. The neurological examination is repeated and recorded, because a deficit present on arrival and a deficit that appears after a journey mean different things to the receiving team. Imaging and the decision about removal both belong to definitive hospital care, and the finding that makes the decision urgent is a deficit that is getting worse rather than one that is stable.

Lead toxicity is the late consequence that makes a retained fragment a long term problem, and it appears after an interval that may be measured in months or in years. Lead dissolves slowly from metal, and the rate rises when the fragment sits in joint fluid or in the acidic environment of the stomach after it has been swallowed, so the findings are vague and easily attributed to other causes, including abdominal pain, constipation, a headache, a general weakness, and a blood picture that shows anemia. Children are the group at greatest risk, because a growing nervous system is more sensitive and a small body carries a smaller margin, and the pediatric patient with a retained fragment is watched more closely for that reason. A shotgun wound is a special case, because the pellets spread as they travel and the removal of every one is neither possible nor desirable, so imaging maps the spread and the surgical team chooses the few pellets near a joint, a vessel, or a nerve and leaves the rest. The wadding and the clothing carried into such a wound are often a greater threat than the metal, because organic material feeds infection, and the finding that reveals that threat is a wound that becomes hot, swollen, and painful over the following days. The measurement at which concern about lead begins differs between guidelines and laboratories, so it must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

A fragment is not always still, and migration is the reason a retained projectile is documented precisely and watched over time. Metal lying in a joint can move within the joint, a fragment in the soft tissue can be pushed along a tissue plane by the movement of a muscle, and a fragment that has entered the lumen of a vessel can be carried by the flow of blood to a place far from the wound. The most feared migration is into the heart or into the lung, where the fragment can produce a rhythm disturbance, a cough of blood, or a sudden collapse, and the finding that reveals it may be a new symptom in a patient whose wound was considered closed. A fragment that travels in a vein moves toward the heart and the lungs, while a fragment that travels in an artery moves downstream toward the limb, and the direction of travel follows the direction of the flow. Gravity also acts on a fragment in a large vein, so the position of the patient during transport can change where the fragment is later found, which is one more reason the receiving team must be told what position the patient was kept in. The response to migration is not a field maneuver but repeated imaging and a surgical plan, and the finding that triggers the imaging is any new symptom the old wound cannot explain.

Imaging is the hospital's method of deciding, and it is also the reason a field responder does not need to know where the fragment sits. The first examination is usually a plain radiograph of the region, taken from more than one direction, because a fragment seen from a single direction only can be drawn as sitting anywhere along the line of the beam. Computed tomography, after which CT is allowed, shows the fragment in relation to the organs and the bones around it and is the examination of choice when the fragment lies near the spine, the abdomen, or a major vessel, while ultrasound can be used at the bedside to look for fluid, for a swelling, or for a fragment close to the skin. Magnetic resonance imaging, after which MRI is allowed, is used with care, because a fragment containing iron can be moved or heated by the magnetic field, and the decision to perform it belongs to the radiologist who knows the composition of the fragment. The number of views and the choice of examination follow the local protocol, and any figure quoted for the smallest fragment the equipment can show must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because machines and techniques differ between hospitals. Localization during the operation is a further step, using imaging in the operating room so that the surgeon can find the fragment with the smallest possible dissection, and none of these steps belongs to the field.

Surgical extraction is a definitive procedure with its own risks, and the listener must hear that clearly because a fragment invisible from the outside looks easy to take. The operation requires an incision through tissue that has already been injured, and the track must be followed without dividing the structures beside it, so a fragment close to a nerve may be reached only by passing instruments immediately beside that nerve. Every operation carries the risk of infection, of bleeding, and of anesthesia, and a fragment deep in the abdomen or the chest may require the opening of a cavity, a far larger act than the wound itself. Removal can also be incomplete, because a projectile may have fragmented into pieces too small to find, and the surgeon must then decide whether further searching does more harm than the pieces that remain. The operation can worsen a neurological deficit when a fragment is embedded in the spinal cord, and in that setting the surgeon may deliberately leave the fragment in place rather than risk converting a partial deficit into a complete one. The decision is therefore a balance of the harm the fragment is doing against the harm of the operation, and it is made by the trauma surgeon together with the orthopedic, vascular, or neurosurgical team that owns the tissue involved.

The field rule is short and absolute, in that a lay first aider and a trained responder never attempt to extract a bullet or any other embedded object. The object is stabilized in place, the patient is transported with it, and the receiving team is told what it is, where it is, and how long it has been there. The reason is not tidiness but hemorrhage, because the object may be the only thing pressing on a torn vessel, and the moment it is drawn out the bleeding may become impossible to control with the equipment that is present. A second reason is that pulling an object drags with it the tissue that has closed around it, and a hooked fragment or a piece of glass with a sharp edge can cut a structure that was intact. The strongest temptation is the urge to find out what the object is doing, and probing with a finger, with forceps, or with any instrument pushes bacteria into the track, breaks the clot that has sealed it, and can push the object into a structure it was only touching. The finding that tells the responder the rule has been broken is bleeding that restarts from a wound that had stopped, or a new deficit in the limb after an object was moved, and in either case the patient needs definitive care immediately.

Foreign bodies other than bullets arrive in the same wounds and behave in ways that change the plan, so the responder should be able to name them without holding a list in the hand. Glass and metal are dense and are usually seen on a radiograph, while wood, thorns, and clothing are often invisible on a plain radiograph and may be found only by ultrasound or by examination of the wound. A fragment of wood or a piece of cloth that remains in a wound is a persistent source of infection, and the finding that reveals it may be a wound that keeps discharging weeks after it appeared to heal. Tetanus is the specific concern that follows any wound contaminated by soil, by clothing, or by an object that carried dirt into the tissue, and the decision about protection against tetanus belongs to the provider authorized to make it under the local protocol. The finding that makes that decision urgent is a wound that is deep, contaminated, or caused by an object that traveled through the environment before entering the body, while the substance, the amount, the route, and the timing belong to the prescriber and to the local guideline rather than to this book. Antibiotics follow the same rule, in that the decision belongs to the clinician who has examined the wound, and a listener cannot take a prescription from these pages.

An impaled object is the extreme case of the same rule, and it demands a deliberate calm because the object is large, visible, and frightening to everyone at the scene. The object is left exactly where it is, the patient is positioned so that it is supported rather than levered by the ground or the stretcher, and the padding around it is built up until the object can no longer move independently of the body. Stabilization is a skill a lay first aider can perform with the materials at hand, and it begins with a clean dressing placed around the base of the object without pressing on it, followed by padding built up on both sides until the object is held in the middle of a firm ring. A cup, a rolled bandage, or a folded cloth can serve as the ring when nothing better is available, and the ring is fixed to the skin rather than to the object, because a bandage tied to the object transmits every movement of the body directly to the tissue it has entered. The responder does not cut the object, does not push it back, and does not use it as a handle to turn the patient, and the object is cut only when a clinician authorizes it and when the cut is needed to free the patient for transport, well away from the skin so that the inner end does not shift. The finding that tells the responder the stabilization has failed is a change in the patient's pain, a new bleeding, or a new deficit that appears when the object shifts, and any of those findings turns the transport into an emergency within an emergency.

Some sites make the object dangerous in a way that is specific to the anatomy, and the responder should know them because they change the urgency rather than the rule. A foreign body in the eye is protected with a rigid shield that rests on the bone around the eye and touches neither the lid nor the object, and the lay first aider does not attempt to remove a fragment from the eye, does not press on the eye, and does not patch the eye tightly. An object in the neck may lie beside the airway or beside a large vessel, so the first question is whether the patient can breathe and speak, and pressure is applied around the object rather than over it. An object in the chest may have entered the heart or a great vessel, and the finding that reveals that possibility is a wound near the heart with a rising pulse, a falling pressure, or distended neck veins, and that patient belongs to immediate surgical care. A projectile retained in a body cavity raises the same question differently, because the hospital does not open an abdomen merely because a bullet is inside it, but because the track has opened the bowel, the liver, or a vessel, and the finding may be a rigid and tender abdomen or a rising pulse. In every one of those sites the rule is unchanged, which is that the object stays where it is and the patient moves, and the difference is only the speed with which the patient must reach the surgical team.

The lay first aider's part in this chapter is small, specific, and more valuable than the dramatic act it replaces, and the first aider makes the scene safe or stays out of it, protects the hands and the eyes, sends for emergency medical services, covers the wound, stabilizes the object, keeps the patient still and warm, and reports everything observed. The first aider does not probe the wound with a finger, does not use a magnet, does not cut the object free, and does not let a bystander do any of those things, because the only fragment safe to remove is one that is not embedded in anyone. The trained responder and the paramedic add assessment, documentation, and transport, and they examine the limb beyond the wound for color, warmth, pulse, movement, and feeling, recording what they find so that the receiving team can compare it with what follows. They repeat that examination after every move, because a fragment can shift with the patient and a deficit that appears during transport is a finding the surgeon must have. They do not remove the object, do not explore the wound with an instrument, and do not enlarge the wound to see the track, because all three belong to definitive hospital care. They may cover the wound, stabilize the object, splint the limb where required, protect the patient against hypothermia, and begin the journey toward the trauma center that can provide imaging and an operating room, while the decision about pain relief belongs to the provider authorized under the protocol, and the substance, the amount, the route, and the timing are set by that protocol and the prescriber rather than by this book.

Documentation is part of the treatment, because the receiving team inherits a track they cannot see and a decision already made to leave it alone, so the record names the number of wounds, the site of each, whether an entry wound has a matching exit wound, and the position of every object left in place. It states that the object was not removed, that it was stabilized, at what time that was done, and in what position the patient was carried, because a change in position can change where the fragment now sits. It states what the responder observed at each examination of the limb beyond the wound, and the time of it, together with any change that followed, because a trend tells the surgeon more than a single measurement, and it carries the mechanism of injury and the patient's account where one can be given. Hypothermia accompanies every patient in this chapter who is bleeding, exposed, immobilized, or simply waiting, and it is a complication of trauma rather than a detail of comfort. A patient who has been undressed to be examined, who lies on cold ground while an object is stabilized, or who is losing blood loses heat from the surface and from the circulation at once, and the responder breaks that circle by covering the patient before the journey, by insulating from the ground, by replacing wet clothing with dry covering that does not disturb the object, and by keeping the vehicle warm. A body temperature below 35 degrees Celsius is often named as the line at which cold itself becomes serious, and that figure, like every other in this chapter, is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because services and editions differ.

To summarize, a retained bullet is usually left where it is, and the reason is that removal can release hemorrhage that no field measure can control and can injure structures the projectile is not touching. The distinction the listener must carry is between the fragment at rest in muscle with an intact limb beyond it, which is watched, and the fragment near a joint, near a major vessel, or producing neurological symptoms, which is planned for removal by the surgical team. Lead toxicity and migration are the late complications that keep a retained fragment a living problem, and both are found by follow up and by imaging rather than by the examination at the roadside. Imaging, which includes plain radiographs, computed tomography, ultrasound, and in selected cases magnetic resonance imaging, is the hospital's method of localization and of deciding, and extraction is a definitive operation with its own risks of infection, of bleeding, of nerve injury, and of an incomplete result. The field rule is absolute, in that a first responder never extracts a bullet or any other embedded object, and the patient is transported with the object stabilized and the wound covered. Every time, temperature, depth, size, and number named in this chapter is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between services, countries, and editions, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of your country.
