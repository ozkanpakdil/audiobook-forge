# Emergency Wound and Trauma Care — Section Briefs

One brief per section. The section must cover every point named here, in depth, and must
follow the contract in PLAN.md. Length and format rules live in PLAN.md, not here.

---

## Chapter 1 — Principles of Emergency Trauma Care

The goals of first aid as a short list of what actually saves life, and the uncomfortable
truth that most of them are boring: keep the airway open, stop the bleeding, keep the
patient warm, and move. Scene safety as the first clinical act, with the hazards that
kill rescuers, which are traffic, fire, electricity, water, unstable structures, an
assailant and contaminated material. Personal protective equipment as what is available
rather than what is ideal. Mechanism of injury as the first piece of evidence, and what
each mechanism predicts about hidden damage. The primary survey against the secondary
survey, and why the order is not negotiable. The principle of life before limb, and the
situations where it decides between two correct actions. When to activate emergency
medical services and what to say. Triage and prioritization when there are more patients
than hands, including the counterintuitive decision to leave a patient who is already
dying. Name the scene hazards and the missed hemorrhage as the two causes of a rescue that
fails, and state plainly that this is educational material that does not replace training
or local protocol.

## Chapter 2 — The Primary Survey

The A B C D E sequence as a single habit performed the same way every time. Airway: how
obstruction happens in the unconscious patient, basic positioning, the recovery position,
foreign material and what may be removed, and cervical spine precautions that do not
prevent the airway from being opened. Breathing: respiratory rate and effort as the most
sensitive signs available without equipment, the chest injuries that kill within minutes,
oxygenation, and the recognition of respiratory distress before failure. Circulation:
major external hemorrhage, the signs of shock, pulse and skin signs, and tissue perfusion
assessed by hand. Disability: level of consciousness, pupillary assessment, and the
neurological examination that can be done in a minute. Exposure: the complete examination
with the patient undressed, and the immediate prevention of hypothermia that must follow
it. Say who performs which part at each provider level, and repeat that any finding which
stops the sequence restarts it.

## Chapter 3 — Recognizing Life-Threatening Injuries

The injuries that kill in the first minutes and hours, and the bedside finding that
reveals each: massive hemorrhage, airway compromise, tension pneumothorax, open chest
wounds, traumatic brain injury, spinal injury, pelvic trauma, major fractures, internal
bleeding and traumatic shock. Why each is dangerous, how quickly it acts, what the first
responder can do before help arrives, and what the hospital does next. The difference
between an injury that is dramatic and one that is lethal, and the trap of attending to
the dramatic. The patient who looks well and is not, and the trend that matters more than
the single observation.

## Chapter 4 — Understanding Wounds

The wound as a mechanical event that tells you what happened inside. Abrasions and
lacerations, incisions, puncture wounds, crush injuries, avulsions and degloving injuries,
contaminated wounds and open fractures, each described by the tissue destroyed and the
structures at risk beneath it. Why a small puncture can hide a deep injury while a
spectacular laceration may be superficial. Wound assessment, the risk of contamination,
the signs that mark a wound as needing surgical evaluation rather than dressing, and the
tetanus question raised at the first contact. Three levels of care throughout: what the
lay first aider covers and does not probe, what the responder may do, and what belongs to
the hospital.

## Chapter 5 — External Bleeding

Bleeding described by its source: capillary, venous, arterial, and the mixed bleeding of a
crushed wound. Recognition of life-threatening hemorrhage by the behavior of the blood
rather than its colour. The ladder of control, each step explained by the physics of
pressure: direct pressure, pressure dressings, wound packing for appropriate deep wounds,
hemostatic dressings where they exist and what they do not replace, and the tourniquet
with its indications, its contraindications, the recording of the time it was applied, the
rule that it is not loosened in the field, and the reassessment that must follow. Who may
do what: pressure and packing for the trained bystander, the tourniquet within the
protocol of the responder. Hypothermia and shock as companions of hemorrhage.

## Chapter 6 — Wound Dressing

What a dressing is for: to control bleeding, to protect exposed tissue, to keep the wound
clean, and to let the patient be moved. Sterile, pressure and occlusive dressings, each
with the situation that calls for it. Packing of deep wounds that should be packed and the
wounds that must not be packed. Contamination control and the protection of exposed tissue,
including exposed bone, tendon and bowel. The materials that must never be placed in a
wound, and why. Dressing as a temporary act: what must be checked after it is applied, and
the signs that it has failed.

## Chapter 7 — Wound Cleaning and Infection Prevention

Irrigation and the removal of superficial contamination, the difference between cleaning a
wound and scrubbing it, and the tissue that must not be disturbed. Sterile technique as a
method rather than a supply. Tetanus considerations at the level of the decision rather
than the dose, and the same for antibiotic prophylaxis, naming who is authorized to decide
and what finding changes the answer. The signs of wound infection and the timeline on
which they appear. The indications for surgical evaluation, and the wounds that look minor
and are not.

## Chapter 8 — Special Wounds

Animal bites and the organisms they carry, human bites and why they are treated more
seriously than their size suggests, puncture wounds and nail penetration with the deep
structures they can reach, foreign bodies and the assessment of what is in the wound and
where it lies. Embedded objects: the reasons they are stabilized in place rather than
removed in the field, since removal may release severe hemorrhage or cause further damage,
and the technique of stabilization and transport. The tetanus and infection questions
raised by each, and the wounds that require operative care.

## Chapter 9 — Knife and Stabbing Injuries

Penetrating trauma and the mismatch between the size of the wound and the depth of the
track. Entry wounds, the search for exit wounds, and the hidden internal injuries that no
external appearance predicts. The anatomical locations that make a stab wound lethal:
chest, abdomen, pelvis, neck and extremities, with the structures at risk in each.
Complications: hemorrhage, pneumothorax, hemothorax, organ injury, major-vessel injury and
shock. First-responder management from scene safety through hemorrhage control,
stabilization of embedded objects, wound protection, airway and breathing assessment, shock
management and rapid evacuation. Hospital management: imaging, surgical consultation,
vascular assessment, exploration when indicated, and repair of damaged structures.

## Chapter 10 — Gunshot Wounds

Basic ballistic mechanisms and what they explain about the wound, including the difference
between the track of a slow projectile and the cavitation of a fast one. Entry wounds and
exit wounds, and the trap of counting holes. Tissue cavitation, the temporary cavity that
injures structures the bullet never touched, and multiple wounds that require a search for
the ones not yet found. Hidden internal injuries, bone fragmentation as secondary
projectiles, and neurovascular injuries. The principle that the wound is the visible part
of an unknown internal injury, and that the number of wounds matters less than the
structures in their path.

## Chapter 11 — Immediate Management of Gunshot Wounds

The sequence at the scene, in the order that saves life: scene safety against the
possibility of a second shot and against traffic, massive hemorrhage control, airway,
breathing, circulation, neurological assessment, recognition and treatment of shock, and
rapid transport to a trauma center. Why the wounds that receive attention first are the
ones that bleed, not the ones that look worst. The balance between staying to stabilize and
leaving to operate, decided by the mechanism, the distance to the trauma center and the
provider's scope of practice. The information that must travel with the patient.

## Chapter 12 — Gunshot Wounds to the Chest

The chest injuries a bullet can produce and how each announces itself: pneumothorax,
tension pneumothorax, hemothorax, open pneumothorax, cardiac injury and major-vessel
injury. Why tension pneumothorax kills quickly, the findings that distinguish it from a
simple pneumothorax, and what the first aider and the responder may and may not do about
it. Open pneumothorax and the occlusive dressing and its monitoring. Cardiac injury and
pericardial tamponade as the injury that presents as shock with quiet breath sounds.
Hospital assessment, imaging and the interventions that belong there.

## Chapter 13 — Gunshot Wounds to the Abdomen and Pelvis

Why the abdomen gives no external sign of the catastrophe inside it. Internal hemorrhage,
hollow-organ injury with its spill of contents, solid-organ injury with its steady
bleeding, peritoneal contamination and the infection that follows it, and pelvic vascular
injury with the hemorrhage that fills the pelvis. The findings that suggest intra-abdominal
bleeding when there is no tenderness to find, and the pelvic fracture that bleeds into the
space it creates. First aid as hemorrhage control, warmth and transport; the responder's
assessment and pelvic management within protocol; hospital imaging, transfusion and
operation. Name the pelvic hemorrhage as the lethal possibility.

## Chapter 14 — Gunshot Wounds to the Limbs

Limb wounds as the most common and the most survivable, and the injuries inside them that
threaten the limb rather than the life. Arterial injury and its signs, venous injury, nerve
injury, compartment syndrome as the delayed complication that follows a limb that was
saved, open fractures and distal circulation assessment. The neurovascular examination that
must be repeated, and the deterioration that occurs after the first normal examination.
What first aid may do, what the responder may assess and splint, and what the hospital
does to restore flow.

## Chapter 15 — Retained Bullets and Foreign Bodies

Why bullets are not routinely removed, and the distinction between a retained projectile
that can be left and one that must come out. The situations that require removal:
projectiles near joints, near major vessels, or producing neurological symptoms, and the
later considerations of lead toxicity and migration. Imaging and localization as the
hospital's method of deciding, and surgical extraction as a definitive procedure with its
own risks. The field rule stated plainly: first responders do not attempt to extract
bullets or other embedded objects, and the patient is transported with the object
stabilized.

## Chapter 16 — Rib Fractures

Rib anatomy as the explanation for everything that follows, including why the lower ribs
protect organs and the upper ribs protect vessels. Simple rib fractures, multiple rib
fractures, flail chest, pulmonary contusion, pneumothorax and hemothorax as the
complications that arrive after the fracture. Management: breathing assessment, pain
management within the provider's scope, oxygen when indicated, monitoring, positioning and
the criteria for transport. The two things to avoid, which are binding the chest tightly
and dismissing an apparently uncomplicated rib fracture in an older patient. State that the
fracture is not the danger; the breathing and the lung beneath it are.

## Chapter 17 — Flail Chest

Definition and mechanism, and the segment of chest wall that moves with the pressure of
breathing rather than with the muscles. Paradoxical movement as the visible sign, the
pulmonary contusion beneath it as the real problem, and respiratory failure as the
destination. Emergency management at each provider level, the positioning and oxygenation
that help, and the trap of the tightly bound chest. Hospital treatment, including the
analgesia, ventilation and fixation that belong there, and why flail chest is a diagnosis
of the whole patient rather than of the ribs.

## Chapter 18 — Open Chest Wounds

Recognition of the wound that communicates with the pleural space, and the sound and the
movement of air that may accompany it. Occlusive dressings, how they work and how they
fail, and the monitoring for tension pneumothorax that must follow the dressing because the
dressing itself can create the emergency. Emergency evacuation and the reasons not to
delay. Advanced interventions, named with the level of training and authorization they
require, and the statement that a first aider treats an open chest wound by covering it and
leaving.

## Chapter 19 — General Principles of Fractures

The vocabulary of fractures, each term explained by what it means for the patient: closed
and open, displaced and nondisplaced, complete and incomplete, comminuted, spiral,
greenstick and stress fractures. Open fracture as the emergency it is, with the wound,
contamination and infection that follow. Assessment of pain, deformity, swelling, abnormal
movement, skin integrity and neurovascular status, and the sequence in which they are
checked. Why the examination before and after immobilization is the same examination. The
three levels of care: what the bystander may cover and support, what the responder may
splint, and what reduction and fixation belong to the hospital.

## Chapter 20 — Immobilization and Splinting

The purpose of immobilization, which is to prevent further injury and to control pain
rather than to correct the position. The principles of splinting, including splinting in
the position found, immobilizing the joints above and below the injury, and padding every
bony prominence. Commercial and improvised splints, with the objects that make a
serviceable splint in a clinic without supplies. Reassessment after splinting, with the
circulation, sensation and movement check repeated and recorded. The critical rule stated
where it belongs: a severely deformed fracture is not straightened to make it look normal,
and the limb is splinted as it lies unless a trained provider with a protocol decides
otherwise.

## Chapter 21 — Shoulder Injuries

Clavicle fractures and why they are common and rarely dangerous, shoulder dislocations and
the neurovascular structures at risk during reduction, and scapular fractures as a marker
of enormous force with the associated chest and spinal injuries that must be sought. The
neurovascular assessment that is done before and after any handling of the shoulder. The
sling and the immobilization that make a patient comfortable and safe for transport, and
the positions that must not be forced.

## Chapter 22 — Humerus Fractures

Fractures of the proximal humerus, the humeral shaft and the distal humerus, each with its
own risks. The radial nerve and the wrist drop that signals its injury, and the importance
of recording the nerve examination before the limb is splinted, because the record protects
both the patient and the clinician. The assessment, the immobilization, and the fractures
that require operative care.

## Chapter 23 — Elbow Injuries

The elbow as a joint where a small injury can destroy the limb. Fractures of the distal
humerus, the radial head and the olecranon, dislocations and the dislocation with an
associated fracture. Neurovascular compromise and the signs that make it an emergency, the
position of comfort and the position that must never be forced, and the immobilization for
transport. The assessment that must be repeated, and the indication for urgent hospital
evaluation.

## Chapter 24 — Forearm Fractures

Fractures of the radius, of the ulna, and of both bones together, with the deformity that
follows and the structures between them at risk. Compartment syndrome as the complication
that must be anticipated rather than discovered, including the pain out of proportion that
is its earliest sign. Splinting, elevation where appropriate, the neurovascular
reassessment, and the fractures that need operative fixation. The distinction between the
isolated fracture of one bone and the both-bone fracture that changes the shape of the
limb.

## Chapter 25 — Wrist and Hand Injuries

The injuries of the wrist and hand that matter and the ones that are missed. Distal radius
fractures, carpal fractures including the one that hides for weeks, metacarpal and finger
fractures, dislocations, tendon injuries and amputations of the fingers. The neurovascular
and tendon examination that distinguishes a sprain from a severed structure, and the
position of function that protects what remains. The splinting, the dressing, and the
injuries that require surgical assessment, with the amputation of a digit handled as the
vascular emergency it is.

## Chapter 26 — Hip and Pelvis

Hip fractures, hip dislocations, and pelvic fractures with the massive internal hemorrhage
they can produce. Why the pelvis is a bleeding space rather than a bone, and the instability
that predicts it. Pelvic binders as an intervention for appropriately trained responders,
with the level of training, the position and the monitoring it requires, and the harms of a
binder applied wrongly. The neurovascular assessment, the immobilization for transport, and
the hospital management including transfusion and fixation. Name the pelvic hemorrhage as
the lethal possibility and say how quickly it kills.

## Chapter 27 — Femur Fractures

Why a fracture of the femur is dangerous beyond the limb: the potential blood loss into the
thigh, the shock that follows, and the deformity that reflects the force involved. The
neurovascular assessment and the distal circulation that must be checked and recorded.
Traction splints where indicated, with the indications, the contraindications and the
provider level, including the pelvic and knee injuries that make a traction splint the
wrong choice. The immobilization and positioning for transport, and the analgesia question
at the level of the decision.

## Chapter 28 — Knee Injuries

Patellar fractures, knee dislocation as a limb-threatening injury rather than a sports
injury, and ligament injuries with the instability they produce. The popliteal artery and
the reason a dislocated knee is a vascular emergency, with the signs of a failing distal
circulation and the time pressure they create. Immobilization in the position found, the
reassessment of pulses and sensation, and the immediate referral criteria.

## Chapter 29 — Tibia and Fibula Fractures

Closed fractures and the much more serious open fracture with its wound and contamination.
Deformity and what it implies about the soft tissue, compartment syndrome as the
complication of the lower leg that is most often missed, and the neurovascular assessment
that must be repeated. Splinting technique for the lower leg, the elevation and padding
that make it safe, and the open fracture as a surgical emergency requiring debridement.
State the rule that a deformed lower leg is splinted as it lies.

## Chapter 30 — Ankle Injuries

Malleolar fractures, ankle dislocation, severe sprains and the open ankle injury. The
neurovascular assessment and the difference between a joint that is swollen and one whose
blood supply is failing. Immobilization with the foot in a functional position, the padding
and support that prevent pressure injury, and the reassessment that follows. The criteria
that turn a sprain into a fracture requiring radiography, and the open injury that must not
be closed in the field.

## Chapter 31 — Foot Injuries

Metatarsal fractures, toe fractures, calcaneal fractures and crush injuries of the foot,
with the machinery and the falls that produce them. Open wounds of the foot with their
heavy contamination and the infection risk that follows, and the neurovascular compromise
that a swollen foot can hide inside a boot. Splinting, dressing, the removal of footwear
where it threatens circulation, and the crush injury that requires assessment for
compartment syndrome and for the systemic effects described later in the manual.

## Chapter 32 — Shock

Shock as a failure of tissue perfusion rather than a number, and the four mechanisms that
produce it: hypovolemic, obstructive, cardiogenic and distributive. Recognition from the
signs available to the hands and eyes, including the skin, the pulse, the level of
consciousness and the trend. Monitoring without equipment. Positioning, warmth, and the
prevention of hypothermia as a treatment rather than a comfort. Fluid and blood
resuscitation as advanced and hospital care, with the decisions rather than the volumes,
and the interventions that only a trained provider with the right authorization may
perform.

## Chapter 33 — Compartment Syndrome

Definition and pathophysiology explained by the fascia that does not stretch, and the
pressure that rises until perfusion stops. Early symptoms, above all the pain out of
proportion to the injury that is worse on passive stretch, and late symptoms as the signs
of a limb already lost. The causes, from fractures and crush injuries to tight casts,
dressings and prolonged positioning. Emergency referral and the rule that the diagnosis is
clinical and must not wait for a number. Fasciotomy as the definitive treatment belonging
to the operating theatre, described as such.

## Chapter 34 — Neurovascular Injuries

Arterial injury, venous injury and nerve injury, each with its own signs and its own
urgency. The distal pulses, capillary refill, sensation and motor function as the four
observations that must be recorded before and after every immobilization, dressing and
movement. Why a pulse that is present does not exclude a vascular injury, and why the
deterioration between two examinations is the finding that matters. The hard signs that
demand immediate hospital care, and the reassessment schedule that makes a limb safe to
transport.

## Chapter 35 — Crush Injuries

The crushed patient as two problems at once: the local injury and the systemic syndrome
that follows release. Crush syndrome, rhabdomyolysis with the muscle contents that enter
the blood, hyperkalemia as the electrolyte disturbance that stops the heart, acute kidney
injury, and compartment syndrome in the crushed limb. Extrication considerations, including
the harm done by rapid release without preparation, the decisions that belong to advanced
providers, and the monitoring and hospital care that follow. Name the cardiac arrest after
extrication as the lethal possibility.

## Chapter 36 — Head Injuries

Scalp wounds and their capacity to bleed alarmingly without danger, and the skull fracture
that may lie beneath. Concussion and the observation that it is a diagnosis of exclusion.
Intracranial hemorrhage as the injury that kills while the patient is talking, with the
lucid interval that must be taught. Altered consciousness, vomiting, seizures and unequal
pupils as the findings that change the plan, the level of consciousness as the most
important repeated observation, and the hospital assessment and imaging that belong there.

## Chapter 37 — Facial Trauma

Nose injuries, jaw injuries, dental injuries and eye injuries, with the airway obstruction
and the severe facial bleeding that make facial trauma lethal. Why facial fractures bleed
into the airway, the positioning that lets blood drain rather than pool, and the eye injury
that becomes blindness if it is pressed. The dental injury and the tooth that can be
preserved. The assessment, the protection, and the transfer that must not be delayed by the
appearance of the face.

## Chapter 38 — Neck Trauma

Penetrating neck injuries with the dense anatomy inside a small space: the airway, the
major vessels, the spinal cord, the esophagus and the nerves. Airway compromise as the
first danger and major-vessel injury as the second, with the bleeding that may be hidden
inside the neck or the chest. Cervical spine considerations and the precautions that
coexist with airway management. The management at each level, the dressing that must not
compress the airway, and the immediate surgical referral.

## Chapter 39 — Spinal Trauma

Cervical, thoracic and lumbar injuries, with the mechanisms that predict each. Spinal cord
injury and the neurological assessment that must be recorded before movement, including
the level of injury and what remains below it. Movement precautions and the handling that
minimizes unnecessary movement, the immobilization according to protocol, and the
extrication of a patient from a vehicle or a confined space. The balance between
immobilization and the airway, and the statement that the spinal precaution must never
prevent the treatment of an airway that is closing.

## Chapter 40 — Traumatic Amputation

Massive hemorrhage as the immediate threat, and hemorrhage control as the first act,
including the tourniquet that is applied promptly and not loosened. Wound dressing and the
control of contamination. Shock and the warmth that must be maintained. Preservation of the
amputated tissue: the wrapping, the cooling, the correct medium and the medium that must
never be used, and the labelling that keeps the part with its patient. Packaging and
transport, and the replantation considerations that belong to the hospital.

## Chapter 41 — Mangled Extremity

The limb with severe soft-tissue destruction, vascular injury, exposed bone, nerve injury
and compartment syndrome at once. Why the decision between salvage and amputation is a
hospital decision made by a surgical team, and what the prehospital provider must preserve
in order to keep that decision possible. Hemorrhage control, wound protection,
immobilization and rapid transport. The honest statement that a mangled limb is a
life-threatening injury when the bleeding is not controlled, and that the priority remains
the life.

## Chapter 42 — Thermal Burns

Superficial, partial-thickness and full-thickness burns, each described by what happens to
the skin and what it means for healing. Circumferential burns and the constriction that
follows swelling, with the chest and the limb as the emergencies. Airway burns and the
findings that predict them, and the burn extent assessment done in words rather than with a
chart. Cooling, covering, warmth and the prevention of hypothermia in a patient whose skin
no longer protects them. The provider levels for each act, and the burns that must go to a
specialist unit.

## Chapter 43 — Chemical Burns

The chemical burn as an injury that continues until the agent is removed, and the
decontamination that must happen before anything else. Protection of the rescuer from the
same agent, the removal of contaminated clothing, and the irrigation that must be
prolonged. Skin exposure and eye exposure, with the eye treated as the emergency it is.
The agents that behave differently, including those that are harmed rather than helped by
water, named generically. The transfer of information about the agent to the hospital, and
the burns that require surgical assessment.

## Chapter 44 — Electrical Injuries

Electrical injury as a systemic event with a small visible wound. Electrical burns at the
entry and exit points that conceal the deep tissue injury between them, cardiac arrhythmias
and the monitor that must be applied, and the deep tissue destruction that follows
high-voltage current. Safety at the scene as the first act, since the source may still be
live. The provider levels for resuscitation and monitoring, and the hospital assessment for
rhabdomyolysis and the injuries of a fall from height.

## Chapter 45 — Immediate Pain Management

Pain relief as part of trauma care rather than a comfort added afterwards, and the
reassurance, positioning, immobilization and cold therapy that are available to everyone.
Pharmacological analgesia as an intervention within scope of practice, described at the
level of the decision and never with a dose, a route or a frequency. The monitoring for
adverse effects that must accompany any analgesia, and the patient whose pain is out of
proportion as a diagnostic sign rather than a complaint. The scope of practice table
spoken as prose: the lay first aider, the first responder, the emergency medical technician
or paramedic, the nurse, the physician and the trauma surgeon, and how the same injury
leads to different acts in each pair of hands.

## Chapter 46 — Imaging

What each modality answers and what it cannot. The plain radiograph for bone and for the
foreign body that is radio-opaque, ultrasound at the bedside for fluid where it should not
be, computed tomography as the reference for the injured trunk and head, magnetic resonance
imaging for the spine and the cord, computed tomographic angiography for the vessel, and
conventional angiography where it is both diagnosis and treatment. What the prehospital
provider should never delay transport for, and what the hospital uses to decide between the
operating theatre and the ward. The limits of a normal image.

## Chapter 47 — Wound Closure

The closure methods as choices rather than a ladder: sutures, staples, adhesive strips and
tissue adhesive, each with the wound it suits and the wound it harms. Delayed primary
closure and the philosophy of the contaminated wound that is left open on purpose. The
wounds that must not be closed immediately, including the bite, the heavily contaminated
wound, the puncture and the wound with a retained foreign body. The conditions that must be
met before any wound is closed, and who is authorized to close it.

## Chapter 48 — Surgical Treatment

The definitive procedures, each described as hospital care to be understood rather than
performed: hemostasis, debridement, vascular repair, tendon repair, nerve repair, fracture
reduction, external fixation, internal fixation, fasciotomy, amputation and foreign-body
removal. Why each exists, what it achieves, and what the prehospital provider must have
done or not done to make it possible. The damage-control philosophy of the patient too ill
for the definitive operation, and the sequence that puts life before limb.

## Chapter 49 — Infection and Tetanus

Tetanus as the disease of the wound that seemed trivial, with the prophylaxis decision
described at the level of the decision and never as a dose or a schedule. Antibiotic
prophylaxis and the same discipline. The infections that follow trauma: cellulitis,
abscess, necrotizing infection with its disproportionate pain and its speed, osteomyelitis
in the open fracture and the contaminated wound, and sepsis as the final common path. The
signs, the timeline, the referral criteria, and the statement that infection after trauma
is a diagnosis made by looking at the patient and not only at the wound.

## Appendix A — Practical Emergency Algorithms

The six algorithms spoken as ordered prose, each step leading to the next in a sentence,
with the reason each step comes where it does. The unconscious trauma patient, from scene
safety and catastrophic bleeding through the A B C D E sequence to the prevention of
hypothermia and rapid transport. Massive external hemorrhage, from recognition through
direct pressure, packing, the tourniquet, reassessment, warmth and transport. The suspected
fracture, from mechanism through the look for deformity and wounds, the neurovascular
assessment, immobilization, reassessment and transport. The suspected spinal injury, from
mechanism through the airway, the neurological assessment, the minimization of unnecessary
movement, immobilization according to protocol and transport. Penetrating trauma, from

## Appendix D — The Core Principle and Scope of Practice

The core principle of the manual spoken as prose: the first responder's job is not to make
the injury look normal but to keep the patient alive, control life-threatening bleeding,
maintain the airway and breathing, prevent further injury, protect wounds and exposed
tissue, immobilize injuries when appropriate, recognize shock and other life-threatening
complications, prevent hypothermia, monitor the patient continuously, and get the patient
to definitive care as quickly and safely as possible. Then the scope limitation: bullet
extraction, surgical wound exploration, fracture reduction, internal fixation, vascular
repair, fasciotomy and deep debridement belong to definitive medical and surgical
treatment, and are never presented as first-aid procedures. The appendix closes by naming
the four things every intervention requires, which are training, equipment, authorization
and a scope of practice, and by restating that this manual is educational and does not
replace certification, supervision, the current guideline or the law of the country where
the reader works.
