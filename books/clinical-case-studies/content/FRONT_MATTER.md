# Clinical Case Studies: Twenty Cases That Went Wrong Before They Went Right

A patient arrives with a complaint. A team of experts meets, builds a differential
diagnosis, ranks it by likelihood and by danger, and commits to a working diagnosis.
A therapy is chosen and begun. A nurse applies it, follows the patient, and watches
the numbers and the patient. And then the therapy does not work. The patient does not
improve, or improves and then slides back, and something that was present from the
very first hour turns out to have been pointing at a different disease the whole
time. The team reopens the case, finds the clue it had read without seeing, changes
the approach, and the patient recovers.

That arc is the whole of this book. It is told twenty times, in twenty different
specialties, in twenty different settings, and each time it fails in a different
way. A collapse in a garden that was written off as a faint. Breathlessness after a
flight that was treated as pneumonia. A cough that outlasted two courses of
antibiotics. An ankle that was hot. A stomach ache that was stomach ache. A woman
who would not sit still, a woman who woke up shaking, a woman who fainted while
shopping, a woman of eighty-four who was agitated, and a lady of eighty-two whose
urine was not an infection. Each case turns on a detail that was there from the
beginning and that nobody read, and in most of them the person who read it was the
nurse.

The twenty cases are constructed composites. No case describes a real patient, no
detail identifies anyone living or dead, and no institution is described in a way
that could be recognized. They are built to make a piece of reasoning visible, and a
real ward is messier, slower and far more surprising than anything on these pages.
Where a number appears it is an illustration of reasoning rather than a fact to act
on, and it must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol
before anyone relies on it, because the numbers differ between countries, between
editions, and between the laboratory and the ward. This book is educational material.
It is not medical advice, it is not a protocol, and it is not a substitute for
training, supervision, or the judgment of the clinician who is actually with the
patient.

The book opens with the cases themselves, grouped into five parts by the system they
concern, and closes with two appendices. The first draws the twenty failures
together into ten patterns of reasoning that go wrong, and the second collects the
twenty nursing observations that opened each case, because in every one of these
stories the nurse is not the person who executes the plan. The nurse is the person
who watches it fail, and who notices that it is failing.

It is meant to be heard in order once, and returned to chapter by chapter
afterwards.
