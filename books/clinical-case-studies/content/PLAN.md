# Clinical Case Studies — Style Contract

This file is the binding contract for every section of *Clinical Case Studies: Twenty
Cases That Went Wrong Before They Went Right*. It overrides habits, preferences, and
anything else you have read. Each section is written by a different author and read
aloud by the same voice, so the rules below are what make twenty chapters sound like
one book.

## What this book is

A narrated collection of constructed clinical cases, each one a complete story that
turns on a mistake. A patient arrives with a complaint. A team of experts meets and
builds a differential diagnosis, ranks it, and commits to a working diagnosis. A
therapy is chosen and begun. The nurse on the ward or in the clinic applies it, follows
the patient, and watches the numbers and the patient. Then the therapy does not work.
The patient does not improve, or improves and then deteriorates, and something in the
record that has been present from the first hour turns out to have been pointing at a
different disease all along. The team reopens the case, finds the overlooked clue,
changes the approach, and the patient recovers.

The point of the book is not the twenty diagnoses. It is the shape of the mistake. In
every case the wrong answer was reasonable, the evidence for it was present, and the
evidence against it was present too but went unread. A reader who absorbs only one
habit from this book should absorb this one: a treatment that fails is information, and
the patient who does not improve has not disappointed the clinician, he has corrected
him.

Every case in this book is a composite, constructed for teaching. No case describes a
real patient, no detail identifies anyone living or dead, and no institution, hospital
or service is described in a way that could be recognized. Real patients are messier
and more surprising than any illustration, and the listener should expect that.

The book is for the nurse at the bedside and the student on the ward round, for the
clinician who wants to see how reasoning goes wrong from the inside, and for anyone
who has ever watched a treatment fail and wondered what was missed. The nurse is
treated throughout as a colleague and as a source of evidence rather than as a
performer of orders, because in these twenty stories it is very often a nursing
observation, a nursing measurement, or something a nurse said to a doctor that reopened
the diagnosis.

## The shape of every case

Every chapter follows the same arc, though the surface of each one is different. The
chapter must contain all seven of these movements, in this order, and must not skip
any of them or compress two of them into a single sentence.

The patient arrives. The complaint, the person, the setting, and the details that will
later prove decisive are placed in front of the listener early, without comment,
because a later chapter cannot introduce a detail that the earlier one did not have.

The team meets. A differential diagnosis is built, ranked by likelihood and by danger,
and a working diagnosis is chosen, with the reason for the choice made audible. The
dangerous alternative is named at this point, not left to the end.

The therapy begins. What is started, and why that was the right decision on the
evidence available at that hour.

The nursing follows. The nurse applies the treatment, takes the observations, keeps
the records, educates the patient, and monitors. This is not background. It is the
part of the story where the treatment either works or fails, and it is written with
the same care as the medicine.

The failure. The patient does not improve, or the improvement is partial and then lost.
A second examination, a repeated test, or a fresh look at the notes shows the failure.
The failure is written from the inside, including what the team felt about it and what
it was tempted to do next.

The overlooked clue. The team goes back to the beginning and finds the detail that was
there all along and pointed elsewhere. It is named as it was actually observed: what
the nurse wrote, what the patient said in passing, what was in the notes from the
first night. The chapter must say plainly how it was missed, because a mistake without
a cause cannot be avoided again.

The change and the recovery. The approach is changed, the correct treatment is given,
the patient responds, and the follow-up is described, including what the team learned
and what the nurse now watches for.

## Contract of style

- One heading per section, exactly the heading given in the syllabus below. Nothing
  else may be a heading.
- Prose only. No bullet points, no numbered lists, no tables, no markdown emphasis, no
  footnotes, no citations, no web addresses, no references to figures or tables.
- Paragraphs of four to eight sentences, separated by one blank line. The final
  paragraph begins with exactly `To summarize, ` and carries no heading.
- Write for the ear. Nothing can be re-read, looked up, or highlighted. Where a chart,
  a table or a scan would help on a page, this chapter must describe the thing in
  words precisely enough that the listener can picture it: the shape of the rash, the
  sound of the breath, the feel of the swollen ankle, the look of the hand that
  trembles.
- Explain the reasoning before the maneuver. A listener who understands why a sign is
  sought will notice it, and will know what its absence means.
- Length: chapters run between three thousand eight hundred and four thousand four
  hundred words. Appendices run between two thousand two hundred and three thousand two
  hundred words. A little over is fine; badly under is not.
- Write the file once, carefully. Do not rewrite, restructure, or trim it afterwards.
- Open each chapter by naming the case, the patient and the reasoning that organizes
  it, and close it with the paragraph that begins `To summarize, `. Vary openings and
  structures between chapters: this is a book of twenty sections, and twenty openings
  of the same shape would be heard as one.
- The people are named. Each case has a patient with a name, and the members of the
  team are named too, because a team that has no names is a mechanism and a team with
  names is a group of people who can be wrong together. Names must be plain and
  ordinary, and must not be drawn from any real patient or any identifiable
  institution.
- Dialogue is written as speech, sparingly, and only where the exact words matter:
  what a patient said in passing, what a nurse asked at the bedside, what the senior
  asked on the ward round. Do not script whole conversations.

## Clinical safety rules

This is educational material. It is not medical advice, it is not a protocol, and it
is not a substitute for training, supervision, or the judgment of the clinician who is
actually with the patient. State that plainly in the first chapter and in every
appendix, and assume it everywhere else.

- **No drug doses.** No dose, no regimen, no rate, no duration of administration, and
  no abbreviated route for any drug. The narrative may name a therapy when naming it
  is part of the recognizable story, as it is for insulin, immunoglobulin, plasma
  exchange, anticoagulation, thyroid blocking drugs, lipid emulsion, antibiotics or
  surgery, but it may never say what was given, how much, how often, by which route,
  or for how long. Where treatment matters to the reasoning, describe the decision and
  the finding that made it right: not what to give, but what would make treatment the
  correct answer and what would make it the wrong one.
- **No threshold presented as universal.** A score, a cut-off, a reference range, a
  pressure, a temperature, a rate, a duration, or a volume named in this book is an
  illustration of reasoning, and the listener must be told so at the point where it
  appears. Follow every such number with the reminder that it must be confirmed
  against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the numbers differ
  between countries, between editions, and between the laboratory and the ward. Write
  that reminder in varied words rather than the same sentence twenty times.
- **Diagnose the patient, not the pattern.** Where a framework is used, say what it
  excludes, what it assumes, and the population it was derived in. A rule validated in
  one setting can mislead in another, and several of these cases turn on exactly that.
- **Name the dangerous possibility early.** In every chapter, the lethal or disabling
  alternative is named where the presentation is described, together with the finding
  that would reveal it. Safety is not an appendix.
- **No real patient, no identifiable case, no institutional detail.** Every case is a
  constructed composite, and each chapter says so once, in its own words, near the
  beginning.
- **No claim of accuracy about a guideline, an organization, or a published rule.**
  Where a named score or rule is used, describe the reasoning it formalizes, not its
  exact contents, and send the listener to the current source. Do not name national
  bodies, colleges, or societies as authorities.
- **Blame belongs to no one person.** These are errors of process, of framing, and of
  attention, not of carelessness. Write the failure as something that happened to
  competent people who were reasoning correctly inside a frame that was wrong, and
  write the correction as something any team could have made.

## Numbers, units and symbols

- Numbers are digits. Units are always written out in full: "38 degrees Celsius", "110
  beats per minute", "90 millimeters of mercury", "5 millimeters", "two liters",
  "12 hours", "3 days", "1 in 4".
- Never a unit abbreviation: not cm, mm, kg, mL, mg, bpm, mmHg, mmol, mg/dL, g/L. Write
  "millimeters of mercury", "beats per minute", "millimoles per liter", "milligrams per
  deciliter".
- Fractions as words: "one half", "one third", "two thirds". Ranges use "to": "10 to 15
  minutes", "36 to 38 degrees Celsius". Ratios are spoken: "one in four", "three times
  more likely".
- Symbols are never used: no per cent sign, equals, plus, minus sign, arrows, degree
  sign, ampersand, greater than, less than, or angle brackets. Write "per cent",
  "equals", "leads to", "degrees", "and", "greater than", "less than".
- Frequencies and routes are written out: "every six hours", "by mouth", "into a vein",
  "into the muscle", "under the skin", "into the airway". Never use the abbreviated
  forms, and never use the Latin abbreviations for them.
- Centuries and ordinals in words: "the nineteenth century", never "19th". A named score
  that contains a number is written the way it is spoken: "the Curb sixty-five score",
  "the Ottawa ankle rules", "the Wells score".
- Latin letters, ordinary punctuation, and the em dash in the heading only. Never
  Cyrillic, Greek, or mathematical characters.

## Acronyms, short forms and mnemonics

A synthesized voice reads an uppercase token as a sequence of letters. That is
sometimes what a clinician would say, and sometimes nonsense, so the rule depends on
what is spoken aloud.

- **Mnemonics that spell a word are written as words.** Never use capitals to spell a
  mnemonic.
- **Letter sequences that are spoken as letters keep their capital letters**, and this
  book tolerates only these: CT, MRI, ECG, ICU. Everything else is written out in full
  at every mention: "arterial blood gas", "intravenous", "intensive care unit" at
  first mention and ICU thereafter, "full blood count", "electrocardiogram" at first
  mention and ECG thereafter, "computed tomography" at first mention and CT
  thereafter, "magnetic resonance imaging" at first mention and MRI thereafter,
  "sepsis", "tuberculosis", "human immunodeficiency virus" and "HIV", "polymerase
  chain reaction" and "PCR".
- **Spell the term out at its first mention in each chapter, in full, then use the
  short form freely** where a short form is tolerated at all. A chapter is listened to
  on its own.
- **Never** WHO, never TB, never the abbreviations of drug classes, never a named
  score without spelling out how it is spoken.
