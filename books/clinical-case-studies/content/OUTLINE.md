# Clinical Case Studies — Section Briefs

One brief per section. The chapter must cover every point named here, in depth, and
must follow the contract in PLAN.md. Length and format rules live in PLAN.md, not
here.

Each chapter carries the same seven movements in the same order: the patient arrives,
the team meets and commits to a working diagnosis, the therapy begins, the nursing
follows, the therapy fails, the overlooked clue is found, and the approach changes and
the patient recovers. The surface of each case differs; the failure mode, the clinical
domain, the setting and the decisive nursing observation must differ too. No two
chapters may fail in the same way, and no two chapters may be solved by the same
observation.

---

## Chapter 1 — The Woman Who Fainted in the Garden

Margaret Doyle is 68, a retired school administrator who lives alone, and who has
been brought to the emergency department three times in six weeks because she was
found on the ground in her garden with no memory of the fall. The team is a cardiology
registrar, a consultant, and a nurse practitioner. The working diagnosis is vasovagal
syncope, and the treatment is a fluid and salt review, a reduction of the
antihypertensive that was increased at the last clinic, and a falls assessment with
the reassurance that this is benign. The nursing is a falls assessment, orthostatic
observations, telemetry and a lies-versus-stands blood pressure chart.

The therapy fails. She collapses a fourth time, this time on the ward, in front of a
nurse who counts the pulse during the episode. The overlooked clue was there from the
first hour: she never had a prodrome, she went pale and grey rather than flushed, and
in the third attendance she mentioned in passing that she had started buying new eye
drops for her glaucoma over the counter at the pharmacy, which nobody had asked
about, because nobody asks about eye drops. The truth is symptomatic bradycardia from
a beta-blocking eye drop absorbed through the eye in a woman who already had a
conduction problem, presenting as Stokes-Adams attacks. The approach changes: the eye
drops stop, the conducting system is assessed properly, and a pacemaker is implanted.
She is symptom-free afterwards. The teaching is that the absence of a prodrome is
information, that a collapse on a ward in front of a witness should be observed rather
than managed, and that a medication list built only from the prescription record is
not a medication list.

## Chapter 2 — The Breathless Woman After the Flight

Sophie Turner is 31, a teacher, who becomes breathless with a sharp pain in the
chest on the side of her breathing nine hours after a long-haul flight. The team is
an emergency department registrar, a consultant, and the nurse who takes her
admission. The working diagnosis is pneumonia, and the treatment is antibiotics,
oxygen, a chest radiograph and observation. The nursing is observations, oxygen
saturation measurement before and after walking, and a fluid and symptom chart.

The therapy fails. She is no better in forty-eight hours, her saturation falls when
she walks to the toilet, and the chest radiograph is clear. The overlooked clue was
written on the triage sheet in the first ten minutes: a swollen calf on the same
side, which the nurse attributed to the flight; the absence of fever, of cough and of
any consolidation; a heart rate out of proportion to the chest radiograph; a
combination oral contraceptive; a mother who died of a clot; and a note that the
triage nurse had written and nobody had read, because it sat below a line of
describe. The truth is a pulmonary embolism. The approach changes: anticoagulation
begun on the strength of the history and the examination rather than the
radiograph, with the leg measured and monitored, and she recovers over weeks. The
teaching is that a clear radiograph is a result and not an answer, that the chest
pain of a clot and the chest pain of pneumonia feel different and can be told apart,
and that a nurse who measures a calf writes down the measurement.

## Chapter 3 — The Asthma That Would Not Budge

Rachel Okonkwo is 27, a nursery nurse, who has been diagnosed with asthma since she
was nine and who attends the respiratory clinic for breathlessness three or four
times a week, for a reliever inhaler four times a day, and for waking at night. The
team is a respiratory consultant and a respiratory nurse. The working diagnosis is
poorly controlled asthma, and the treatment is a high dose of inhaled
corticosteroids, a spacer, a written technique check, and a peak flow diary. The
nursing is inhaler technique teaching at the bedside, the peak flow diary, and the
nurse asking about how the attacks start and how they end.

The therapy fails. Six weeks of perfect technique and perfect adherence change
nothing, and the nurse is left holding the box that the patient empties. The
overlooked clue is what the nurse heard with her own ears: the noise of the attack is
on the way in as well as on the way out, the voice is hoarse between attacks, the
patient cannot sing though she can walk to the shop, the attacks settle in minutes
without any inhaler, there is an urgent need to pass urine during them, and there has
never been an objective test of the diagnosis in eighteen years of asthma. The truth
is inducible laryngeal obstruction alongside genuine asthma, and the treatment that
changes everything is breathing and speech therapy with the inhaler treatment
continued for the asthma that is real. The teaching is that two conditions can share
one symptom and one name, that the absence of response to correct treatment is itself
a finding, and that the sound of breathing is worth listening to.

## Chapter 4 — The Hot Ankle

Barry Heslin is 62, a retired dockworker, whose right ankle has been hot, swollen and
agonizing for three days. He has no history of gout, no history of kidney stones, and
a history of type 2 diabetes that he manages with tablets and one injection. The team
is an emergency department registrar and a nurse practitioner. The working diagnosis is
gout, and the treatment is an anti-inflammatory, rest, ice and elevation. The nursing
is observations, temperature, the limb circumference chart, and the nurse checking
what the patient has been doing to his foot.

The therapy fails and the patient gets worse. The overlooked clue is in the nursing
observations rather than in the history: the temperature is 38.9 degrees Celsius, the
pulse is fast, the joint is exquisitely painful when moved passively even slightly,
the whole foot is involved rather than the joint alone, and the nurse finds a small
punctured wound on the sole from a thorn in the garden, and notes that the patient
cannot bear weight at all. The truth is septic arthritis of the ankle in a man with
diabetes, which is an emergency measured in hours, not days. The approach changes:
joint aspiration is done urgently, the fluid is sent for culture, antibiotics and
surgery follow, and the leg is saved, with physiotherapy afterwards. The teaching is
that gout does not make a person systemically unwell, that a hot joint is a joint
that needs a needle in it, and that the diabetes is the reason it went wrong.

## Chapter 5 — The Stone That Was Not a Stone

Alan Whitfield is 51, a warehouse supervisor, with severe pain in the upper abdomen
that started eight hours ago and has been vomiting since. The team is an emergency
department registrar, a surgeon on call, and the nurse who admits him. The working
diagnosis is renal colic, and the treatment is analgesia, an antiemetic, an
intravenous drip for hydration and a request for a kidney ultrasound. The nursing is
observations, a strict fluid balance chart, and hourly urine measurement.

The therapy fails. Eight hours later he is no better, he is vomiting what little he
takes, and the fluid balance chart that the nurse has kept without being asked shows
that he has passed very little urine. The overlooked clue is the direction the pain
travels and the way he behaves: the pain goes through to his back and he leans
forward to relieve it, and it does not come in waves, and he does not move about
searching for comfort, and there is no loin tenderness and no blood in the urine;
and he had gallstones on a scan done four years earlier, which was filed and never
read again. The truth is acute pancreatitis. The approach changes: the diagnosis is
named, the fluid and analgesia plan is rebuilt around the pancreatitis pathway, the
urine output is watched as the sign that matters most, and he recovers over a week,
with the gallstones dealt with later so the pancreatitis does not come back. The
teaching is that the direction pain travels is part of the examination, and that a
chart kept for no reason is often the chart that carries the answer.

## Chapter 6 — The Bellyache Too Severe to Touch

Samuel Nkemelu is 71, a retired civil servant, with abdominal pain and vomiting of
twelve hours standing. The team is a surgical registrar, an anaesthetist and an
emergency nurse. The working diagnosis is bowel obstruction, and the treatment is
nothing by mouth, intravenous fluid, an antiemetic, a nasogastric tube on suction,
and a plan for a computed tomography scan in the morning. The nursing is
observations, a fluid balance chart, and a nurse who keeps a pain score.

The therapy fails. In the night he becomes more confused, his lactate rises, and the
scan is still pending. The overlooked clue is the pair of observations that nobody
connected: the pain is out of proportion to the examination, so that the abdomen is
soft and barely tender while he describes the worst pain of his life, and he has an
irregularly irregular pulse that the nurse noted on the triage sheet and that nobody
passed on, because the focus was the abdomen. There is also blood in the vomitus.
The truth is acute mesenteric ischemia from an embolus in a man whose atrial
fibrillation was never in the referral letter. The approach changes: the scan is
brought forward, the surgeon is called at two in the morning, and the patient goes to
theatre, where a length of dead bowel is removed and the cause is dealt with, and
recovery on the ward is slow and is run by the nursing team. The teaching is that pain
out of proportion with a quiet abdomen is a diagnosis in itself, and that a pulse
irregularity on a triage sheet is a piece of information and not a line to be ruled.

## Chapter 7 — The Indigestion That Would Not Settle

Harold Pettigrew is 69, a retired postman, with six weeks of epigastric discomfort
and heartburn that is worse when he bends, and with four kilograms of weight that he
has lost and explained to himself as stress and to his doctor as a busy month. The
team is a general practitioner, a gastroenterology consultant, and a district nurse
who reviews him at home. The working diagnosis is gastritis or oesophagitis, and the
treatment is acid suppression, advice about eating late and lying down, and a review
in six weeks. The nursing is a weight on the scales, a food diary, and advice about
symptoms that should bring him back sooner.

The therapy fails because the therapy was never aimed at anything, and six weeks later
he returns with worse pain that now wakes him, and his weight has fallen further. The
overlooked clue is the weight, which was measured, charted, and then explained away by
everyone including the patient; and the fact that he had begun to cut out so many
foods that he was living on bread and tea, which he described as his new diet rather
than as a symptom. The truth is a stomach cancer, found at endoscopy because the
district nurse insisted that a fall of four kilograms in a man of 69 who had no
reason to lose weight was a test that had to be done. The approach changes: staging,
then surgery with recovery, and a change in how the service treats unexplained
weight loss in an older patient. The teaching is that weight loss is a sign and not a
symptom, that it is measured by somebody who writes it down, and that a symptom that
is explained away has not been explained, it has been tolerated.

## Chapter 8 — The Girl with the Stomach Ache

Amara Nwosu is 24, a barista, who has had abdominal pain and vomiting for two days
and has treated herself at home with oral rehydration salts. The team is an emergency
department registrar and a nurse. The working diagnosis is gastroenteritis, and the
treatment is oral rehydration, an antiemetic, analgesia and a plan to discharge her.
The nursing is observations, intravenous access, and the questions that a nurse asks
when she gets the patient settled.

The therapy fails, and the failure is visible in the observations rather than in the
complaint: she is pale and clammy, her pulse is fast, her blood pressure is low, and
she is worse sitting up than lying down. The overlooked clue is in the answer to a
question nobody had thought to ask: she has not had a period for eight weeks, she
uses an intrauterine device, and she is certain she cannot be pregnant because of
it. The nurse asks the question, the pregnancy test is positive, and the story of a
gastroenteritis ends. The truth is a ruptured ectopic pregnancy with blood in the
abdomen, and the treatment is resuscitation and emergency surgery, after which she
recovers and is taught, before she leaves, what to watch for. The teaching is that no
woman of reproductive age with abdominal pain and vomiting has gastroenteritis until
the pregnancy test is in the notes, that a coil does not prevent pregnancy, and that
the observations of a nurse who has taken a proper history are a diagnostic test.

## Chapter 9 — The Swelling That Was Not Heart Failure

Derek Wharton is 64, a retired electrician, whose legs have been swelling for three
weeks and who is now breathless climbing stairs. The team is a medical registrar, a
consultant cardiologist, and the ward nurse. The working diagnosis is congestive
heart failure, and the treatment is a diuretic, salt restriction, fluid restriction
and a daily weight. The nursing is a daily weight on the same scale, a strict fluid
balance chart, urine output measurement, and a leg circumference measured at the same
point every day.

The therapy fails in a way the chart makes obvious: after five days he has lost two
kilograms where he should have lost five, his weight has gone up by three, and his
swelling has spread to his back and his face. The overlooked clue is in the nursing
observations rather than in the examination: his urine is frothy, his neck veins are
not distended, his heart is not enlarged, his blood pressure is high rather than high
and falling, his lips are not blue, and he is not orthopneic. The truth is nephrotic
syndrome, and the treatment that changes matters is the kidney disease itself, with
the edema managed deliberately rather than by diuresis alone, because a patient who is
short of breath because his blood is thin needs a different answer from a patient who
is short of breath because his pump is failing. The approach changes, the edema
resolves over weeks, and the cause is found. The teaching is that the daily weight is
the single most useful number on the ward, and that an edema with a clear chest and
frothy urine is a kidney problem until proven otherwise.

## Chapter 10 — The Bruises That Were Not Bruises

Lisa Nowak is 34, a teacher, with bruising and small purple spots that came up over
two weeks, tiredness, and a headache that started three days ago. The team is a
haematologist, a registrar, and the nurse who takes her admission. The working
diagnosis is immune thrombocytopenia, and the treatment is a corticosteroid with
bleeding precautions and a plan for a platelet count. The nursing is bleeding
precautions, neurological observations, a temperature four times a day, and the
monitoring of urine output.

The therapy fails and the patient deteriorates. On the fourth day she is febrile,
her headache is severe, she is confused, and she is passing very little urine. The
overlooked clue is the combination that the team split up and gave to three different
specialties instead of reading as one: the headache and confusion attributed to low
platelets, the fever attributed to the steroid, the kidney failure attributed to
dehydration, and a blood film with fragmented red cells that was reported as showing
no blasts and filed. The truth is thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura, and the
treatment is plasma exchange, begun the same day, with the nurse running the
exchanges, caring for the line and balancing the volume in and out. She recovers, and
the follow-up describes the relapse risk and the warning signs. The teaching is that
purpura with fever, confusion and failing kidneys is one disease and not four, and
that a blood film must be read by someone who is looking for the wrong thing.

## Chapter 11 — The Acidosis That Was Called Sepsis

Gordon Mellor is 68, a retired printer with type 2 diabetes of twelve years, a
chronic kidney condition, and twelve years of metformin that nobody has reviewed.
He presents with three days of nausea, vomiting and breathlessness. The team is an
emergency department registrar, a nurse and an intensivist. The working diagnosis is
sepsis, most likely urinary, and the treatment is the sepsis bundle of fluids,
antibiotics and hourly observations. The nursing is a fluid balance chart, hourly
urine output, glucose monitoring and observations four times an hour.

The therapy fails, because the frame is wrong rather than the treatment. His blood
pressure holds, his temperature stays normal, his white cell count is only mildly
raised, and the nurse notices that he is breathing deeply and deliberately, which is
not the breathing of a person who is anxious or septic but the breathing of a person
who is acidotic. The overlooked clue is the medication: the nurse, reading the
chart, asks when the metformin was last reviewed and finds that his kidney function
has deteriorated and that his blood glucose is normal, which she had read as
reassurance and which is in fact what the drug does. The truth is lactic acidosis
associated with metformin in a man whose kidneys can no longer clear it, brought on
by vomiting and dehydration. The approach changes, the drug stops, the acidosis is
treated, the precipitant is treated, and he goes home with a plan for what to do when
he becomes ill. The teaching is that sepsis is a label that stops people asking
questions, and that a deep steady breathing pattern with a normal temperature deserves
the drug chart read again.

## Chapter 12 — The Woman Who Would Not Sit Still

Ruth Kelleher is 45, a school administrator, who has had palpitations, tremor, heat
intolerance and weight loss for six weeks, and who was started on an antidepressant
four weeks ago by her general practitioner for anxiety. The team is an emergency
department registrar, a nurse, and an endocrinologist who is called in the following
morning. The working diagnosis is anxiety with an arrhythmia, and the treatment is a
beta blocker, a sedative for the nights, and a cardiology referral. The nursing is
observations, a cardiac monitor, a thermometer, and a nurse who documents what she
sees.

The therapy fails and makes her worse. The overlooked clue is the heat: she is hot
and clammy, her pulse is fast and irregular, her hands are warm, she has a fine
tremor, her eyes are staring, and she has lost weight without trying. Her flushes
were called the hot flushes of the menopause, and she is 45, and she has had a viral
illness three weeks ago, and nobody took a history of her neck or her thyroid, and
the temperature that was recorded was attributed to anxiety. The truth is thyroid
storm, precipitated by the infection and unmasked by everything that was done for the
anxiety. The approach changes: the treatment for the storm is given under the
endocrinologist, the care moves to the intensive care unit, and she recovers over
days, with the underlying disease then treated properly. The teaching is that anxiety
does not explain a fever with a rapid irregular pulse, and that a woman of 45 with
palpitations and tremor is not a woman having hot flushes.

## Chapter 13 — The Woman Who Woke Up Shaking

Nadia Haddad is 52, a hospital cleaner, who wakes at three in the morning shaking,
sweating and confused, and whose partner brings her to the emergency department. She
has type 2 diabetes and attends a clinic for it. The team is an emergency department
registrar, a nurse, and later a specialist in diabetes and a surgeon. The working
diagnosis is a hypoglycaemic episode, and the treatment is glucose, food, and
advice. The nursing is bedside glucose checks through the night, a food and symptom
chart, and the taking of a full medication history by a nurse who asks what happens
between appointments.

The treatment works and the patient goes home, and then it fails again, three times
in a month, always in the early hours, always after a missed meal. The overlooked
clue is in the medication history that the nurse took in full: a sulfonylurea from
the diabetes clinic, prescribed at a dose that had been increased at a review she did
not attend, and a pattern of eating little during the day because of her shift
pattern. The first change is to stop the tablet, and the episodes stop for six weeks.
When they return, the team has to look further, and the imaging that was done the
first time, which found nothing, is done again with better preparation and finds a
small tumour in the pancreas. The truth is an insulinoma, and the treatment is
surgery, after which she is free of the episodes and is taught by the nursing team
how to recognise and treat a low glucose for the rest of her life. The teaching is
that a treatment that works and then stops working has still taught you something,
and that a medication list assembled at three in the morning, question by question,
is worth more than one assembled from the notes.

## Chapter 14 — The Migraine That Was Treated as a Stroke

Claire Dubois is 44, a teacher, who is admitted twice in three weeks with an episode
of numbness down one side of the body and disturbed vision, both of which resolved
within the day. The team is a stroke physician, a registrar, and the stroke unit
nurse. The working diagnosis is a transient ischemic attack, and the treatment is a
computed tomography scan, an antiplatelet drug, a statin, risk factor assessment, and
admission to a stroke unit. The nursing is neurological observations, mobility
assessment, and the taking of a detailed account of what happened.

The treatment fails and the patient is harmed by it: a further episode occurs on the
stroke unit, and she then has a gastrointestinal bleed. The overlooked clue is the
sequence of events, which the nurse reconstructed on the second admission from the
patient, her husband and her own diary. The episodes begin with a zig-zag of light
in one half of the visual field that lasts about twenty minutes, they are followed by
the numbness, they last over an hour, she vomits, she wants the lights off, and her
mother had the same thing called migraine all her life. The truth is migraine with
aura, and the treatment that changes matters is the correct acute and preventive
treatment, with the antiplatelet stopped after the bleed, because an antiplatelet
drug given for a migraine is a drug given for a stroke that never happened, with its
own risk, and the combined oral contraceptive is reviewed as well. The teaching is
that an aura is not a transient ischemic attack, and that a treatment which is not
indicated has harms of its own.

## Chapter 15 — The Fits That Stopped When She Was Distracted

Zoe Brennan is 22, a student, who is brought to the emergency department after her
third collapse in a month, each time accompanied by shaking that the family calls a
seizure. The team is a neurologist, a registrar, and a nurse who takes a full account
of the events from the patient and her flatmate. The working diagnosis is epilepsy,
and the treatment is an antiseizure medicine, driving advice, and a neurology
appointment. The nursing is neurological observations, a seizure chart, and the nurse
who writes down what an event actually looked like rather than what it was called.

The treatment fails, and it is the seizures that stop, not the patient who gets
better: the medicine causes drowsiness and low mood and the events continue. The
overlooked clue is in the description. The eyes stay closed throughout, the
movements are not rhythmic and change shape from moment to moment, the tongue is
never bitten, there is no incontinence, there is no confusion afterwards, and she can
describe what was happening while it happened. The episodes occur in the waiting room
and in the shop and never at home, and the last one stopped within seconds when her
flatmate took her phone and asked her to read the message. The truth is a functional
neurological disorder, which may exist alongside genuine epilepsy and is not the same
as pretending, and the treatment that changes matters is the removal of the
medicine under supervision, the explanation given carefully, the rehabilitation, and
a plan the family are taught, since how the family responds is part of the treatment.
The teaching is that the description of an event is a diagnostic instrument and that
the words used to report it decide what the team investigates.

## Chapter 16 — The Fit That Was Not a Fit

Callum Reid is 28, an electrician, who has a nerve block for a day-case operation on
his shoulder and who becomes agitated in the recovery room and then has a seizure.
The team is the anaesthetist, the surgeon, the recovery nurse, and the resuscitation
team. The working diagnosis is malignant hyperthermia, and the treatment is the drug
for malignant hyperthermia, the attempts to cool, and the theatre team called back in.
The nursing is recovery observations, the ECG, the blood pressure, and the nurse who
writes down everything the patient says in the minutes before the collapse.

The treatment fails because the diagnosis is wrong, and the ignored evidence is
almost all the work of one nurse. Her notes say that before he became agitated he
said the room was ringing, that his lips and tongue felt numb, that there was a
metallic taste, that he was anxious and could not settle, and that his arms were
twitching before the fit began. His temperature is normal, his muscles are not rigid,
and the carbon dioxide is not rising. The truth is systemic toxicity of the local
anaesthetic, from an inadvertent injection into a vessel during the block, and the
treatment is to stop everything, to support the airway, to control the seizure and to
give the intravenous lipid emulsion, which is why the nurse who recognized the
prodrome is the one who calls for it. He recovers without brain injury, and the
service changes how the block is done. The teaching is that the prodrome of local
anaesthetic toxicity comes before the collapse, and that the difference between two
catastrophes on a table is a set of observations taken before the worst moment
rather than after it.

## Chapter 17 — The Cough That Outlasted the Antibiotics

Grace Mwangi is 37, a teacher who was born and raised in a country where
tuberculosis is common and whose mother has been coughing for two years and losing
weight. She has had a cough, fever, weight loss and night sweats for eight weeks,
and two courses of antibiotics from a community clinic have made no difference. The
team is a respiratory consultant, a registrar, and the ward nurse who is assigned to
her. The working diagnosis is a resistant pneumonia, and the treatment is intravenous
antibiotics, a chest radiograph, and sputum for routine culture. The nursing is
observations, a temperature chart, sputum specimens requested early, repeatedly and
at night, and the exposure history taken properly.

The therapy fails, because the disease is not a bacterium and never was. The
overlooked clue is the history that nobody took in full and the observations that
everybody filed: the night sweats, which she mentions in passing as having to
change her nightdress, the weight loss, the mother, the classroom, the country of
origin, and the chest radiograph, whose apical shadowing was read at first as
patchy consolidation and was only looked at again when the patient did not improve.
The truth is pulmonary tuberculosis with cavitation, and the treatment is
combination antituberculous therapy, notification, isolation while the diagnosis is
confirmed, contact tracing of the household, and the education of the family that
the nurse gives over several days. She recovers over months, and the follow-up
describes what is watched for afterwards. The teaching is that a sputum specimen is a
procedure and not a box to be ticked, that a cough which has outlasted two courses of
antibiotics deserves a radiograph and a conversation about exposure, and that the
family is part of the patient.

## Chapter 18 — Five Days of Fever

Mei Lin Chow is 4, and has had a fever for five days, a rash that appeared on the
third day, and red eyes that her parents have assumed are from a cold. The team is a
pediatric registrar, a consultant, and the ward nurse who has been taking her
temperature. The working diagnosis is a viral illness with a viral rash, and then
measles, and the treatment is fluids, an antipyretic, and antibiotics for a possible
bacterial superinfection. The nursing is the temperature chart, the fluid balance
chart, and the systematic examination of a child who has been crying for five days.

The treatment fails and the child worsens. The overlooked clue is the examination
that the nurse has been doing every four hours and that nobody wrote up as a
pattern: the eyes are injected on both sides with no discharge and no crusting, the
tongue is red and the surface is bumpy like a strawberry, the lips are cracked and
red, the hands and feet are swollen and the skin of the palms is red, there is a
large lymph node in the neck, and the rash fades and returns, and there is no cough
and no runny nose to explain the eyes. The truth is Kawasaki disease, and the
treatment is intravenous immunoglobulin and aspirin under the pediatric team, with an
echocardiogram to look at the coronary arteries, and the fever is gone the next day,
which is both the diagnosis confirmed and the treatment working. The teaching is
that a fever lasting five days in a child is a diagnosis that has to be looked for,
and that the nurse who examines the hands, the eyes and the tongue of a febrile
child is doing the examination that makes the diagnosis.

## Chapter 19 — Agitation in a Lady of Eighty-Four

Evelyn Hart is 84, lives in a nursing home, and is admitted after a fall at home.
The team is a geriatrician, a registrar, and the ward nurse. The working diagnosis is
dementia with behavioural disturbance, and the treatment is an antipsychotic to
control her agitation, a review of her regular medicines, and a plan for a bed in the
ward. The nursing is the observation chart, the medication round, the toileting
routine, the reassurance of a woman who cannot find her way, and the nightly fight
about washing.

The treatment makes her worse. She is calmer for the first day and then cannot be
roused, she falls trying to reach the toilet at night, and the ward round describes
her as more confused than her admission. The overlooked clue is the shape of the
disturbance and the things nobody looked for: she is lucid in the morning and
confused at the evening, she is calling for a dog that died six years ago, she is
picking at the sheets, and she is incontinent for the first time in her life. Her
abdomen is hard and full and the bladder can be felt. She has not eaten. A new
antihypertensive was started three days before the fall. And she cannot bear weight
on the left leg and screams when the leg is moved. The truth is delirium on a
background of dementia, with several precipitants, including an occult hip
fracture, urinary retention, constipation, a new medicine and poor hydration, and
the treatment that changes everything is the opposite of the treatment that was
given, since the drug for the agitation was deepening the delirium and the fractured
hip was the thing making her scream. The approach changes, she improves within days,
and the ward writes down what it learned about fluctuation and about the clock.

## Chapter 20 — The Urine That Was Not an Infection

Joyce Fairweather is 82, who fell at home and was brought in, and whose triage nurse
records a positive dipstick for nitrites and white cells and, in the notes, writes
that there is no urinary symptom of any kind. The team is an emergency department
registrar, a nurse practitioner, and a nurse. The working diagnosis is a urinary
tract infection with confusion, and the treatment is intravenous antibiotics, fluids
and a plan for a urine culture. The nursing is observations, a fluid balance chart,
pain assessment, and the repeated examination that a confused older patient in the
emergency department requires.

The therapy fails. Forty-eight hours of antibiotics change nothing, and then she
becomes worse rather than better, and the confused state is put down to the
infection. The overlooked clue is that the dipstick was never an infection: there was
no frequency, no pain on passing urine, no fever, her temperature stayed normal,
her pulse was slow, her blood pressure was raised and she was alert and oriented, and
she was dry rather than flushed. And the leg was never examined. The nurse notices
that the left leg is short and turned outward, that the hip is painful when it is
moved, and that she will not put weight on it at all. The truth is a displaced
fracture of the neck of the femur, with a urine result that was always going to be
positive in an old woman with a catheter or with poor hygiene, and the treatment is
surgery, careful analgesia, hydration, and the prevention of delirium by getting her
up and moving, with the antibiotics stopped. The teaching is that a positive
dipstick is not an infection, and that a confused older person in the emergency
department needs their legs examined as well as their heart.

### Appendix A — Ten Ways a Reasonable Team Gets It Wrong

The twenty cases in this book fail in ways that are worth naming, because a failure

shows the pattern behind them: the early and the cheap explanation that fits the

assumed the improvement would continue on its own. Each is discussed in the abstract,
with the cases that illustrate it named in words, and with the countermeasure that
would have caught it. The appendix closes on the general point that none of these
failures is a failure of care, and that the defences against them are procedural
rather than personal.

### Appendix B — The Nursing Observation That Opened the Case

Every case in this book was turned by something a nurse did, and this appendix
collects those twenty acts and examines them. The pulse counted during a collapse in
the garden of a ward; the calf measured on the triage sheet; the inhaler technique
checked and the listening to the breath; the temperature of a hand and the joints
moved; the direction of the pain and the way a man sat; the weight on the same scale
every morning; the frothy urine noticed because the bedpan was being emptied; the
blood film pulled from the notes; the urine output on a chart nobody asked for; the
glucose at three in the morning; the account of an event written down in full instead
of in a word; the words the recovery nurse wrote in the minutes before the fit; the
sputum request that was not let go; the temperature chart of a child and the
examination of her hands and eyes; the observation that the confusion came and went
with the clock; and the leg that was examined when the urine was blamed. The
appendix sets out what makes such an observation work, which is that it is specific,
that it is written in words rather than in ticks, and that it is brought to the
team by someone who is prepared to be asked why it matters. It ends with the
argument that the nurse holds the longitudinal record of the patient, that the
record is the diagnosis, and that the teams that get these cases right are the teams
that read the notes.
