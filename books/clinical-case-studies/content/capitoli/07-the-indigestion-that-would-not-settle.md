# Chapter 7 — The Indigestion That Would Not Settle

Harold Pettigrew was sixty-nine years old, a retired postman, and this case is worth telling because of a number on a set of scales in a hallway. It is a constructed composite, assembled for teaching, and it does not describe a real patient; the details have been changed so that nobody could be recognised, and the only thing reported faithfully here is the reasoning. The reasoning that organises the story is simple to state and hard to carry out in a busy week: weight loss in an older person is a sign a clinician has observed, not a symptom the patient has reported, and the two behave in completely different ways. Pettigrew never told anybody that he had lost weight. He said he had been busy and had been worrying about his daughter, and both of those statements were true, and neither of them was the finding.

He came to the surgery on a wet Tuesday in the autumn with six weeks of discomfort in the upper part of his stomach and of heartburn behind his breastbone. The burning came in the evening after his meal, and it came again when he bent to tie his laces or to lift the heavy bowl the cat had knocked over, and lying flat after ten o'clock made it worse than sitting up did. There was no vomiting, no difficulty swallowing, and no blood in the mouth or in the stool, and he had never had an ulcer. He had come because his daughter had said that he was eating differently. When the general practitioner asked him about his weight he said he had not really noticed it, and then he said that his daughter had been going through a difficult year and that he had not been eating properly. The duration of a complaint like this is itself information, and how many weeks of it should prompt a wider look is set out differently in different services, so confirm the interval that matters against the current guideline and the local protocol before you build a plan on any particular number of weeks.

The alternative that had to be named out loud in that room, and was named out loud, was a cancer of the stomach, because it is the diagnosis that kills quietly and the one that is easiest to postpone. What would reveal it, if it were there, would be weight falling without any change of appetite or intention, iron deficiency anaemia on a full blood count, a firm mass felt in the upper abdomen, and at endoscopy a heaped and ulcerated area rather than the shallow reddened patches of gastritis. A bleeding ulcer was the other danger worth carrying in mind, and the finding that would give that away would be pain waking him in the night, or vomiting blood, or a black stool. Pettigrew had none of those at the first appointment, and that was genuine good fortune rather than good reasoning, because the first appointment contained everything that would have settled the question and nobody asked for it in the right form.

Dr Adrian Musgrave examined him and found a soft abdomen with no tenderness and no mass, and he wrote a note that read like a note about reflux. Before he wrote the treatment he weighed him on the practice scales, as he weighed everybody who walked in, and recorded the number. It was the only number in that consultation that was going to matter, it was written in the same hand as everything else, between the blood pressure and the plan, and nobody looked at it again that day. A weight on its own is an unremarkable number, and how much change matters depends on the person's own baseline, on age, and on whether the change was noticed or merely measured; check the current guideline and the local protocol for the threshold your own service treats as significant rather than assuming one from this account.

Dr Musgrave ranked his thinking out loud, and the ranking was reasonable. The most likely explanation was oesophagitis, the inflammation of the tube that carries food from the mouth down to the stomach, which fitted his symptoms, his timing and his habit of eating late and lying down afterwards. Behind that came gastritis, an inflammation of the stomach lining, and behind that a peptic ulcer, a sore in the stomach or in the first part of the small bowel, which can bleed and can perforate. Fourth on the list, and the one that could not simply be set aside on the evidence available, was the cancer he had named aloud, and it sat fourth not because the evidence against it was strong but because there was an explanation for everything else. The working diagnosis was gastritis with oesophagitis, and the reason for that choice was audible and reasonable: the heartburn had the textbook shape, the examination was reassuring, and a man who is uncomfortable for some weeks and otherwise well is rarely in danger today. The hole in that reasoning was not in the ranking but in the explanation he had been given for the weight, which made the weight invisible rather than irrelevant.

The treatment was acid suppression, which is the right treatment for reflux and for gastritis and which many people find genuinely helpful, and it was begun straight away. He was told to stop eating late in the evening, to leave a good gap before bed, to raise the head of the bed, and to give up the bottle of cider he had been having with his supper and had not mentioned until it was asked about. He was told he would be reviewed in six weeks with a gastroenterology consultant, and that he should come back sooner if anything changed. The advice about returning sooner came on a printed sheet on which the items about bleeding and about weight sat among two dozen unemphasised phrases, and he folded it into his wallet behind his bus pass and did not read it again. The interval, and the set of warning signs that should bring a patient back before it ends, is set out in the current guideline and the local protocol for your own service, so confirm it there rather than working from memory.

Meanwhile a district nurse had been asked to visit, because the suggestion that weight and food intake should be recorded at home had come from the practice nurse as a routine thing for a patient who was not clearly improving. Her name was Carole Winslow, and she was to hold the thread of this case without ever holding the title. On her first visit she asked about the day, the food, the sleep, the bowels and the medicines, and she put him on the scales in his kitchen and wrote the number down with the date beside it. She also gave him a food diary, a single page with columns for what he ate and when, and explained that it was not a test of him but a way of seeing the pattern. He took the diary seriously and filled it in faithfully for week after week, and she read every page, and she noticed something in it that nobody else was looking for.

What she noticed in the diary was that the food had been narrowing. Week by week the entries became shorter, and what was left on the lines was bread and tea, with butter if she was lucky. There was no meat, no vegetables and no fruit, and there were days when the entry said simply nothing eaten. When she asked about it he said that he had started a new diet, and he said it cheerfully, and he explained that cutting out the heavy fatty food had helped the heartburn and that he was going to keep it up. She asked whether he had noticed any change in his weight and he said he thought he had lost a bit, and then he said that he was not really a man who weighed himself. The new diet was not a diet. It was a symptom, and it was the symptom of a disease that was slowly taking his appetite away, and he had relabelled it as a success and was proud of it.

What she wrote in her own notes that day matters more than what she said. She wrote the weight, and the previous weight, and then in the free text she wrote the sentence that would turn out to be the hinge of the whole case: that he appeared to be losing weight steadily, that he was eating very little, and that this was not being accounted for. That sentence went into her district nursing record, where it was visible to anyone who opened the file. It did not reach the surgery, and it did not reach Dr Musgrave, and the reason was not that anybody had decided it was unimportant. The reason was that nothing in the way the service was organised asked for it to travel anywhere, and a note that stays in the file where it was written is a note that has been recorded but not used. Nothing in that was a decision by any person; it was simply the shape of the service, and any team working inside that shape would have behaved the same way.

For the length of the planned interval the arrangement did what arrangements do, and nothing happened, and nothing happening felt like the treatment working. Pettigrew took his acid suppression and was, by his own account, somewhat better in the evenings, so the improvement was as real as it was uninformative. He did not come back, so the advice about returning sooner never had to be tested, and Dr Musgrave did not see him at all. Carole Winslow visited repeatedly through that period and each time she weighed him and each time the number was lower, and each time she told herself that this was the kind of complaint that settled down in the end.

There was a moment in that period when the service could easily have gone on doing what it was doing, and it nearly did. Dr Musgrave had a list of patients waiting, a review date already in the diary, and no new information reaching him, and the tempting thing was to see him at the planned appointment and, if the heartburn had improved, discharge him. Carole Winslow had a chart of falling numbers and a patient who was cheerful and a theory he was pleased with, and the tempting thing for her was to accept the explanation, because accepting it was comfortable and because she had no route to offer anything else. What saved them was not a flash of insight but the shape of the arrangement: the review appointment was still in the diary, and a man who came back early and unwell could not be sent home to wait for it.

He came back to the surgery himself, without an appointment, eight weeks after the first consultation rather than six, and he came because the pain had begun to wake him in the small hours of the night, which it had never done before. He was sick in the morning for the first time since this started. He could feel a lump in the upper part of his stomach when he pressed on it, and his clothes had gone loose enough that his daughter had commented on it. His skin was pale, there was a small firm swelling under the skin at the base of his neck on the left side, and his blood pressure was lower than the one recorded in the autumn. None of this was expected by anybody, and the return arrived as a bundle of new findings rather than as the single change that should have triggered a rethink. The interval that should have applied to a man of this age with a falling weight is set out in the current guideline and the local protocol.

Dr Musgrave listened to all of it and understood, before he laid a hand on him, that the case had changed shape. What he had spent the intervening weeks treating was a symptom he had never actually confirmed, and he now had a man of sixty-nine with a mass, a falling blood pressure, a new lump above the collarbone and a history of night pain, and those together were not reflux. He said so to the patient in plain words, which took some doing, and he said that the earlier plan had been built on the wrong idea and that nobody had done anything wrong in the sense of the word he could use. He examined him again thoroughly, and then he did something he had not thought of doing before: he went back through the interim notes, including the district nursing record, and he found Carole Winslow's sentence about the weight.

What Carole Winslow did that afternoon was the part of the case that mattered most. The practice had asked her to bring in the weight chart and the food diary so that the entries could be copied into the notes, and she came with both, and she found him in a room with a doctor who was talking about a mass. It was she who read the numbers out loud, because nobody else in the room had seen them together: the weight at the first visit, the weight at every visit since, the fall of four kilograms across the whole period, and then the diary with its shrinking entries and its repeated blank days. And it was she who said the sentence that changed the case, which was that a loss of four kilograms in a man of sixty-nine with no reason to lose it was not something to be explained away but a test that had to be done, and that she had written it down at the time and nobody had asked her to send it anywhere. Whether a loss of four kilograms in a man of that age counts as significant is a question with different answers in different services, and the number that should be acted on should come from the current guideline and the local protocol rather than from this account. What does not depend on the answer is the shape of the argument, which is that the number was real, that it was moving in one direction, that it was written down, and that it was never read by anybody in a position to act on it.

The gastroenterology consultant, Dr Hester Danbury, saw him that afternoon and did not leave the diagnosis until the week after next. She took a full history and examined him, and she asked the question that nobody had asked since the autumn: what was he managing to eat, and how had that changed. She asked it because the answer would decide whether this was still a benign peptic disease, and she wanted the answer before she reached for the tube rather than after. She also asked about smoking, about his appetite and about his family history, and what he had done about the pain when it woke him, and she heard the answer that he had done nothing because he believed the diet was working. Her working diagnosis was a cancer of the stomach with an obstructing component and probable spread beyond the stomach, and she said so to Dr Musgrave in those words and in front of the patient, because she had learned that a person who is going to have cancer would rather hear it early and clearly.

A full blood count was taken before anything else was done, and it came back as she expected: a haemoglobin well below the reference range for a man of his age, with small red cells that go with a chronic loss of blood, which is the picture of an iron deficiency anaemia rather than of a recent bleed. That number is an illustration of reasoning rather than a threshold, and reference ranges for haemoglobin differ between laboratories and between populations, so the value that matters should be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. An endoscopy was arranged for the following morning as an urgent one, and the reason for the urgency was written into the note. The reason was that a man of sixty-nine with a mass, iron deficiency anaemia and a history of night pain has a stomach that must be looked at.

She did it herself the next morning, and the finding is the one that changes everything. Instead of the shallow reddened stomach she had expected, there was an area in the lower part of the stomach that was ulcerated and heaped up at the edges, with a raised and irregular rim and a floor that was not clean granulation but dirty, necrotic tissue. That is not the shape of anything that acid suppression will ever heal, and an experienced endoscopist can often tell from the look of it whether a change is invading more deeply than the stomach wall. She took biopsies from the edge and from the base, sent them to the laboratory as urgent, and told the ward staff and the recovery nurse what she had found, so that the news would be given by a team and not by accident on a round.

The histology came back as an adenocarcinoma, a gland-forming cancer of the stomach, which is the commonest cancer of that organ and the one that behaves in this way. The diagnosis was given by Dr Danbury with the gastroenterology consultant present, in plain language, and with the next steps written down so that nobody had to hold them all in their head at once. Pettigrew asked whether he would be all right, and she answered honestly, telling him what the treatment could achieve and what it could not achieve without softening either half of it. Staging followed with a computed tomography, or CT, scan of the chest, the abdomen and the pelvis, and it showed a tumour in the stomach with enlarged lymph nodes around it, a small volume of fluid in the abdomen, and disease in the liver. The case went to a meeting where the images were discussed and a plan was agreed: he would have a form of surgery to control the bleeding, the obstruction and the vomiting, and treatment for the cancer that had spread would be given alongside it. The meeting was not about blame, and there was nothing to blame, and what it was about was the fact that a man with a mass and iron deficiency anaemia had spent a planned interval on an acid suppression tablet and a food diary.

He went to theatre within days, and the surgeon explained beforehand what the surgery would do and what it would not do, because the difference between controlling a tumour and curing it is the hardest conversation in this kind of medicine and it has to be had properly rather than hopefully. He came through the operation and the days after it, the recovery was slow in the way that this recovery always is, and it was run day to day by the nursing team on the ward. He needed a great deal of fluid, the wound was watched, his pain was managed, and by the time he was eating he was being taught how to manage his own symptoms at home. The cancer treatment was explained to him simply enough that he could repeat it to his daughter, who had been the one to notice the loose clothes.

Carole Winslow was there for the discharge, and she did the things a district nurse does, but the conversation was a different one from the one she had had in the same kitchen a few months before. She asked him what he wanted to be told if the pain came back, and what she should telephone her about, and wrote down the numbers she would need. She gave him a sheet again, this time with the warning signs that mattered for him rather than the generic ones, and she asked him to promise that he would ring rather than wait. Then she did something that had not been part of her job before and became part of it afterwards: she went back to the surgery and asked what the service intended to do about unexplained weight loss in an older patient, and she and the general practitioner wrote down what it would do.

The rule they wrote says that an unexplained loss of weight in an older adult is a finding that travels, rather than a note that waits. The weight is to be recorded with the same care as a temperature, to be compared with the previous number rather than with a memory of it, and a fall which is not understood is to send the patient back to the door of the service rather than forward to the next appointment. The person who notices does not have to be the person who treats, and the weight is discussed where the nurse's chart is part of the record. Whether the threshold is a particular number of kilograms, a proportion of body weight, or a rate of loss over a given period is set out in the current guideline and the local protocol, and those thresholds should be checked rather than invented.

There is a second lesson underneath the first, and it is the one that generalises furthest. A symptom that is explained away has not been explained; it has been tolerated. Pettigrew's new diet, his busy month, his worry about his daughter, the improvement in his evening heartburn on the tablet: each of these was a reasonable thing to believe, and together they formed a frame through which everything else was read and found compatible. The frame was held by the doctor, by the patient, by the nurse who liked him, and by a service that had a slot for reviewing reflux and no slot for reviewing a falling weight. The thing that would have broken it fastest was the most ordinary thing in medicine, which is somebody writing down a number and somebody else reading it.

The general practitioner's difficulty is not ignorance, and the difficulty is not unusual. Weight is a noisy measurement, because it changes with the season, the shoes, the time of day and the mood of the person on the scale, and a number that moves for ordinary reasons is a poor basis for alarming anybody. The safeguards against this are procedural rather than personal. The number is written where the next clinician will see it without needing to be asked, and the service must have somewhere for the nurse to send an observation that worries her. A community nurse who has found something troubling and has no route to send it will learn, over time, to stop finding things that trouble her, and the amount of change your own service acts on should come from the current guideline and the local protocol.

The people carrying this case were not careless, and none of them can honestly be said to have failed. Dr Danbury asked the question that had been avoided, and she asked it early enough for the answer to matter. Carole Winslow noticed what was in the diary and in the numbers and refused to let it be explained away, and the refusal was not cleverness but attention. What made the difference was not brilliance. It was that somebody weighed him on the same scale, wrote it down, and said out loud what the number meant.

To summarize, this case turned on a number that everybody had seen and nobody had read, and the diagnosis that was wrong was reasonable at the moment it was made and then became a frame that made everything after it invisible. The patient was given a treatment aimed at nothing in particular, so that nobody could say what success would look like or when failure had arrived, and he was given a review date that functioned as a licence to wait. The overlooked clue was never hidden: it was a weight falling steadily, a food diary narrowing to bread and tea, and a symptom the patient had relabelled as a success, all of it recorded by a community nurse who had nowhere to send it. The correction came when the chart was brought into the room and read aloud, when the abdomen was examined again, and when the diagnosis was named out loud so that the treatment could finally be aimed at it. The lessons are that weight loss in an older person is a sign that somebody observed and must be carried to somebody who can act on it, and that a symptom which has been explained away has not been explained but tolerated. Any team with a nurse who is heard, a record that is read, and a plan with a target could have made this correction, and nothing in it required a cleverer physician or a luckier patient than the ones who were there.
