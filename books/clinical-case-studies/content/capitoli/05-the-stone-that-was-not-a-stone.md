# Chapter 5 — The Stone That Was Not a Stone

Alan Whitfield arrived at the emergency department at nine in the evening with pain in the upper abdomen that had started eight hours earlier and had never stopped. He had been vomiting for most of that time, and he was 51 and supervised a distribution warehouse. He had driven himself in, because his wife was on an evening shift. What he said about the pain matters more than anything else in this chapter, so it is worth hearing. He said the pain was not in one place, that it went through to his back, and that it did not come and go. Then he was sick into a bowl, and the registrar stopped writing and let him finish.

This is a constructed composite, and none of it describes a real person; the details are assembled from the way these presentations arrive, and real patients are always messier than any illustration. The department was busy that evening, with the night team still in handover and the formal ward round not yet begun. How many hours a pain has lasted, and how old the patient is, both feed the decision about waiting or acting. The figures that count as urgent differ between conditions and between services, so confirm them against the current guideline and the local protocol.

Hannah Ferris was the senior sister on the resuscitation side, and she took the history and the observations herself. She asked him to put his own finger on the pain, and he laid his hand flat over the upper middle of his abdomen and said that it went back towards his spine. She asked whether it came in waves, and he said no, that it had been steady from the start, and that leaning forward helped it while lying flat made it worse. She asked about his urine, and he said he had passed a small amount in the morning and nothing since. She wrote it in her own words, and she asked the questions that the form did not have.

Dr Marcus Aldridge was the emergency department registrar, and he examined Alan Whitfield with the curtains drawn. He palpated lightly before deeply, so that he knew how much the patient objected to. There was tenderness in the upper abdomen and a little guarding, but the abdomen was not rigid and had no rebound tenderness. He looked for the hernia, the old scar and the groin, and found none. He put a hand under the loin and asked whether it hurt when he pressed there. It did not, and that was the moment the stone became a little less likely, and nobody said so.

The list he made was a stone, a perforated peptic ulcer, pancreatitis and a bowel obstruction, ranked by how often each came through the door in this shape. A perforated ulcer was the dangerous one, and he said what would have shown it: a rigid, board-like abdomen, an onset that was sudden rather than drifting, and free air under the diaphragm on the film already taken. A stone was the most likely by a long way, because it is the commonest cause of severe pain with vomiting in a man of this age. Pancreatitis sat in the same list, and the finding that would have separated it from a stone was already written in the nursing notes. It was read as radiation, because radiation is what stones do.

The treatment was chosen quickly, and it was not wrong on the evidence available at that hour. He was given analgesia strong enough to control the pain, an antiemetic, and intravenous fluid for the vomiting and the dehydration. A request was put in for a kidney ultrasound that would confirm the stone and show whether it was obstructing. Mr Julian Ashworth, the surgeon on call, was telephoned for advice, because the registrar wanted a surgical opinion on the abdomen. He agreed with the plan, and the ultrasound went on the morning list.

What Hannah Ferris did next is the other half of the treatment, and she did it without being asked, which is why it worked at all. She started a strict fluid balance chart, so that every litre that went in and every millilitre that came out was written down with a time against it. She measured the urine from a bottle and recorded the volume every hour, because a man who is vomiting and is being given fluid needs to know whether his kidneys are working. She also set the pain score going, at rest and on movement, because pain that is being treated needs to be judged rather than described.

In the first hour the entries on her chart began a pattern she could not yet describe but could see. What came out was small amounts of vomit, and the urine she measured went onto the chart at the end of his bed and not onto the observations the team would read in the morning. Alan Whitfield sat up on the trolley with his arms folded over the edge and said that this was the position that made it easiest. He stayed like that most of the night without being asked to move, and when she asked whether he had tried other positions he said sitting forward was the only thing that helped.

Dr Aldridge came back at twenty past eleven and found what he expected: a man still in pain. He checked the observations and the abdomen, found both unchanged, and wrote that the patient was stable. The interesting part is not that he was wrong but that the diagnosis had become a frame rather than a hypothesis. Once a stone had been chosen, every observation that arrived was read as a stone. The vomiting was what stones make people do, and the failure to change was read as a large stone, or a stoic patient, or insufficient analgesia. What nobody did was ask whether a stone would explain a man who had passed almost no urine.

The ultrasound was still on the morning list, and when the registrar considered bringing it earlier he found the request had gone in as routine, and that moving it would mean waking the imaging staff. He decided against it because the patient was stable, which was reasonable, and it is the kind of decision a busy night produces without anyone noticing a decision has been taken. The handover between the evening team and the night team was where the working diagnosis became fact, because what compresses best is a simple noun. Stone travelled from one pair of hands to the next until it was no longer a possibility but a thing happening to a man on a trolley.

The observations through the night were unremarkable, and Hannah Ferris wrote them on the hour without drama: the pulse regular, the blood pressure steady, the temperature not raised, the breathing normal, and the oxygen saturation good on air. His pain score sat high and did not move, and the score on movement was worse than the score at rest, and she recorded both each time. He was sick at eleven and again in the small hours, and each time she recorded the volume and the colour and settled him back into his position. How often a patient is observed, and how long a deterioration takes to show, differ between wards, so check the frequency and the interval against the current guideline and the local protocol.

The hourly urine volumes were the other thing she wrote down: small, and staying small, hour after hour, while the fluid going in did not slow. By the small hours the arithmetic on the chart was stark, because the column for what went in was full and the column for what came out was nearly empty. She did not think of it as a discovery but as a discrepancy, of the kind explained by a patient drinking less than he says or a drip running slow, and she checked both. Neither was true. He was simply passing very little urine, and the chart said so, in her handwriting, with nobody reading it.

By three in the morning the night team had settled into the fact that this was a stone, and the settling was almost physical. The handover had said stone, the ward round would say stone, and the man was as comfortable as a man with a stone could be. The temptation at that hour was not to be clever but to be comfortable: to increase the analgesia so the night would pass, to accept the vomiting as a stubborn feature of a stone, and to treat the chart as a nuisance rather than a finding. The intervals at which a patient who has not improved is reassessed, and at which a scan is brought forward, are local decisions, and should be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol rather than assumed.

At about half past four the registrar came back, because Alan Whitfield had been sick again, and this time he stayed longer. The pain score was unchanged, the vomiting had continued, the abdomen was as it had been, and the observations were still unremarkable. He said that the analgesia needed increasing and that the scan needed to be brought forward. Hannah Ferris said that it had been in the notes all night that he had passed almost no urine, and she said it while holding the chart, without any edge in her voice. She did not say that she had been waiting hours for somebody to look, because a colleague who has kept a record for eight hours has more claim to be heard than one who has just arrived.

He took the chart and read it from the top down. At the bottom, in her handwriting, was the total, and the total was a fraction of what had gone in. He asked how long the chart had been running, and she said since before his drip was attached. He asked who had asked her to start it, and she said nobody had. Then he said the thing that changed the case, and he said it to her. A stone blocking one kidney should not stop the other kidney from working, he said, and whatever this man had, it was not simply a stone.

A stone that obstructs produces colic, because urine cannot pass, but only the kidney on that side is affected. A urine volume that has collapsed while fluid is going in freely is not the picture of one obstructed ureter. It is the picture of a body that is not distributing the fluid it is being given, which points to the circulation, or to an organ that has swollen and blocked itself, and both are graver than a stone. The nurse had been writing numbers onto a chart for hours without interpreting them, and the registrar had been reading a story for hours without checking a number. Neither of them had touched the same evidence.

Then they went back to the bed, and Hannah Ferris took him through the movements again, slowly, because watching an answer is worth more than writing one down. She asked him to point to the pain again, and he put his hand over the upper middle of his abdomen and said it went through to his back. Then she asked the question nobody had asked: why had he not moved about, when everybody with a stone is up and down the ward unable to settle? He said that this pain was like being held, and that holding still was the only thing that helped.

The examination that followed undid the diagnosis. There was no tenderness over the loin when the kidney was pressed from behind, which in a stone large enough to cause this much vomiting is unusual. The urine dipstick showed no blood, and an obstructed stone causing pain this severe would usually be bleeding somewhere in the system. The pain was not coming in waves but was steady at the level of the upper abdomen. The abdomen was not rigid and there was no temperature. Each of these had been available from the first hour and each had been read as a stone, because the stone had been chosen first and the absences were treated as confirmation.

Nobody blamed anybody, and that was not politeness but accuracy. It was a competent team reasoning correctly inside a frame that was wrong, and everybody who touched the case was doing what any reasonable colleague would have done. The frame had been built properly, from a common disease, from a presentation that did fit it, and from the absence of features that pointed elsewhere. What a frame does is turn those absences into support. Any team could have made this correction, and any team might have made it a day earlier, because the correction was not a matter of cleverness but of attention. Attention is a property of the system rather than of the people inside it.

Then somebody asked the question nobody had thought to ask in eight hours, which was whether Alan Whitfield had ever had anything like this before. Hannah Ferris went to look at the whole folder rather than the top sheet. In the notes from four years earlier, filed at the back, there was a report from an abdominal scan, read, signed and filed. It said, in a line nobody had looked at since it was typed, that there were gallstones in the gallbladder and that they were not causing symptoms at the time. Alan Whitfield had had stones in his gallbladder for four years and nobody had ever told him, because a report that says not causing symptoms is filed in the way a report nobody needs is filed.

The bloods taken on admission were reviewed, and the pancreatic enzymes that had not been thought to ask for were added and sent. An urgent ultrasound was arranged with the imaging staff. The enzymes came back raised, and the ultrasound showed a gallbladder full of stones and an enlarged, inflamed pancreas, and the diagnosis was named: acute pancreatitis, with gallstones as the cause. Mr Ashworth came back to the department, and when he was told the working diagnosis had changed he did not ask why the team had been so certain for so long. He asked instead what would be needed to change the plan now, which is the better question.

It is worth setting out exactly how the mistake was made, because a mistake without a cause cannot be avoided a second time. The first cause was that the diagnosis was chosen before the examination had finished, so that the examination became a search for support rather than a test of a proposal. The second was that absences were read as reassurance, so that a quiet abdomen and a urine without blood became evidence for the stone rather than against it. The third was that a nursing chart was treated as a record of nursing rather than as clinical information. The fourth was that a result filed four years ago was never read again, though it would have changed the diagnosis.

The plan was rebuilt around the pancreatitis pathway, and the rebuild was more than a new label on a chart. The intravenous fluid given to correct a dehydration became the treatment in its own right, because the deficit in this illness is large and the inflamed pancreas leaks fluid around it. The fluid was titrated to the urine that came out. The analgesia was continued and actively reviewed, because the pain of pancreatitis is severe and does not resolve by itself. The surgical team took on the question of the gallbladder, because the pancreatitis will return while the stones remain, and that question was deferred until Alan Whitfield was well and recorded as a plan. The volume of fluid given in this illness differs between patients and between protocols, so confirm it against the current guideline and the local protocol.

Hannah Ferris rebuilt the nursing plan on the same chart, because the chart had already proved it was worth having. Urine output became the number the ward was watching, measured and written down every hour, because in this illness the kidneys and the circulation are what the fluid is being given for. The blood pressure, the pulse and the breathing were watched as signs of a patient being overwhelmed, and the temperature was watched because a fever here means something has gone wrong in another direction. She set herself thresholds for calling the registrar, and told Alan Whitfield what she was watching for, and that the number she cared about most was the one in the bottle on the trolley.

The change was visible within hours, which is the best evidence that the diagnosis had been wrong rather than the treatment. The pain came under better control, the vomiting stopped, and by the morning the urine output had risen from the trickle of the night before to a volume the registrar looked at twice, the first entry in the whole admission that looked the way it was supposed to look. The temperature stayed down, the pulse settled, and the abdomen was less tender than it had been at midnight. The target for urine output in this illness is written down in the current guideline and the local protocol, and it is not a figure to be trusted from memory, so confirm it against both before you rely on it.

The ultrasound and the surgeon's review together made the plan concrete. The gallbladder was full of stones, the pancreas was swollen, and there was fluid around it, and the surgeon's view was that the stones had passed through the ducts and set the pancreas alight, a day before Alan Whitfield reached the department. The stones themselves were not the emergency; the emergency was the inflammation and what it did to the vessels around the pancreas and to the fluid balance of the body. He would come back for the gallbladder to be dealt with once the pancreas had settled, because a patient whose stones remain after a first attack is a patient who will have another. The interval at which that surgery is done depends on the recovery and on the service, so confirm the timing against the current guideline and the local protocol.

He recovered over the following week, and the recovery was slow and dull, and dull was what the ward wanted. He began to eat, and he and Hannah Ferris developed a routine in which she weighed him, measured his urine, and asked him about his pain. On the day he was discharged they went through the plan for the gallbladder and the signs that would bring him back in a hurry, and she wrote them on a sheet he would put on the fridge at home. She made sure he could say them back before she let him go. The team took the case apart afterwards, and what came out of it was not a search for whoever had missed something, but a list of the places where a question could have been asked and was not.

The chart is the part of this story that generalises most easily, and it is worth saying why it worked. It was started for no reason at all, other than that the nurse who started it believed a patient being given fluid and vomiting ought to have a record of what went in and what came out, and that this was not somebody else's decision to make. It was kept in hours rather than in days, so that it showed a trend rather than a total, and a trend is what reveals a mismatch. Nobody had asked for it and nobody had reviewed it for most of the night, and it did its work anyway, because a number written down at the time is still there in the morning, and a number only in somebody's head is not. The teaching in that is not that nurses hold the answers and doctors hold the diagnoses, but that a record nobody is asked to keep and nobody is asked to read is doing nothing at all.

Alan Whitfield was unremarkable about the whole thing, and his last comment before he went was that the thing he would have wanted somebody to notice was where the pain went. He had said it at the beginning and it had not been picked up, and he had spent the night beside a chart he could not see that somebody was keeping for him. He also said that the worst hour was not the pain but being sick for the fourth time and being told to wait for the scan, because waiting felt like not being taken seriously. Both remarks were about the experience of being ill rather than the biology of the illness. They were the reason the ward agreed that every patient with abdominal pain would be asked out loud where the pain goes, and what makes it worse and what makes it better.

None of this was a failure of care, and it is worth saying so plainly, because the story of a wrong diagnosis is usually told as a story about somebody being wrong, and it was not. The pattern is one of the commonest there is: a common disease in a common shape, an examination performed to confirm rather than to test, absences read as reassurance, a chart kept but not interpreted, and a result filed where nobody would look again. What makes these dangerous is that they arrive together and reinforce one another. By the small hours the evidence for the stone was in the room and the evidence against it was in the room too, and only one of them was being read. The nurse had written it down, the surgeon had seen it, and the registrar had the notes in his hand more than once.

The teaching of the case is simple and it is about the examination. The direction in which pain travels is part of the examination, not a detail to be collected once the diagnosis is known; it is one of the things that makes it. A stone in the ureter produces pain that comes from the side and goes down towards the groin, and it comes in waves, so that a patient with one cannot sit still. An inflamed pancreas produces a pain high in the middle of the abdomen that goes through to his back, that is relieved by leaning forward, and that a patient protects by staying still. The reason a team misses this is not that the sign is obscure but that it is asked about only in the first ten minutes, when the diagnosis is still a possibility.

The countermeasure, then, is not to be cleverer but to be more systematic at the moment the diagnosis is chosen, and any team could do it. Write down the working diagnosis and the two findings that would prove it wrong, and go looking for those two findings deliberately, in the first hour, while they are still cheap to find. Treat an absent sign as information rather than as the absence of information, and read the nursing chart as clinical information rather than as a record of nursing. Ask the patient where the pain goes, and what makes it better and what makes it worse, and take the answers at face value when they disagree with the expected pattern. And when a treatment has not worked by the end of a night shift, treat that not as a failure of the treatment but as the strongest single piece of information available, because the patient who has not improved has not disappointed anybody, he has corrected somebody.

To summarize, Alan Whitfield's pain was not a stone at all but acute pancreatitis caused by gallstones that had been sitting in a report filed four years earlier. The team treated a common disease competently and wrongly for eight hours, because the evidence that pointed elsewhere was quiet rather than dramatic. The failures were procedural rather than personal: a diagnosis chosen before the examination had finished, absences read as reassurance, a chart kept without being interpreted, and a result filed where nobody would look at it again. The nursing observation that turned the case was a strict fluid balance chart started without being asked, together with the posture of a man who would not move and whose pain went through to his back, because a stone blocking one kidney should not stop the other from working. Every number quoted in this chapter is an illustration of reasoning only, and each must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol before it informs the care of a real patient. This chapter is educational material, not medical advice and not a protocol, and it does not replace the training, supervision or judgment of the clinician who was with the patient. And the lesson that travels furthest is a small one: where the pain goes is part of the examination, and a chart kept for no reason is often the chart that carries the answer.
