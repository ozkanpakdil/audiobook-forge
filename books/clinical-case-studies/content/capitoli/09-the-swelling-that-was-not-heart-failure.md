# Chapter 9 — The Swelling That Was Not Heart Failure

The first number Rhona Sinclair wrote on Derek Wharton's chart was his weight, and she wrote it on the same scale, in the same corner of the ward, at the same hour every morning, because a weight taken on two different scales on two different days is not a weight but an opinion. He was sixty-four, a retired electrician with thick forearms and a habit of standing very still while he waited for things to happen. His ankles had been swelling for three weeks, and climbing the stairs to his flat had left him breathless enough that he had started to take them one at a time. He came in on a Friday afternoon with a bag of his tablets and a letter from his general practitioner, and he apologised for the letter.

The case that follows is a constructed composite, assembled from patterns that wards see rather than from any one person's record, and no detail in it belongs to anyone living or dead. It is here because the swelling in it is the commonest kind there is, and because the commonest kind is precisely the kind that can hide a rarer and more serious illness inside it. The story is about a frame rather than about a pair of legs, a diagnosis that was reasonable when it was made and that everybody then read the world through. Every number spoken in it is an illustration of reasoning and not a fact to act on.

The letter from his general practitioner was two lines long and both of them were about the heart, and Wharton's own account to the registrar followed the letter more closely than he knew. His face was puffy in the way that a face is puffy in the morning and less puffy by the afternoon, though on this afternoon it was still puffy. The skin of his lower legs was stretched and shiny, and pressing his shin left a dent that filled in over several seconds, and the flesh at the base of his spine was dimpled in the same way. He had brought his walking shoes in a plastic bag because they would not close over his toes.

On the ward Dr Peter Naismith, the medical registrar, took the history and the observations himself: a blood pressure of 168 over 96 millimeters of mercury, a pulse of 84 beats per minute, a temperature of 36.8 degrees Celsius, and an oxygen saturation of 96 per cent in air. Those are the numbers your own service will read differently, and the line between a raised reading and an alarming one is drawn in different places and in different people, so treat every number spoken here as an illustration and check the current guideline and the local protocol before a decision is built on it. What mattered more was what the examination did not show, because the whole case turned on it. He arrived with a diagnosis attached to his name and did not leave the ward without it.

Wharton lay flat on the bed without asking for more pillows and without any difficulty breathing in that position, which is the first thing that argues against a heart that is failing him. There was no jugular venous pressure to see above his collarbone with him reclined at a low angle, the lungs were clear to the ear front and back, the heart sounds were regular and unremarkable, and there was no third sound and no crackling at the bases when he breathed deeply. His lips were pink rather than blue and his hands were warm, and he had no chest pain at all, which he mentioned twice and apologised for twice. None of this was written down as a finding against heart failure, because nobody was yet working against it.

Two other explanations were doing their quiet work in that room, and one of them is the reason this chapter exists. The first is kidney disease, and the dangerous form of it is nephrotic syndrome, in which the kidneys leak so much protein that the blood loses the substance that holds fluid inside the vessels, so the body fills with water while the vessels themselves run dry. The finding that would reveal it is urine that froths and will not settle, and a quantity of protein in the urine that no tired heart explains. The second is clot, because a patient losing protein in his urine is also losing the protein that stops blood clotting, and that would be revealed by a sudden painful swelling in one calf or by breathlessness arriving in minutes rather than weeks.

On the Saturday morning the team met in the small side room off the ward, and the consultant cardiologist, Dr Alison Trevelyan, did most of the talking. Her reasoning was careful and, at the time, entirely reasonable. The commonest cause of new swelling in both legs in a man of sixty-four is a heart that has stopped pumping effectively, and swelling of that kind is also what brings people to hospital breathless on stairs. He was not jaundiced and had no liver disease in his history, his legs carried no varicose veins or ulceration to suggest a purely local venous cause, and the letter named no medicine that would hold salt, so the differential she took the team through was heart failure ranked first, kidney disease second, liver disease third and venous disease fourth.

The dangerous end of that list, though, was not the same as the top of it, and Dr Trevelyan said so aloud. A patient who is breathless because his pump is failing can be managed within hours, and a patient who is breathless because his blood has turned thin as water and his kidneys are failing him needs a different answer within days. The difference between those two answers is worth several days of thought at the start of the week, and it was given. She asked for the urine to be sent for protein and for the albumin in the blood to be measured, the team agreed, and then the argument moved on to the more interesting question of what to do about the water in his legs.

Those results came back over the next two days. The full blood count was unremarkable apart from a haemoglobin a little below the lower limit of the laboratory's reference range, the urea and creatinine were within limits, and the urine dipstick was strongly positive for protein. The chest radiograph showed a heart of normal size, clear lung fields and no fluid in the pleural spaces, and the electrocardiogram, or ECG, showed no acute change. None of those results was alarming in isolation and each was reasonable to have asked for, and the registrar, who had a queue of other patients, read them and moved on to the next one.

The working diagnosis became congestive heart failure with fluid retention, and on the strength of it the team began the treatment that any ward in any country would begin, a diuretic, a salt restriction, a fluid restriction and a daily weight. Dr Trevelyan was explicit with the patient about why, because the reasoning was worth hearing out loud. His kidneys were holding on to water because the heart was not pushing it forward, and the way to shift fluid out of the tissues was to make the kidney let it go, and the way to know whether that had happened was to weigh him every morning before breakfast. Nobody disagreed with any part of that.

Rhona Sinclair was the registered nurse assigned to him, and she took the treatment seriously in the sense that she took the measurements seriously. She weighed him before breakfast in the vest and trousers he had arrived in, on the ward scale that was wheeled into the corner because the bathroom scale disagreed with it by more than a kilogram, and on the first morning he weighed ninety-four kilograms. She measured and recorded his urine, she kept the strict fluid balance chart that nobody had specifically asked her for, and she marked a point on each shin with a pen so that the circumference would be measured in the same place every morning, in the same way, by the same person. She also began a list of what he had been able to do before he came in, which she kept beside the chart and which turned out to matter later.

The rest of the nursing care was less numerical and more exacting. She taught Wharton to use a soft toothbrush and to report any blood in his mouth, which matters more than it sounds when the face is swollen and the gums are pushed up by the fluid. She sat with him at the first meal and went through what the kitchen would send and what it would not, and she noticed that he was a man who had spent his life repairing things and who found being told not to eat crisps an insult rather than an instruction. She wrote down what he said about his breathing in his own words, because she had learned that the words a patient uses carry more than the observations being charted.

By the third day he was no better. The leg circumference at the marked point had not fallen by even the small amount it usually falls, the pitting at the base of his spine was unchanged, his face was fuller than it had been on Friday, and the sacral pad had spread. He was passing less urine than he had been passing on the ward, his weight had drifted down by two kilograms where five were expected over the first few days of a diuretic, and at ninety-two kilograms he was still three kilograms above the weight he had carried in his trousers a month before he became ill. The expected weight loss moves with the patient, the agent and the laboratory, so take it as an illustration of reasoning and confirm what your own service uses against the current guideline and the local protocol.

The team was frustrated, and the frustration was reasonable, because a diuretic that does not shift fluid in a patient with fluid retention is either the wrong diuretic or the wrong diagnosis, and every person on the ward round had a view about which. The registrar wanted to increase the dose and add a second agent. The consultant wanted to establish that he was not quietly eating something salty in the day room, and she was right to want that checked. What nobody said aloud was the other possibility, which was that the fluid was not where the team thought it was, and that if it was in the blood vessels rather than in the tissues, then pushing it out of the vessels would empty them instead of filling them.

There is a particular kind of quietness that settles over a ward when a treatment is not working, and it deserves naming, because that is where blame usually grows. It is tempting to believe that the patient is not taking the treatment properly, that he has eaten a whole packet of crisps in the afternoon, that he has been hiding his fluid intake, and that the next step is a conversation about adherence with a man too tired to argue back. That explanation is attractive because it puts the fault in the bed, because it is easy to check, and because it is available before the alternative has been thought through at all. Nobody on the team was being unkind when they wanted it, and the process gave them a tidy place to put the difficulty.

What Rhona Sinclair noticed was not in the notes, or rather it was in the notes and had been read past. She had been emptying his urine for four days without thinking much about it, and on the third evening she looked at the bedpan after she had tipped it and saw a layer of white foam sitting on the surface that would not go away. She ran a little water into the empty pan and did the same thing with the water, and the water did not foam at all, and the difference was the answer. She wrote in the nursing record that the urine was frothy and that the froth had not settled on standing, and on the ward round the next morning she mentioned it in passing and was answered with the observation that his heart was not good and the water would follow.

She went back through her own notes for the week, and the picture they made was not the picture being described on the medical ward round. His neck veins had never been distended. His chest had been clear every time she had listened to it. His blood pressure had stayed high and had not fallen as the diuretic did its work, which is not what a heart under strain and being unloaded usually does. He had slept flat on two pillows from the first night and had never asked to be raised, and his lips had been pink every single time she had looked at them. And on the second day he had said, unprompted, that he had never been breathless in his life until this summer, in the tone of a man not yet sure whether the information mattered.

She took the problem to the ward round on the fifth morning, and she took it to Dr Naismith first, because she had a clear view of where her own job ended and his began. He listened with the particular expression of a clinician who has been awake since five in the morning and is being told that the answer he has been giving for four days is wrong. She said that she could not make the heart fit the picture, that his heart was not enlarged and had never sounded unwell, that he was not short of breath lying down, and that his urine had carried protein and froth since the day he arrived. He said that he wanted to believe her, and that he was going to go and look again.

Dr Trevelyan came back that afternoon and did the thing that turns a ward round into a diagnosis, which was to read the notes as a stranger. The protein in the urine was heavier than anything a tired heart explains, the albumin in the blood was low enough to account for the swelling by itself, and the radiograph had said in plain words that the heart was of normal size and the lungs were clear. The consultant asked for the urine protein to be quantified and the blood film to be reviewed, the registrar asked for a renal opinion, and both requests were made that afternoon rather than the following week. That was the shape of the correction, not a revelation but a re-reading.

The results arrived the next morning and they were not subtle. The urine protein was many times the amount used to define nephrotic syndrome in most laboratories, the albumin in the blood was low enough to explain the swelling on its own, and the estimate of kidney filtering function, though still recorded as adequate, was the lowest it had ever been for him. Every one of those numbers moves with the laboratory, the method and the moment at which it was taken, so confirm the values and the cut-offs in use against the current guideline and the local protocol rather than against a remembered number. The registrar wrote the diagnosis into the notes that afternoon, and then sat down to think about what to do with it.

Once the diagnosis was named, the shape of the previous five days suddenly made sense, and it made sense in the wrong direction. In nephrotic syndrome the volume inside the vessels can be very low while the body looks swollen, because the protein that holds fluid in the vessels has gone out in the urine and the fluid has left the vessels for the tissues. A diuretic given to such a patient takes salt and water out of the blood and can push the kidney into holding on even harder, so the swelling of the legs goes down a little while the dangerous emptiness inside goes further. The team's next instinct had been to push harder, and it would have been the most dangerous thing available to them.

The renal physician, Dr Tolu Adeyemi, came the same day and took the fluid problem away from the heart team, and the first thing she changed was the assumption that too much fluid was the only problem. The volume inside the vessels was expanded deliberately with albumin rather than taken away with more diuretic, the diuretic was kept but put under review, and the salt and fluid restrictions were relaxed rather than tightened, because a man who is thirsty and salt-starved is a man whose kidneys will cling harder to what he has. She asked about the anticoagulant protein as well, since a patient who loses it is a patient at risk of a clot in a leg vein or in the renal veins. And she asked who had read the chest radiograph, because that film had been in the file since Friday and had been read once.

A renal biopsy was arranged and carried out, and it showed the pattern of membranous nephropathy, in which the filters of the kidney are damaged in a way that lets protein escape. It is one of the causes of nephrotic syndrome that appears in a man of this age with no other illness to account for it, and it was therefore a genuine answer rather than a label. The immune process behind it was then treated under the renal team with immunosuppression, a decision made jointly and explained to Wharton carefully, because the word frightens people and because the person who takes the tablets is the one who lives with the consequences. The biopsy was not the end of the work but the beginning of it, because why his immune system had decided to damage his kidneys was still open and still needed an answer.

The swelling did not go quickly and nobody expected it to. Over the following weeks the weight came down by grams rather than by kilograms, the leg circumference at the marked point fell steadily, the pitting at the sacrum softened and then went, and his face flattened out at last. His urine output rose, the froth went from the surface of the urine, and the climb up the stairs at home became a walk he could manage without stopping. Nobody called it a cure, and the nursing team wrote in the notes that the daily weight was still the number that told them whether any of it was true.

Before he went home, Rhona Sinclair went through the things that would bring him back, and she went through them with him at the bedside with the curtains drawn and the television off. He was to watch his weight and report a rapid rise, and he was to report a swollen calf or a sudden pain in the chest, because the risk of a clot was real in this disease and was not something he had been carrying before. He was to have his blood tested at intervals to follow the kidney function and the albumin, and to telephone the ward rather than wait to be seen. The nursing team changed one thing about the ward itself, which was that the scale in the corner was never moved again.

The consultant wrote to the general practitioner and to the ward about what had happened and why, and the writing was unusually careful about blame. Nothing here was a mistake by a careless person at the bedside, but a reasonable diagnosis doing what reasonable diagnoses do, which is to explain everything that arrives afterwards. Swelling in a man of sixty-four is heart failure until the chest says otherwise, and the chest had said otherwise from the first hour. The correction was available to any team that had stopped to read the chart with the patient's chest in mind, and the fact that it was not taken at the time says something about ward rounds rather than about the people on them.

There is a second question here, and its answer is what made the correction right rather than merely different, namely that the treatment was changed to match the mechanism instead of the appearance. The appearance was swollen legs, and the mechanism was the loss of protein through the kidneys, with a fall in the albumin that holds fluid inside the vessels and a fall in the circulating volume that the body then tries to replace by holding on to salt. Treating the appearance means more diuretic, and in this situation more diuretic makes the circulation emptier and the kidney more stubborn. Treating the mechanism means replacing what was lost, protecting the kidney, and dealing with the immune process that is doing the damage.

The wider lesson is about frames. Once the team had decided that this was a heart, every later observation was read as a heart observation: the breathlessness was the heart, the water in his legs was the heart, the failure of the diuretic was the heart, and even the fact that his blood pressure was high was a fact about the heart. Nothing in that reading was dishonest and nothing in it was stupid, which is exactly what makes it dangerous. A frame is not a set of opinions a person holds but a set of expectations about what will be seen next, and a clinician who is not looking for the diagnosis is not looking for the evidence against it.

The daily weight deserves a sentence of its own, because it is the most useful single number on a ward and very few wards treat it that way. It is the only measurement that says something about the whole patient at once, it costs nothing, it takes a minute, and it is the number that says whether the treatment is working before anything else does. On this ward it was being used as a chart that got filled in, and it took the nurse asking for the daily change rather than the daily number, and asking why it had not come down to where it ought to be, to make it a measurement again. That line on the chart, with its small fall and its stubborn plateau, was the thing the team eventually read, and it was read after four days of arguing about the dose.

Wharton went home with kidneys that had been damaged by a process nobody had looked for, and with a set of tablets he did not enjoy, and understanding more about his own body than he had ever understood before. Rhona kept in touch with him for a while, as she did with the patients whose numbers she had followed closely, and she asked him about his weight and his ankles and his breathing on the telephone in a way that nobody had ever asked him before. It is easy to imagine a ward as a place where a machine manufactures a decision, and this was not that. It was a place where a group of people, including one who had noticed something odd about a bedpan, argued with a set of numbers until the numbers agreed with the patient.

To summarize, Derek Wharton's swelling was never a heart problem, and the heart was a reasonable answer that the whole team then read the world through. The failure was not in the diuretic and not in the ward round and not in any person; it was in a frame that turned every later observation into a confirmation of itself. A clear chest with a big heart is not the only chest, frothy urine is a bedside finding that costs nothing, and a weight that stays high while fluid is pulled out of the tissues is telling you that the fluid was never where you thought it was. The nurse who emptied the bedpan noticed, wrote it down in words, and said so at the wrong end of a busy week, and the diagnosis changed because somebody read the chart. Every number spoken here is an illustration of reasoning and must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the values differ between laboratories, between countries and between editions, and the patient in front of you is not one of the illustrations.
