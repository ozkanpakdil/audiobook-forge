# Chapter 16 — The Fit That Was Not a Fit

This chapter is told in the order the minutes happened, because in this case the order was the diagnosis. The case is a constructed composite built for teaching, and no part of it describes a real patient, a real theatre or a real service; the sequence of events has been invented so that the reasoning inside it can be examined. What follows is a young man on a table in a day-case unit, a nerve block and a recovery room. It is also a set of observations taken by a nurse in the minutes before a fit, each of which was written down in words and each of which was read by nobody in time.

Callum Reid is 28, an electrician, admitted as a day case for a repair of the rotator cuff of his right shoulder, an operation he has been putting off for some time because of the sling and the weeks of ladder work that follow it. The block is an interscalene nerve block of the shoulder, done under ultrasound guidance with local anaesthetic, and it works well: his arm is numb and immobile when he comes out of the operating room. The general anaesthetic used for the operation itself includes an inhaled agent and a depolarizing muscle relaxant. The surgeon's part of the story ends with a wound that closed easily, a sling, a set of discharge instructions, and a young man who is awake, oriented and comfortable in the recovery room.

He comes back into the recovery room complaining that the room is noisy and that the lights are too bright. The recovery nurse on that shift is a senior sister called Fenella Marlowe, and she notes the time and writes down what he said, because what a patient says in the first few minutes after an anaesthetic is worth more than anything on the observation chart. He is anxious and cannot settle, which is unremarkable after a general anaesthetic and in a young man, and the observations are taken and recorded as they always are. He is talking, which is why the oxygen mask has not yet been put on him, and he says that the room is ringing, and that his lips and his tongue feel numb, and that there is a metallic taste in his mouth.

His arms begin to twitch, and then he says that his whole body feels strange. Not long after the block, and a few minutes after the first of his complaints, he has a fit in the recovery room: his body stiffens, his jaw tightens, and the movements are rhythmic and generalized, and it lasts rather longer than most people expect a fit to last. The recovery nurse calls for help and stays with him. The two minutes that follow are the ones that everybody present would remember differently, and the difference in the memories is the subject of this chapter.

The dangerous alternative had to be named first, and in this setting it has a name, a treatment and a clock. Malignant hyperthermia is a reaction of the muscle to certain anaesthetic agents, and it begins in the operating room or the recovery room with a rising carbon dioxide, a rising heart rate, muscle rigidity and a rising temperature; the findings that would reveal it are those four things in that order, rigidity often appearing before the temperature does. A second danger sits behind it, and it is the one that actually threatens this patient: a fit that is not treated can injure the brain, and an anaesthetic complication mistaken for a different one is treated with the wrong medicine while the clock runs. Both possibilities were on the table from the first minute, and the team had to work on them at the same time.

The anaesthetist, Dr Martin Ravenscroft, and the surgeon, Mr Hugh Tansley, are called, and the recovery nurse stays at the bedside because the observations are hers and she has been with him since he came back. The two doctors rank the possibilities in the way that ranking is done in a recovery room: first what is most likely, then what is most dangerous if missed. Malignant hyperthermia is at the top of both lists, because the anaesthetic included an inhaled agent and a depolarizing muscle relaxant, because the reaction is known to follow both of those, and because the alternatives are rare. The rest of the list is longer and more ordinary: an emergence phenomenon after a general anaesthetic, a reaction to the local anaesthetic, a low blood pressure, a low blood sugar, a seizure of another cause, and the possibility that the shoulder is simply sore in a way he cannot interpret through a numb arm.

The reason for that ranking is sound. A young man in the recovery room, agitated, tachycardic and twitching after an anaesthetic that included a muscle relaxant, with a rising carbon dioxide on the capnograph, is a patient with malignant hyperthermia until somebody proves otherwise. The capnograph is what tips the balance, because a rising carbon dioxide in the recovery room is the earliest and most reliable sign of the reaction, and it separates the reaction from an emergence phenomenon, in which the breathing is shallow but the carbon dioxide is falling as the anaesthetic wears off. The working diagnosis is malignant hyperthermia, the drug used to treat it is given, active cooling is begun, and the theatre team is called back in to bring the anesthetic machine and to help.

Meanwhile the recovery nurse does the thing that later turns out to have mattered most, and she does it without any idea that it matters. She writes the words he used, in his own words and in quotation marks, with the time beside each line. The room is ringing, he said, at four minutes past the hour. His lips and tongue feel numb, he said, at five minutes past. There is a metallic taste, he said, and the lights are too bright. He cannot settle, he said, and his arms are twitching, he said, at nine minutes past, and then he said that his whole body feels strange, and at twelve minutes past he said nothing at all, because by then he was fitting.

The treatment fails because the diagnosis is wrong, and the failure is slow, which is what makes it instructive. Over the following half hour the observations refuse to move in the way the diagnosis requires them to move. His temperature stays at 37.4 degrees Celsius and never climbs; these values illustrate a patient whose temperature is not rising, and the pattern that should send you back to the diagnosis is a temperature that climbs with the muscle activity, so the values and the trends should be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol rather than taken from this account. His muscles are not rigid; he is floppy rather than stiff, and the arm that was blocked is the arm that is twitching. The end-tidal carbon dioxide, which had been rising in the first minutes, falls instead of continuing to climb, because as the anaesthetic wears off his ventilation has increased and he is breathing more deeply.

The other observations refuse to fall into place as well. The electrocardiogram, or ECG, shows a fast rhythm without the changes in the shape of the complexes that the team were expecting, and the blood pressure, at 92 over 58 millimeters of mercury, is low rather than collapsing, which is a familiar recovery room finding after a general anaesthetic and a shoulder operation. That pressure is an illustration rather than a rule, and the point at which your own service would start vasopressors is a local decision to be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol. The arterial blood gas shows a carbon dioxide that has come down and a bicarbonate that has not moved much, and nothing in it points to a metabolic cause of the agitation.

And the situation now is the one every team finds itself in when a treatment is not working, which is uncomfortable, because the working diagnosis is the reason the drug was given and giving more of it feels like the only available action. What they are tempted to do is what they would have done in the theatre if the diagnosis were right. More cooling, more of the drug, a repeat blood gas, an urgent blood test for the muscle breakdown that malignant hyperthermia causes, and a plan to take him back to the operating room so that a different anesthetic machine can be used and a different circuit can be flushed. The anaesthetist is thinking about the fact that the drug does nothing for a fit and everything for a reaction of muscle, and he is aware, in the way experienced clinicians are aware, that if the working diagnosis is wrong the treatment is not merely useless but is consuming time that the patient does not have.

There is a further reason for the strength of the conviction, and it is worth naming because it is the kind of reason that makes a team slow. The anaesthetist saw a case of this long ago as a junior doctor, and he has never forgotten it, and everybody in the room knows the story. That history is not a mistake and it is not bias; it is what a dangerous event does to the people who witness it, and it makes the correct response here harder to reach rather than easier. Without that memory, the same man would have looked at the observations that refused to fit and been uncomfortable in a different way. With it, he is uncomfortable and certain at the same time, and certainty is what keeps the frame in place.

And then somebody reads the notes. The recovery nurse asks the anaesthetist to look at the lines she wrote before the fit, and she reads them out loud in a recovery room into which the theatre team is now walking. The room was ringing. The lips and the tongue felt numb. There was a metallic taste. The patient was anxious and could not settle, and his arms were twitching before the fit began. The anaesthetist stops. He asks for the notes and reads them twice, and then he says that numb lips and a metallic taste and a ringing in the ears and twitching are not features of malignant hyperthermia at all, but features of something else entirely, and that this patient has been awake for every moment since he came back and has been telling them so.

The surgeon supplies the second half of the reasoning from a different direction, in the way that a person who has been concentrating on the operation rather than on the anaesthetic does. He asks whether the block is still working, and then, when the answer is that the arm is entirely numb, he asks how long the local anaesthetic will still be active and whether any of it might still be circulating. It is not that the anaesthetist did not know any of this. It is that the question was asked from the point of view of the shoulder, by a man who had spent the last hour thinking about tendons and sutures, and a question asked from a different angle lands where the same question asked from the obvious angle does not.

The name for that something else is systemic toxicity of the local anaesthetic, and it is a complication of the block performed in the operating room rather than of anything that has happened since. The local anaesthetic was meant for the tissues around the shoulder, and in a proportion of blocks the needle enters a vessel and the drug is delivered straight into the circulation, so that the heart and the brain meet a concentration the tissues around the shoulder would have tolerated perfectly well. The recognized early picture is the one that was written down: numbness of the lips and of the tongue, a metallic taste, ringing in the ears, a sensation of going away, anxiety, restlessness, and twitching of the muscles. It comes before the collapse, it is not specific, and it is easy to attribute to something else, which is exactly what happened here.

Why was it missed? For the same reason that governs every other failure described in this book. The frame had been set by the block and the anaesthetic, both of which are known causes of catastrophe in a recovery room, and once malignant hyperthermia had been named the observations were read as evidence for it rather than as evidence against it. The normal temperature was read as an early sign of the reaction rather than as a serious argument against it. The twitching was read as the muscle activity of the syndrome rather than as the twitching that precedes this other toxicity. The prodrome was in the notes and in front of people who were looking for a different disease, and the same words would have been invisible to them in a different frame.

The treatment changes inside a minute. Everything is stopped: the anaesthetic agent, the muscle relaxant, the cooling, and the drug that was given for malignant hyperthermia, which is now understood to have been given for a condition he does not have. The airway is supported, with oxygen into the airway and the jaw lifted, because the fit itself is not the main threat but what happens to the chest during one is. The fit is controlled with an anticonvulsant, and the intravenous lipid emulsion is brought and given, because it sequesters the drug in a lipid compartment, which lowers the free fraction reaching the heart and the brain. From that moment the room changes from managing one catastrophe to managing another, and everybody in it can feel the difference.

The nurse is the one who calls for it, and the way she calls for it matters. She does not announce that the diagnosis has changed, because that is not her role and because an announcement invites an argument. She says that the symptoms of local anaesthetic toxicity are written in the notes, and that she would like the intravenous lipid emulsion used now, and that she knows where it is, because she checked the emergency trolley when she brought the patient back from theatre. The anaesthetist agrees and the drug is given, and the order of those two sentences is the order in which the case was saved. Nothing in this was the work of the most senior person in the room; it was the work of the person who had written down what the patient said.

What follows is an hour of work that would have been easier to describe if the diagnosis had been right. His blood pressure comes up. His heart rate falls from 148 beats per minute towards 80 as the drug is prevented from reaching the heart, and the number that matters is the trend rather than the number, so compare the rate and the rhythm against the current guideline and the local protocol for your own service rather than with any figure given here. The fits stop, or shorten and stop. He does not arrest, and the arrest that follows this toxicity is the one that kills, so the absence of an arrest is the single most important outcome of the hour. The nurse keeps the observations, the times, the record and the intravenous infusion running, and she is the one who notices that his consciousness comes back.

The resuscitation team arrives in the middle of this and finds the room already working, which is the best possible thing that can happen to them. They bring the trolley and the monitoring the recovery room does not have, take over the observations for a while, and the anaesthetist briefs them in a sentence and a half. Nothing about the handover is dramatic; the value of it is that the people who arrive do not have to work out what is happening from a confused room, and in this case the whole treatment of the last hour has been a case of somebody knowing what was happening. He wakes, and he is confused and frightened and in pain from the shoulder, and the confusion is the expected kind rather than the kind that suggests brain injury.

The numbness of his lips goes. The metallic taste goes. The ringing goes. He asks several times why he cannot remember the last hour, and the nurse answers him the same way each time, in plain words, because the explanation of what happened is part of the treatment of what happened. He is admitted to the intensive care unit, or ICU, for observation, and he goes home within a few days. The operation and the block are reviewed afterwards, and the review is not about blame. The block was done with ultrasound guidance, the best available method, which does not remove the possibility of an intravascular injection, and the technique already involved dividing the local anaesthetic and checking for blood by aspiration between injections.

What the review changes is the setup rather than the man. The intravenous lipid emulsion moves from a locked cupboard in the emergency department to the recovery room itself, and the local protocol is rewritten so that the trolley holding it is checked at the start of every list and every patient who has had a local anaesthetic block has a documented plan for who is called if they become unwell. Callum Reid comes back to the clinic a few weeks later. The shoulder is healing, the block site is not, and he has no neurological deficit and no memory of the fit, which he finds harder to live with than the shoulder does. He says that the worst part was the fit and the second worst part was the room before it, and he has thought about the metallic taste often since, because the taste is the thing he can still bring back and the fit is the thing he cannot.

He is told, carefully, what happened and why, and that the overwhelming majority of blocks do not cause this, and that the signs had been written down before anything happened. He is asked to write his own account of the recovery room, and he does, and it is his to keep, and it is not filed in the notes because it is too blunt to be filed. That last detail matters, because a service that cannot accept a patient's blunt account of its own failures will hear the next account only in its sanitized form. He also leaves with written advice about the warning signs of the same thing happening again, in words he can carry in his wallet, and he agrees to be the reason the protocol changes, because the alternative is that the next patient on a table writes the same four lines and has them read by nobody.

The teaching of this case is about timing rather than about drugs. Between the first symptom and the fit there were only a few minutes in which a nurse wrote down what the patient said, and each of those things pointed away from the diagnosis the team was holding and towards one they had not considered. Any one of them would have been enough, and the combination was decisive, and it existed only because somebody had asked the patient what he was feeling and had written the answer in his own words. Between two catastrophes on a table, one of which kills within minutes and the other of which is survivable, the difference is often a set of observations taken before the worst moment rather than after it; the size of that window is set by the local protocol for recovery room observations, which is worth checking against the current guideline rather than assumed.

It is worth saying that the recovery room is the least well-documented place in most hospitals, and this is not a criticism of any individual but a property of the work. Observations in a recovery room are taken to a routine, a standard and a time limit, and the person taking them is often the only one in the room with the patient, which makes the record both more and less valuable. It is more valuable because it is contemporaneous and unprompted by anybody with a theory. It is less valuable because it is written to satisfy a list rather than a question, so that the ringing in the ears and the metallic taste become free text that nobody reads, or do not get written at all.

Every observation in this case was correct, and that is the part people find hardest to believe. The temperature was normal, and it was recorded accurately. The carbon dioxide was falling, and it was measured accurately. The blood pressure was low, and it was written down accurately. Nobody made an error of measurement and nobody made an error of technique, and the case went wrong anyway, because the errors were all of attention rather than of observation, and because in a system organized around the observation chart an observation that is not on the chart is one that nobody will find. The practice change that follows is small and unglamorous. The recovery room chart is altered so that it contains a space for what the patient says, in his own words, in the first hour after an anaesthetic, and the anaesthetist who reviews the chart is told to read that space first.

A standing instruction goes into the local protocol that any patient who has received a local anaesthetic block is asked about the symptoms of that block's toxicity while the block's effect is still on them and they can still answer, and it is put to the trainees that this question is worth asking every time, not only when something has already gone wrong. The juniors are told that the answer will usually be that everything is fine, and that this is a good enough reason to have asked. There is a general rule here for recovery rooms anywhere, and it is simple enough to be posted up. The information that decides the outcome of a perioperative collapse is usually in the patient's own words, usually available before the collapse rather than after it, and usually dismissed as a symptom of the very condition the team is treating. The nurse who wrote it down had no way of knowing that she was writing the diagnosis; she was doing the ordinary work of a nurse on a routine afternoon, which is the only way decisive observations get made at all.

The aftermath reaches further than the service that contained it. A man who has had a bad experience in a recovery room will tell people, and some of them will read the story as a story about incompetent hospitals rather than as a story about a few minutes of writing, and the honest answer to that is that it was mostly about those few minutes. The protocol that comes out of this case will be read by anaesthetists and nurses for years, and most of them will never know the name of the man whose shoulder operation it was. The improvement is built out of exactly those unremarkable minutes, which is how improvements in safety are built, one unremarkable minute at a time, all the time.

To summarize, this case went wrong because the observations were read through the diagnosis rather than against it, and because the words that would have changed the diagnosis were written down and not read. The dangerous possibility was named early, and malignant hyperthermia was a reasonable working diagnosis for a young man who was agitated, tachycardic and twitching after an anaesthetic that included a muscle relaxant. What it was not was supported by a temperature that stayed at 37.4 degrees Celsius, by a patient who was floppy rather than rigid, and by a carbon dioxide that came down instead of going up, and each of those numbers is an illustration of the reasoning rather than a standard, to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol for the service concerned. The prodrome of local anaesthetic toxicity comes before the collapse, and it was written in the patient's own words by a nurse trained to record what was said rather than what it was called, and she is the one who asked for the intravenous lipid emulsion. He recovers without brain injury, and the change that outlasts him is a piece of writing: a space on the recovery room chart for the patient's words, a trolley in the recovery room rather than in a cupboard, and a protocol question asked of everybody who has had a block. The difference between two catastrophes on a table is a set of observations taken before the worst moment rather than after it. This account is educational material and carries no clinical authority, and the decision about what to do belongs to the clinician who is with the patient.
