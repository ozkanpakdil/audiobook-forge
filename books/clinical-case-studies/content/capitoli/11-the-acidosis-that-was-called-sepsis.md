# Chapter 11 — The Acidosis That Was Called Sepsis

The emergency department at half past four in the afternoon had a drip stand, a hard chair and a man in it, and the first thing Miriam Pemberton noticed was not the chart on his knee but his breathing. He breathed in through his nose, down and slow, and out through his mouth, longer than the way in, and he did it with a concentration that made her stop and look twice. Gordon Mellor was sixty-eight, a retired printer who had come in by car with a plastic bag of medicines in his lap because he had been told to bring them. He had been sick for three days and could not keep anything down, had been short of breath since that morning, and said it in the flat voice of a man who has decided not to make a fuss. Whether three days of vomiting still counts as an acute illness is a question for the current guideline and the local protocol rather than for a story, and on that afternoon both readings of him were still open. The reason this chapter is worth telling is not the man but the word that formed around him within the hour, because sepsis was the right word for the ward and the wrong word for the man.

What follows is a constructed composite and it does not describe a real patient. No detail in it identifies anyone living or dead, and the events have been assembled to make one piece of reasoning visible. The piece is the moment when a reasonable diagnosis stops being a hypothesis and becomes a frame, and everything the patient says after that moment is read through it. Mellor arrived with a frame already attached to him, because the frame was in his own history and in his own medicines, and neither of them was wrong, and both of them were being read in a hurry.

His record was a record of a chronic illness that nobody had looked at properly for a long time. He had had type 2 diabetes for twelve years and a chronic kidney condition of the kind that is written down as stable and reviewed in the way that stable things are reviewed. He had been taking metformin since the year he was diagnosed, and the drug had never once been the subject of a conversation that anybody could find in the notes. Those years carry no meaning of their own, they are simply the length of an unreviewed medicine in a man whose kidneys are no longer the ones he had when it was started, and the intervals your own service uses for reviewing a long-standing medicine are written in the current guideline and the local protocol.

At triage his temperature was 36.8 degrees Celsius, his pulse was 96 beats per minute and regular, his blood pressure was 128 over 74 millimeters of mercury, and his respiratory rate was 24 breaths a minute. He was alert and mildly uncomfortable rather than frightened, his mouth was dry, his tongue was coated, and his eyes were a little sunken. There was no rigor, no rash, and no confusion beyond the mild muddiness of a person who has been vomiting. Each of those figures is a reason rather than a fact, and the values that count as abnormal vary with the person and the place, so check them against the current guideline and the local protocol before anybody builds a decision on them.

The dipstick on his urine was positive for protein and for blood, and the sample was cloudy to the eye, which in a man with a chronic kidney condition and three days of vomiting is the sort of result a busy clinician reads as confirmation of whatever the clinician has already decided. His abdomen was soft and not tender, his chest was clear, and there was no obvious source of infection anywhere that anybody could find. He had no rash, no tenderness over either kidney, and nothing about him pointed at a source. Pemberton did that examination with him and wrote all of it down, and she wrote down the breathing as well, because she had been told on her first day that when the breathing does not fit the story you write down the breathing and not the story.

Two possibilities were missing from the list at that moment and should not have been. The first was a surgical abdomen, because three days of vomiting in a man of sixty-eight can hide an obstruction or a perforation, and the findings that would reveal it are a distended tender abdomen, a rigid quiet belly, and vomit that looks like old coffee, and none of those were present. The second was a metabolic cause of the deep breathing, and the finding that would have revealed it was sitting in front of her in the waiting area: a deep, slow, regular and deliberate pattern of breathing in a man whose temperature was normal. In sepsis that pattern belongs to a body compensating for a metabolic upset, and it is also the pattern of a body that is acidotic for a reason nobody has looked for.

The registrar on that shift was Samir Haldane, who had worked in the emergency department for three years and had the habit the nursing staff valued of saying out loud what he thought was most likely to be the reason the person in front of him might die. That habit would have saved this patient, and it did not, because what he said out loud was sepsis, and sepsis was in fact the most likely reason a man like this dies in an emergency department. When the shift changed and Julia Renwick arrived as the intensivist, she was handed a story that had already been written, and she read it, and it fitted the evidence she had been given. The frame arrived with the patient and nobody noticed it arrive.

Haldane built the differential aloud at the bedside, because that is how he worked and because the nursing staff had learned to listen. A urinary infection with an obstructed outflow came first, on the reasonable grounds that an older man with diabetes and cloudy urine is a common patient and the commonest thing is usually the right thing. Acute gastroenteritis was second, since three days of vomiting and nothing else fits it better, and then came a bowel obstruction, a chest infection, and a flare of his kidney condition. What none of them said aloud was that the vomiting, the dehydration and the tablets on his lap might together be producing a chemical problem rather than reflecting one, and the reason none of them said it is that the story had a name and the name was doing the work.

The working diagnosis was sepsis of urinary origin, and the reason for it was audible and reasonable. He was sixty-eight with diabetes and a raised white cell count, his urine was cloudy and contained blood, and a normal temperature in sepsis in an older person is common rather than reassuring, because many who die of it never have a fever at all. His lactate was raised at 3.9 millimoles per liter, and a lactate raised in a very unwell patient is read by almost everybody as evidence of the very illness being treated. The figures here are illustrations of how the reasoning went rather than facts to be acted on, and the thresholds your own laboratory uses should be read from the current guideline and the local protocol.

So the treatment began, and it began properly. He was given intravenous fluid, because a man who has vomited for three days and is passing little urine needs volume, and antibiotics were started, because if the diagnosis is right then delay is what kills people, and blood and urine were sent for culture, and a chest radiograph was requested, and a venous blood gas was taken. The nurse began a fluid balance chart, observations were set at four times an hour, a glucose was checked because he had diabetes, and the hourly urine output was measured into a jug that was emptied and recorded. Nothing about any of that was wrong, and it was what a well-trained team does when it believes it has sepsis, and the belief was reasonable and the treatment was appropriate to it.

The fluid balance chart was the other document that was telling the truth and being read as something else. Pemberton kept it because it was hers to keep, the way a nurse keeps a chart when nobody has told her to, with the columns in order and the totals at the foot of the day. What it showed on the first evening was a large volume going in and very little coming out, and beside it she wrote that his mouth was still dry after all that fluid and that the breathing had not changed. A chart showing input far exceeding output is often read as proof that a patient is still dehydrated, and here it was proof that his kidneys were not responding to fluid in the way healthy kidneys do.

What Pemberton could not put down was the breathing. She had seen it in the waiting area, and she saw it at eleven, and again at one, and again at four, and each time it was the same deep slow purposeful rhythm. There is a particular kind of tiredness that comes with a patient who has a diagnosis and a chart and a plan, because once the plan exists the observations become a matter of confirming it, and a nurse on a busy bay will fill in a chart that agrees with the plan without asking whether the plan agrees with the patient. She was not sure what she wanted to say at four in the morning, and she said nothing, and that was the whole of her part in it.

She can still describe the breathing precisely, because she described it that night in the nursing notes, and that is the only reason anybody reading this case can see what she saw. He breathed in through his nose with a slow count and out through pursed lips, and the out-breath was longer than the in-breath, and the movement of his chest was deep, deliberate and regular. It did not speed up when she came to the bedside and it did not stop when she left. There was no noise in it and no wheeze, and he was not using his shoulders or his neck to breathe, and he was not fighting for air the way people with sepsis fight for air, he was doing something closer to blowing out a candle, slowly and with control, over and over. She had seen that pattern before, in renal failure, and she had been taught that it is a body correcting its own chemistry.

He was no better in the morning, which is not in itself a failure, because nobody expects a man with sepsis to be well by morning, and his blood pressure had held all night and his temperature had not moved. What had changed was the second set of bloods, and the venous blood gas that was repeated because his breathing rate had not come down. The lactate had risen from 3.9 to 7.8 millimoles per liter, the pH had fallen to 7.14, the white cell count had barely moved from the value it had held overnight, and the urine culture was growing nothing so far. The first of those results was read as proof of uncontrolled sepsis, and it was the one result in the file that should have been read as a change of diagnosis, and the line between a raised lactate and a dangerous one is drawn in the current guideline and the local protocol rather than in this account.

The intensivist came in that morning and did what intensivists do, which is to look for the thing that will kill the patient soonest, and she found the same answers the registrar had found, because the answers were inside the frame. His blood pressure was 128 over 74 millimeters of mercury and his urine was still being made, and the lactate was high, and she began the calculation everybody begins, which is which antibiotic to change to and how many hours are left, and the pressures and rates she was weighing are the ones set out in the current guideline and the local protocol. There was a moment that afternoon when the registrar said the words that would have changed everything, and he said them as a joke, which was that this man was not going to die of a urinary infection, he was going to die of something nobody had thought of. Nobody asked him what he meant by that.

And it was Pemberton who asked the question, on the afternoon of the second day, when she went back through his plastic bag of medicines because she had run out of things to write on the chart. She did it as a routine, the way she would for any man of sixty-eight with diabetes, and read the labels out loud to him to check them, and then asked the question nobody had asked him in twelve years, which was when his metformin had last been reviewed. He told her it had never been reviewed, that it had been given to him when he was first found to have diabetes, and that he had seen the same doctor every year and had never once had blood taken. She wrote that down exactly as he said it, because a sentence in a patient's own words is worth more than a paragraph in a summary.

That afternoon she rang the practice and asked for his most recent results, and the ones they sent back were eleven months old, and they were already drifting in the wrong direction, and the note said the kidney function was stable and the medicine should continue unchanged. That eleven months is an illustration of how long a result can sit in a file unread, which is a gap in the system and not in anybody's care, and the intervals your own service sets for reviewing a long-standing medicine are written in the current guideline and the local protocol. She sat down with the chart on her knees and read the drug list again, and this time as a list of things the body had to clear. Metformin is cleared by the kidneys, and his kidneys were no longer the ones he had had when it was started.

The other thing she found was in her own handwriting on the nursing chart, and it was the reason she had not worried. His glucose had been normal every time it was checked, at 5.4 and 5.8 millimoles per liter, and she had read that as reassuring, because a normal blood sugar in a man with diabetes is what a well-controlled diabetic looks like. It was not reassuring at all, it was the drug doing what it does, and a blood sugar that stays normal in a man who has been vomiting for three days and passing little urine is being kept normal by something that is no longer being cleared. The values here are illustrations of how a number can be read backwards, and the ranges your laboratory uses should be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

The reason it was missed is worth saying plainly, because this case has no villain in it. The medication reconciliation on the second day listed metformin as one current drug among eleven, entered by a system that carried it forward from an earlier admission, and nobody read it, because a person reading it would have had to ask why a drug for diabetes was being continued in a man who was vomiting. Nobody asked why the metformin was still going, because the frame said the problem was an infection, and the frame said a drug for diabetes had nothing to do with an infection. The clue was not hidden, it was printed on the front of the chart in the drug list, in the same handwriting as every other drug, at the same size, on the same page.

The change began with a telephone call to the ward registrar, and then it began with a sentence written into the notes, which was that the metformin was being stopped. Everything that followed was a consequence of that sentence and nothing else. Pemberton had made the case in four sentences and she did not overstate it: a man whose kidneys have deteriorated, who has been vomiting for three days, who has a normal temperature and a normal blood sugar and a deep steady breathing pattern, and whose lactate has risen while his blood pressure has held. She was right about all of it and right for reasons she could not yet explain, and she said so, because a nurse who claims more than she knows has her next observation discounted.

Then the treatment changed, and it changed in the way treatment changes when a name changes. The metformin stopped, the acidosis was treated with intravenous fluid and with insulin, and the question of whether his blood should be filtered was raised early, which is a decision that has to be taken early and that many services cannot offer at any hour. The intensivist went looking for the reason his kidneys had been failing silently, and when the urine culture grew nothing the antibiotics were stopped, because continuing them would have kept the frame in place and done nothing for the man. The vomiting was treated and his fluid replaced carefully rather than pushed, and his diabetes was reviewed with a plan for the future that did not depend on a medicine his kidneys could not clear.

From the moment the drug stopped, the nursing notes changed character, and the ward noticed before the laboratory did. The respiratory rate came down over the afternoon, and Pemberton documented the change with the times, and she wrote that the out-breath was shorter and that he was able to speak in full sentences, which he had not been able to do since triage. She changed the frequency of the observations because the patient was improving rather than because a protocol told her to, and kept the fluid balance chart running because the fluid plan was now a delicate thing. Nothing about that appears on a chart as an intervention, and that is the point, because what changed was that the nurse was reading the patient as a question and not as a set of results.

He improved over the two days that followed in a way nobody had expected, because the treatment that changed him was not an antibiotic. His lactate came back into the normal range, his blood gas came right, his blood pressure never needed support, and by the second morning he was eating breakfast and complaining that the food was bland, which every ward receives as good news. The intensivist was clear that this was not luck: the pattern had a metabolic cause with a precipitant, the drug had been the fuel, the vomiting and the dehydration had been the spark, and once the drug stopped the body had been able to clear itself. The intervals over which such a recovery is expected are set out in the current guideline and the local protocol rather than in the length of this account.

What Pemberton watched for in the days that followed was not the lactate, which was somebody else's number, but the breathing and the record of it. She wrote a short note for the next nurse, and it said that the rate and depth of his breathing were the first things to change and the last to change back, and that a patient breathing deeply and steadily with a normal temperature has a story that has not been read yet. She also wrote that if his glucose was normal she should not be reassured, and why, because she had learned it on a Thursday and there was no reason to learn it twice. That is the whole of her contribution, and without it the treatment would have gone on in exactly the same shape for another two days.

The discharge plan was not a list of medicines, although the medicines were on it, and the list was shorter than it had been on any day in hospital. He went home with a plan for what to do when he became ill, which is a piece of paper saying what to do when you are vomiting and cannot keep your tablets down, and who to telephone, and when, and he was told to stop the metformin permanently and to have his diabetes managed by a different plan. He was also told, in plain words, that his illness had been caused by a medicine his body could no longer clear, and that the fault was not his. He was told it twice, once to him and once to his wife, because people frightened by a diagnosis need to hear that they are not the cause of it.

Two things changed outside the hospital as well. The general practice agreed to review the long-standing medicines of everyone over sixty-five and to check kidney function before renewing any medicine the kidneys clear, and the emergency department agreed that a medicine reconciliation on admission would be a conversation rather than a copy, which means somebody would ask the patient what he actually takes. Neither change would have saved him if the nurse had not asked about the metformin, and either would probably have caught the same case months earlier, and that is the honest way to describe what a system change is. It is not a wall against a mistake, it is a set of hands that happen to be holding the same piece of paper.

The teaching of the case is about the label, because sepsis is a label that stops people asking questions. It is a good label and a dangerous diagnosis to miss, and it is the correct first thought for a man with a raised white cell count and cloudy urine, and thinking of sepsis first is not the error in this case. The error is that once the label was attached it became the explanation for everything, including the breathing, the normal temperature, the normal blood sugar and the tablets in the bag, and each finding was folded into the sepsis rather than being asked about. A label is a tool for remembering, and a tool that has been picked up should be put down and looked at once in a while, and the moment to do that is the moment the patient's story stops fitting it.

There is a habit here that goes beyond the case, and it is the habit this book is built on. A treatment that fails is not a failed patient, it is information, and the person who does not improve has not disappointed anybody and has corrected them instead. Mellor was given fluid, antibiotics and observations, all of them were appropriate, all of them failed, and the failure was not in the treatment but in the frame that had chosen the treatment. The nurse who watched that failure did nothing dramatic: she read the drug chart and asked a question, and the question was the whole of the treatment.

There was no moment in this story when anybody was unkind, inattentive in the way that makes a story easy to tell, or wrong in a way that could be shown. Haldane thought sepsis and he was right to think it. Renwick looked for the thing that would kill him soonest and she looked in the places the frame told her to look. The nurse saw something she could not name and wrote it down and said nothing, and then, on the second afternoon, said something, and medicine is not a series of clever people being careless and it is not a series of careful people being lucky, it is a series of people reasoning correctly inside frames.

To summarize, Gordon Mellor was treated for sepsis when what he had was lactic acidosis caused by a medicine his kidneys could no longer clear, and the sepsis was a reasonable diagnosis made competently and then allowed to explain everything that followed. The evidence that gave the game away was in front of the team from the first hour: a normal temperature, a normal blood sugar, a blood pressure that held, and a white cell count that barely moved. The deep steady breathing pattern was there too, and nobody read it as what it was until a nurse wrote it down and then went back to the drug chart. Every number in this account is an illustration of reasoning and must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol before it is used to act, and the teaching is that sepsis is a label that stops people asking questions, and that a deep steady breathing pattern with a normal temperature deserves the drug chart read again.
