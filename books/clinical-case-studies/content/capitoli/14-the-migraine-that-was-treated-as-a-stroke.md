# Chapter 14 — The Migraine That Was Treated as a Stroke

The account of a stroke is usually taken once, quickly, and in the words of the person who was frightened. The account of Claire Dubois was taken twice, and the second time it took an hour and a half, and the difference between those two conversations is the whole of this case. She was forty-four years old, she taught secondary school, and in three weeks she was admitted twice with the same complaint, which was numbness down one side of her body and disturbed vision, and both episodes had resolved before the end of the day. On the second admission a nurse sat down with Claire and with her husband, Marc, and with a notebook, and took the story again from the beginning, slowly, and asked about the words.

What follows is a constructed composite, assembled for teaching rather than lifted from anyone's record, and it does not describe a real patient; the names are ordinary names and no detail here identifies anyone living or dead. The reasoning that organizes it is a single question asked twice: what did this episode actually consist of, and what should have been done with the answer. The answer changed nothing about the treatment for the first three weeks, and then it changed everything, and the thing that changed was a set of words. A stroke unit is a place where people are treated for a disease they may not have, and that is not a rare situation, and the harm of it is not always visible from inside.

Here is what happened the first time. Claire was at home on a Tuesday evening in the autumn, marking work at the kitchen table, when she noticed that the page in front of her had gone strange. She said the words she used were that a light was moving in the corner of her vision, like a zig-zag, and that she could not look at it directly. It passed after a while, and then her right hand went numb from the fingertips to the elbow, and the numbness spread up the right side of her face, and she felt sick and vomited twice. Marc found her in the bathroom, frightened and disoriented but able to walk, and by midnight the numbness had gone and the headache that came with it had settled. She did not go to hospital that night, and she went the next morning, and by then she was well, and the referral was written on the strength of an account she gave in six sentences.

Dr Miriam Carmody was the stroke physician who saw her, with Dr Owen Radcliffe the registrar, and Nadine Osei the stroke unit nurse who took the admission. The working diagnosis was a transient ischemic attack, and the reasoning was audible and reasonable: a woman of forty-four, a focal neurological deficit that came and went within a day, a scan that showed no bleeding, and no cardiac cause found on an electrocardiogram, later an ECG. Two readings were kept in front of them. The dangerous one was a stroke that had declared itself, and the findings that would reveal it are a deficit that does not resolve, a change on the imaging, or a deterioration over hours. The other was bleeding around the brain, and the findings that would reveal that are a sudden severe headache, vomiting, neck stiffness and an abnormal scan. Neither was present, and the team said so, and they were right to say so.

The treatment was the correct treatment for what they believed, and it was begun for the reasons any stroke unit would begin it. A computed tomography scan of the head, a CT scan, was done to exclude bleeding before any drug was given, and it was normal. An antiplatelet drug was started, a statin was started, and a risk factor assessment was begun with her blood pressure, her weight, her smoking history and her contraceptive. The blood pressure at the clinic had been 146 over 92 millimeters of mercury, and where a pressure counts as raised differs between populations and between documents, so the number worth working from is the one your own current guidance uses. She was admitted to the stroke unit rather than sent home, and that decision was also reasonable, because a deficit that has resolved once can resolve again and be worse the second time.

The nursing on the unit was the same nursing that every patient there receives, and it was done well. Neurological observations were kept to a schedule, and the schedule is set by how unwell the patient is rather than by habit, and the frequency your own service works to is written into the current guideline and the local protocol. A mobility assessment was done, and Claire walked unaided with a frame she did not need and would not use. The nurse took a detailed account of what had happened, which is a nursing task that sounds like administration and is not, and Nadine Osei took twenty minutes over it on the first afternoon and wrote it out in full. She wrote that the patient reported visual disturbance before the numbness, that the numbness was on the right side, that there had been vomiting, and that the episode had lasted most of a day. She wrote what the patient had said, not what the patient had been admitted with.

Nadine's own account of that afternoon has the quality that good nursing notes have and bad ones do not, which is that a stranger could read it and know what had happened. She wrote that the light had been in one half of the visual field and had moved, that Claire had turned away from the light and had asked for the blinds to be closed, and that she had been sick twice. What she wrote, and what everybody else read, was the phrase visual disturbance, and she wrote it because that was the phrase Claire used. The word disturbance is neutral. It says something was wrong with the sight and nothing about what, and a word that says nothing cannot be questioned later, and that is how a detail dies.

Claire went home after three days with a plan for what to bring her back, a supply of the antiplatelet drug, and a date for the risk factor clinic. She was told that the episode had been a warning rather than a stroke, and that the tablet was there to reduce the risk of the warning becoming the thing itself, and she believed that, and she was entirely willing to be a person who had had a warning. Her pulse was 76 beats per minute and her heart was regular, and the rate that counts as a normal resting pulse is worth confirming against the current guideline and the local protocol if you are going to reason from it. She went back to teaching. She had had, the team believed, a small stroke that left no trace, and the treatment was aimed at the next one, and the whole arrangement was coherent and wrong.

Three weeks passed, and in that time she went to the risk factor clinic, which is where a stroke unit sends the people whose diagnosis it believes. The clinic took her weight, which had been taken before, and her blood pressure, and asked about smoking, and she had never smoked, and asked about alcohol, and asked about her contraceptive, which she had taken for years without any trouble. The nurse who took the history wrote that she was a non-smoker with a raised blood pressure and no other risk factors, and then wrote in the free text box that the family history was negative, because when the specialist asked, Claire had said that there was nothing in her family. Nobody asked what her mother had, or what her mother's episodes had been called, and so the one piece of information that would have changed everything was excluded by the word nothing. The clinic adjusted her blood pressure treatment and asked her to come back, and she came, and she was a good patient.

Three weeks later she came back. This time the visual disturbance came first and lasted longer, and the numbness started in the same right hand and spread in the same direction, and she was sick three times and could not keep fluids down. The episode lasted most of the working day and she went to the emergency department in the afternoon rather than the next morning, and the registrar on duty was Dr Radcliffe, who had seen her before. Her blood pressure was 138 over 86 millimeters of mercury, and the threshold that should concern a team looking at a blood pressure is one your own protocol states, not one carried between units. A CT scan was done and it was normal, and magnetic resonance imaging, an MRI, was considered and arranged, and the working diagnosis was again a transient ischemic attack. How soon a second episode should be allowed to change a working diagnosis is a question for the current guideline and the local protocol, and nobody asked it here. She was admitted to the stroke unit again, and she was reviewed by the same physician, and nobody anywhere said the word migraine.

The further episode happened on the ward, on the second night. Claire asked for the lights off and asked the staff to turn the television down, and she was sick twice, and at some point in the early hours she was found by her husband sitting up in bed with her right arm held across her chest because she said it felt wrong, though it was not weak and it was not painful. She was not slurred, and she was not confused, and she was oriented to person, place and time, and she answered questions accurately. The neurological observations were unchanged, the pulse was 84 beats per minute and regular, and a rate that should concern a team looking at a ward patient is set out in the current guideline and the local protocol rather than in a habit. The scan on the ward was normal. The team found themselves where stroke teams often find themselves, which is with a patient they are certain about who is having another episode of the thing they are certain about.

The feeling in the room was not anxiety so much as a kind of professional despair. Dr Carmody's thought, which she did not say aloud at first, was that this was what a transient ischemic attack looked like when it was going on to be something bigger, and that the second admission should have been the last warning before a stroke. The registrars' feeling was that the right response to an incomplete picture was to make the picture more complete, which in practice meant more imaging, more tests and a longer stay. Nobody thought about the antiplatelet drug in a way that anyone would want written down, and then Nadine thought about it, and she thought about the fact that a woman who was bleeding from the bowel had been given a drug that makes bleeding more likely.

What happened next was that Claire developed a gastrointestinal bleed on the ward, which is to say she became anaemic, passed black stool, and became dizzy on standing, and she had to be taken out of the unit. The bleed was treated by the team that manages it, and the decision about the antiplatelet drug was taken by the specialist team together with that team, and the drug was stopped. The registrars' feeling about the bleed was that it was bad luck, and possibly an ulcer, and possibly the stress of the admission, and the stroke physician's feeling was that this was the price of doing the right thing for a woman with a real vascular risk. All of those feelings were reasonable and all of them were wrong, because there was no vascular risk to protect her from. The harm had been done by a treatment for a disease she did not have.

That is the sentence the case turns on, and it is worth slowing down on, because the idea that a treatment given for a wrong reason can do harm is obvious in other settings and is very often invisible in this one. An antiplatelet drug given to somebody who has had a stroke is a drug with a real purpose and a real cost, and the cost is paid in the blood. Given to somebody whose episodes were a migraine with an aura, the same drug buys nothing, because there is no clot to be prevented, and the cost is still paid in the blood. The person who pays it does not know she has been given the wrong diagnosis, and she is not in a position to argue with it, because she has just been told that she had a warning sign of a stroke.

The clue that had been in front of them since the first afternoon was not hidden and it was not subtle, and the chapter has to say plainly how it was missed. The nurse reconstructed it, and the reconstruction is the most important piece of nursing in this book. It was done by someone who had been keeping a record of her own for years and who had assumed that everybody else kept one too. On the second admission Nadine Osei went back to the notes and to the nursing diary she had kept during the first admission, and she sat with Claire and with Marc separately and then together, and she asked the same question of each of them in the same words: what happened first, and what did it look like, and how long did it last.

The answer came out in a different order from the one in the notes. The visual symptom came first, and it was not a blurring or a loss of sight but a zig-zag of light in one half of the visual field, which spread and then faded, and it lasted about twenty minutes. Then came the numbness, in the right hand and then the right side of the face, and then the sickness and the vomiting, and the whole thing lasted over an hour. The time course of an aura is one of the numbers a service should have a view about, and the guide you use should be the current guideline and the local protocol rather than a remembered number. Claire wanted the lights off and the curtains shut for the whole of it, and she could not bear the television, and she was quiet and would not be drawn into conversation. Marc added the last piece, because he had been there, and he said that her mother had the same thing and that it had been called migraine all her life.

That is the diagnosis, and it is a common one, and the reason it was missed is not that the symptoms were exotic. It is that the symptom arrived through a door that was already labelled. The team had been told that Claire had had a transient ischemic attack, and every finding was read through that label, so the zig-zag of light became a visual disturbance, the vomiting became nausea, the wish for darkness became a preference recorded by the night staff, and the mother's history became a family history of stroke because nobody had asked what the family called it. A label does not merely describe a patient; it selects what will be noticed, and a diagnosis that has already been made selects against the very observations that would overturn it.

The nurse was not outside this and it would be dishonest to place her outside it. Nadine had written the words visual disturbance into the notes herself, and she had written them because they were the words the patient used, and using the patient's words is usually the right thing to do. She had also been the one who wrote that Claire had asked for the blinds closed, and she had filed that as a nursing note about comfort rather than as a symptom, because it was a note about comfort and because nobody had asked her to think of it as anything else. What she did not do was go back and ask what the light looked like, and what she had not done was ask the family what they called it, and the correction was available to her on the first afternoon.

The team now had to reconsider what they had done and why, and the reconsideration was uncomfortable in a specific way. The scan had been normal and that had been reassuring, and a normal scan had been allowed to stand for more than it was worth, because in a woman of forty-four with no risk factors beyond a mildly raised pressure and a contraceptive, the scan being normal was less informative than it would have been in an older patient. The absence of a cause on the scan had been treated as the absence of a cause in general. The team also had to face the plainest fact in the case, which is that a drug they had given her without question had put her in a hospital with a bleed, and that a drug given for a stroke that never happened is a drug given for a stroke that never happened, with all of its risks and none of its benefits.

So the treatment was changed, and it was changed in two halves. The antiplatelet drug was stopped and stayed stopped, and the specialist team documented why, because the default after a bleed is to restart a drug that has obvious benefits and to think hard about one that has not. The statin was also reconsidered, and if she has no vascular disease then there is no argument for it, and a drug that is continued for a condition she does not have is another treatment with a cost and no purpose. Then the correct treatment for migraine with aura was begun: a migraine-specific acute treatment for the attacks themselves, and a preventive treatment for the attacks, which is the part that matters, because it is what reduces how often they come, how severe they are, and how often she ends up on a ward.

The contraceptive was the other thing that had to be reviewed, and it was reviewed with the same principle applied to it. She had been on a combined oral contraceptive, and in a woman who has migraine with aura, an oestrogen-containing contraceptive is not a neutral choice, because the two together raise the concern that brought her to the unit in the first place. The decision was taken by the specialist team together with the family planning service, and the advice about what to use instead was given in writing, and the follow-up was arranged. That was the point at which the case stopped being about a misdiagnosis and became about a woman with a real diagnosis and a real future, which is a better use of everybody's attention.

Claire improved, and the improvement was not only that the attacks became less frequent. She stopped being a woman with a warning sign in her history and became a woman with a condition she could treat and explain. The headache specialist, Dr Ingrid Solberg, sat with her and went through the whole sequence again in a calm room, and the description was familiar to her, and the familiarity was itself a finding, because a specialist who has heard this story before recognises the shape of it instantly and a general team looking at the same words does not. Claire went back to teaching in the following term. She stopped describing herself as someone who had had a stroke, and she started describing herself as someone who had migraine, and she told her colleagues what the warning signs were, which is a form of treatment that the ward cannot provide.

The stroke unit changed what it did, and the changes are worth naming because they are small and general and any unit could make them. The detailed account of an episode is now taken by a nurse with a set of specific questions, and the questions are about the shape of the symptom rather than about the diagnosis, so that the first half, the kind of symptom, its side, whether the person can see something or has lost something, how long it lasted, what came before it and what came after it. A nurse is told to write what the patient said rather than the label the patient arrived with, and to write the words for the light and the words for the numbness, because the words are the evidence. The unit also now asks the family what they call it, which takes four seconds and has changed more diagnoses than any scan.

There are two things in this case that are easy to confuse, and the chapter would be worth nothing without separating them. The first is that an aura is not a transient ischemic attack, and that the difference is made by the description of the event rather than by any test, because a scan of a normal brain does not distinguish the two and neither does a normal neurological examination. The second is that a treatment which is not indicated has harms of its own, which is why the question about a diagnosis is never only about what is best for the patient but also about what will be done to her while the team is still deciding. The second point is the one that a stroke unit is least equipped to hear, because in a stroke unit the cost of not treating is so much higher than the cost of treating that the instinct to treat is almost automatic.

Nadine Osei kept the diary after the case, as she keeps it for everybody now, and it is the reason she caught what nobody else caught. She had written it for herself and for the next shift, and on the second admission she read it back and found her own words from the first afternoon, and the words were not the words she had meant to write. She had written that the patient reported visual disturbance and requested low lighting, and she had meant both of those as description and both of them were diagnosis, and the one that did the damage was the first. What she took from it was a rule she now uses with every patient on the unit, which is to ask the family what the episode looked like at home and to write that down in their words, because the word a family uses for an episode is usually the word that knows what it is.

To summarize, Claire Dubois was not having transient ischemic attacks, she was having migraine with aura, and the treatment given for the wrong diagnosis put her in a hospital with a gastrointestinal bleed. The decisive observation was made by a nurse who reconstructed the sequence of the episodes from the patient, from her husband and from her own nursing diary, and the decisive findings were ordinary ones that were in the file from the first afternoon: a zig-zag of light in one half of the visual field lasting about twenty minutes, then numbness on one side, then vomiting, then a wish for darkness, and a mother whose identical episodes had been called migraine all her life. An aura and a transient ischemic attack can produce the same complaint, and only the description of the event separates them, so the language used to report an episode decides what the team investigates. A treatment that is not indicated has harms of its own, and no individual in this case failed through carelessness: the failure belonged to a label that selected the observations the team was willing to see, and to a service that treated a woman it had already labelled. This chapter is a constructed composite and describes no real patient, and it is educational material rather than medical advice, a protocol, or a substitute for the judgment of the clinician who is with the patient. Every number quoted here, including the blood pressures, the pulse rates, the duration of the aura and the intervals at which neurological observations are kept, is an illustration of reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol before it informs any real decision.
