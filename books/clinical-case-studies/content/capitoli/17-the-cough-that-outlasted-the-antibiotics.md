# Chapter 17 — The Cough That Outlasted the Antibiotics

Grace Mwangi is thirty-seven and she teaches thirty primary school children in one classroom, and she has had a cough for eight weeks. She has had a fever that comes and goes, weight she has lost without trying to lose, and night sweats she mentions once and then never again. Two courses of antibiotics from a community clinic have made no difference, and that is the fact she puts first, because in her view it ought to have sorted her out by now. She was born and raised in a country where tuberculosis is common, and her mother, a day's journey away, has coughed for two years and lost weight. She has come in because a chest radiograph has mentioned something at the top of her right lung that nobody explained to her.

This case is a constructed composite, assembled for teaching, and it describes no real patient and identifies nobody living or dead. It is here because the failure in it is not a failure of care but a failure of reading, in which competent people reasoned correctly inside a frame that was wrong. The frame was a bacterium, and it was held for three days by people trying hard to make the evidence fit it. The things that would have broken it were written down, in the notes, by the nurse looking after her. Everything turns on a specimen, a temperature chart, a conversation about a mother, and a radiograph read twice.

Sister Angela Brannigan is the nurse assigned to her, and she took her on the day of admission and stays with her for the whole of it. The observations on arrival are ordinary, which is to say unremarkable, with a temperature of 37.9 degrees Celsius, a pulse of 104 beats per minute, and a blood pressure of 104 over 66 millimeters of mercury. Those are the numbers a ward admits every day, and the values your own service treats as abnormal and the rate at which a pulse like that should prompt a second look are set by the current guideline and the local protocol rather than by this account. What is not ordinary is the chest, where the film shows patchy shadowing in the upper part of the right lung and the report describes consolidation and says nothing about a cavity.

Two possibilities have to be named before anything else is decided, because the one that is missed is the one that matters. The first is a resistant bacterial pneumonia, likely for reasons the team can hear in the history, and the finding that would support it is a consolidation that settles when an antibiotic covering the organism is given. The second is pulmonary tuberculosis, revealed by a cavity in the upper zone of the affected lung and by acid-fast bacilli in the sputum on microscopy, and it is dangerous precisely because it is curable and because the delay after a missed diagnosis is measured in months. A third is a lung tumour, which the same film has to be able to exclude and which would show as a mass.

The team meets on the morning after admission. Dr Yvonne Fenwick is the respiratory consultant, Dr Daniel Okafor is the registrar, and Sister Brannigan is there because she has been on the ward since the previous afternoon and because what she has written is part of the evidence. They build the differential together and rank it twice, once by likelihood and once by danger. Resistant bacterial pneumonia comes first on both lists, because she has had a febrile illness for eight weeks with consolidation on a film and because two courses of oral antibiotics have failed, which is the classic reason to change the antibiotic rather than the diagnosis. She is not diabetic, not immunosuppressed and not an inpatient, and the working diagnosis is a resistant bacterial pneumonia including an atypical organism.

Tuberculosis is named and ranked third, and the reasoning that puts it there is worth recording, because it is the kind of reasonable-sounding judgement that is hard to unpick later. She is young and well-nourished, nobody has confirmed a contact, the mother has coughed for two years without anyone testing her, and the film has been reported as consolidation rather than as the upper zone disease it actually shows. There is also a version of the reasoning that is frankly wrong, which is that two courses of antibiotics ought to have worked if this were a bacterium. The team is not careless; the frame was handed to them by the referral, by the word pneumonia, and by a report that used the word consolidation.

What is started is intravenous antibiotics chosen to cover the resistant hospital organisms and the atypical ones, a repeat chest radiograph for comparison with the film from the clinic, sputum for routine culture, blood cultures, and the ordinary supportive care of a chest infection. The written plan includes a review the next morning and a change of antibiotic if she is no better. It is the right plan for the diagnosis they believe, and the review on which it depends will not happen in the form they expect, because what she has neither improves nor deteriorates. The most anyone can do at that hour is to be right about the evidence available, and at that hour the evidence did point to a bacterium.

What happens next belongs to the nurse, and it is not background. The exposure history is taken properly, which means not a tick on a form but a conversation held in words and written down in words. Grace's mother coughs and has lost weight and has never had a chest radiograph. The mother lives with Grace's older brother, his wife and two children, and the brother's own chest illness two years ago was treated with something and tested for nothing. And Grace has been coughing in a classroom of thirty children since the beginning of term, which nobody regards as anybody's business but hers. How long a cough should count as chronic is a question for the current guideline and the local protocol rather than a matter of opinion.

The sputum is the other thing, and the nurse treats it as a procedure rather than a box to be ticked, which is the whole lesson of this chapter in one behaviour. The first request comes up on the ward round at eleven in the morning, and the specimen that comes back is clear and frothy and is, on inspection, saliva. She carries it back to the laboratory, is told it will be rejected, and says she will get a proper one at six in the morning, because that is when sputum comes up after a night lying still. She writes on the request the time the specimen was produced and what it looked like. It is a small act and it changes what the laboratory is able to do with what it receives.

The nursing continues with a temperature chart kept and read properly, with observations, and with a daily weight on the same scale, which turns a loss of weight that everybody had been explaining into a number that moves. On the second night the nurse finds the bed damp and the nightdress folded over the chair, and Grace says, without being asked, that she has changed it twice. The nurse asks what the sweating is like, and Grace says it comes on in the small hours and leaves her soaked and shivering. The nurse writes it down in words with the time beside it, and nobody on the medical team reads it, because it reads as a symptom of fever and they believe she has fever from a bacterium.

There is an upstream failure before the hospital, and it deserves naming because it is the same failure at a cheaper price. Two courses of antibiotics were given over eight weeks by a community clinic without a chest radiograph and without anyone asking what else the cough might be. A cough of eight weeks is not a cough that a course of antibiotics should end, and the interval that should trigger a radiograph is written in the current guideline and the local protocol rather than in a clinician's memory. In the weeks before the film was taken the illness was in a household with a mother, three children and a school class.

The therapy fails in the quietest way there is, which is that nothing happens. The temperature chart shows the fever returning each evening with the highest readings in the night, and she sleeps badly and eats almost nothing, and the cough is no better. The sputum culture reports no growth of any organism after the full reporting period. The repeat film is reported as no significant change, and the antibiotic is changed in the hope of a better fit, because that is what one does when a chest infection has not responded. Nothing on the ward is going wrong. Everything on the ward is going exactly as the working diagnosis says it should.

The registrar feels it first. He does not want to be the man who stopped a treatment a colleague had started and who had it right, so he finds himself looking for reasons to carry on rather than reasons to stop. There is also a version of this that would have been easy, and it deserves naming because it is what many of these cases were: discharge her with oral antibiotics to complete at home, a review at the community clinic, and a letter asking the general practitioner to reconsider if she does not improve. It would have been defensible, it would have been kind to her, and it would have moved the entire problem out of the building.

The consultant is slower. She is inclined to read a fever that has not settled as a sign that the antibiotic has not had long enough rather than as a sign that it never will, and she cannot switch that off. What she does instead, on the second morning, is go back to the beginning, starting with the first film, and ask for the report from the clinic to be sent over. She also asks, in passing, whether anyone has taken a proper exposure history, and Sister Brannigan says that she has, and that it is in the nursing notes, and the consultant says she will read it, and does not. The interval after which a patient who has not improved should have the diagnosis reopened is set out in the current guideline and the local protocol.

The nurse, who has no power to stop anything and no vocabulary for a diagnosis, notices something simpler than either doctor. She has kept the chart on the wall for three nights and the shape of it has not changed since the day before Grace came in, and the same fever at the same hours is not a fever that is being beaten. She has asked twice at handover whether anybody was minded to look at the sputum culture, and has been told it is not back yet, and it has been back for a day. She is not dramatic about it, and what she feels is mostly the particular frustration of holding information she has no route to deliver.

The clue that turns the case had been in the notes since the second morning and nobody read it, and it is the sputum. The culture reported no growth, and the team read that as a laboratory that had not caught up with an unusual organism. Sister Brannigan says something different on the ward round, and what she says is a question rather than a conclusion. She collected that specimen herself, produced at six forty in the morning after the patient had been sitting up and coughing, and she wrote on the request that it was thick and mucopurulent and streaked with blood. A specimen like that ought to grow something, and if it does not then either it was not what it was labelled as, or the organism is not one the laboratory was asked to find.

There are three others, and each was present from the first hour and each was read as something ordinary. The weight is in the admission note as eight kilograms lost over the summer, attributed by Grace to the school term and by the doctor to stress, and the amount of weight loss that should start a search for a cause is set out in the current guideline and the local protocol rather than in the impression of a consultation. The mother is in the nursing notes too, because the medical team asked the exposure question about hospitals and about countries, and the nurse asked it about the people in the house. And the film was there from the first afternoon, showing shadowing at the top of the right lung.

The radiograph is looked at again on the third morning, and here the team recovers. The shadowing at the top of the right lung, read as consolidation on a single projection, has a rounded lucency within it, and there is a fluid level in the lower part, and the wall of it is thin. The consultant says the sentence that reorganises the case, and says it to the registrar rather than to the team, because that is how these things are said in a corridor: nobody has been treating a resistant bacterium, because there is no bacterium in the top of that lung. She says it quietly and stops, and the registrar stands there for a moment, because the sentence is also an accusation and because it is not one, since everyone in that room has been reasoning as they would have reasoned.

What happens in the next hour would be reassuring if it were not so instructive. Sputum is sent for microscopy and for a polymerase chain reaction, or PCR, test, and the earlier specimens are recalled and re-examined rather than thrown away. The registrar cannot write the referral note the way he would have, because the writing changes when the answer is already known, and the consultant asks him to write what is known and what is still suspected. The mother is telephoned, and Grace is told first and is told with the nurse in the room, and the hardest part is not the diagnosis but the sentence about the children at home and the children in her class. Grace moves into a single room, and the ward manager does the arithmetic about who has been near her.

The treatment that changes the course of the illness is combination antituberculous therapy, chosen on the basis of the confirmed organism and given under the specialist team rather than by guesswork on the ward, and what was given and for how long is a matter for the current guideline and the local protocol and not for this account. The reasoning is not hard, and it is worth saying aloud, because the organism grows slowly, so a single drug allows resistant strains to appear and the illness to return, and the combination is chosen to make that unlikely. Alongside it come notification, which is both a duty and a practical protection, and the isolation of the patient while the diagnosis is confirmed. The examination of everybody close to her is part of the same plan.

The mother is the part of the story the team would rather had not happened, and it is the part that changes what the service does afterwards. She travels a day's journey to the nearest clinic, because the nurse telephoned her and explained what was being looked for. The clinic sends her for a chest radiograph the same morning, and the film shows the same picture in the other lung, and she has had two years of coughing and three years of weight loss and nobody had ever looked. The household is examined, the brother whose chest illness nobody diagnosed included. Grace's own household are examined and are well. Her classroom is a separate matter, handled with the local protocol: a letter to every parent, an evening for questions, and a plan for the children who sat closest to her.

She improves slowly, which is what everybody afterwards says they should have expected. The fever settles within a fortnight and the cough thins without ever disappearing quickly, and the weight comes back in the right order. The nurse spends several days with her on the education, because the treatment only works if it is taken, and she teaches it to Grace, to the mother and to Grace's partner, in the words each of them uses. What she teaches them to watch for is the point at which the treatment has to be protected rather than abandoned, which is the cough getting worse, the fever returning, the weight falling, the sputum changing, any of which means a telephone call to the team rather than a decision made at home. She also teaches them what to say to the neighbours, because the fear of the word is one of the reasons people quietly stop taking their tablets.

The follow-up is described in the same spirit, because this is an illness that can come back and one that can damage the lung even after the sputum has cleared. The nurse is at the clinic appointments, weighs the patient on the same scale, and asks where and when the sputum she was given was produced. The radiograph is repeated at intervals set by the specialist team, and the interval that matters is the one written in the current guideline and the local protocol rather than in this account. She watches for the two things that undo a treatment like this one, which are a patient who feels well and stops taking the tablets, and a patient who feels worse and tells nobody because she has been told that feeling worse is part of getting better.

What the service changes afterwards is small and unglamorous and is the point of the chapter. A chest radiograph and a sputum request become a paired action for any patient whose cough has lasted beyond a set interval, and the interval itself is set in the current guideline and the local protocol rather than here. The sputum request form gains a box for the time of production and for what the specimen looked like, because a specimen without those two facts cannot be interpreted afterwards. The exposure history on the nursing admission form is rebuilt so that it asks about people rather than places, and specifically about anyone in the household with a long cough, because those are the answers that carry the disease. And a retrospective review finds two other patients in the previous year whose sputum was sent for routine culture only, and one of them is written to.

Why was it missed? For the reason that governs every other failure described in this book. The frame had been set by the referral, by the word pneumonia, and by a report that used the word consolidation, and once that frame was in place the observations were read as evidence for it rather than against it. The fever that did not settle was a sign that the antibiotic needed changing. The sputum that did not grow was a laboratory that had not caught up. The weight loss was a busy term and a bad autumn. The night sweats were the sweats of fever, and the mother was a mother, and a cough that runs in a family is the most comfortable evidence in medicine because it explains a great deal and predicts nothing.

None of those readings was stupid, and each was made by a person working carefully inside a frame that had been handed to them. The nurse in this story is not the person who noticed something dramatic, and she would not describe what she did as making a diagnosis. She did three ordinary things: she asked the question nobody had asked and wrote down the answer, she insisted on a specimen of the right quality and said so on the request, and she wrote a description of a wet bed in words that could be read aloud on a ward round. It is not that they were ignored by careless people. It is that the notes that carry a history are not the notes that carry a nurse's observations, and the words of a patient at the bedside are filed where the ward round does not go.

The fix is not to ask nurses to be more heroic. The fix is to put the words where the decisions are made, so that a sputum request carries its time and its quality, so that an exposure history has somewhere to live that the ward round will actually read, and so that a nurse who has been asked a question can see the answer acted upon. The nurse who asks twice is not being stubborn; she is offering the team a second chance at a decision. And the offer is worth something only if somebody on the team can take it, which on that ward, for those three days, nobody could.

Grace is asked, near the end, what she thought was happening to her, and she gives an answer the team had not expected. She says the worst part was the afternoon she was told the diagnosis, and the second worst was the afternoon before, when three different people had told her that she was getting better. The consultant goes back and apologises, not for a mistake that belonged to anybody alone but for the days she spent inside a frame that had been handed to her, and the apology is accepted by a woman too tired to be angry, who asks instead whether the children in her class are all right. That question is the reason the letter to the school goes out that week, with a nurse's name at the bottom of it.

The last thing this case teaches is the plainest one, and it is the one the family makes. A patient is not a body that has failed a treatment. A patient is a body with a mother who coughs, a classroom of thirty children, a house with four people in it, and a network of contacts along which the illness travels exactly as it travels along the air. The community clinic that gave two courses of antibiotics without a radiograph was not at fault, and neither was the general practitioner or the registrar. Each was doing reasonable work inside a frame that had been handed to them, and the frame was the thing that failed.

What the frame was, in the end, is a sentence nobody said aloud: that a cough should respond to an antibiotic, and everything afterwards was read in order to keep that sentence true. The defences against it are procedural and they are cheap: ask the exposure question in a form that expects an answer, treat a specimen as a procedure with a time and a quality, put a radiograph next to a sputum request, and re-read the film when the patient does not improve rather than when the film changes. None of these requires a different kind of person. They require a different arrangement of the ordinary ones, and an arrangement is something a service can change on a Tuesday morning.

To summarize, this case went wrong because a diagnosis that was reasonable on the evidence available was allowed to become a frame, and because every finding that did not fit the frame was read as something else rather than as something that mattered. The dangerous possibility was named early, and a resistant bacterial pneumonia was a reasonable working diagnosis for a woman with eight weeks of fever, weight loss, night sweats and consolidation on a film. What it was not supported by was a sputum culture that grew nothing from a thick, purulent, blood-streaked specimen, a fever that returned at the same hours night after night, a bed found damp in the small hours, and a film whose upper zone shadowing was read as consolidation. Those observations are illustrations of reasoning and not thresholds, and each must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol before it informs any real decision. The nurse collected a proper specimen at the right hour and wrote on the request what it was, took the exposure history nobody had taken, and wrote down in words what the night sweats looked like, and the change came from her. The truth was pulmonary tuberculosis with cavitation, treated with combination antituberculous therapy, with notification, isolation, contact tracing of the household and the classroom. The teaching is that a sputum specimen is a procedure and not a box to be ticked, that a cough which has outlasted two courses of antibiotics deserves a radiograph and a conversation about exposure, and that the family is part of the patient.
