# Chapter 6 — The Bellyache Too Severe to Touch

Samuel Nkemelu came through the doors of the emergency department at half past seven in the evening with a plastic bag in one hand and a referral letter in the other, and the first thing Ngozi Duru noticed was that he apologised for taking up a bed. The second thing she noticed was his pulse. She counted it twice while she was talking to him and got a different answer each time, and she wrote it in the box where the pulse goes, because there was a queue behind him and no reason on the form to say anything more. The referral letter was folded in his shirt pocket and she read it while she was wheeling him into a bay. It was three lines long, and two of them said that he had been vomiting since lunchtime.

This is a constructed composite, and no part of it describes a real person; the details are assembled from the ordinary way these presentations arrive, so that the reasoning can be examined rather than the history of anyone living or dead. The letters that come with him are the letters that come with everybody: a name, an age, a problem, and the names of the tablets he takes, written down by somebody in a hurry who had never met him. How old a patient is, and how many hours the symptoms have been going on, both feed the decision about waiting or acting. The intervals and the ages that count as urgent differ between conditions and between services, so check them against the current guideline and against the protocol your own service works to.

The team that night was a surgical registrar, a consultant who could be called, and the emergency nurse who took the admission, with an anaesthetist who came in early because the working diagnosis was surgical. The registrar was Harriet Bexley, who had a way of asking a patient to lie still while she examined him that made most of them try harder than they needed to. The anaesthetist was Nina Karlsen, who came because an obstruction in a man of seventy-one might turn into an operation within a day. The nurse was Ngozi Duru, who took the history, the observations and the triage sheet, and who is the reason this case turned out the way it did. She was not a person who carried out orders on this admission. She was the person who asked the questions nobody else asked, and on this night she was right.

Samuel Nkemelu was 71 and had spent thirty-eight years as a civil servant, and came by taxi because his wife was unwell herself. The pain had started at lunchtime the day before and never stopped, and it was worse over the last few hours, and when she asked him to describe it he said it was the worst pain of his life, and then apologised for saying that, as though it were an exaggeration. The vomiting had begun about six hours after the pain and had continued, and the fluid he brought in the plastic bag was the colour of coffee grounds, which she noted and moved on from, because there were eleven other people waiting. He had had a heart complaint for as long as anybody could remember, and took tablets for it every morning, and she wrote heart complaint and tablets in the notes and did not ask which tablets, because the working diagnosis was obstruction and the tablets were not the working diagnosis.

Dr Bexley examined him on a trolley with the curtains drawn, and asked him to point to the pain first, which he did, and his finger moved a little rather than settling anywhere. The abdomen was soft, she noted, and barely tender, with no guarding, no rebound tenderness, no distension and no obvious scar, and the bowel sounds were present. She looked for a hernia and found none, and no lump and no sign of a strangulated hernia. She pressed gently in the right lower quadrant and he said it hurt a great deal.

The tenderness was out of all proportion to what his abdomen gave back to her hands. She wrote that down. She did not draw any conclusion from it, because at that point she was working down a list. The list was obstruction.

The differential she made out loud for the nurse was bowel obstruction, pancreatitis, a perforated ulcer, and bowel ischemia, and she ranked obstruction first because he was vomiting, and because he had had abdominal surgery a long time ago and adhesions are the commonest cause in somebody with a scar. Pancreatitis was second, and she said what would have shown it: a raised level of the pancreatic enzymes in the blood and a gallbladder full of stones on an ultrasound. Perforation was third, and she said what would have shown that: a rigid abdomen and free air on the film. The dangerous one, the one she put at the end of the list and thought about longest, was ischemia of the bowel, and she said what would have revealed it: pain far worse than the examination, a belly that stays quiet, blood in the vomit, and a pulse that is not regular. She had said all four of those things out loud in the bay, and the nurse had heard them, and the pulse had been in the triage sheet for an hour by then.

The treatment was chosen on the evidence available, and the choice was the ordinary one. He was kept nil by mouth, given intravenous fluid for the dehydration that twelve hours of vomiting had produced, and given an antiemetic. A nasogastric tube was passed and put on suction, because a patient who vomits into a bed cannot be examined, and because emptying the stomach takes the vomiting away. The plan was a computed tomography scan of the abdomen in the morning.

A scan in the morning was reasonable, because the scanner was not staffed at night, because he was not peritonitic, and because his observations were unremarkable. The reasoning was sound, and the whole of it rested on one assumption that nobody questioned. That assumption was that the pain was coming from something in the abdomen that the abdomen could tell them about. The point at which prolonged vomiting becomes a finding in itself rather than a symptom depends on the condition, so check that interval against the current guideline and the local protocol.

Ngozi Duru started a fluid balance chart and a pain score chart, and she did both without being asked, because fluid was running into him and a nasogastric tube was coming out of him. She wrote his observations on the hour, and the pain score at rest and on movement beside each observation, because a patient who rates his pain as the worst he has ever had deserves a number on the chart rather than a word in the notes. She also wrote the things nobody had asked her to write, which was the colour of the vomit, the fact that he had apologised four times in an hour, and the fact that the pulse she counted at triage came out differently the second time. She wrote them because she wrote everything, and because the things which look like small observations at the time are often the things that turn out to have been the whole story.

Harriet Bexley came back at ten o'clock with the night registrar, and she did the thing that doctors do and nurses watch, which is to examine the patient. The abdomen was as it had been, soft and barely tender, and she wrote that it was unchanged, and she read the fluid chart and the observations and wrote that he was stable. The pain score chart was on the bed, and the score beside each set of observations was high and did not move, and she did not pick it up, because the numbers on it belonged to the nurse who had written them and those on the observation chart belonged to everybody. That is how a nursing chart becomes decoration, and it usually happens in the space of one week. How a pain score is recorded and what counts as a safe score are set by the local protocol, so confirm the scale and the thresholds against it rather than working from memory.

Dr Karlsen came at half past ten to do the pre-operative assessment the registrar had asked for, and she asked about the heart complaint and was told again that there were tablets every morning, and she asked what the tablets were for and Samuel Nkemelu said he could not remember, and that the box was at home and his wife had it. She wrote heart complaint, tablets, names unknown, and moved on. She examined him, wrote that he was fit for theatre, and told the registrar that the morning list would take him. Then she looked at the pain score chart on her way out, and said to the nurse that a man who says his pain is the worst of his life has an unusual abdomen, and the nurse said yes, and the registrar was on the telephone at the time and did not hear the remark.

The night settled. He was sick twice more, and each time the nurse recorded what came up and how much of it there was, and the nasogastric tube was still draining, and the observations held their shape. The score on his pain chart was the same at eleven and at midnight as it had been at eight, and the nurse wrote that down and thought about it, because a pain that is exactly the same every hour is not a pain that is easing, and she had learned to be suspicious of a flat line on a pain chart. He asked her twice whether the scan was really going to happen in the morning, and she said it was, and she said it warmly, and she believed it. She did not know yet that the belief she had expressed to him was the belief the whole team was holding, and the belief which would have to be broken.

At about two in the morning the registrar was called to the ward because the nurse had noticed that he was not making sense. He was not raving or agitated, but he was answering questions a little to one side, his blood pressure was lower than it had been and his pulse faster. The nurse had also sent a venous blood gas, and the lactate had come back higher than she expected. The registrar came and checked him over, and thought first of sepsis in a dehydrated man with an obstruction, then of a lactate that did not fit dehydration.

She had a third thought, and she did not say it aloud: something was wrong with the bowel in a way that obstruction did not explain. He wrote down the numbers, and did not ring anybody, and did not bring the scan forward, because he could not think what he would do differently at three in the morning. The nurse did not go back to sleep, and she went and read the triage sheet again. The lactate level and the other blood gas values at which a team should escalate differ between protocols, so take the figures from the current guideline and from the local protocol.

She read it from the top, which is what she did at the end of every shift, and this time she read it with a purpose, because she wanted to see what she had written at seven in the evening. She read the pulse box, where she had written a number and then, a few lines lower, a second number, because she had counted again when the first one did not feel right. The two numbers were different by more than you get from counting at two different moments in a heartbeat. She sat with that for a while. Then she put the two readings from the triage sheet beside the ones she had taken since, and they were all different from each other, and none of them was a regular rhythm. She went and looked at her patient, and she counted the pulse herself, with two fingers and her watch, and it came and went, and she wrote it down as irregular.

That was the observation that reopened the case, and it was worth asking why nobody else had made it. The pulse had been written in a box on a form at seven in the evening, and read by nobody, because the triage sheet is a summary of a person and this particular line did not look like a summary of a person, it looked like a number. The registrar had seen the abdomen and the blood pressure and the vomiting and had found in all of them an obstruction, and the nurse's irregular pulse had no place in that story, so it went into the story as nothing. And the other half of the observation, the one the nurse had been building since ten o'clock, was the gap between what the man said and what his abdomen said, and that gap was visible only if you held the pain score chart and the abdominal findings side by side, which is nobody's job and therefore everybody's.

Ngozi Duru went and found Dr Bexley, and said to her that she thought the pain and the abdomen did not go together, and that she had counted the pulse on triage twice and written down two different numbers, and that she had counted it again tonight and it was irregular. She said it without drama and in the order she had discovered it, and she said the words pain out of proportion. She did not say bowel ischemia, because that was not a nurse's diagnosis to make, and the registrar had a name for it and the nurse did not. Dr Bexley listened to the whole of it, and then said, almost to herself, that a man with an irregular pulse and pain out of proportion and a quiet abdomen had a bowel that was dying, and the registrar at the end of the bed said that she had said exactly that on admission, and everybody in the room heard the same sentence arrive twice, eight hours apart.

That is how these cases turn, and it is worth noticing what actually happened, because it was not that one person was cleverer than the others. On admission the registrar had ranked ischemia fourth and had said out loud what would reveal it. At two in the morning the nurse had put the three parts of that same sentence together: the pain, the abdomen, and the pulse. The information had never left the room.

What had left the room was the connection between the parts. A connection is not carried in a box on a triage sheet, and it is not carried in a pain score either. The nurse had not been given a job to make the connection. She made it anyway, at two in the morning, because she was the one still holding the whole picture, and the team had handed the picture over in pieces.

The scan was brought forward, and the night registrar went to the imaging staff and asked for it to be done as soon as it could be done rather than on the morning list, and the answer was that it could be done within the hour. They did not argue about it, and they did not spend twenty minutes deciding who should have thought of the pulse. The blood gas was repeated and the lactate was higher again, and the nurse wrote the two results side by side with the times beside them, and the line that went up was not a line that could be explained by a man who had not eaten since yesterday morning. The registrar rang the consultant at home and then rang the anaesthetist, and the consultant was awake in three rings, and the anaesthetist said she would come in, and the registrar wrote in the notes that the plan had changed and that the patient was going to theatre. She also asked the ward to stop the drip that had been running, because the anaesthetist wanted the stomach empty, and she asked for the nasogastric tube to be left in place. How quickly the scan should be brought forward in a patient who is deteriorating is a local decision, so confirm the interval against the current guideline and the local protocol.

In the twenty minutes before the team moved, the registrar took a history again, and this time she asked the questions she had not asked at seven in the evening. She asked what the tablets were, and Samuel Nkemelu said his wife had the box and would bring it, and the nurse telephoned the wife, and the wife read out the names, and one of them was a drug used to thin the blood for a heart condition called atrial fibrillation, which is a rhythm of the heart that is not regular and which allows clots to form in the chamber. The referral letter had not mentioned any of it, because the letter had been written by a general practitioner who had seen a man with vomiting and had put the two things that mattered to him on the line above the line. Nobody had asked the man what his tablets were for, and the man had said heart complaint, and heart complaint is not a diagnosis.

The abdomen was opened in the small hours, and the bowel was found to be black and dead in a length running from the small bowel into the colon, and the cause was a clot that had come from the atrium of the fibrillating heart and lodged in the artery that supplies the bowel. The length of dead bowel was removed, and what remained was joined, and the anaesthetist took him to the intensive care unit afterwards, where he stayed for a night with the circulation watched closely. The surgery was the part that fixed the bowel. It did not fix the thing that had caused it, because the heart still fibrillates and still holds the place where the clot came from, and that second problem was solved on the ward rather than in theatre, and the anticoagulation decision was made with the surgical team and the patient, who was seventy-one and had previously declined a blood thinner because he did not want to be on a drug for the rest of his life.

Recovery from this operation is slow, and slow is the point of the story. For days the ward's work was not diagnosis but protection: the fluid balance maintained, the wound watched, the lungs kept clear, and the nutrition built up carefully while the bowel that had been sewn back learned to work again. Ngozi Duru wrote the nursing plan for him herself, around the things that had gone wrong the first time rather than the things that had gone right. Her plan said what his pulse was expected to be, what his abdomen should feel like, what his temperature should be, and what she should do if the wound leaked or he became confused again, and she put the numbers beside the actions. She wrote, in the space at the bottom of the page for anything else the nurse should know, that this man had described pain out of proportion to his examination, and that if he ever again said something that did not match what his body showed, it should be believed first and checked second. The observations expected in recovery, and the rate at which they are taken, are set by the local protocol, so check them against the current guideline rather than against a general idea.

He left the hospital after a long stay, thinner and weaker than he had been, with a frame and a plan for his tablets that he had not had before. He said that the part of the night that frightened him was not the pain but the hour when he could not make sense of himself and the young woman was speaking to him and he could not answer her. He said that he had been the most frightened of his life in that hour, that nobody had told him what was happening, and that he had thought he was dying and the ward had not noticed. He said it without any bitterness, and the registrar who was on the ward that week wrote it in the notes, because it is the kind of thing that is worth writing down and almost never is.

Nothing that happened to Samuel Nkemelu was the fault of any individual, and it is worth saying so clearly, because the story of a nearly fatal delay is usually told as the story of somebody being slow. The nurse had not been slow. The registrar had not been slow. The team had been working hard and reasoning carefully on a presentation that fits bowel obstruction almost perfectly, and the facts that did not fit had been present from the first hour and had been present in writing.

The nurse had counted an irregular pulse and written it down and had not been asked what it meant. The abdominal examination had found a quiet belly and had been recorded in those terms. A referral letter had been shortened to three lines by a general practitioner doing a good job with the time available to him. The information about the heart had been left out of this one.

The two lessons in this case are both about reading what is written rather than what is expected. The first is that pain out of proportion to the examination is a diagnosis, not a puzzling symptom, and that a quiet abdomen in a patient who is screaming is a warning, not reassurance, because the bowel that is dying is the bowel that has not yet had time to inflame the tissues around it. A patient whose pain is the worst he has ever had and whose abdomen can be pressed without much difficulty is not reassuring to examine, and the abdomen is examined last and relied on most. The second lesson is about the irregular pulse on the triage sheet. An irregular pulse is not a line to be scored out of a form and read past. It is a piece of information, it was there at seven in the evening, and this patient's treatment turned on it, and it reached the registrar only because a nurse went back and read a form she had already filled in.

The countermeasure for both is small and unglamorous. When the pain and the examination do not agree, that disagreement is the finding, and it should be written down where it cannot be skimmed past, because every diagnosis in the list was argued on the abdomen. And when a triage sheet records something unusual, it should be passed on at the time, in words, to whoever is taking the patient, because a box on a form is not a handover, and the information that changes a case is almost never the information that was expected. Any team could have made this correction, and any team might have made it earlier, because the correction was not a matter of cleverness but of attention to what the form already said.

To summarize, Samuel Nkemelu did not have a bowel obstruction but acute ischemia of the bowel, caused by a clot thrown from an atrium that had been fibrillating for years and about which nobody in the hospital knew, because the pulse that would have said so was written in a box on a triage sheet at seven in the evening and read by nobody until two in the morning. The failure was not in anybody's skill or in anybody's care. It was a working diagnosis that explained almost everything, a pain score chart that everybody could see and nobody read, an abdominal examination that was treated as more reliable than the account of the pain, and a piece of paper that summarised a man in three lines and left out the part that mattered. The nursing observation that turned the case was an irregular pulse counted twice at triage and again at two in the morning, read alongside a pain score that had not moved all night, and the two together said that the pain was coming from somewhere the abdomen could not show. Every number given in this chapter is offered as an example of the reasoning rather than as a figure to work from, and each one must be verified against the current guideline and the local protocol before it is applied to a patient. The chapter is teaching material rather than advice, and it does not stand in for the training, the supervision, or the judgment of the clinician who was at the bedside. And the lesson that travels furthest is that pain out of proportion to a quiet abdomen is a diagnosis in itself, and that an irregular pulse on a triage sheet is information, not a line to be ruled.
