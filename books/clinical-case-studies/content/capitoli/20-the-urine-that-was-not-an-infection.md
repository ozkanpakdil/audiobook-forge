# Chapter 20 — The Urine That Was Not an Infection

The most dangerous result in an emergency department is the one that makes everybody feel better. Joyce Fairweather was eighty-two, she had fallen at home in the early morning, and the dipstick taken from her urine at triage showed nitrites and white cells, and that result turned a worried room into an orderly one. The story that follows is a composite written for teaching, and no part of it describes or identifies a real person. The case is worth telling because the diagnosis was not wrong in the way a wrong diagnosis is usually wrong, and because everything that was true about it was also true about the right diagnosis. The urine result answered the question the team had asked, and nobody had asked the question that mattered.

This chapter is educational material and not medical advice, and it is not a protocol or a substitute for training, supervision, or the judgment of the clinician who is with the patient. Every number in it is an illustration of reasoning, and a dipstick result read on its own is exactly the kind of finding that has to be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol before anybody builds a plan upon it. The reasoning that organizes the story is a single question: what would have to be true for this dipstick to mean an infection, and had anybody established that? Nothing in the history supported it, and the leg had been in the department for the best part of a day and a half.

Joyce Fairweather lived alone in a ground-floor flat with the bathroom downstairs, and she had memory problems that were mild enough for her to manage and severe enough for her daughter to worry about. Sandra Bell, who came in several mornings a week to help with shopping and the heavy work, found her on the bathroom floor with the cold tap running. She could not get herself up, and she had been there a while, and she was cold and wet through and had been sick twice. Joyce Fairweather had a urinary catheter in place, which had been changed by the district nurse a few days earlier after a spell in hospital with a urinary infection, and it was that catheter, and the way she now lived, that made her urine so interesting to the department.

Her daughter, Hazel Fairweather, had telephoned the ambulance and then the department, and what she said on the telephone was that her mother had been muddled for a day or two, had not been eating, and had become unsteady on her feet. That account was accurate about the eating and the unsteady walking, and it was wrong about the muddling. Joyce Fairweather was not confused when the nursing staff first spoke to her. She gave her name, she knew she was in hospital, she knew that she had fallen, and she could describe her discomfort, which she located in her hip. She said she had thought the room was turning and that she had caught the edge of the sink on the way down. The family had used the word confusion because the family had been told, at some point in the past year, that memory trouble was a form of confusion.

The nurse who took her history was Verity Macalister, and she did the triage examination the way she always did it, which is to say slowly and without the assumption that the answer was already known. She asked about the urine, and Joyce Fairweather said she had no pain on passing it, that she had not noticed herself going more often than usual, and that she had noticed nothing at all about her urine because she could not feel that she was passing it, which is what happens to a person with a catheter in place. She asked about the fall, and about the days before it, and about eating and drinking. Then she took the sample from the catheter, and the dipstick was positive for nitrites and for white cells, and she wrote the whole thing down, and the line that was to matter was the one she wrote next.

The note read, in full: no urinary symptom of any kind, no frequency, no pain, no urgency and no blood, the catheter in situ and draining, the specimen taken from the catheter, and the family reporting muddling over a day or two. Above that she had written that the patient was alert and oriented and knew where she was. The dangerous alternative to a urinary infection was not a remote one, and it was not far to seek. It was the hip. An old woman who has fallen and cannot get up may have broken it, and a fracture in a person who cannot report pain properly, or who has been labelled confused, may be the reason for the fall rather than a consequence of it, and the finding that would have revealed it is pain when the hip is moved and a leg that is short and turned outward.

Joyce Fairweather was dry rather than flushed, and her mouth was dry, and she had been drinking poorly for a day or two. Her temperature was 36.9 degrees Celsius, her pulse was 62 beats per minute, and her blood pressure was 174 over 92 millimeters of mercury. A heart rate that should worry you in a woman of this age, and a blood pressure that should make you look for something rather than reassure you, are set by the current guideline and the local protocol in the unit you work in, and the point of these figures is not their separate values but what they do together. She was alert, she was oriented, she was not flushed, she was not tachycardic and she was not febrile. That combination is the opposite of the picture the team went on to carry in their heads for the best part of a day and a half.

The team was an emergency department registrar, Elena Ruiz, a nurse practitioner, Nnamdi Obi, and the nurse who had taken the history. They met at the bedside with the triage note, the observations and the first results, and they did what teams do, which is to build a differential and rank it twice. In order of likelihood they put a urinary tract infection, a dehydration from poor intake and a chest infection, and then a fracture of the hip, a stroke, a bowel obstruction and a metabolic disturbance. In order of danger they put the sepsis, the fracture of the hip and the bowel obstruction, and the honest difficulty was that the same two things, the reported confusion and the positive urine, were doing all the work in both lists at once.

The ranking of sepsis deserves a word of its own, because the team did not dismiss it, and that is a point in their favour. A normal temperature does not exclude sepsis in a person of this age, and neither does a slow pulse, and what your own service counts as a fever in an older adult is a matter for the current guideline and the local protocol. The difficulty was not that the team lacked a threshold but that the dipstick had supplied them with a cause, and a cause is the most reassuring thing a clinician can be given at two in the morning. Having settled on infection as the explanation, they no longer had to decide separately whether she was septic, because the two questions had collapsed into one, and the question that was lost was the one about the leg.

The working diagnosis was a urinary tract infection with confusion, and the reasoning was audible. She was eighty-two, she had fallen at home, the family reported muddling, she had a catheter that had been changed recently, and the urine had nitrites and white cells in it. Nnamdi Obi said that in an older patient with a new change in behaviour and a positive dipstick the infection was the most likely explanation and the most treatable, and that to be fair to the dipstick it had answered the question that had been asked of it. The plan was antibiotics, a fluid plan and a urine culture to be sent, with observations and a fluid balance chart, and the plan for the confusion was to treat the infection and watch. It was a reasonable plan. It was aimed at the right disease and the wrong patient.

The strength of that plan was that it explained everything that had been noticed, and the weakness of it was exactly the same thing. A catheter that has been in place is a foreign body in the bladder, and urine from such a catheter very often grows something, and a dipstick read on that urine is very often positive. The specimen that was sent came from the catheter rather than from a fresh sample, and it grew a mixture of organisms rather than one, and when that result came back the team read it as an infection that was not responding to the antibiotic they had chosen rather than as a specimen that was never going to mean anything. A result that answers a question nobody asked is the most persuasive kind of evidence in medicine, because it looks like evidence and it is not.

The nursing was where the day and a half were actually spent. Verity Macalister kept the observations, and she kept a fluid balance chart that recorded every drink taken and every void, and she did a pain assessment on a regular cycle, and she did the thing that mattered most, which was to examine her again. Not to look at her, which is what happens on a busy afternoon, but to examine her: the chest, the abdomen, the feet, the skin over the sacrum, the mouth and both legs. She had a reason for that and she had held it for years. A confused older person in an emergency department cannot give a history, so the examination is the history, and if nobody examines her, then nobody knows anything about her that was not written in the triage note.

The first day passed without drama, and that was part of the problem. Joyce Fairweather took her fluids, and she passed urine, and the fluid balance chart filled in with reasonable numbers, and she was oriented and polite to the nursing staff and asked for a newspaper. She was eating little, but she had eaten little for a week, and the family had said as much. Nnamdi Obi came and reviewed her twice and wrote in the notes that she was no worse and that the antibiotic would be changed if she had not improved by the following morning. Verity Macalister wrote something shorter and less comfortable, which was that she remained alert and oriented, that she had asked twice when she was going home, and that the family were still describing her as confused.

She got worse in the ordinary, unremarkable way that older people get worse. She slept a great deal and was hard to rouse for the breakfast. She refused the food and then refused the drink, and she turned her face to the wall when the staff tried to wash her. Her temperature stayed normal and her pulse stayed slow, and she was no more flushed than she had been the day before, and the family were told that the infection was not settling and that the treatment was being changed. Elena Ruiz felt the pull towards a second agent and towards imaging, and she felt the pull towards the phrase that was making everybody comfortable, which was that an old woman with infection in her urine and a fracture she cannot feel is a common and well recognised combination. And she also knew, in a way she could not put in a note, that she had never examined the legs.

The family were told, kindly and wrongly, that the infection was proving difficult to treat, and Hazel's relief at having an explanation that named something visible was almost audible on the telephone, and that relief was the most expensive thing in the case. Meanwhile the whole team was busy, because there was a real job to do in keeping alive a woman who was not eating and was drifting, and the job of a comfortable diagnosis is that it makes the discomfort invisible. Nobody was unkind. Nobody was careless. Everybody had a reason. And a day and a half of it is enough for a team to become completely fluent in a story that is not true.

The nurse who found the answer did it in the most ordinary way possible, by reading her own note again. Verity Macalister had written it at triage, and she read it the way you read a note when the story it tells has stopped working, which is slowly, and without the frame you brought to it. The first line said that there were no urinary symptoms of any kind. The dipstick was positive. Both of those things had been true for the best part of a day and a half, and nobody had put them next to each other, because to do so would have required someone to say out loud that the dipstick might be telling them nothing, and nobody wants to be the person who says that about a test that has already been acted upon.

And then the examination was done, and it took four minutes. Joyce Fairweather's left leg was short. The foot was turned outward, so that the toes pointed across the midline, which is the posture a hip takes when the neck of the femur breaks and the muscles above it go into spasm. The hip was painful when it was moved, and she said so, and she had not said so before because nobody had asked in a way that let her answer. She would not put weight on it at all, and she had not walked since she came in, and nobody had noticed that she had not walked since she came in, because a patient with an infection is not expected to walk and a patient who is confused is not asked.

The fracture explained the fall, and it explained the failure to eat and the failure to drink, and it explained the slow pulse and the raised pressure and the dry mouth and the pain that the chart had recorded as one out of ten because the question had been asked at the moment she was being washed and she had answered about the wash. The score that should have raised concern depends on the instrument your service uses, and the way pain is assessed in a person with memory problems is worth setting against the current guideline and the local protocol. The fracture explained the confusion as well, in the way a broken hip explains confusion in an older person, through pain, through the stress of the injury, through the fever that sometimes accompanies it, and through the plain arithmetic of a person lying still in a strange bed who cannot get to the toilet. A hip fracture in an older adult is a medical event as much as a surgical one, and the confusion is not a separate problem to be treated alongside it but part of the same event.

The film confirmed it, a displaced fracture of the neck of the femur, and the appearance of the leg had been the evidence for a day and a half while nobody read it. The treatment was surgical, and the surgical team came early and involved the team looking after her, and the decision was made quickly, because a fracture of the neck of the femur left untreated and then repaired late carries its own complications of immobility, and because every hour of waiting is an hour of pain and an hour of delirium. The two teams planned the operation together, because the decisions that decide whether an older person gets well after a broken hip are made before the operation as much as during it, and because a hip that is repaired but that is then nursed as though the surgery were the whole of the problem is a hip that will not do well. The antibiotics were stopped, because there was no infection to treat, and the specimen already sent for culture was allowed to finish and was read for what it was, which was the flora of a catheter and not the cause of anything.

Before she went to theatre she was made as ready as an eighty-two-year-old with a broken hip can be made. Her fluid plan was rebuilt around what she actually drank, and her mouth was looked after, and she was moved onto the regular ward where the light was better and the night staff knew her. Pain was controlled properly, because pain that is untreated in an older adult produces delirium, and because a hip that is not comfortable cannot be moved. An electrocardiogram, or ECG, was recorded, and the team looked at the anaesthetic question and the question of her blood pressure together, because a woman with a raised pressure and a slow pulse is not a woman to be hurried into theatre without thinking about it.

She came back from theatre with the hip fixed and with a plan that had been written before she went, and the nursing that followed was organised around three things, which were pain, movement and the night. Pain was assessed at rest and on movement rather than at rest only, because the patient who is comfortable lying still and screaming when turned is the patient whose chart says the pain is under control and whose thigh is not. Movement began the next morning and was done with the frame and with two people, and it was done before lunch, because patients who are sat up in the morning are less confused in the evening. And the observations were recorded with the times of day written beside them, which was the same discipline that the triage chart had used against them, turned now to good account.

The nights were planned as carefully as the days, because the night is where this kind of case comes apart. A clock was in the room, and the curtains were open, and somebody sat with her for a while, and the order of the medication and the observations was arranged so that she was not woken unnecessarily in the small hours. She was helped to the toilet and was never asked to use a bedpan in the dark, and the reason for that was written on the chart, because the reason for it was the whole case. Joyce Fairweather had gone to the toilet alone at home, had fallen, and had spent the best part of a day and a half in a hospital bed while a positive dipstick was given the credit for it.

She stood within a couple of days, and she walked to the end of the ward, and she went home with a frame and with a plan and with the knowledge that her hip had been broken and not infected. Her confusion went with the pain and the darkness and the hunger, and it went quickly, which is itself the evidence that it was never the infection. The urine in her catheter remained positive, because it always had been and always would have been, and by the time she was discharged nobody was treating it. A result that stays positive in a patient who is well is a result that has been reinterpreted, and reinterpretation is a clinical act rather than an administrative one. Hazel Fairweather was taught, before her mother went home, that a catheter left in place will nearly always produce a positive result, and that a result is a question about a sample rather than a verdict about a person, and Sandra Bell was asked to report any change in the way Joyce Fairweather walked.

What Verity Macalister watches for now is different, and the change is the point of the case. She watches for pain that is absent from the chart but present in the face, and she watches the record of what was asked rather than only what was answered. She watches the legs of every older person who comes through the department, and she counts them, and she asks when they last walked, because the number of steps a patient has taken since admission is a sign written nowhere and worth more than most of the results on the chart. She watches the dipstick, and she asks where the specimen came from and how long the catheter had been in place, because a sample taken in the wrong way carries no information at all.

Two things changed in the service afterwards, and neither of them was about antibiotics. Every confused older person in the emergency department now has a documented examination that includes the legs, the feet, the skin and the mouth, written in words by the nurse who did it, and every such patient is asked to stand or to walk to the toilet before the end of the first hour, and the answer is written down whether it is yes or no. And every urine result from a patient with a catheter in place is now discussed as a specimen and not as a diagnosis, because the sample and the patient are two different things, and the result belongs to the sample. The reason for both changes was not a rule about urine at all but a rule about looking, and the team that wrote them had been taught that lesson over a day and a half by a woman who could not teach it any other way.

The first lesson is the plain one: a positive dipstick is not an infection. It is a piece of paper with a few coloured squares on it, and what it means depends on where the urine came from, whether the patient has a catheter, whether she can report her symptoms, and what else might be going on. The second is that a family who says a person is muddled is giving a description and not a finding, and the person in front of you may well be perfectly alert, and the disagreement between the two is itself a sign to examine rather than to treat. The third is that nobody asks an older patient to stand, because standing is not a test anybody orders, and because the answer is written on a chart that nobody reads.

It is worth asking how much earlier this could have been found, because the value of a teaching case lies in the turning point rather than in the outcome. At triage, the question that would have changed everything is the one the nurse had already answered in writing, which is whether there were any urinary symptoms, and the answer was no, and a diagnosis that requires symptoms needs them to be looked for. At triage, the second question is whether the leg looks right, which takes seconds and which nobody asked, because she had come in with a fall and the team had found a diagnosis to put on the fall. And on the first morning, the third question is whether she has walked since she arrived, and the answer was that she had not, because nobody had asked.

No individual in this case did anything wrong, and that matters, because the mistake here was not a mistake of care but a mistake of frame. Elena Ruiz, Nnamdi Obi and Verity Macalister were reasoning correctly inside a story that the triage note had started and that every subsequent observation fitted, and the reason it fitted is that a woman who has broken her hip and is lying still will have a positive dipstick and no fever and a slow pulse and a dry mouth and a frightened family. Any team seeing those five things together would have had difficulty seeing past them, and any team who had looked at the legs would have found the answer in a minute. What changed was not the people but the order of the questions.

To summarize, Joyce Fairweather came to the emergency department with a broken hip and was treated for a urinary infection, and for the best part of a day and a half the infection was the reason she was not asked to stand. The clue was in the notes from the first ten minutes, in a sentence written by the triage nurse that said there was no urinary symptom of any kind, and it went unread because a positive result and a frightened family make a comfortable pair. The nurse who found it did the least dramatic thing available and did it twice, reading her own note again and then examining a leg, and the fracture that explained the fall, the poor intake, the confusion and the constant urine was found. The treatment that worked was surgery, adequate analgesia, hydration, early mobilisation and the stopping of an antibiotic that was never indicated, and every figure in this chapter is an illustration of reasoning to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, and this chapter is educational material rather than medical advice, so the patient in front of you always outranks the page.
