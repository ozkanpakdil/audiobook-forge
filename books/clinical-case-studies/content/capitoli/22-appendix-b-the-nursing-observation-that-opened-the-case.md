# Appendix B — The Nursing Observation That Opened the Case

This appendix is a teaching synthesis rather than a case, and the twenty stories in this book have been told already. It sets the twenty decisive nursing acts side by side and asks what they have in common, since they are not the same act repeated twenty times. One is the pulse counted during a collapse, and another is the calf measured on the triage sheet with a tape, at the same point, by the same person. Another is a specimen produced at the right hour and described on the request, and another is a question about a period nobody thought to ask. What the twenty share is a quality of attention rather than a technique, and such a quality can be taught and defended.

Before the substance, the ground rules, said as they are said at the front of this book. This is educational material. It is not medical advice, it is not a protocol, and it is not a substitute for training, supervision, or the judgment of the clinician who is actually with the patient. Every case below is a constructed composite assembled for teaching, no detail of any of them identifies a person living or dead, and real patients are messier than any illustration of this kind. Nothing here should be used to decide the care of a real patient without that judgment and without the rules that the current guideline and the local protocol require.

What the twenty acts have in common is that each one is specific, each one is written in words rather than in ticks, and each one is carried to the team by somebody prepared to be asked why it matters. These three qualities are separable, and separating them matters, because a service can improve one without the others and improve nothing at all. Specificity is a property of the observation, prose is a property of the record, and delivery is a property of the person who makes it and of the arrangement around them. Most of what follows concerns the first two, because the third usually fails for reasons of organisation rather than character. None of the three is a matter of natural talent, and all three are habits any team could install.

Begin with specificity, because it is the easiest to teach and the most often lost. The calf measured on the triage sheet in The Breathless Woman After the Flight, the weight on the same scale every morning in The Indigestion That Would Not Settle, and the limb measured at a point marked on the skin are measurements, and a measurement differs in kind from an impression. A note that says the leg is swollen records a sensation, whereas a note that says the left calf measured two centimeters larger than the right, at the same point, with the same tape, records a fact anyone can check tomorrow. That difference in a calf is an illustration of reasoning rather than a threshold to act on, and the difference worth acting on is the one written in the current guideline and the local protocol of your own service.

The same principle applies where nothing can be measured, and there the observation becomes a description precise enough to be tested against what happens next. The direction in which pain travels and the way a man sat are the clearest example, in The Stone That Was Not a Stone, since pain that goes through to the back and is relieved by leaning forward is not pain that comes in waves. Asking where the pain goes, what makes it worse and what makes it better, and taking the answer at face value when it disagrees with the expected pattern, costs nothing. What defeats it is asking in the language of the diagnosis already chosen, which turns a question about the site of the pain into a question about the stone.

Elsewhere the specific finding was the joints moved and the temperature of a hand. Moving a swollen joint and watching where the pain appears separates an inflammation of the joint from an infection of the tissues around it, and a limb that needs a needle in it from one that needs only a drug. The temperature of that patient was 38.9 degrees Celsius, and that reading is an illustration of the reasoning rather than a standard, with the threshold your own service treats as a fever to be taken from the current guideline and the local protocol. In The Woman Who Would Not Sit Still the decisive detail was not a number but the temperature of a hand, hot and dry rather than cold and clammy, written down at the bedside while the team looked at a pulse.

The second quality is that the observation is written in words. A chart of ticks records that something was done and not what was seen, and a laboratory report is written to answer the question asked of it, which means that a report cannot answer a question nobody put. So the nurse who pulled the blood film back from the notes in The Bruises That Were Not Bruises, read the whole report rather than the reassuring line inside it, and asked one precise question about whether the red cells were fragmented, did something no laboratory could do on her behalf. She read the sentence the report had been written to contain, and asked for the answer to a question the request never made, which is a general habit rather than a rare skill.

Sometimes what the record needs is not a measurement but an account, and the account has to be long. The account of an event written down in full instead of in a single word is the whole difference in The Fits That Stopped When She Was Distracted. An event called a fit and an event described as shaking whose eyes stayed shut throughout, whose movements changed shape, and which stopped when the patient was given something to do, are different objects, and only the second can be investigated. The same applies to the episode in The Migraine That Was Treated as a Stroke, which began with a zig-zag of light in one half of the visual field and lasted about twenty minutes, with the intervals your own service uses to be read from the current guideline and the local protocol. The words decide what gets investigated, and they must be the words of the person and of the family.

The written word carries the same weight in two other places. The recovery nurse who wrote down what a patient said in the minutes before a fit, in The Fit That Was Not a Fit, in his own words and with the time beside each line, produced a record that could be read aloud and turn a working diagnosis inside out. And the nurse who checked the inhaler technique and then listened to the breath in The Asthma That Would Not Budge, hearing a noise that arrived on the way in as well as on the way out, found a disorder a single label had hidden for years, because a sound that begins and ends with an episode is not made in the lungs. One needed the nurse to write down what she heard with her own ears, the other to ask what the sound was rather than what the episodes were called.

The third quality is delivery, and in most of these cases it began as a question, not a finding. When was your last period, asked of the girl with the stomach ache, and what have you been doing to your foot, asked in The Hot Ankle. When did this medicine last change, and what does the leaflet actually say, asked at three in the morning in The Woman Who Woke Up Shaking. When did she last pass urine, asked of the woman admitted in agitation, and who lives in the house, and who in the house has been coughing, asked in The Cough That Outlasted the Antibiotics. These are the questions a form does not ask, and in several of the twenty cases the answer ended the story that had been told.

What these observations have in common in time is that most were made after the treatment had failed, by somebody still holding the whole picture. The frothy urine noticed because the bedpan was being emptied, and the froth that would not settle on standing, in The Swelling That Was Not Heart Failure. The urine output written hour by hour on a chart that nobody had asked for, in The Stone That Was Not a Stone. The glucose measured at three in the morning in a patient whose dose had been changed at a review she had not attended. The sputum request that was not let go, and a culture reporting no growth of anything from a specimen thick and streaked with blood. And the temperatures of The Woman Who Would Not Sit Still, written out in the order they had been taken, showing a temperature that did not settle while the heart rate was treated.

Two of these observations were about time rather than content, and both are easily lost because neither looks like a measurement. One was the temperature chart of a child together with the examination of her hands and her eyes, in Five Days of Fever, where the finding was the pattern and not the height of the fever: the rash faded and returned, the eyes were injected on both sides, the tongue was red and bumpy, the hands and feet were swollen. A duration such as five days illustrates how the timing of an illness is reasoned about, and the interval at which a service should look for a cause is set in the current guideline and the local protocol. The other was the observation that the confusion came and went with the clock, which opened the case of Agitation in a Lady of Eighty-Four, lucid in the morning and lost by the evening, a different illness from a dementia measured in years rather than hours. A chart written in words can be read backwards in a way that a chart of ticks cannot.

One observation is worth isolating, because it is the commonest failure in the assessment of an older person and it happened twice in these pages. The leg that was examined when the urine was blamed. In The Urine That Was Not an Infection a woman came in with a urine result read as an infection, and the decisive act was a nurse who noticed that the leg on one side was short and turned outwards, that the hip hurt when it was moved, and that she would not put weight on it. The same examination in another patient admitted in agitation found a fracture that the first film had not shown, and the agitation turned out to be pain, a full bladder, an empty bowel, a new medicine and a dark room at night, none of it an illness in itself. In the first of those two cases the nurse had also written, at triage, that there was no urinary symptom of any kind, because a positive result with no frequency, no pain and no fever is not evidence of an infection.

The discipline of the absence runs through the rest of these cases. A collapse with no warning of any kind is a different event from a faint that announces itself, and in The Woman Who Fainted in the Garden there had never been a prodrome. A clear chest radiograph is a result and not an answer, and the breathless woman after the flight had nothing to account for a heart rate that was too fast. In The Bellyache Too Severe to Touch the pulse was irregular from triage and read past by everybody except the nurse who counted it twice, and a quiet abdomen in a patient who calls it the worst pain of his life is a warning, not reassurance. Each was available from the first hour, and each was read as confirmation because the diagnosis had already been chosen, so a failed treatment was read as proof it had not been given long enough.

It is worth being honest about two awkward things. A nurse who says that a patient's urine does not look like an infection, or that the number on a triage sheet does not fit the story, is telling a doctor that a diagnosis may be wrong, and no training removes the cost of that. One of these observations was met with the remark that it belonged in a different part of the notes, and a nurse made to feel that she has said something wrong will think twice before she says it again, and the next patient pays the cost. Nothing in that is a reason to expect less of the next observation, and a service that treats the nursing as an instrument will hear these things more quietly.

The second awkward thing is that the nurse was usually inside the failure rather than outside it. In The Asthma That Would Not Budge she had listened for years with a filter on, because the patient had a diagnosis and the filter was the diagnosis. In The Migraine That Was Treated as a Stroke she wrote the words visual disturbance into the notes because those were the patient's own words, and in The Acidosis That Was Called Sepsis she saw the deep steady breathing for a whole night and said nothing until she had somewhere to say it. What turned each case was the decision to ask a more specific question than the notes were asking for, and to write the answer where somebody else could read it. That is a habit, and a habit can be installed.

Underneath all of it is a proposition about what a record is for. The nurse who opens a case is usually the only person present at every stage of it, and so holds the longitudinal account: what the patient said on arrival, what was measured on the first night, what was given and when, and what the family noticed afterwards. The medicine sees a slice of that account, usually the slice that matters today, and a slice cannot be interpreted without the rest of it. This is why the recurring feature of these twenty failures is not that the information was absent, but that it was present and filed where nobody went to look for it.

So the record is the diagnosis, or at least the record is where the diagnosis was either visible or invisible. A temperature chart of a child, a fluid balance chart started without being asked for, a nursing note that describes rather than summarises: each is a place where the diagnosis either was or was not visible. That is not a claim that the nurse should be making diagnoses, and it is the opposite. The nurse is not licensed to name the illness and does not need to, and the diagnosis is made by the team from what has been written down, which is why the person who writes is not an assistant to the diagnosis but part of it.

And here is where the argument ends, or nearly ends. The teams in this book that got their cases right were not those with the most senior person in the room, but the teams that read the notes. They read them because somebody had taken the trouble to write them in a form that could be read, and because somebody had asked why the urine foamed and would not settle, or why the confusion came back at the same hour every evening. A note that says the patient is unwell changes nothing, and a note that says what was seen, in words, at the time, is the thing a diagnosis is built on. Almost none of the changes that followed these cases required a different kind of person: a triage sheet read from the top, a sputum form with a box for the time and the appearance of the specimen, a recovery room chart with a space for the patient's own words, a weight taken on the same scale in the same corner. Twenty such acts, in twenty unrelated cases, are not a coincidence of talent but a consequence of arranging a service so that the person holding the record is also the person who is listened to.

To summarize, every one of the twenty cases in this book was turned by a nursing observation, and the observations were twenty variations on one habit: look at the patient rather than at the diagnosis, and write down what was found in words the next person can use. What made each work was that it was specific, that it was recorded as language and not as a tick, and that it was carried to the team by somebody prepared to be asked why it mattered. The nurse holds the longitudinal record of the patient, and the record is the diagnosis, so the teams who get these cases right are the teams that read the notes. Every number used here is an illustration of reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. This appendix is educational material, not medical advice, not a protocol, and it does not replace training, supervision, or the judgment of the clinician who is with the patient.
