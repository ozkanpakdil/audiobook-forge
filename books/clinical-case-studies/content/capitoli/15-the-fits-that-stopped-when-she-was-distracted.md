# Chapter 15 — The Fits That Stopped When She Was Distracted

Everything about this case turned on a single word, and the word was seizure. The family used it, the referral letter used it, and by the time Zoe Brennan arrived in the emergency department on a wet Tuesday evening the diagnosis had effectively been made before anybody in the building had laid a hand on her. This is a constructed composite assembled for teaching, and no part of it describes a real patient, a real family or a real service; the details are invented so that a piece of reasoning can be examined. What follows is a young woman whose episodes were described accurately enough for a specialist to change his mind, and whose description was written down by a nurse who was told, politely, that she was writing the wrong thing in the wrong place.

Zoe Brennan is 22, a second-year student, brought in by her flatmate after what the flatmate called her third collapse in a month. She is on her feet, embarrassed, and more angry about the visit than frightened by it, because the previous two visits ended with a leaflet and a letter to her general practitioner and no change in anything. She tells the registrar that she has never lost consciousness, though she cannot recall much of what happened, and she asks, before anything else is done to her, whether this is going to be on her record forever. The nurse who takes the history is a senior sister called Nuala Mackay, and she begins by asking what the episodes look like rather than what they are called, because the two are not the same question.

The family account, given by the flatmate, is that Zoe goes stiff, shakes, and then comes round after a minute or so with no memory of any of it. On that description epilepsy is close to unavoidable, and the registrar writes it down as the provisional diagnosis before he has finished the history. What the flatmate has not said, because nobody asked in a way that would draw it out, is a great deal about what actually happened. Her eyes stayed closed for the whole event. The shaking was not the same movement repeating; it changed shape, sometimes faster and sometimes slower, sometimes pausing in one arm while the other carried on. She did not bite her tongue, she did not wet herself, and she was wide awake immediately afterwards, asking what had happened and how long she had been gone.

Zoe herself can describe something of the inside of the events. She was aware throughout, she says, of a sensation of going somewhere else, of seeing the room without being part of it, and of being unable to move for a short time. She has never been incontinent, has never bruised her tongue, and has never woken confused or drowsy or with a headache afterwards. The three events so far have happened in the waiting room of a library, in a queue at a shop, and once outside a lecture theatre, and never in her own flat, where somebody is always present. The nurse writes each of these as a sentence, in words, in the space on the chart reserved for describing an event, and then asks the flatmate to say the whole thing again from the beginning.

The dangerous alternative had to be named before anything else was decided, because it is the one that changes everything. A genuine seizure can be followed by a period of continuous activity without recovery, and that condition, called status epilepticus, can injure the brain and kill; the finding that would reveal it is a convulsion that does not settle, with no return of consciousness between episodes. The other dangerous possibility is a structural cause behind the events, a scar or a tumour, which would be revealed by an onset beginning in one limb, by an abnormality on an electroencephalogram that localizes, or by something visible on a scan. Neither is what the family describes, but neither can be excluded on the word seizure alone, and the length of an event is part of the distinction, so the cut-off your service works to should be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

On the ward her observations are unremarkable in the way that matters. Her temperature is 37.2 degrees Celsius, her pulse is 88 beats per minute, her blood pressure is 118 over 76 millimeters of mercury, and her oxygen saturation is 99 per cent in air; these figures illustrate a patient who is well between events, and the limits your service uses for each of them should be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol rather than taken from this account. The neurological observations recorded at the bedside are of a young woman who is oriented, speaks in full sentences, moves all four limbs equally, and has no focal deficit. Nothing on the chart supports a brain that is currently injured, and nothing on it supports one that is not.

The case is discussed on the ward round the following morning, with the neurologist Dr Neil Corrigan, who has come to see the notes rather than the patient, and with the registrar who admitted her. They rank the possibilities first by how likely they are to be true and then by how dangerous they would be if missed. The list is long: epilepsy of generalized onset, focal seizures from a structural lesion, seizures secondary to a metabolic or toxic disturbance, fainting with abnormal movements, and a functional neurological disorder, a disturbance of the nervous system that is not caused by a structural or electrical abnormality and is not the same as pretending. The registrar is uncomfortable with that last entry, because the word seizure has been in the notes since the triage desk and it is difficult to take a word out of a chart.

The argument for epilepsy is a good one and deserves to be stated fairly. Three episodes of shaking with a period of unresponsiveness in a young adult is a classic pattern, and the team knows that a normal examination between events and a normal electroencephalogram do not exclude epilepsy, because the test only catches the abnormality while it is happening. The neurologist asks what else would account for a change in consciousness, and the answer, on the evidence available, is not much. There is no fever, no alcohol or drug history, no head injury, no family history of anything ever called a fit, and no abnormality of the heart to suggest fainting. The computed tomography scan and the magnetic resonance imaging scan arranged alongside the electroencephalogram are normal, and the blood tests show no metabolic cause, but none of those results was going to move the argument.

The working diagnosis is therefore epilepsy, most likely generalized tonic-clonic seizures, and the neurologist says it out loud and writes it down with the reason attached. He proposes the obvious next step, which is also correct on the evidence of that hour: an antiseizure medication, because an unprovoked seizure of this description carries a real risk of recurrence and of injury during a further event. Driving advice is given in the same breath, because the patient has to be told and because the law has something to say about it, and a neurology appointment is arranged with an electroencephalogram and a brain scan to follow. Nothing in the plan is wrong, and every step in it is defensible; the frame is wrong, and the frame is what the plan was built from.

The medication begins that afternoon, and the ward nurse, Nuala Mackay, stays with her through it and records what she sees. Zoe is warned that she will be drowsy at first and that she will need to keep taking the medicine, and she asks how long, and the honest answer, that it will be reviewed, does not reassure her. She asks again whether the word epilepsy will appear on her discharge letter, and she is told that it will, and she says, in a flat voice, that this is the part she minds most. The neurological observations continue, and a seizure chart is started, with columns for the date, the time, the duration, the description and the witness, because somebody has decided the shape of an event matters as much as its name.

What the nurse does next looks like nothing, and it is the thing the whole case turns on. She sits down with the flatmate and asks her to describe the last episode from the first second, and she asks Zoe separately, without letting either hear the other's answer, because two people who have talked an event through together will begin to tell the same story. She asks what the eyes did, what the mouth did, whether the tongue was injured, whether there was any loss of bladder or bowel control, and how long it was before Zoe was herself again. She writes what she is told in words: the eyes remained closed throughout, the movements varied in shape and rate, there was no tongue injury, no incontinence and no confusion afterwards, and the patient reports awareness throughout.

At the first clinic review, the news is bad in two directions at once. The events have continued, two of them since the medicine was started, and one happened in the supermarket queue and was witnessed by a stranger who called an ambulance; how often events like this are treated as epilepsy, and after how many, is a matter of local practice, so the threshold your service applies should be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol. The medication is making her sleepy in the mornings, dull in the evenings and low in mood, and she has stopped going to the lectures she used to attend because getting through a day feels like swimming. Her flatmate says she has become someone who checks her pockets for the medicine she is sure she has taken. The neurologist changes nothing on that visit, because the events on the medicine are as frequent as the events before it.

The failure is not dramatic; it is the failure that goes on. The neurologist is troubled and slightly defensive, because the diagnosis was reasonable and the medicine is not working, and there is a version of the next few months in which he increases the dose, adds a second agent, requests a video electroencephalogram, and eventually works through the list of epilepsies resistant to treatment. He thinks about it in the corridor and thinks about it again the next morning. The registrar wants to add a psychological assessment, and the flatmate wants to know whether this is something she has brought on herself, and each of these is a reasonable thing to want. What none of them has done is go back to the beginning and ask what the events looked like, because every question the team now wants to ask is a question about the disease, and not one of them is a question about the description.

Nuala Mackay does not take the case to the ward round. She takes it back to the flatmate and asks for the account of the supermarket episode to be played back in the order it happened, in the flatmate's own words, without using the word seizure at all. She writes down what she is told, and it takes four lines, with no neurological disease in it. The eyes were closed the whole time and did not open until a hand on her shoulder made them open. The shaking started hard in the right arm and then went to the left, then stopped and started again in a different pattern. Nothing was bitten and nothing was spilt, and Zoe said at the time that she could see the shelf and could not reach her own arm. And then it was over, in under a minute, and Zoe was asking what she had been doing.

The last piece arrives by accident, and the nurse was there when it happened. The third event had been in the shop two weeks before the clinic, and the flatmate had described it more than once without ever mentioning how it ended. On this visit the flatmate says, almost as an afterthought, that the thing that actually ended it was her taking Zoe's phone out of her hand and asking her to read the message that had come up on it. The shaking stopped within seconds, the way a person stops shaking when they have been given something to do. Zoe confirms it and looks, for the first time, uncomfortable in a way that is not embarrassment. A seizure does not stop because somebody has handed you a telephone.

The neurologist reads Nuala Mackay's notes on the ward, and then reads them again, and what happens in his face is not a conversion but a rearrangement. He says that he has been treating a description he did not take, and that the description does not support epilepsy: the eyes stay closed and never open, the movements are not rhythmic and change shape, there is no tongue injury, no incontinence and no confusion afterwards, and the patient is aware and can describe the event while it is happening. He notes the distribution too, because the absence of any event at home, in a young woman who is never alone there, and the presence of events in waiting rooms and in shops, is itself a pattern. He asks for the electroencephalogram to be repeated with an event captured on video, and then changes his mind, because a request that assumes an event will not produce the evidence he needs.

Testing follows, and it is reassuring in the way he wanted it to be: the electroencephalogram is normal, the CT and the MRI are normal, and the blood tests show no metabolic cause. The dangerous alternatives are now properly excluded rather than informally assumed away. There is no status epilepticus to fear, because every event has ended spontaneously and none has been followed by the long confusion a genuine convulsion leaves behind. And there is no structural cause, because the imaging is normal and the events do not begin in one place and spread. He is willing to say that the events are not epileptic, while keeping the door open, because a functional neurological disorder can exist alongside genuine epilepsy, and a future event with the features of a true seizure will be treated as one.

The change of approach begins with the medicine, and it is done carefully rather than quickly, because stopping it abruptly carries its own risk and because the confidence with which it is done will matter more than the speed. The antiseizure medication is withdrawn under supervision, with the neurology team involved and with the nurse explaining what is happening and why as it happens. The explanation to Zoe is not that she was not having seizures, which would be a denial of what she has lived through, but that the events do not have the features of epilepsy, that the medicine was treating a description that did not fit, and that she has a real and treatable disturbance of the nervous system. The drowsiness and the low mood are what the medicine was doing to her, and they will lift as it leaves.

The family is brought into the same conversation, because how the family responds is part of the treatment and not a matter of good manners. The mother has spent a fortnight searching, arrives with a folder of possible causes, and is asked to close it once the neurologist has read it. She is asked to stop describing the events as fits, because the word has been doing the work of a diagnosis all along, and she is asked instead to write down what she sees: the eyes, the mouth, the movements, the breathing, how long it took, and how Zoe was afterwards. If an event does not stop, if Zoe injures herself, if she does not come back to herself, if she cannot breathe, or if the pattern changes, then the family calls for help at once, because rushing to an emergency department with an event that resolves is one of the ways this pattern is kept alive.

The rehabilitation is the part of the story with the least dramatic moments and the most consequence. A physiotherapist works with Zoe on movement, on the return of ordinary physical confidence after months of watching herself for signs of danger, and on the physical consequences of the drowsiness and the low mood the medicine produced. Psychological therapy is offered and taken, and it is not a form of talking that asks her to stop the events by an effort of will, because that is not how it works and it is a cruel thing to suggest to somebody who has already been told, implicitly, that she is the problem. She is taught that the events have a pattern of their own, that distraction and engagement are part of it, and that the attention she cannot help paying to them tends to make them more likely.

And then the fits stop, and it is important to be precise about what that means, because this is the place where a case like this most easily goes wrong in the telling. What stopped was the count of events. The patient did not get better in the sense that anything was cured or explained away; for a while after the medicine was withdrawn she was tired, apprehensive, ashamed of the label attached to her, and slow to trust her own body again. She had spent months learning to watch herself for the onset of a fit, and she had reorganized her flat, her lectures and her friendships around that watching. The disappearance of the events was the removal of a drug and a change in what she and her family were looking for, and it was not by itself a recovery.

What the recovery actually looks like is boring and slow. Zoe returns to her lectures in the second term and finds, at first, that she cannot concentrate, and then that she can. The flatmate goes back to being a flatmate rather than a nurse, and the mother's folder goes into a drawer. Zoe takes up a sport she gave up the year before, and the first time she falls over she laughs, and the sound of that laugh is the moment her flatmate dates as the real recovery. The drowsiness lifts as the medicine leaves and the low mood lifts with it, and she says that the worst thing about the whole business was not the events at all but the tiredness and the flatness, which nobody had thought to ask about because everybody was too busy watching for the next fit.

The service changes what it does with events, and the change is a piece of writing rather than a piece of equipment. A structured description card is added to the seizure chart, and every event, whatever it is called by whoever reports it, is documented in words: what the eyes did, what the mouth did, whether the tongue was injured, whether there was incontinence, whether the movements kept the same pattern, how long it lasted, and what the person was like afterwards. The card exists because the word seizure was, in this case, doing the work of a description. No test can be ordered sensibly until somebody has said what the thing actually looked like, and the order of the questions in the chart decides which test gets ordered.

Some listeners will hear this story and take it to be a story about a young woman who was not ill, and it is important to say why that reading is wrong. A functional neurological disorder is not a manufactured symptom and it is not a deception; the symptoms are real to the person who has them, they are not produced by choice, and the person is usually not aware of what they are doing. Its cause lies in how the nervous system is running rather than in a structural injury, and it commonly appears in people who have been frightened, or under strain, or managing something difficult, often with none of that visible on the surface. It can coexist with genuine epilepsy, and it does; it is not an alternative to being told the truth.

The question a fair-minded listener will ask is how competent people got this wrong, and the answer is not that they were careless. The frame was set before the first doctor saw her. The family used the word seizure because they had watched the episodes and believed them to be seizures, and they were not being unreasonable. The triage nurse wrote seizure because that was what the referral said and what the family said, and the neurologist read a coherent story with a classic pattern and acted on it, which the evidence at that hour invited him to do. The error was not in any individual's reasoning; it was in the reasoning of a system that could not see a description, because it had been handed a word.

It is worth saying something about the nurse, because the temptation to treat her as an instrument rather than a colleague is exactly the mistake. For a while after Zoe's first visit Nuala Mackay believed she had misunderstood what she was looking at, and she carried the knowledge that she had written something different from what everybody else had written, without being able to explain why it mattered. She was not confident, because the medicine was on board and the neurologist was the neurologist and the family believed what they had been told. She did the thing she had been trained to do, which was to keep writing, and she kept writing the description of the events and not the label, and when the flatmate mentioned the telephone by the way, she took those four lines to the ward round and read them out.

When the plan changed, the handover was written carefully, because the failure had been a failure of communication as much as of medicine. The new summary states what the events are, what they are not, why the medicine stopped, and what would change the plan, and it says in plain words that a future event with the features of an epileptic seizure will be treated as one, immediately, without argument. The neurology team has kept her under follow-up, and the follow-up is not a formality. The nurse continues to see her in the clinic, and the description card goes with her, and the first question at every visit is whether there has been an event, and the second is what it looked like. By the following spring Zoe is in her third year and has had no events, and she accepts the arrangement without resentment, which surprises the team more than anything else.

The overlap between these two conditions is not a technicality but a practical warning. Zoe may yet have a genuine seizure; the door is open, and she knows that it is open. The nursing team has taught her, her flatmate and her mother, in writing, the features that would make an event a seizure rather than a functional episode: a gradual onset of stiffness, movements that keep the same pattern throughout, an injured tongue, an eye that opens during the event, confusion and drowsiness afterwards, and an event that does not stop when a person is distracted. A person who knows those things has a way of taking her own history that no clinic can offer her, and it is the most durable thing the service gave her.

To summarize, this case failed not because the diagnosis was unreasonable but because nobody described what they were looking at. The dangerous possibilities, a status epilepticus and a structural cause behind the events, were named early and excluded properly once the description was available. The antiseizure medication was started for good reasons, made the patient worse in ways mistaken for the disease, and was withdrawn under supervision once the events were recognized as something other than seizures. The decisive observation was written in words by a nurse who asked what the event looked like instead of what it was called, and it consisted of eyes that stayed shut, movements that changed shape, an event that ended when somebody gave the patient something to do, and no event at home. A functional neurological disorder is real, it can coexist with epilepsy, and the rehabilitation and the family's response are part of its treatment rather than a softer option.
