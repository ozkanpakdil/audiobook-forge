# Chapter 3 — The Asthma That Would Not Budge

The most ordinary sentence in respiratory medicine is that somebody's asthma is not controlled, and the most ordinary thing to do after hearing it is to prescribe more of the same. Rachel Okonkwo came to the respiratory clinic with that sentence already attached to her, and she had been carrying it since she was nine years old. She was twenty-seven, she was a nursery nurse, and by the time she sat down in the consultant's room she had already been told, more than once, that hers was a difficult asthma to control. What follows is a constructed composite rather than a record of any real person, no detail here identifies anyone living or dead, and the case has been assembled to make one particular piece of reasoning visible, because the reasoning is the point and the person is only the illustration.

She had come because of three things happening at once and none of them getting better. She was attending the clinic three or four times a week for breathlessness, she was reaching for her reliever inhaler four times in a day, and she was waking in the night with the sense that she could not get enough air. Three or four visits a week is the level at which most services would regard asthma as out of control, though the threshold that matters in your own clinic is the one written in the current guideline and the local protocol, and it has tightened considerably over the years. She had worked out her own theory, which was that this was simply what being an adult with asthma was like, and that the little ones at the nursery who wheezed through every playgroup had been lucky and would grow out of it. She had been managing on her own for most of her adult life, and she had never been given a plan she could find written down anywhere.

The referral letter was two lines long and it had been written the way referral letters are usually written, which is to say by someone who believed they already knew what the problem was. She was referred to the respiratory clinic as difficult asthma, and the box on the form for other conditions had been left empty, because there was nothing in the notes that made the general practitioner think of diabetes, of a hormone problem, of a heart that was not pumping properly, of a thyroid that had stopped doing its job, or of anything at all in the throat. The clinic that received her was a specialist asthma clinic, which meant that everyone in the building was expecting to see the same thing she had been told she had. A referral is a frame, and a frame that arrives before the patient does is already half an hour into the consultation.

Before any of that was discussed, the team had to hold two readings of the story at once, because one of them is comfortable and one of them is safe. The first is what the referral says, which is uncontrolled asthma, and uncontrolled asthma is one of the few respiratory diagnoses that can kill a young person, so it cannot be softened. The findings that would reveal that danger are a peak expiratory flow well below the patient's own best, attacks that do not settle, breathlessness that is present at rest, and waking at night with the conviction that this time something bad is happening. The second reading is the one nobody had written down, which is that the breathlessness may not be coming from the lungs at all but from the throat, and the findings that would reveal it are a high noise on the way the breath goes in as well as on the way out, a voice that is hoarse between attacks, and attacks that stop as suddenly as they began.

Dr Helen Marsh, the respiratory consultant who saw her, heard the story with the unfamiliar parts held slightly apart from the familiar ones. She was careful to say out loud what everyone in the room was privately thinking, which was that some people's breathing is worse at the level of the throat than it is in the chest, and that this condition is often mistaken for asthma and often exists alongside it. That was where the thought went, into a folder marked for later, and the folder was not opened again for six weeks. The working diagnosis on the day was poorly controlled asthma, and the reasoning behind it was audible and reasonable, because this is a woman with a lifelong diagnosis, a great many symptoms, and nothing unusual on examination today.

Nothing about her examination that day was dangerous, and every one of those findings was also what you would expect in a well woman between attacks. She was not febrile, her chest was clear to listen to, she was not distressed, and she was not cyanosed. Her pulse was regular and her saturation in the room was 96 per cent, and the number at which a saturation of this kind should begin to worry you is a matter for the current guideline and the local protocol in your own service rather than for anybody's recollection. The consultant wrote in the notes that the examination was unremarkable, which was true, and then wrote that the problem was poor control, which was an interpretation, and the notes did not distinguish between the two because notes rarely do.

What the file showed after eighteen years of asthma was a folder of letters and a box of inhalers, and it contained no measurements at all. Rachel had never had her breathing measured by a device in her life; she had been diagnosed as a child in a way that nobody now could reconstruct, and every decision about her treatment since had been made by asking how she had been and then believing the answer. She had never had a chest radiograph as an adult, her airways had never been tested to see whether they narrow when they are made to narrow, and nobody had ever asked her what her best reading on a peak flow meter had been. Her record consisted of the diagnosis, the inhalers, the number of visits, and the word asthma, repeated with great consistency and for a very long time, and the absence of anything else had become as hard to see as the diagnosis itself.

Two more things were said in that first consultation and neither of them was pursued. Rachel's voice was hoarse, and the consultant attributed it to a cold she had had the week before, and the nurse wrote in the notes that the voice sounded hoarse. And Rachel said, almost as an aside while she was putting her coat on, that she had stopped going to the choir she had sung in for six years because she could not hold a note any more, and that singing was one of the things that set her off, along with talking and coughing. What she could do was walk to the shop and carry the shopping home without trouble, and it was talking and singing that took her breath away. It was all in the record from the first hour, and it was in the record because a nurse asked her how the attacks started and how they ended.

The third thing she gave was the one that should have stopped the clock. She said that the attacks came on suddenly and went just as suddenly, often before she had found her inhaler, and that a great many of them never needed anything at all. She said, with the particular embarrassment of a young woman who has told nobody, that during an attack she has an overwhelming urge to pass urine. Neither of those is a feature of asthma, because asthma does not switch on and off like that and does not cause an urgent need to pass urine. The team noted them, and the notes read, in the flat way that notes read, that the patient describes a variable pattern with urinary urgency, and then the team went on to the treatment.

What else could produce breathlessness, a noise on breathing out, and waking at night in a young woman with a long-standing label of asthma? The list was built and ranked twice, once by how likely each thing was and once by how much damage it would do if it were true and missed. The likely candidates were the asthma itself, badly controlled; a breathing pattern disorder of the kind that comes with panic, which she did not have the features of; and the narrowing of the upper airway that everyone had mentioned and then set aside. The dangerous candidates were the ones that are ruinous when present, among them a swallowed object sitting in a bronchus, a collapsed lung, a heart that was failing without saying so, and an infection in the lung arriving quietly for weeks. The asthma was the most likely by a long way and the laryngeal problem was the one everybody could say the name of and no one could explain.

The treatment that started that day was entirely correct for the diagnosis they were working with, and it was started for reasons that were sensible. The inhaled corticosteroid was stepped up, a spacer was provided, a written technique check was put in place with a date beside every item on it, and she was asked to keep a peak flow diary, twice a day, before anything else. The reason for checking technique before adding more medicine was the honest one, that a corticosteroid which is being inhaled badly is not a treatment at all, and it is kinder and cheaper to discover that before adding anything further. She was asked to come back in six weeks, and how long to allow before a response can reasonably be judged is set out in the current guideline and the local protocol, which differ between services and between editions.

Carla Mendes was the respiratory nurse who would spend the next six weeks with this patient, and the nursing in this case is not background to the medicine, it is where the case is actually decided. Carla did the technique teaching at the bedside, which in this clinic means sitting down with the patient, watching a dose go into the spacer, and then watching a second one, because a person can perform perfectly for a clinician and badly alone when nobody is watching. She checked the spacer and the mouthpiece, the rinsing afterwards, and the order in which the two inhalers were taken. She explained what the numbers on the diary sheet meant and how to record them, and she asked the question she would go on asking again and again, which was how does an attack start and how does it end.

The answer the first time was the answer most people give. It comes on, it goes on, you use your inhaler, it settles. Carla wrote that down and moved on, because there was no reason to press on it. She also checked at each contact whether the treatment was actually being taken, and she did it without suspicion, by asking what a day looked like, and what came back was a nursery nurse with two small children, an awkward shift pattern, and a diary filled in every single day without a gap. The inhaler in Rachel's bag was empty more often than any treatment schedule could account for, and Carla found that encouraging rather than worrying, because an empty canister is the most visible proof of adherence there is.

Six weeks of perfect technique and perfect adherence changed nothing, and that sentence is the one the whole case turns on, so it is worth stating plainly rather than telling this as a story of gradual deterioration. The diary was complete. The technique was checked and re-checked and checked again, and it was good every single time. The frequency of the visits, the waking in the night and the use of the reliever did not improve, and the reliever was being used as often in the sixth week as in the first. Carla was left holding an empty inhaler box and a patient who had done everything that had been asked of her, and there is no comfortable place to put that.

The failure was felt by everyone, and it was felt in a particular way, because nothing had gone wrong in the doing of it. The consultant's first feeling was that the treatment had not had long enough, and the second was that the next step was a long-acting bronchodilator and then a tablet that worked in a different way, and the third, which she was honest enough to say aloud, was that some people simply had difficult asthma and were difficult. The nurse's first feeling was that the diary must be wrong, because the story and the numbers did not sit together, and her second was the one she disliked most, which was that a young woman who filled in a peak flow diary with this much care might be filling it in with what she hoped was true. The third feeling, which arrived later and which nobody acted on, was that the treatment might be working perfectly and the disease might not be what they thought.

There was one set of numbers in the folder that did not fit, and the fit was visible on the face of the diary. Her best reading had been recorded once, on the first day she used the meter, and the readings over the following six weeks sat between 380 and 420 liters per minute, and they moved very little from day to day, including on the days when she had written that she had had an attack. Which numbers count as low, and how much day-to-day variation is expected in a patient whose asthma is genuinely uncontrolled, is written in the current guideline and the local protocol rather than in anybody's memory, and the personal best remains the more useful guide in most services. What the diary showed was a chest that was not behaving the way an uncontrolled asthmatic chest behaves, and nobody asked what it was behaving like instead.

What turned the case was not in the diary and not in the numbers, it was a sound. Carla had heard it many times without naming it, on the telephone when Rachel rang to say she was having an attack, in the corridor outside the clinic, and in the room itself. She had written it in the notes every time, in the words she had been given, as wheeze, because wheeze is what this patient had. On the telephone she heard it more clearly than anyone else could, because a microphone held against a mouth filters nothing at all. What she heard was a high squeak when the breath came in as well as when it went out, and she had been hearing the outgoing half of it for eighteen years and had never once questioned the incoming half.

At the six-week review Carla said it out loud, which is the hardest thing in medicine and the most useful. She said that the noise was arriving on the way in and not only on the way out, and that a sound which arrives with the breath going in is not made in the lungs. The consultant stopped. Then Carla asked the question she had been asking for six weeks, about how the attacks start and how they end, but this time she asked for the sound rather than for the story, and Rachel, who had given the same answer before, sat up and said that the noise came on at the start of an attack and stopped completely when it was over, and that her chest was clear inside a minute or two. A noise that begins and ends with the episode, and is not made in the lungs, is made at the larynx, and the larynx does not tighten in asthma.

So the clues were these, and every one of them was in the file before the review. The hoarse voice between attacks had been written down and explained away as a cold. The inability to sing had been offered as a passing complaint and never followed. The attacks that settled in minutes without any inhaler had been read as a fast response to treatment, which was the opposite of what they were. The urge to pass urine during an attack had been recorded as an odd symptom and never asked about again. The noise on the way in had been heard by a nurse on the telephone and written down as wheeze. And there had never been an objective measurement of this patient's asthma in eighteen years, and that absence had been read as reassurance rather than as a gap.

The reason all of that was missed is not that it was subtle, and it is not that any of it was invisible at the time. It is that a name, once it has been attached to a person for eighteen years, stops being a description and becomes the explanation. From the first visit every observation was read through asthma, so the hoarse voice was a cold, the noise was a wheeze, the fast settling was a good response, and the missing measurements were there because she was stable. A diagnosis in that position is self-confirming, and the more years a label has been carried the harder it becomes to see past it, because each year adds a little more apparent evidence that it is right.

The nurse was not outside this, and it is worth saying so plainly. Carla had listened with a filter on, the filter being the expectation that this patient wheezed, and a filter applied to the ear works exactly as one applied to the eye works. She had also asked about the attacks in the language of the diagnosis, which is to say she had asked how often they were happening, and frequency is the wrong question when the point is what is making them happen. What she had not done was ask what the noise was, or take the patient's own account of her voice seriously, or notice that a woman who cannot sing but can carry her shopping has a problem that is not in her lungs. Any team with a nurse who asked that question would have found it, and the correction was available a long time before the review.

The next step was to stop reasoning from the notes and go and measure. The first test was breathing tests with a flow volume loop, which records the shape of the breath as it comes in and as it goes out rather than producing a single number, and the shape was not the shape of asthma, because the limb of the curve showing breathing in was flattened and the airway was narrowing on the way in. The second was a laryngoscopy, in which a small camera is passed to the back of the tongue so that the voice box can be watched while the patient is having an attack, and it showed the vocal cords closing when they should have stayed open, which is what makes the squeak and the sense of being strangled. The third was a challenge test of the airways, which asks whether they narrow when they are made to narrow, and in her case they did, and that is how the team established that the asthma was real.

She had two conditions and not one, and they had been sharing a symptom and a name for eighteen years. She had genuine asthma, with airflow that narrowed and then widened, and she needed to keep taking her inhaled corticosteroid for it. And she had inducible laryngeal obstruction, in which the voice box closes when it should stay open, and which had been counted as asthma attacks for eighteen years and treated with escalating amounts of a drug that could never have helped it. This is one of the commonest and most instructive patterns in chronic respiratory disease, which is two conditions, one complaint, one name, and a treatment that only ever reaches one of them.

The change in treatment had two halves and only one of them was a prescription. The inhaler treatment for the asthma continued unchanged, because that asthma was real and the team had measured it, and stepping it down would have been as wrong as stepping it up. The larger change was breathing and speech therapy, which for this condition is the treatment that makes the difference, and what it involves is teaching a person to recognise the early tightening, to breathe out rather than in, to use the voice differently, and to stop the episode becoming a fight. It works because the closing of the voice box is driven by the effort of breathing hard against a narrowing, and the skills change the shape of that effort. It is not a talking cure and it is not quick, and the improvement usually shows in weeks rather than days.

Carla's role changed as well, and this is the part of the story the clinic kept afterwards. She became the person who listened to the sound rather than the person who recorded it, and she taught Rachel to describe an attack in a fixed set of words, so that what happened could be compared with what happened last time. She kept checking the technique, because that was still worth doing. She watched the voice, which had been a minor line in the notes and became the most useful observation in the file, and she telephoned the consultant when it was worse rather than waiting for the appointment, because a hoarse voice on the day of a review is a finding. And she began to ask the question about how the attacks start and how they end as a routine for every patient with a breathing problem, so that the next one did not have to be unlucky.

Rachel improved, and the improvement was not subtle. The attacks came on less often and, more importantly, they stopped themselves, because she learned the early signs and used the breathing skills and the squeak resolved in a couple of minutes instead of lasting the rest of the evening. She went back to the choir, which after some years is a considerable thing to say about a case. She was not woken at night by the end of the second month of therapy. She still had asthma and still needed her inhaled corticosteroid, and she was not troubled by that, because she had been told what it was for and what it was not for.

There are three lessons here and they are related to one another. The first is that two conditions can share one symptom and one name, and that when they do the second one stays invisible for exactly as long as the first one is confident. The second is that the absence of a response to correct treatment is itself a finding, and not a failure of the treatment or of the patient, and it deserves the same seriousness as a result that comes back unexpected. The third is that the sound of breathing is worth listening to, that a nurse is often the only person in the building who hears it, and that a noise which arrives with the breath going in is not a noise the lungs make.

To summarize, Rachel Okonkwo's asthma was real and so was the disorder that had been counted as asthma for eighteen years, and the mistake was not a mistake of treatment but a mistake of naming. The nurse heard the decisive thing with her own ears, which was that the noise of an attack arrived on the way in as well as on the way out, and she heard it properly only once she stopped hearing it as asthma. Correct treatment that produces no response is itself a finding, and the correct answer to it is to reopen the question rather than to escalate the therapy. The management that changed her life was breathing and speech therapy alongside continued treatment of the asthma that was real, and she was able to sing again within a few months.
