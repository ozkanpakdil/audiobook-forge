# Chapter 12 — The Woman Who Would Not Sit Still

Dr Anwen Pryce has seen a great many women of forty-five in her room, and most of them arrive with a word they have brought from home or from the internet, and the word is usually one it is a relief to be wrong about. Ruth Kelleher came with two. She was forty-five, she administered a school office, and she sat down with a folded page on which she had written the things she wanted to be asked about, the first line being the word anxiety. She had six weeks of palpitations, a tremor in her hands, intolerance of heat, and a weight loss of seven kilograms that she had been explaining to herself as the effect of being busy. She said the word anxiety and Dr Pryce agreed with it, because the woman looked anxious and everything she described could be described by that word. The trouble with a word that fits everything is that it will also fit everything that comes later.

The case that follows is a composite assembled for teaching, and it does not describe anybody who has ever been a patient. No detail in it identifies anyone living or dead, and the case has been assembled to make one piece of reasoning visible. That piece is the way a diagnosis made in one place becomes a frame in another, and the way a frame protects itself from the evidence that would break it. The failure in this case was not a missed sign so much as a sign that was seen, written down, and explained away, and the person who put the case back together was the nurse who had been keeping the record.

The consultation lasted twenty minutes and most of it was useful. Ruth had palpitations that came in waves and lasted minutes at a time, usually in the evening, and they woke her from sleep. Her hands shook when she held a cup, and she had spilled two coffees that week. She was sleeping badly, she was irritable with her staff, she was sweating in a sweater in a cool room, and she could not tolerate the radiator in the office. She had lost seven kilograms in six weeks without trying, and she said she assumed it was stress. She had been buying the same brand of hand cream for three weeks because her hands were hot, and she mentioned this as a small strange thing rather than as a symptom. How much weight loss over how long should start a search for a cause is set by the current guideline and the local protocol rather than by the impression of a consultation.

Dr Pryce examined her and found the things you would expect to find in a woman who was frightened of her own heart. Her pulse was 104 beats per minute and regular, her blood pressure was 118 over 64 millimeters of mercury, and her temperature was 37.4 degrees Celsius, which is not a fever and is also not quite normal. Her hands were warm and dry, she had a fine tremor of the outstretched fingers, and her eyes were wide and staring, and she was sweating in a sweater in a cool room. The figures that decide whether any of that matters are the ones your own service uses, so check the current guideline and the local protocol rather than carrying somebody else's numbers around in your head.

Here is where the case turns, and it turns on one sentence in a consulting room. The findings in front of Dr Pryce had a second explanation, and the second explanation was an overactive thyroid, because weight loss with a fast pulse, tremor, heat intolerance, sweating and staring eyes belong to that diagnosis as much as they belong to anxiety. What would have settled it were a temperature that stayed up, a pulse that stayed fast at rest, and a neck that moved when she swallowed, and none of those had been looked for. Thyroid function blood tests had been done at the start of the year and had been normal, and a normal result is read as the end of a question as often as it is read as the beginning of one, and so the note said anxiety and the note said a review in six weeks.

Dr Pryce started an antidepressant and wrote to the emergency department when the palpitations did not settle. The referral letter was helpful in every particular except the one that mattered. It said a forty-five-year-old woman with six weeks of palpitations and tremor, it said anxiety, it said she had been started on an antidepressant four weeks ago, and it did not say weight loss of seven kilograms, because that had been explained to herself as stress and never written down where the hospital could see it. She had also said, as she was leaving, that her mother had had her thyroid removed years ago for a lump, and the note did not record that either.

Three weeks before she came to the emergency department she had a viral illness of the kind that does not deserve a name, and it knocked her flat for a week. She was tired, she had a sore throat and a cough, she had a temperature for two days, and she stayed in bed. She felt better afterwards in the sense that a person feels better after a bad week, but she never went back to what she had been: her palpitations did not stop, the heat intolerance did not stop, and she went on losing weight. She told the emergency department about the virus and it was written in the notes, because a viral illness is the sort of thing everybody mentions, and it was never joined up to the weight loss or to the pulse. Whether two days of a raised temperature counts as anything is the question the current guideline and the local protocol in your own service will answer, rather than the impression of a story.

On the evening she came in, she had been sitting in the car in the school car park for twenty minutes because she did not think she could make it from the gate to the door. She was the administrator of a secondary school and the mother of a boy of twelve, and she had come with her husband Martin, who had driven her and then sat in the corridor unable to answer a single question about the previous four weeks, because she had been the one talking. She told the triage nurse that she had been in a state for six weeks. She told her that she could not sit still. She told her that she was hot, and that she was not sure whether that was the menopause or something else.

Delia Farrow was the nurse who took her in, and the first thing she noticed, before she took a single observation, was that the woman was hot and damp and did not stop moving. The heat came off her in a way that made the triage room feel warm. Her hands and wrists were warm and red, she had a fine tremor of the outstretched fingers, her eyes were wide and staring, and the sweat stood on her upper lip. She said she had not been able to sit still in the car, and she said it as a complaint, and she said it again in the bay, and again to the nurse who brought her a blanket, and every time as though it were the explanation for everything else.

The triage observations were recorded and they did not agree with each other. Her pulse was 128 beats per minute and irregularly irregular, her blood pressure was 118 over 64 millimeters of mercury, her temperature was 38.1 degrees Celsius, her respiratory rate was 22 breaths a minute, and her oxygen saturation in air was 97 per cent. An irregular pulse at that rate, with a temperature above 38 degrees Celsius, is a different animal from the palpitations of an anxious woman. The limits that separate them are the ones written in the current guideline and the local protocol rather than in the memory of a triage nurse.

Toby Farrant was the registrar that evening, and he did what good registrars do, which is to read the referral letter before he reads the patient. The referral letter said anxiety. He read Ruth Kelleher through it, and everything she told him was filed under the word in the letter: the palpitations were anxiety, the tremor was anxiety, the sleeplessness was anxiety, the flushed face was anxiety, and the weight loss was anxiety, because the letter did not mention that she had lost any. A referral letter is a summary written in a hurry by somebody who met the patient once, and such a summary is often more certain than the patient standing in front of you.

He said his reasoning out loud, as registrars are taught to, and he ranked it. First, anxiety with an arrhythmia, because she had been anxious for six weeks, the antidepressant had not helped, she had a rapid irregular pulse, and she was now frightening herself. Second, an overactive thyroid, because the weight loss, the heat, the tremor and the irregular pulse all belonged to that, and the findings that would have confirmed it were a temperature that stayed up, a fast pulse that did not settle with rest, a neck that moved on swallowing, and staring eyes. Third, an infection, because she had had a virus three weeks ago and had been febrile since the day before she came in.

The second was set aside, and this is where the case turns, because the reason was not a clinical judgement. It was the letter. A diagnosis that arrives in writing from a colleague who has met the patient and has a good reason for their opinion is given more weight than the same diagnosis made at the bedside of a stranger, and the result was that a nurse's observation about the warmth of a hand carried less weight than a general practitioner's note written after twenty minutes. The team accepted the frame because the frame had been made by somebody they respected, and that is the most ordinary and least blameable way for a case to go wrong.

The treatment began, and by the lights of the ward it was correct. She was given a beta blocker, because a fast irregular pulse in a frightened woman is treated with a beta blocker, and because the rate is part of what is making her frightened, and it is a good drug for that. She was given a sedative for the nights, because she had not slept and the one she was already taking had not worked. An electrocardiogram, or ECG, was done and showed an irregular rhythm, and a cardiology referral was written for the outpatient clinic, because an arrhythmia in a woman with anxiety needs a rhythmologist and not another bottle.

The nursing that night was the nursing the ward does for a woman with palpitations, and it was done well. Observations were taken hourly and the cardiac monitor was applied, and the nurse documented what she saw rather than only what the monitor said. She wrote the temperature at every set of observations. She noticed that the woman was hot rather than cold, that her hands were warm and dry rather than cold and clammy, and she wrote that down as a sentence, because she had been taught to write down what she saw and not only what the numbers said. She noticed that the woman was talking continuously and that her sentences were getting shorter and shorter as the night went on.

The failure was not obvious, and it was the kind that looks like success from the foot of the bed. The beta blocker did what beta blockers do, and by midnight the rate had come down from 128 to 96 beats per minute and the monitor showed a slower, more organised-looking rhythm, and the registrar came to see her and was satisfied. The nurse was not, because the rate was the only thing that had improved, and her temperature had gone up rather than down. The sedative had made her sleepy and unsteady, and she was found on the floor of the bathroom at three in the morning, and nobody was sure why, and it was put down to the sedative and to a long and anxious day. The rates and pressures a team treats as adequate are the ones written in the current guideline and the local protocol, and a number that moves in the right direction is not the same as a patient who is improving.

The team did what teams do with a patient who is not improving but is not obviously worse either, which is to adjust the plan rather than to abandon it. The registrar considered changing the antibiotic that had been started for the fever, on the grounds that she had had a virus three weeks ago and might have a chest infection. He considered whether the sedative should be increased, and whether she should be moved to a bed where she could be watched more closely. None of those thoughts was unreasonable, and every one of them was a way of not asking a harder question, and the harder question was not about the pulse at all.

Delia Farrow is the reason this case has a different ending, and what she did was unglamorous and completely decisive. She had written the temperature at every set of observations since eight in the evening, and before the end of her shift she laid the figures out on a sheet of paper in the order they had been taken. The temperature had not fluctuated, had not settled with the beta blocker, and had risen in step with the rate all night, and the highest readings were at the times when she was hottest and least able to settle. She had done the same thing many times with fever charts on children, and it had never occurred to her that a chart of a woman's temperature would be the thing that reopened the diagnosis.

She went back to the bay at half past seven in the morning, at the end of a long shift, and did two things that nobody had done in the previous six weeks. The first was to ask Ruth Kelleher about her neck, and when she put her fingers on it she felt the gland sitting low and moving when the patient swallowed, and she asked her to swallow again, watched it move, and wrote it down. The second was to ask about the family history, and Ruth said, without being asked, that her mother had had her thyroid removed years ago for a lump, and then said that she had mentioned it to the doctor four weeks earlier and had been told it was nothing. She said she had thought it was nothing too.

Toby Farrant came in at eight and was told the two things, and he did not dismiss either of them, because he had held the diagnosis of anxiety for less than a day and had not yet begun to defend it. He put his own hand on her neck, and he wrote the words thyroid storm in the notes and then crossed them out, because it was not his to write yet. He rang the endocrine team and asked for the endocrinologist to come in that morning. He stopped the antidepressant and the sedative as well, because both had been given for a diagnosis that was now in doubt, and he told Ruth Kelleher that he was sorry she had been given them and that he wanted to go over everything again from the beginning.

The bloods that came back put the whole picture on a single page. The thyroid stimulating hormone was suppressed, the free thyroxine was far above the range the laboratory reports, and the potassium was low, which is what happens when a body is driven at that rate for that long. Her ECG showed the irregular rhythm of atrial fibrillation, and the story that had been built on anxiety had a name now that everybody in the room could say. The criteria that define a storm differ between sources and between patients, and the rates and thresholds that decide whether a heart is coping are written in the current guideline and the local protocol in your own service, so check them there rather than against a story.

Thyroid storm, which is the name for an overactive thyroid running away with the body, precipitated by the viral illness three weeks earlier and unmasked by everything that had been done for the anxiety. The antidepressant had not caused it, but it had treated a symptom of it and had kept her inside a diagnosis. The sedative had made her slow and vague, so that the confusion of a storm was read as the effect of a sleeping tablet. The beta blocker had brought the rate down, so that the storm looked as though it was settling, when what it did was slow the heart while the temperature kept climbing, and it is the temperature and the confusion and the heart together that tell you which of those two things is happening.

She was moved to the intensive care unit, or ICU, that afternoon, and the care was taken over by the endocrinologist, who set the plan. The treatment for the storm was given: drugs to block the gland and stop it making more hormone, and the beta blocker continued because the rate is part of what is killing her, and corticosteroids because the body has run out of its own way of coping. Supportive care with fluids and cooling went with it. The precipitant was looked for as well, because a storm that is not given its cause will come back, and the chest infection that followed the virus was treated with antibiotics.

Farrow went with her, and the nursing in the intensive care unit was the nursing a person in a storm needs, which is continuous observation of the heart, the temperature and the level of consciousness, and a chart that everybody reads. The nurse brought the sheet of paper with the temperature figures written on it, and it was taped to the end of the bed, and it stayed there for three days. She also did the thing that patients in this state need most, which is to be talked to, and she told Ruth Kelleher what was happening and why. She asked her about her mother and her mother's operation, and the answer was a lump and a thyroid and a scar, and the answer was in the room with them.

The recovery took days, and the first sign of it was not the heart rate but the temperature, which came down over the afternoon of the second day and stayed down, and the confusion lifted with it. By the fourth day she was sitting up, and she remembered the three in the morning on the bathroom floor, and she was mortified, and the nurse told her that the medicine had done that, and not her illness and not her character. She was moved back to the ward, the beta blocker was continued and reviewed, the sedative and the antidepressant were not restarted, and the discharge letter went to her general practice with the thyroid results in it and the medicine list changed, because the letter is how one part of a system tells another what it has learned. How long a recovery of this kind should take is set out in the current guideline and the local protocol rather than in the length of this account.

Once the storm was over, the underlying condition was dealt with properly rather than as an afterthought, because the storm is the emergency and the thyroid is the illness. The team discussed what would stop the gland making hormone for good, and whether that meant an operation or a course of treatment that blocks the gland, and she was referred on for that decision once she was well. Her heart was re-examined and the rhythm was watched, because a heart that has been driven at that speed for days is not automatically a heart that has recovered. She was told plainly what had happened to it, which is that the rhythm had gone out of her and would need watching, and that many people recover it completely.

The general practice changed its own practice as well, and the change was small and specific. A woman under fifty with palpitations, tremor and unexplained weight loss is to have thyroid function tested before she is given a drug for anxiety, and a result that is normal once is not a result that is normal for ever, and if the symptoms continue then the test is done again. The practice also began recording the family history that patients mention in passing, on the understanding that a mother with a thyroid operation is not small talk. None of it would have saved Ruth Kelleher on the night she came in, though all of it would have caught her three weeks before she arrived.

Before she went home, and again in the clinic, Farrow taught Ruth Kelleher the things that would have brought her back sooner, and she taught them in the language of a woman who administers a school office rather than the language of a ward. She told her that a heart beating at this rate, a body that is hot, and a mind that is confused are three separate events that belong together. She had been given one name for all three, and the name was the problem. She told her to keep a diary of her pulse and her temperature for a month, and Ruth Kelleher, who is an organised woman, kept it, and the diary is why the next episode was caught in a day rather than a week.

The teaching of this case is about a word and about what a word does. Anxiety is a real diagnosis and a useful one, and it explains a fast pulse, a tremor, sleeplessness, a flush and a frightened woman, and it is the right diagnosis very often. It does not explain a temperature that climbs through the night while the rate is being treated, and a single symptom that does not fit is not a detail to be tidied away; it is the point at which the diagnosis has to be reopened. And the second half of the teaching is about the menopause, because hot flushes at forty-five are not a diagnosis, they are a description, and a description cannot be allowed to stand in the place of an examination.

There was no careless person in this story and there was no unkind one. Dr Pryce saw an anxious woman whose thyroid tests were normal and wrote an honest letter about her. The registrar believed a letter from a colleague and treated a real arrhythmia with a real drug. The nurse wrote down what she saw and was not believed for a few hours, which is what happens to the person who says something the team has not been thinking. The failure was in the sequence of the words, in a referral that arrived with a diagnosis already in it, and in a system where one word was used for a feeling, a mechanism and a disease at the same time, and any team could have caught it.

What Farrow takes from it is not that nurses should overrule doctors, because that is not what happened and it would not be safe. She has thought about the moment she looked at her sheet of paper and thought that she might be wrong, and about how long that thought took. The quietness of that doubt is the thing that nearly cost this patient another three days. She takes the smaller lesson that a temperature chart is not a formality, and that the woman on the monitor was not the woman who had come in, and that anybody who watches a person for a whole night can see that.

To summarize, Ruth Kelleher was treated for anxiety with an arrhythmia when what she had was a thyroid storm, and the anxiety was a reasonable diagnosis made competently and then allowed to explain a temperature, a pulse and a confusion that it could not explain. The evidence was in front of the team from the first hour: a woman who was hot rather than cold, with warm dry hands, a fast irregular pulse, a fine tremor, staring eyes, and seven kilograms of weight that nobody had written down. Every number in this account is an illustration of reasoning and must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol before it is used to act. The teaching is that anxiety does not explain a fever with a rapid irregular pulse, and that a woman of forty-five with palpitations and tremor is not a woman having hot flushes.
