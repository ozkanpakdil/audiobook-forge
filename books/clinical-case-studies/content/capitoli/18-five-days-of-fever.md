# Chapter 18 — Five Days of Fever

Three in the morning is the hour when a children's ward is at its most honest. The lights are off and the doors are open and the temperature charts hang from the end of the beds like small flags, and Mei Lin Chow is awake and crying for the fifth night running. She is four years old and she has been admitted for two of those five nights, and the line of ink down the edge of her chart has the same shape on every one of them. Sister Bethan Llewellyn is the nurse on the night shift, and she is the person who has been putting the thermometer into the axilla every four hours and writing down the answer.

The case below is a composite, built for teaching, and it does not describe a real patient or identify anyone living or dead. It is here because the failure in it is a failure of the ordinary examination, which is the kind paediatric teams know well and rarely admit to. Nothing exotic goes wrong in this story. A child has a fever, and the fever does not break, and a rash is called viral, and the eyes are called a cold, and five findings that belong together are written on five different charts by five different people and never read in the same breath. The illness is not rare, the treatment is not complicated, and the window in which it does the most harm is measured in days rather than in years.

Mei Lin Chow came to the emergency department on the second day of her illness with a temperature and a rash and a mother who was frightened. Her mother has said from the first morning that the red eyes are from a cold. The rash began on the third day, on the trunk and then the arms, and the notes describe it as maculopapular and blanching, and it has faded and come back several times since. Her temperature was 39.4 degrees Celsius on admission, her pulse was 132 beats per minute, and she weighed 15.2 kilograms. Those figures are the working context for everything that follows, and the values your own service treats as concerning in a child of this age and weight come from the current guideline and the local protocol rather than from this account.

She has not had the combined vaccine against measles, mumps and rubella, and the reason is ordinary and common. The family moved from one part of the country to another a year ago, the records did not transfer, the community clinic had a backlog, and the appointment was made and then the mother was working and the appointment was made again and then not. Two of the children in her street have the same story. There has also been a cluster of measles in the district, which is why this ward has an isolation room, and why the infection control team has been asking about the child's immunisation status since she arrived. None of this is unusual, and the mother has apologised twice for being a difficult patient.

The team on the ward is small. Dr Felix Underwood is the paediatric registrar, Dr Susan Hargreaves is the consultant paediatrician, and Sister Llewellyn is the nurse who has been taking the temperature and doing the four-hourly examination of a child who has been crying for five days. Two possibilities have to be named at the outset, at the bedside in the first hour. The first is meningococcal infection, and the findings that would reveal it are a rash that does not blanch under pressure, purple spots, a child who becomes floppy and cold, and a breathing rate out of proportion to the fever. The second is Kawasaki disease, and the findings that would reveal it are a fever of five days or more with red eyes without discharge, red cracked lips, a red tongue with a bumpy surface, swollen hands and feet with red palms, a large node in the neck, and a rash that fades and returns. The number of days of fever that should prompt this thinking is set in the current guideline and the local protocol rather than in this account.

The working diagnosis is a viral illness with a viral rash, and measles is what the registrar says aloud, because the child is unvaccinated, the rash fits, the eyes fit, and there is a cluster in the district. The absence of a cough and a runny nose is noticed and set aside, because measles without them is said to be less common. Kawasaki disease is named and set aside, and the reasons are given out loud. The rash is called viral, and a viral rash does not do that. The eyes have a discharge, because the mother says the eyes are weeping, and conjunctivitis with a discharge is not the eyes of this illness. The hands and feet are not swollen, because nobody has looked at them properly, and because a four year old in a hospital bed does not describe the swelling of her own fingers.

What is started is what is started for a febrile child with a rash in this situation. Fluids, an antipyretic for the discomfort, and antibiotics in case there is a bacterial superinfection on top of the viral illness. Measles is infectious for several days before the rash appears and for a few days after it, so the single room, the gowns, the advice to the parents, and the letter to the community clinic about the other children in the street are all part of the plan on the first day. The plan is written down, and at the bottom of it is a review in the morning with the temperature chart in front of them.

The nursing is the part of this story that everything else turns on. The temperature chart is kept properly, with the time of every reading and the method used. The fluid balance chart records what she drinks and what comes back out, because a child who stops drinking is worse before she looks it. The four-hourly examination is done at every set of observations: the mouth, the eyes, the lips, the hands, the feet, the neck and the rash, in that order, every time. The interval at which that examination is repeated, and the observations that go with it, are set out in the current guideline and the local protocol rather than in this account. And every time, the answers are written down in words, because a chart of ticks cannot be read back as a pattern.

What Sister Llewellyn wrote across five days is a set of findings that nobody has put together. The eyes are injected on both sides, with no discharge and no crusting at the edges. The tongue is red, and the surface of it is bumpy, like the surface of a strawberry. The lips are cracked and red and dry. The hands and the feet are swollen, and the skin of the palms and of the soles is red. There is a large node in the side of the neck, bigger than a child's own head is wide. The rash fades and returns, and at one point it is gone altogether for most of a day and comes back in the evening. And the nurse has asked about the cough and the runny nose twice, because the mother has blamed a cold for a week.

On the sixth day the child is worse rather than better, and the reason she is worse is not obvious from the numbers. She is irritable in the morning and floppy in the afternoon, and that order is the wrong way round for a child with a viral illness. The rash is more intense and more red than it has been, and the skin of the hands looks tight and shiny. The fever has not broken once in six days, and the chart shows it, the same peaks and the same troughs and not one reading back to normal, and the interval after which a fever of this kind should send a team back to the diagnosis is set in the current guideline and the local protocol.

The registrar is where the thinking goes wrong, and it is worth saying how, because he is not being stupid and he is not being careless. He has a febrile child with a rash and a probable measles and a child who is not drinking, and the pressure on him is to keep looking for a complication of the measles rather than to start again. The tempting moves are all reasonable. Add something to the fluid plan and ask the infectious diseases team to review. Send the laboratory tests again and add a blood test for a bacterium. Consider that the antibiotics have not worked, which is odd, and think about a reaction to them. Consider a complication such as pneumonia or an encephalitis, and send a chest radiograph and arrange a lumbar puncture.

Two of those thoughts are checked and two of them are not. The drug reaction is checked, because the rash began on the third day and the antibiotic was started on the fourth, and the sequence is written in the chart. The other checks do not happen, and the reason is a sentence in the notes on the first evening that everybody has read since: viral exanthem, probable measles. Once that sentence is in the chart it becomes the frame, and the swollen hands are a rash, and the red lips are a viral rash, and the eyes are a cold, and the neck is a normal node in a thin child, and the fever is the fever of the measles. Everything that does not fit is folded into it, and nothing that fits is looked for.

The phrase itself deserves a moment, because it is worth knowing how sentences like that get into charts. It was written by the registrar at ten in the evening, on a busy ward, in the shorthand the emergency department had used that day, and it was written to be helpful, because it told the next person that this looked like the other two children seen that week. It was never a formal diagnosis and was never meant to be one, and no one would have defended it as one. And yet it was the sentence the night shift read, and the sentence the ward round quoted, and the sentence the discharge summary would have carried out of the hospital if the child had gone home that night.

The observation that opens the case is made by the nurse, and it is made in the most ordinary way there is. At the handover on the sixth evening Sister Llewellyn says that she has been writing the same things in the same box on the same chart for five days, and that nobody has ever put them together, and that the fever has never come down once. She lists what she has written: the eyes with no discharge, the bumpy red tongue, the cracked lips, the swollen hands and feet with the red palms, the large node, and the rash that fades and comes back. She asks the registrar to look at the five charts together rather than one at a time.

The registrar looks, and the findings are there, because she has been writing them down for five days while everybody else was looking at the child. He checks the eyes with the light and finds the injection without discharge, and looks at the tongue and at the palms and the soles, which are red and swollen. Dr Hargreaves comes in, and the consultant repeats the examination herself in front of the nurse, and the diagnosis is named out loud on the ward round: Kawasaki disease, an inflammation of the medium-sized blood vessels of a young child, which in the untreated case damages the coronary arteries of the heart. The name is said quietly and then it is said again, because a team needs to hear itself say it before it can believe it.

What follows is the treatment under the paediatric team, and it is two things rather than one. The child is given intravenous immunoglobulin, and aspirin, and what is given and how much and for how long is written in the current guideline and the local protocol and not in this account. The reasoning is straightforward. The illness is an inflammation of the vessel wall rather than an infection that an antibiotic can reach, and the treatment that works is an antibody mixture that calms the immune response together with an anti-inflammatory drug that takes down the inflammation in the vessel. The echocardiogram is done to look at the coronary arteries, because the damage this illness does is to those arteries.

The fever is gone the next day. That single fact is both the treatment working and the diagnosis confirmed, and the team says so to the nurse first, because she was the one who asked the question. She says that she is glad, and that she is not finished, because the damage to the coronary arteries can develop for a week or more after the fever has settled. The consultant writes that into the plan in front of the family, and the mother writes it down in her own hand on the back of the discharge sheet, and the nurse underlines it. The interval that applies to that watch is set in the current guideline and the local protocol, and the family is told what it means in words they can repeat.

The follow-up is arranged in the specialist clinic, with echocardiograms repeated at intervals, and the nurse is at those appointments. She does what she did on the ward, which is to check the things that will go wrong first. The first of those is the fever, and then the rash, the eyes, the cracked lips, the hands and the feet, and any joint pain, because the joints of a young child can swell again after the fever has settled, and that recurrence is the point at which the coronary arteries are most likely to be involved. She also asks how the child is eating and how the family are coping, because the parents are watching every symptom now and need to be told which ones matter.

The mother needs more than the discharge letter, and the nurse gives it to her over several days. She is carrying the belief that she did this to her child by not giving her the vaccine, a belief other people have given her and that nobody on the ward thinks to take apart. The nurse explains what the illness is in plain words, that it happens to children who have been perfectly well, and that what the child needs now is ordinary care and a series of appointments. She also explains what she cannot promise, which is that everything will be fine, because that would be a promise she cannot keep, and because a family that has been promised certainty cannot forgive a recurrence.

What the ward changes afterwards is written into the local protocol, and it is a form and a habit rather than a new drug. A fever of five days or more in a child now triggers a structured examination with a printed list of the things to look for and where to look for them, and the list is short and is printed on the observation chart itself, so that it is in the nurse's hand rather than in a policy document. The number of days that should trigger that examination is set in the current guideline and the local protocol rather than in this account. The instruction to write findings in words rather than in ticks is repeated, and the ward round is instructed to read what the nurses have written before it reads what the laboratory has reported.

A review of the ward's febrile children over the previous year is done as well, and it finds several children whose fever lasted for days with a rash and a normal white cell count, and one child in particular, whose rash had been called viral and whose eyes had been called a cold by two different people who were both busy and both trying to help. Nothing was done wrong in any of those cases. The same sentence was written in the same way, and the sentence did the work, and the sentence was not challenged by anybody who had not been told that it needed challenging. The change in the protocol is aimed at the sentence, and not at the people.

Why was it missed? For the same reason as every other miss in this book, and this case is the clearest example in the book of a diagnosis that was named and then dismissed on the evidence rather than on the disease. Kawasaki disease was on the list from the first morning, and the reasons for putting it at the bottom of that list were given out loud and were reasonable-sounding in exactly the way that reasonable-sounding reasons are. A viral rash does not do that. The eyes have a discharge. The hands are not swollen. Each of those statements was true, and none of them had been checked, because the nurse who had checked them had written them in the notes and the notes had not been read. The frame was not a failure of attention but a failure of reading, and the reading was one person, at four in the morning, writing down what she saw.

The nurse in this story did not make the diagnosis and did not claim to. She did what nurses do when a child has been crying for five days and the charts say the same thing every time: she examined the child properly, in the same order, and wrote down what she found in words that could be read aloud. The system then read the chart as a set of separate observations belonging to a viral illness, because five findings spread across five days and five sheets do not look like a diagnosis to the person reading them one at a time. The registrar says afterwards that what he would take from it is not a list of features but a habit: write the whole set down, in one place, in one column, and read it as a set.

The consultant says something slightly different, which is that a child with a fever and a rash is not a child with a fever and a rash, and that the difference between the two is a systematic examination and the willingness to be told by it. There is one more detail that she goes back to, and it is about the child rather than the disease. On the third and fourth days she stopped complaining and the tiredness was read as improvement, and on the fifth day she was floppy and it was read as deterioration. Both readings were half right and both were wrong, because a child with this illness becomes quiet when the inflammation is high, and a quiet child is easy to mistake for a better child and then for a worse one.

The nurse had been recording the child's mood at every set of observations, and the record of it shows the change that nobody noticed, and it is the same lesson as the rest of this chapter in a smaller key. Three things went wrong here, and none of them was a mistake of technique. Nobody measured the temperature wrongly, nobody wrote down a finding that was not there, and nobody withheld anything. The findings were stored in a way that hid them, and a measurement stored apart from the finding it belongs to stops being evidence and becomes a series of facts about a sick child.

The mother is asked at the end what she thought was happening, and she says that she thought it was the measles and that she was frightened, because she had heard that measles can make a child blind and can kill. The father says that he had been the one telling everybody that the eyes were a cold. The nurse tells him that he was not, and that a parent who brings a child in and believes the wrong thing is doing exactly what the ward should want. Before the child goes home, the nurse speaks to both of them about what to say and what not to say, because the fear of the word is one of the reasons families stop bringing children back.

There is a general point at the end of this case, and it is the thing the book is for. A fever that has lasted five days in a child is not a fever to be waited out and not a fever to be explained away. It is a diagnosis that has to be looked for. The set for this illness is small. It is the eyes, the tongue, the lips, the hands, the feet, the neck and the rash, and a nurse who examines them every four hours in a child who has been crying for five days is doing the examination that makes the diagnosis. That is the ordinary heroism, and from the inside it does not look like heroism. It looks like a list on a chart and a nurse working down it.

The chart is the instrument and the written word is the instrument, and the institution that arranges for the words to be read together is doing something more valuable than any individual can do alone. The other children in her street who are unvaccinated are offered the same review that she has had, which is a consequence nobody planned and which turns out to be the best thing about the whole case. The parents are told what the echocardiogram showed and what it did not show, in words they can repeat, and they are told what the next appointment is for. And the diagnosis was never hidden from the team. It was distributed among them: the eyes in one chart, the tongue in another, the hands in a third, the fever in all of them, and nobody given the job of putting the five together at the same moment.

That job belongs to the ward round, and it belongs to the nurse who has been with the child for five days, and it can only be done if the notes are written in a form that can be read together. The change that would have made this case shorter is a list of seven findings on the front of the chart. The change that would have made it shorter still is somebody asking, at handover, what the nurses have been writing. Which is the whole of the lesson, and it is worth saying plainly that it costs nothing at all.

To summarize, this case went wrong because a diagnosis named on the first morning was dismissed on assumptions that nobody checked, and because five findings belonging to a single disease were recorded separately and read separately. The dangerous possibilities were named early, and a viral illness with a viral rash, and then measles, was a reasonable working diagnosis for an unvaccinated child with a fever, a rash and injected eyes. What it was not supported by was an examination nobody did properly: the eyes injected on both sides with no discharge and no crusting, a red tongue with a bumpy surface, cracked and red lips, swollen hands and feet with red palms and soles, a large node in the neck, a rash that faded and returned, and no cough and no runny nose. Those findings are illustrations of reasoning and not criteria to be applied from memory, and the days of fever and the observations that should trigger this thinking belong in the current guideline and the local protocol. The nurse examined a crying child in the same order every four hours, wrote down what she saw in words, and was the one who said at handover that nobody had put the five findings together. The truth was Kawasaki disease, and the treatment that changed everything was intravenous immunoglobulin and aspirin under the paediatric team, with echocardiography of the coronary arteries, and the fever was gone the next day, which was the diagnosis confirmed and the treatment working. The teaching is that a fever lasting five days in a child is a diagnosis that has to be looked for, and that the nurse who examines the hands, the eyes and the tongue of a febrile child is doing the examination that makes it.
