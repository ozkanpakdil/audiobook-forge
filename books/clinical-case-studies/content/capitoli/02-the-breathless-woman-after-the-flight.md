# Chapter 2 — The Breathless Woman After the Flight

Sophie Turner described her chest pain to the triage nurse as a knife, on the left side, and she said it caught her every time she breathed in. She said it exactly like that, without stopping to find better words, and the nurse wrote the sentence down as it was spoken rather than as it would appear in a textbook. Sophie Turner was 31, she taught primary school, and she had been unwell for nine hours. Her age and the number of hours between landing and collapse are illustrations of the reasoning and not thresholds to act on, so the numbers your own service uses should be read from the current guideline and the local protocol. The nurse was Joanne Mercer, new enough in that department to still do things the old way.

Nothing in this chapter describes a real patient. It is a constructed composite, assembled to make one particular failure visible, and it identifies no one living or dead. The failure it examines is a frame that formed in ten minutes and held for the two days it took to notice it, and it held because every test the team asked for came back reassuring while the patient got no better. The lesson of this case is that a clear chest radiograph is a result and not an answer, and that a result which does not fit the patient in front of you is a reason to ask a better question rather than a reason to stop asking.

The flight had been long, with a change and a long delay in the middle, and Sophie Turner had spent most of it folded into a seat with her knees up and her feet swollen by the end of the first hour. She had drunk a bottle of water and had not moved. She had also started her period on the aeroplane, and not long before she flew a check-up had given her a new combined oral contraceptive, and her mother had died of a clot in her late forties. None of these is a diagnosis, and no single one of them would have made anybody stop and think, but they are the details that a breathless patient is asked about, and they were asked about on that morning, because Joanne Mercer had been taught to ask about the legs.

Joanne Mercer took Sophie Turner's temperature, her pulse, her blood pressure and her oxygen saturation, and wrote the numbers where she could not misread them later. The saturation was 92 per cent on air, the pulse was 110 beats per minute and regular, the respiratory rate was 24 breaths per minute, and the temperature was 37.2 degrees Celsius. Every one of those numbers is an illustration of reasoning rather than a fact to act on, and the values your own service treats as abnormal should be read from the current guideline and the local protocol. The blood pressure was 120 over 75 millimeters of mercury, the chest was clear on both sides, and the heart rate was the only thing on the sheet that did not fit a person with a cold.

What Joanne Mercer did in the next few minutes is the reason this case ends well, and she did it because she had been taught to ask a breathless woman about her legs. She asked about the flight, how long Sophie Turner had been sitting still, whether she had been drinking, and whether she smoked. She asked about the contraceptive, when it had been changed, whether she had ever been told not to take it, whether she had ever had a clot in her leg or in her chest, and whether she had had any surgery. She asked about her family, and Sophie Turner told her that her mother had died of a clot in her late forties, suddenly, and that she had been warned about it at the time. Then Joanne Mercer looked at the leg.

The left calf was swollen, tight and tender halfway between the knee and the ankle, and the ankle on that side pitted to the touch as well. Joanne Mercer measured both calves at the same point with the same tape and found the left one to be two centimeters larger than the right, and wrote the measurement in the notes rather than writing that the leg was swollen. The difference between two calves is a measurement and not an impression, and the difference worth acting on varies with the patient and the setting, so confirm it against the current guideline and the local protocol. Sophie Turner said the swelling had come on during the flight, as though apologising for it, and she had crossed her legs for most of the journey and had not thought about the calf since.

The triage sheet in that service had the observations at the top and a space for a description underneath, and the shape of the sheet decided what happened next. Joanne Mercer wrote her description below the line, where a description belongs, because that is what she had been taught. She wrote the measurement of the left calf, that there was no cough, no sputum and no fever, that the mother had died of a clot, that the flight had been long, and that the contraceptive was new. Then she handed the sheet to the registrar, who read the top of it, asked a question about the chest, and moved on. Nothing about that was unusual, and nothing about it was a decision; it is simply that a description written in the bottom third of a page is a description nobody reads.

The registrar was Priya Raman, and she took the history again from Sophie Turner, and the history sounded like pneumonia. The patient had felt tired and achy for a couple of days before she flew, she had a sore throat on the aeroplane, and she had been coughing a little since. She had no fever and no rigors, and she had been sick twice in the night. The blood tests showed a white cell count at the upper end of the reference range, a finding that supports many diagnoses and settles none, and the range that matters should be read from the current guideline and the local protocol and from the laboratory's own interval.

The dangerous possibility was named at triage and it was named again on the ward round, because a clot in the lung has to be considered in every breathless patient and considered early. Priya Raman asked aloud what would have to be true for this to be a clot rather than an infection, and the consultant, Douglas Kerr, answered with a list. Breathlessness out of proportion to what the chest shows, a heart rate faster than the chest radiograph would explain, pain in the chest that is sharp, one-sided and worse on breathing in, and a swollen calf on the same side as the pain. Two of those were present, and one was present in a way that had not been noticed at all, and the consultant put the clot at the bottom of the list because the patient was young and well and had a clear chest radiograph.

Douglas Kerr's reasoning was not foolish and it was not careless. Pneumonia is common in adults and it is serious, and a breathless woman with pleuritic pain and a raised white cell count is a chest infection until she is shown otherwise. The chest radiograph had been reported as showing no consolidation, and the registrar had said that the patient was too unwell for that film to be true, and that a chest infection can hide behind a normal picture early on. The consultant accepted that. The consequence was that the clear film was read as reassuring rather than as a question, and the swollen calf went into the notes and stayed there.

So the working diagnosis was pneumonia, and the treatment followed from it: antibiotics, oxygen, a chest radiograph and observation, with the plan being to review her in the morning and to expect her to be no worse and no better at that point. The team ranked the alternatives in the order they would think of them: pneumonia first, a viral infection of the lung second, an anxiety reaction to travel third, and the clot at the bottom of the list despite being the one that could kill her quickly. The description at the bottom of the list was not that it was impossible. It was that it was unlikely, and that was the difference that mattered.

The nursing on that ward was done with care, and the care made the failure more visible rather than less. Joanne Mercer took the observations, kept the fluid and symptom chart, and did something that the ward had been doing for years without anybody having a name for it, which was to measure the oxygen saturation before and after the patient had walked. She explained it to Sophie Turner as a way of seeing how hard the lungs were working. Sophie Turner was not an unwell-looking young woman. She was frightened, she was apologising for taking up a bed, and she had a bag of marking to do that night.

The chart recorded that her saturation while sitting was in the low nineties and that it fell after a walk to the toilet and back, and that it came back up over the following minutes. The saturation at which a team should worry is set by the current guideline and the local protocol, and it moves with the patient, the position and the effort, so the number that matters should be taken from the same place rather than from this chart. The team looked at it and saw a young woman who was anxious, who had been lying down all afternoon, and who became breathless when she stood because a long flight had deconditioned her. The desaturation was filed as anxiety, and it was the clearest thing on the chart.

The first night was unremarkable in the way that a well-run ward makes things unremarkable. She slept, she was not sick again, the line came down because she was drinking, and she was able to keep the antibiotics down. In the morning she said she felt slightly better and no more than slightly better, and she asked whether she could go home to mark her books. She had no fever, her chest was still clear, and her saturation was better sitting up than the day before. The registrar wrote that this was what a chest infection on treatment looked like, and she was not being careless, only faithful to the diagnosis she held.

Two days in, she was no better. The antibiotics had been given without any evidence that they were needed, and there was still no fever, no cough, no sputum, and the chest radiograph was still clear. The white cell count had fallen towards normal while she remained breathless, which should have been the most informative result of the admission, because a count that falls as the patient does not improve was never measuring an infection. Her saturation fell again when she walked to the toilet, further than it had the day before. How long a patient should be observed before the diagnosis is revisited, and what should happen to the treatment then, are matters for the current guideline and the local protocol.

The team discussed this on the morning round, and the discussion was honest, which is how these cases usually get lost. The consultant said that a chest infection could still be the answer, that two days of treatment was not enough to be certain, and asked for the radiograph to be repeated and the antibiotics continued. The registrar wanted to send her home with oral antibiotics and a review, and wanted to know why the saturation dropped when her patient walked. What nobody said was the sentence that would have cracked the case: a patient no better after two days of treatment for a disease the chest film does not show does not have that disease. The length of a treatment before it is judged to have failed belongs to the current guideline and the local protocol, not to the mood of a ward.

Joanne Mercer went back to the triage sheet after the round, and she read it from the top, which she had not done since she handed it over. She read the observations, and then she read the description underneath, and she found her own words. The left calf, the measurement of it, the mother who had died of a clot, the new contraceptive. Then she went and measured the calf again, at the same point, with the same tape, and it was still larger than the right by the same amount, and she telephoned the registrar and asked her to come and look at a leg rather than at a chest.

Priya Raman came and looked at the leg, and then asked Sophie Turner to tell her again about the flight, her mother, the contraceptive, and whether she had ever had a leg that swelled. Sophie Turner remembered, after a moment, that she had had exactly that on a flight some years before, put it down to sitting badly, and mentioned it to nobody at all. The chest was clear, the radiograph was clear, the white cell count was falling, the saturation fell when she walked, and the pulse was fast with nothing in the chest to account for it. Priya Raman wrote all of it down at the bedside, and none of it was the sentence that would have cracked the case.

That is a constellation, and a constellation is a different kind of evidence from any single sign inside it. A structured score was used to organise the reasoning rather than to replace it, because a score counts features known to travel together and puts a number on the whole, so that the weight of the pattern is not lost in the noise of any one item. The threshold that would have triggered a scan is set by the current guideline and the local protocol, and a rule built for one population deserves care when it is used in another. What the score did was make it impossible for anyone to keep saying that the radiograph was reassuring, because the score did not care about the radiograph.

The argument in favour of waiting was not stupid, and it was made by good people. If she had pneumonia, treating her as though she had a clot would expose her to the risk of bleeding for nothing, and she was young, she had a clear radiograph, and a great many young women with breathlessness and pain on breathing in have neither of the two dangerous things. The argument against waiting was that the failure of treatment is itself information, and that a patient who is no better is saying that the diagnosis is wrong, which is a finding and not an embarrassment. The balance was not a close one once the calf had been measured twice.

So the treatment changed that morning, and it changed on the strength of the history and the examination rather than on the strength of the radiograph. Anticoagulation was begun, because the risk of a clot was high enough to treat while the investigation was arranged, and because the harm of waiting had begun to look larger than the harm of treating. The antibiotics were stopped. The left leg was measured and the measurement written down every morning, and the limb was kept comfortable, so that a swelling that grew would be seen by somebody other than the patient.

A computed tomography scan of the vessels in the chest was arranged the same day, with an ultrasound of the leg, and the ultrasound showed a clot in the deep veins of the calf, and the scan showed that clot sitting in the branches of the pulmonary artery. The extent was not the worst, there was no strain on the right side of the heart, and she was young and previously well, and that combination was the reason the outcome was as it was. Priya Raman wrote in the notes that the diagnosis should have been considered on the first day, and Douglas Kerr agreed and did not defend the decision, because the failure was not in the knowledge, which was available to everyone, but in the reading of it. The scan also settled the question the ward round had argued about.

Speaking to the family is part of this case and not a small part. Sophie Turner had been told she had a chest infection, had spent her first night on the ward in a bed she was going to be told she did not need, and had marked her books on her knees in the day room while a line was in her arm. The registrar sat down with her and her partner and said what had happened, in plain words: the breathing was not an infection, a clot had come up from her leg into her lung, the chest film had been clear and had been read the wrong way, and the note about her calf had been there from the beginning. People often describe this as the moment they lose trust in a service, and what restores it is not an apology but a specific account of what happened.

She recovered over weeks rather than days, which is the usual shape of it, and the anticoagulation was monitored with regular blood tests by the team that manages it. The swelling in her calf came down over several days with the leg elevated and the anticoagulation working, and the breathlessness settled, and she could walk to the end of the ward and back without the saturation falling. She was taught what to watch for, she was told explicitly what to do about the contraceptive, and she was told to say, to anybody who asked, that she had had a clot and to say it before anybody prescribed her anything. The first thing she was taught to watch for was the breathlessness returning, and the second was a swollen leg that would not go down.

Joanne Mercer was asked to write up what she had done, and what she wrote was not a list of nursing actions. It was an argument for a particular habit: the observation that changes the plan is the one written in a form that can be compared with tomorrow. A note that says the calf is swollen is a description. A note that says the left calf measured two centimeters larger than the right, at the same point, is a measurement, and the difference worth acting on is set by the current guideline and the local protocol rather than by a remembered number. A measurement can be repeated, and a repeated measurement can be trusted. She also wrote about the form, and about how a sheet that puts the description below a line keeps it below the line, and how the medicine that changed this case was found in a space designed for something else.

The service changed two things. The whole triage sheet is now read at the medical review, and if a description has been written below the line it is read out loud rather than left in the bottom third of the page. And every breathless patient now has both calves measured and the measurement written down, with a rule that any difference is reported rather than noticed. Both changes cost nothing, and both of them depend on somebody reading a piece of paper carefully.

The other lesson is about the pain itself, because the two conditions feel different and the difference can be heard in the words. The chest pain of an infection settles in over a day or two and is a deep, dull ache made worse by coughing, and it comes with a fever, a cough, and a change in the sound of the chest. The chest pain of a clot is sudden, sharp, one-sided and worse on taking a deep breath, and it is out of proportion to the general condition of the patient, who may look well and complain of one specific pain. Neither is reliable on its own, but the way a patient describes the pain is a piece of evidence, and it should be written down in her words rather than translated into a diagnosis at the bedside.

And then there is the chest radiograph, which deserves its own word because it is the reason this case went wrong. A chest radiograph is a result. It is a shadow on a film at one moment, taken by someone else and read by someone else, and it can be normal in a disease that has not yet made a shadow and normal in a disease that never will. It became a clear film, and the clear film was allowed to end the question rather than to narrow it. The reason it could end the question was not the film but the agreement that had formed around it, in which everybody had agreed that a chest infection was the right answer and the film would be made to look like it.

This failure was not anybody's carelessness, and it is worth saying that plainly, because the case is more useful for it. Every person in it was working hard and reasoning correctly inside a frame built by sensible people in a sensible order. Priya Raman had a breathless woman with pain on breathing in and a raised white cell count, pneumonia was the right first answer, and the anticoagulation she did not give on the first day would have been a serious error in a different patient. Joanne Mercer had written a good history and a good note and put them where the form asked her to put them, and the fault was in the form and in the reading of it. Any team would have built this frame, and any team could have made the correction, which is to treat the failure of treatment as a finding and to read the whole page.

There is a lesson for anyone who travels, and it is not a request to be frightened of aeroplanes. A long flight in a young woman who takes a combined oral contraceptive is not by itself a diagnosis of anything, and the overwhelming majority of the people who fly have nothing at all to worry about. What matters is that the combination is known, that it is written in the notes, and that anybody who sees a breathless traveller thinks about it for long enough to ask about the legs. Sophie Turner had no idea that her mother had died of a clot, which is the most human thing in this case, because she had been a child at the time.

One last thing about the nursing, because it is easy for a nurse in a story like this to become the person who noticed and not the person who reasoned. Joanne Mercer did not have a flash of inspiration at the bedside. She had a habit of taking a full history of the parts of a person a chest film cannot see, and a habit of writing what she measured instead of what she thought, and she had been mildly uncomfortable about a young woman who was not getting better, and she had not let the discomfort go. That last habit is the one to take from the case, because it is the one that most people lack.

To summarize, Sophie Turner was breathless with a sharp pain in the chest nine hours after a long flight, and she was treated for pneumonia for two days by people working hard inside a reasonable frame. The clue was on the triage sheet from the first ten minutes: a swollen calf on the same side as the pain, a mother who died of a clot, a new contraceptive, and a fast heart rate that the chest film could not account for. The nurse who found it had measured the calf and written the number down, and a measurement that can be repeated can be trusted. The lessons are that a clear radiograph is a result and not an answer, that the chest pain of a clot and the chest pain of an infection can be told apart by careful listening, and that the note nobody reads changes nothing until somebody reads it. Every threshold, score and measurement in this chapter is an illustration of reasoning, and each must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol before it influences the care of a real patient. This book is educational material, it is not medical advice, and the clinician with the patient always outranks the page.
