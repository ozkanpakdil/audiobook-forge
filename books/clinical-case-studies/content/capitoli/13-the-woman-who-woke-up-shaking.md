# Chapter 13 — The Woman Who Woke Up Shaking

Three in the morning is the hour when medicine is least well equipped to be subtle. Nadia Haddad was fifty-two years old and she cleaned wards on the early shift. She woke beside her husband shaking so hard that she could not get her hands under the blanket. She was sweating in a cool room, did not know where she was, and her heart was beating fast and hard. Karim, her husband, brought her in because he had been awake with her for an hour and was frightened by what he saw.

What follows is a constructed composite, assembled for teaching, and it does not describe a real patient; no detail identifies anyone living or dead. The case turns on a question asked at three in the morning that nobody had thought to ask, and on a medication more complicated than any note said. It is a case in which the first treatment worked, which is normally taken to be the end of a story, and in which the second half began only when that treatment stopped working. Every number in it illustrates reasoning.

The presenting problem was a woman with diabetes who was shaking, sweating and confused in the small hours, and the team had to hold two readings of it at once. The first was the obvious one, that her blood glucose had fallen too low, which fits those symptoms exactly and which the bedside meter confirmed within minutes. The second was the dangerous alternative in the other direction, a hyperglycaemic emergency, in which the sugar is high rather than low; the findings that would reveal it are a high reading on the meter, deep and deliberate breathing, a dry tongue and concentrated urine, and not one of them was present. The third possibility was the one the case turned on, which is that the pancreas itself makes too much insulin, and the finding that would reveal it is hypoglycaemia that returns after the tablet causing it has been stopped.

Between the meter reading and the treatment there were ten minutes in which the history had to be taken properly, and that is where the case begins. Nadia had been diagnosed with type 2 diabetes years earlier and had attended a clinic for it since then. She worked the early shift, so she slept through the afternoons and woke through the night, and she had eaten nothing since a sandwich at eleven o'clock the previous morning. She had not taken her usual tablets that day because she had slept through the alarm she set for them. When Aisha Farouk, the nurse, asked what she had eaten, Nadia said that she had been frightened of eating in the small hours ever since she started this job, because of what she had been told about her sugar.

Dr Jonas Petrie was the emergency department registrar who took her, and the nurse working with him was Aisha Farouk, who had nursed in that department for years and had a way of asking questions people wanted to answer. The working diagnosis was a hypoglycaemic episode, and the reasoning was audible and entirely reasonable: a person with diabetes, a textbook pattern of tremor, sweating and confusion, and a meter that confirmed the low. They ranked the possibilities twice, first by likelihood and then by how much harm would follow if one were true and missed. The only entry high on the second list was the hyperglycaemic emergency the meter had excluded, and the possibility that her pancreas was making too much insulin went into the notes without anybody owning it.

The examination in the resuscitation area was quick, and reassuring in the way examinations are once a number has answered the question. Her pulse was 124 beats per minute, which is fast and not by itself alarming, and the rate at which a fast pulse should worry a clinician is taken from the current guideline and the local protocol in your own service rather than from recollection. Her blood pressure was 104 over 68 millimeters of mercury, and a pressure that low means something different in a person whose pressure is normally high, so the reading that should concern you has to come from the same documents. Her temperature was 35.9 degrees Celsius, and the degree at which a temperature is called low is another matter for the same documents. She had no weakness in either arm or leg and no sign of dehydration, which argues against the hyperglycaemic emergency.

The treatment started at once, and it was correct for what they had found. The meter had read 2.8 millimoles per liter, and she was given glucose, which is the right answer to a low blood glucose whatever the cause, and when she could swallow safely she was given food and kept warm, and the plan for the night was written as observation rather than discharge. The registrar was explicit about his reasoning, and the explicit part was this: the meter had answered the question asked of it, and a person whose sugar is low in the small hours after a long shift has an ordinary explanation in a missed meal and a tablet. Aisha wrote that reasoning down and added the two things that did not fit it: the episodes always came in the small hours, and they always came after she had eaten almost nothing.

The nursing that night was not a backdrop to the medicine, because the medicine had one job and it did it. Aisha checked the glucose at the bedside through the night rather than trusting the value that had brought her in, because a reading taken at four in the morning tells a different story from a reading taken on arrival. The level at which a person is called hypoglycaemic differs between laboratories and is written into the current guideline and the local protocol rather than into anybody's memory. She started a food and symptom chart, and on it she recorded the time of every episode, what had been eaten and when, and the time of every check. At the bottom she left a note for the next nurse: this patient eats almost nothing between eleven at night and six in the morning, and her husband said she had begun eating at the bedside because she was frightened.

She went home the following morning with ordinary advice, and most of it was good: eat regularly, do not skip meals, carry something sweet, and check the sugar if she felt unwell. She was given a written plan for what to do at three in the morning if the shaking started again, and a number to telephone. The registrar thought the case was closed. He was not being careless; he was reasoning correctly inside a frame that was already the wrong shape. What he did not know was what was written in the clinic record, and neither did anyone else in the room, because that record had been copied rather than asked for.

The recurrence came three weeks later at four in the morning, was managed exactly as the first one had been, and was followed by two more in the month after that. Every one of them was in the early hours, every one followed a meal she had not eaten, and every one was over within the hour once she had taken glucose. She was admitted each time and discharged with a smaller variation on the same advice, until it amounted to eating a biscuit before bed, which she did for a fortnight and which changed nothing. How many episodes in what period should make a service call a pattern rather than a coincidence, and how long an episode should be allowed to run, are questions for the current guideline and the local protocol, and nobody here had asked either, so the pattern was never named. The third time she came back she sat on the edge of the trolley and said she was frightened of going to sleep, and Aisha sat down beside her and asked what she had eaten the day before.

Between those admissions the team's responses became steadily more elaborate and steadily less directed. The food before bed was increased, a night-time snack was arranged, she was asked to set an alarm for two in the morning, and the timing of the tablet was moved. She was offered a repeat clinic review and asked to bring her tablets, and she brought them, and the nurse counted them and wrote the number down, the number that had always been in the box. Every one of these interventions was reasonable and every one treated a missed meal.

The failure in this case was quiet, and quiet failures are the hardest to see. The team had not been careless; they had been consistent, and consistency is what a wrong frame buys you. The registrar's first feeling was that she was not taking her tablets properly, his second was that she was eating irregularly because the work was hard and the clinic had not got through to her, and his third, which he was honest with himself about afterwards, was that some people have brittle diabetes and that brittle diabetes is managed rather than cured. Aisha's feeling was harder to defend, because she had kept the charts, and she had a chart that said the patient ate nothing for hours and another that said the sugar was low every time. What she did not have was a chart that said what tablet the sugar had been low on.

So on the third admission Aisha did the thing that turned the case, which was to stop asking what medication the patient was on and start asking about every tablet, one at a time, wherever it had come from. Nadia's answer took ten minutes, and it was the answer that the notes had not been asking for. She took a tablet from the diabetes clinic, and she had been given a new box at her last review with instructions to take a different number of them, and she had never attended that review because she was on shift. She had asked a neighbour to read the new instructions and had not understood them, and the leaflet had two numbers on it with the newer one written on in pen. It was a sulfonylurea, a tablet that works by making the pancreas release more insulin, and it is exactly the class of drug that causes the blood sugar to fall in the early hours after a short fast.

That last point is the hinge of the whole case. A sulfonylurea taken by a woman who eats almost nothing during the day produces a blood sugar that is unremarkable at four in the afternoon and dangerously low at four in the morning, a well known behaviour of the drug rather than a rare thing. The tablet had been doing exactly what it is designed to do, the shift pattern was exactly the situation in which it does the most damage, and the two had been working against each other for months while everybody assumed the two were unrelated. Nobody had looked at the two together, because the shift pattern was discussed on the ward round and the tablet was in the notes, and people read the notes. The decision to raise her dose had been made at a review she had not attended, a process most systems have and never examine.

The moment the team understood what had happened is worth describing, because it was not dramatic and nobody enjoyed it. The registrar read the leaflet in Aisha's hand, looked at the number in pen, and said that the dose had been increased for a woman who eats nothing for eleven hours a day, and then he sat down, which he had not intended to do. Aisha's feeling was that she had held the answer in her chart for a month and never asked the question that would release it. Nothing had gone wrong that anybody could name: a count of tablets had been taken, a number had been written down, and the number had been the wrong number.

The first change was the smallest one available, and it was made because of the history. The sulfonylurea was stopped, and Dr Helena Bramwell, the diabetes specialist who had reviewed her in the clinic, rebuilt her treatment around a regimen that did not carry the risk of a low blood sugar in the small hours. Nadia was told plainly why, in language she could act on: the tablet was making her sugar low at night because she eats so little during the day. The episodes stopped, completely, and stayed stopped for six weeks. Six weeks is a description of what happened rather than a threshold, and how long a response should be observed before a case is closed is a question for the current guideline and the local protocol. The team wrote the six weeks down as a good outcome, and nobody asked what would happen when the tablet was no longer there to do part of the work.

They came back in the seventh week, and this time the episode was worse rather than better. Karim found her on the floor of the bathroom at four in the morning, calling her name for a while before she woke. There was a bruise over the side of her head and no recollection of how it had come, the glucose at the bedside was very low, and she did not rouse as quickly to the treatment that had worked so well the first three times. Aisha stayed with her while the registrar was called, and said out loud the thing nobody else had said, which was that this was the first episode that had left a mark. A fall in a person whose blood sugar falls without warning is not a minor addition to the picture, because the injury that brings her in is also one a low blood sugar will not let her feel.

The team now had to look further, and the first thing they found was a decision made months earlier that nobody had ever read again. During the very first admission, because she had already had more than one episode and the cause was not obvious, a scan of the abdomen had been requested to look at the pancreas, and it was reported as showing nothing abnormal, and it was filed. It was not a bad scan and not misreported: a small tumour that secretes insulin is much easier to find when the insulin is high and the sugar is low, and that scan had been done when neither was. A scan done at the wrong moment is not a negative result but an uninformative one.

The scan was therefore repeated, and this time prepared for the question. That meant reproducing the low blood sugar under supervision with the glucose measured at the same time as the scan, agreeing the fast with the current guideline and the local protocol rather than improvising it, and doing it once, by a specialist team, with the ability to treat the low at the bedside when it happened. It is an uncomfortable thing to do to a woman whose blood sugar has already fallen dangerously low, and that discomfort is why it belongs to a specialist team. The patient and her husband were told what would be done and why, and Aisha was there, because a nurse who has watched a patient's sugar fall knows about the fear a scan does not.

The scan found a small tumour in the pancreas, and the blood taken at the same moment supported it by showing the hormones that control sugar moving in the pattern such a tumour produces. The diagnosis was an insulinoma, a tumour made of the cells that make insulin, and every part of the case now fitted it. A tumour of this kind keeps the blood sugar low at the hours when the body should be releasing glucose to fuel the night, which are the small hours, exactly when her episodes had always come. It also explains the six weeks of freedom, because the tablet and the tumour had been producing the same effect together and stopping the tablet removed only half the cause: she had been low because of the tablet and because of the tumour, and for six weeks the tablet had been doing the tumour's work.

The surgeon was Mr Simon Harcourt, and the reason for operating was that an insulinoma is one of the causes of hypoglycaemia in which the tumour can be removed and the patient cured, which is not true of most causes and which is why the effort of finding it was worth making. The approach was discussed with Nadia and Karim, and the decision was shaped by what she wanted: to be free of the episodes and to sleep through the night. The nursing around the operation was planned rather than improvised, with the glucose watched closely before and after, because the sudden withdrawal of all that extra insulin makes a low blood sugar in the first days after surgery a real risk. Aisha worked some of those nights, and she could say to the patient what nobody else in the building could say: she knew what it was like to watch this happen.

The first night back on the ward was the first night in months without an episode, and it was strange for everybody. Nadia slept, which she had not done properly for a long time, and Karim slept in a chair beside her, and nobody woke her to check anything at three in the morning because there was nothing to check. Aisha came in at the usual hour anyway, found them both asleep, and wrote the night down, because a nurse who has watched a patient fail for weeks does not trust a good night until it has happened twice. The blood sugar she had learned to fear became something she measured rather than something that happened to her, and by the end of the first week the shaking and the confusion had gone, and the chart Aisha had kept for months still hung on the wall with nothing new written on it.

She went back to work and did her shifts, and slept in the afternoon with a glucose meter beside the bed for the first few weeks, not because she needed it every time but because she wanted to see it behave. She told people at work that she had had a tumour of the pancreas that made her sugar fall, which was more than she had told anyone about the episodes themselves, and found that saying it plainly took an enormous amount of energy at first and none at all after a month. The diabetes was still there and still needed treating. The treatment for it was rebuilt around the fact that she works nights and eats in shifts, which is a thing a clinic can do if it thinks about it and which had never been thought about.

What the nursing team did with her afterwards was, in the end, the most durable part of the treatment. Aisha taught her to recognise the early signs, which are tremor, sweating, hunger and confusion, and taught her that the treatment of a low blood sugar is fast carbohydrate first and something with protein in it afterwards. She was given a written plan specific to her hours rather than a generic sheet, and shown how to time her diabetes medication around the times she eats, and told what would happen if she did not. She was also told what to say in an emergency, which is that she has had a tumour of the pancreas that made her blood sugar fall, because a woman who says only that she is diabetic will be treated as one whose diabetes is uncontrolled, and that is a different problem with a different answer.

The follow-up was arranged with the diabetes clinic, and the change that mattered was small and entirely within the gift of any team. A person who does not attend a review does not have her treatment quietly left as it was on the assumption that she is taking it, and a person who misses a review because of a shift pattern needs it offered again at a time she can attend. The clinic began to ask about the interval since the last review and about the medicines actually in the house, and began to treat the clinic list as the beginning of a conversation rather than the record of one. And the team wrote down what the case had taught them, which was that the response to a treatment is evidence about the treatment and not a verdict on the diagnosis.

Three things came out of this case, and the first is not the one a listener expects. The first is that a treatment which works and then stops working has still taught you something, and the something is usually not that the diagnosis was wrong, since a treatment that worked is often the reason a diagnosis is not questioned for longer. The second is that the most valuable document in a case is often the one nobody asked for, and here it was a medication list assembled at three in the morning, one question at a time, from the patient herself, worth more than one assembled from the notes. The third is that a failure of correct treatment is a finding in its own right, and that the proper response to it is to go back to the beginning and look again rather than to add something else.

Aisha was clear afterwards that she had missed nothing a reasonable colleague would have missed, and she was right. She had measured the glucose accurately, kept the charts, written accurate notes, and done everything the job of a nurse on a night shift consists of. What she had not done was treat the medication as a question rather than a formality, and the reason was that she had asked it in the wrong form, which was whether the patient was taking her tablets, an answer to which is yes for almost every patient who has not skipped a morning. The question that worked differed in every particular: it was not more medical and not more expert than the one she had been asking, but simply more specific. It is now the question she asks of anybody who comes back a second time with the same story, and she asks it at the first visit, because on the first visit it changes nothing and on the second it changes everything. Noticing is partly luck and asking is a habit, and the habit is the part that transfers.

What Nadia took away from the whole business was more practical than any of the teaching. She sleeps through the afternoon and eats at odd hours and now has a plan that matches her life rather than one that asks her to change it, and she has written down what a low blood sugar looks like for her specifically, which is not what it looks like in a textbook. She has written down what she will say to any doctor or nurse who meets her, and she has told Karim what to watch for and what to do about it. She also says that the part she minds most is being asked whether she takes her tablets, because that is the question she could never answer honestly and never has, and it is the one question she now answers without any difficulty at all.

To summarize, Nadia Haddad had a low blood sugar because of a tablet and because of a tumour in her pancreas at the same time, and the tablet hid the tumour for six weeks while everybody congratulated themselves on the good result. The decisive act was a nurse asking about the medicines one at a time at three in the morning rather than accepting a medication list assembled from notes, and the decisive finding was that the tablet had been increased at a review the patient did not attend, with the new instructions read aloud by a neighbour and misread by everyone including the patient. A treatment that works and then stops working is not the end of a case; it is the first piece of evidence that the working diagnosis was incomplete, and the right response to it is to reopen the case and look again. The case was solved by surgery, by a medication history taken question by question, and by a nurse who watched the treatment fail and asked what the tablets were actually called, and no individual in it failed through carelessness, since the failure belonged to a frame that closed around a reasonable answer and to a system that prescribed for a woman who was not in the room. Every number quoted here, including the blood glucose values, the pulse, the blood pressure and the intervals at which a response is judged, is an illustration of reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol before it informs any real decision.
