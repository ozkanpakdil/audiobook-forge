# Appendix A — Ten Ways a Reasonable Team Gets It Wrong

This appendix is a teaching synthesis rather than a case. It draws the failures of the twenty chapters together, names the ten patterns behind them, and sets out the countermeasure for each. The cases are constructed composites and describe no real patient. The material is educational only: it is not medical advice, it is not a protocol, and it does not replace training, supervision, or the judgment of the clinician with the patient. Any figure in it illustrates reasoning rather than a standard, and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. The clinician with the patient outranks the page.

The twenty chapters failed in twenty different ways, and it is the shape of the failure that makes them worth reading. Beneath them are ten recurring shapes of error, and an error that can be named is an error a team can look for. Naming does not make a team safe, since a frame is comfortable to hold. It does make a team likelier to notice the moment when the frame has stopped describing the patient. What went wrong in every case was not care but attention, and attention is arranged by the shape of a service.

The first pattern is the early and cheap explanation that fits the complaint and then governs everything afterwards. There is nothing stupid about it, because the cheap explanation usually is the common one. The mechanism is that the questions asked afterwards become the ones the explanation calls for, and the question that would have threatened it is quietly never put. In The Asthma That Would Not Budge a diagnosis made in childhood was still governing the examination long afterwards, so a noise made in the throat was heard as a wheeze in the chest. In The Girl with the Stomach Ache a young woman with pain and vomiting was given gastroenteritis, and nearly enough was allowed to be everything.

The countermeasure has to happen early, while the diagnosis is still a possibility rather than a fact. Name the working diagnosis out loud, write down the two findings that would prove it wrong, and go and look for them. Ask the question the complaint makes inconvenient, even when the answer seems unlikely, because the cost of a cheap explanation is paid on the day nobody asks it. The Fits That Stopped When She Was Distracted is the sharpest illustration, because nobody described what was being watched; the team had been handed a word, and a word selects the observations a team will make. In every chapter of this book the findings that would have changed the plan would have fitted on one line.

The second pattern is the diagnosis that becomes the frame through which every later finding is read. A frame is not an opinion a person holds but a set of expectations about what will be seen next, and it works quietly, because every new observation is filed as confirmation rather than as evidence. The Acidosis That Was Called Sepsis is the purest example: the label was reasonable enough to act on, and then it explained a normal temperature, a normal blood sugar and a deep steady breathing pattern. The Swelling That Was Not Heart Failure failed the same way, with a swollen body read as a failing heart. The trap is that a frame makes the observer feel more secure rather than less, so a frame must be broken by a person.

The third pattern is the symptom that is explained away as part of the working diagnosis. It differs from the frame in being handled one finding at a time, always locally and always reasonably, which is why no individual can be shown to have done anything wrong. The Indigestion That Would Not Settle is the clearest case, because a weight that fell steadily was attributed to a busy season, to worry about a daughter and finally to a successful new diet. The countermeasure is to ask of every finding what it would mean if the working diagnosis were wrong, because a symptom explained away has not been explained but tolerated.

The fourth pattern is the observation that was written down and then filed. This is the most expensive of the ten, because the decisive finding existed in writing, in the correct words, and the only thing missing was a reader. The Breathless Woman After the Flight has a nurse's measurement of a swollen calf written in the lower part of a triage sheet that the medical review read from the top. The Stone That Was Not a Stone has a report of gallstones in notes from years earlier that was signed, filed and never looked at again. In none of these was the finding hidden; the system had no way of moving a piece of paper to where a decision would be taken.

The countermeasure is to make observation travel, and it is easier to arrange than it sounds. A finding written where the decisions are made is a finding that will be read, and a finding passed on in words has crossed a gap that a form cannot. The Indigestion That Would Not Settle shows the other half of the problem: the district nurse who watched the weight fall had nowhere to send the observation, so she stopped being heard rather than stopping noticing. The service that fixed it wrote a rule that an unexplained loss of weight in an older adult is a finding that travels rather than a note that waits. Every ward in this book eventually found a version of that rule, and every version was about the shape of the service and not about the diligence of the people inside it.

The fifth pattern is the reflex to treat anything that looks like sepsis. The reflex is understandable: sepsis is the commonest cause of a rapidly unwell patient in an emergency department, it kills quickly, and a team that waits for certainty may be too late. The failure is in what the word then absorbs: once sepsis is named every later finding is folded into it, and the questions become questions about antibiotic choice rather than about the patient. In The Acidosis That Was Called Sepsis a nurse recorded a deep steady breathing pattern with a normal temperature, which is a pattern of acidotic breathing and not of infection, and nobody read it as the question it was. The countermeasure is to name the label and then, once a day, say out loud what the label does not explain.

The sixth pattern is the assumption that a treatment which has not worked by morning must be the wrong treatment rather than the wrong diagnosis. It is also the most costly in time, because each escalation narrows the field without reopening it, and a patient can spend a long day receiving a second version of a treatment for a disease they do not have. The Stone That Was Not a Stone shows it in its purest form, with analgesia increased through the night and a scan left on the morning list while a man who had stopped passing urine waited. The Cough That Outlasted the Antibiotics shows the same shape with the drug changed more than once, and The Asthma That Would Not Budge shows what happens when perfect technique and perfect adherence change nothing.

The countermeasure is to treat a failure to improve as a finding of equal standing to a result, and to ask what the observation of no improvement would have to be for the working diagnosis to survive. That question is uncomfortable, because a treatment that fails is often felt as a statement about the team, and a question about the diagnosis is felt as a statement about the patient. The interval at which a patient who has not improved should be reassessed belongs to the current guideline and the local protocol rather than to the mood of a ward, and so does the interval after which a diagnosis should be reopened. A useful way to put it is to ask what result would have been expected by now, to look for that result, and then to notice if it is not there.

The seventh pattern is the appeal to a test result that answers a question nobody asked. A laboratory does not think; it returns whatever the request implied it was looking for, so a result can be true and still answer a question nobody posed. In The Bruises That Were Not Bruises the film was examined for blasts because leukaemia was in the clinician's mind, and the sentence about fragmented red cells was never read as an answer, because nobody had asked for it. The Migraine That Was Treated as a Stroke offers the mirror image, in which a normal computed tomography, or CT, scan of the head stood in for the absence of a cause in general.

The countermeasure is to decide the question before the request, to write it down, and then to read the report for the thing that was actually asked. This costs a few seconds and changes the meaning of everything that follows, because a report read against a question nobody asked will always look reassuring in the middle and alarming at the edges. The same discipline applies to bedside observation, since an observation chart is a report too, and the questions asked of it are the questions the ward decided to ask. In The Cough That Outlasted the Antibiotics a sputum culture that reported no growth was read as a laboratory that had not caught up, and the answer came only when the earlier specimens were recalled and examined again by a polymerase chain reaction, or PCR, test nobody had thought to request.

The eighth pattern is the examination that was not done because the patient was admitted on a diagnosis. The reasoning is short and sounds impeccable, which is why it survives: the diagnosis is established, the patient is admitted, and an admission on a diagnosis is read as permission to stop looking. In The Urine That Was Not an Infection a woman was admitted with a urinary infection and confusion, and the leg later shown to be broken was never examined, because a dipstick had said what she had. In The Agitation in a Lady of Eighty-Four a woman admitted as behaviourally disturbed was never asked to stand or to have her hip touched, because she could not say where it hurt. In The Hot Ankle the sole of a foot was never looked at, because the complaint was the ankle.

The countermeasure is to write the admission diagnosis as a hypothesis rather than an answer, and to make examining the parts of the body that were not the complaint a standing part of the first day. A person who cannot give a history needs to be examined rather than asked, because then the examination is the history, and a patient admitted with a label is the one most likely to be carrying something that was never looked for. The teams that corrected these cases did it with the simplest tools in the building: a nurse who lifted a blanket and looked at a leg, and a nurse who asked when a woman had last passed urine. None of that requires a grade or a permission, and any of those examinations could have been made on the first day by anybody who thought to make it.

The ninth pattern is the epidemiology that was never asked for, about age and exposure and risk and season. It is the cheapest of the ten to correct and the most often missed, because the questions are conversational rather than technical and nobody thinks of them as clinical. The Cough That Outlasted the Antibiotics failed on this point alone, with a mother who had coughed for years, a brother who had been unwell, a classroom of children and a country of origin all available to anyone who asked. The Breathless Woman After the Flight needed a family history of clot, a contraceptive and a long journey, and The Girl with the Stomach Ache needed a question asked of every woman of reproductive age. The Five Days of Fever turned on the age of the child, on the fact that she had never been vaccinated, and on a cluster of cases in her district, because the causes of a fever that persists are not the same at four years as at forty.

The countermeasure is to ask about people rather than about places, because the history of exposure is almost always a history of people. The Cough That Outlasted the Antibiotics rebuilt its exposure form around the people in the house and the people in the classroom, and asked about anyone with a long cough or anyone treated for a chest illness. Age belongs in the same list, and season belongs with it, and so does risk: the contraceptive, the medicine, the occupation, the journey, the previous episode, the family. None of these is a diagnosis, and each changes the weight of all the others, which is why a history taken without them is not a shorter history but a differently weighted one.

The tenth pattern is the discharge that assumed the improvement would continue on its own. It is often the most consequential, because it converts an unresolved question into a settled one and hands the patient the risk without anyone noticing. In The Indigestion That Would Not Settle a man was given a review date and a printed sheet of warning signs that he folded into his wallet behind a bus pass and never read again. In The Woman Who Fainted in the Garden a reassurance letter was the reason the patient stopped telling her family that she felt faint, and the silence was taken for improvement. In The Woman Who Woke Up Shaking a period of freedom was written up as a good outcome, and nobody asked what would happen when the tablet was no longer doing part of the work.

The countermeasure is to treat the discharge as a second decision layered on the first, and to build it out of the specific things this patient must watch for, a number to telephone, and a plan for when the plan fails. The woman who fainted was taught to report a pulse that was slow at rest rather than a feeling of faintness, and the man who was losing weight was taught to weigh himself and to ring rather than wait. The Cough That Outlasted the Antibiotics ended with weeks of teaching the family what would change and what must not be decided alone, and The Stone That Was Not a Stone ended with a written plan for the gallstones, deferred until the patient was well enough to have them treated. None of that is a kindness added at the end. It is the treatment.

Looked at together, the ten patterns are not ten accidents but ten properties of ordinary attention under pressure, and they arrive in combination rather than singly. A cheap explanation produces a frame; the frame explains away a symptom and files an observation; the filing makes a test look more reassuring than it is; and the reassurance removes the examination that would have reopened the question. The order is predictable, which is good news, because a predictable sequence can be interrupted at any of its joints. None of what has been described was a failure of care: the people involved were working hard and reasoning correctly on the evidence they had, and any team meeting the same patient would have built a version of the same frame. The defences against these failures are therefore procedural rather than personal: a question asked in a different form, a number read by somebody else, a rule written down before the next shift.

To summarize, the twenty chapters in this book failed in ten recurring ways, each of them nameable: a cheap first explanation that fits the complaint and then governs everything, a diagnosis that becomes the frame for later findings, a symptom explained away inside that frame, an observation written down and then filed, a reflex to treat anything resembling sepsis, an assumption that a failed treatment means the wrong treatment not the wrong diagnosis, a test result answering a question nobody asked, an examination skipped because the patient was admitted on a diagnosis, epidemiology never asked for, and a discharge that assumed the improvement would continue on its own. Every countermeasure is cheap, and every one is a change in process rather than a change in effort. None of these failures was a failure of care, and the defences against them are procedural rather than personal, which means a team can adopt them on any given morning. This material is educational and not medical advice, it is not a protocol, and it does not replace training, supervision or the judgment of the clinician who is with the patient. And because a treatment that fails is information, the patient who does not improve has not disappointed the clinician but has corrected them, and this book argues for listening to that correction.
