# Chapter 8 — The Girl with the Stomach Ache

The observation chart in the emergency department had a column for pulse and a column for blood pressure, and on that Thursday night both of them were quietly disagreeing with the rest of the note. Amara Nwosu was twenty-four years old, worked as a barista, and had come in with two days of abdominal pain and vomiting which she had treated at home with oral rehydration salts bought from a pharmacy. This is a constructed composite, assembled for teaching, and it does not describe a real patient; the details have been changed so that nobody could be recognised, and only the reasoning is reported faithfully. What organises the story is a question that takes moments to ask and was never asked at all, and a set of observations that everybody had been taught to read as dehydration. The nurse who settled her into the cubicle was called Pauline Craddock, and she is the person who watched the treatment fail and then asked the question.

The triage assessment recorded that she was in considerable pain and vomiting everything she drank, that she had been off work for two days, and that she had tried to keep herself hydrated with the rehydration salts. She had no diarrhoea to speak of, which she noted herself, and she was dismissive of the possibility that anything serious was happening, because she was young and she had a job and she could not afford to be ill. The pain was low in her abdomen and she described it as cramping and one-sided, though when the nurse asked her to point she put a finger on the right lower side and then moved it about without settling anywhere. She had asked for something for the pain and something for the sickness, and she had not mentioned anything else that seemed worth mentioning. How long a woman should be observed for vomiting and abdominal pain, and which observations should be repeated during that observation, is set out differently from place to place, so confirm the interval and the set of observations against the current guideline and the local protocol.

The alternative that had to be named when she was described, because it kills quickly and hides itself, was a ruptured ectopic pregnancy, a pregnancy that has implanted outside the cavity of the uterus and has torn through as it grows. What would reveal it would be a pregnancy test that came back positive, a pulse that stayed fast and a blood pressure that stayed low despite fluids, pain that was sharply one-sided, pain at the top of the shoulder when blood irritates the diaphragm, and a period that had not arrived. Not one of those findings is hard to look for, and none of them requires experience to recognise. What they require is that somebody thinks of them, and the reason nobody thought of them is the subject of this case.

The emergency department registrar was Dr Duncan Wetherby, and he built the differential in front of the patient, saying the words out loud, which is how a differential should be built when there is time. The most likely explanation was gastroenteritis, an infection of the stomach and the bowel, and there was a good deal behind it: vomiting, a working week spent handling food, a shared kitchen at work, and the general chaos of a young woman's month. Behind that he put a urinary tract infection, and behind that a bowel obstruction, and behind that appendicitis, because the pain was one-sided and she was sick. The ectopic pregnancy sat on the list as well, and he said so, and he said what would make him act on it. Then he ranked the list and it came out with gastroenteritis first, and the reason was that he had a story that fitted almost everything she had told him.

The reason is the part of the consultation that nobody can reconstruct afterwards without wishing it had gone differently. She had not mentioned any bleeding, because she had not thought it relevant and because she was embarrassed and wanted to be seen and sent home, and she had not mentioned that her periods had stopped, because she had put that down to the stress of a new job and a shared flat and a rota she hated. There was no box on the triage sheet for either, and the registrar had not asked for either, so the two facts that would have changed everything were absent from the record rather than from the patient. He was not being careless. He was asking the questions that the story he had already heard called for, and the story he had heard was a story about a virus.

The treatment was sensible for the diagnosis. She was given oral rehydration, which is the right treatment for the vomiting and the dehydration and which she had already started herself, and an antiemetic, and analgesia for the cramping, and a plan to discharge her once she had kept something down and her observations had settled. There was no blood taken for a pregnancy test, because on the day as it had been presented there was nothing to point at. The plan was to keep her under observation for a few hours, give her something to read, and let her go home with a leaflet about the warning signs of dehydration and what to eat when the vomiting had settled.

What happened over the following hours is written in the notes by the nurse who was with her, and it is the part of this case that anybody reading the record afterwards would pick out first. The nursing care was observations, a cannula into a vein so that fluids could be given if needed, and the questions a nurse asks when she gets a patient settled, the ones that are not on the form. Those questions are the ordinary ones: when the pain started and where it is now, how much she has been able to drink and how much she has kept down, and what her bowels and her urine are doing. They also include what she has taken for the pain and when, whether she has any medical history and any regular medicines, whether she is allergic to anything, and who is at home with her.

Somewhere in the middle of that, while she was settling her and while the antiemetic was being given, the nurse asked a question that had not been asked: when was her last period. It came out easily, in the way that things do when nobody has made them sound important. She had not had a period for eight weeks. The gap between periods, and the length of time that should make a clinician ask about a pregnancy, are set out in the current guideline and the local protocol, so confirm the interval your own service uses before you treat a gap as normal. She had put the gap down to stress, and she had been certain that she could not be pregnant, because she had an intrauterine device, a small device fitted inside the uterus to prevent pregnancy, which had been fitted some time before and had never given her any trouble. She said the words no period and I cannot be pregnant without any sense that she was handing over two pieces of information that did not belong in the same diagnosis at all.

The treatment did not work, and the way it failed was not in the complaint but in the chart. She stopped vomiting, which was good, and the pain eased slightly with the analgesia, which was also good, and the observation chart told a different story the whole time. She was pale and clammy, and the sweat on her forehead dried before she had finished speaking. Her pulse was 118 beats per minute and stayed there, thin and hard to feel, and her blood pressure was 88 over 56 millimeters of mercury, and when the nurse sat her up to measure it she became grey, her eyes rolled, and she had to be caught. She was worse sitting up than lying down, and that single fact, more than any number on the chart, told the nurse that this was not a young woman with a stomach upset.

How fast is too fast and how low is too low are questions with different answers for a young woman, for a woman on the oral contraceptive, and for a woman in her earliest pregnancy. The values that count as abnormal also change with age and with the medicines a woman is taking, so the readings that should be acted on should come from the current guideline and the local protocol rather than from this account. What mattered in that bay was not the exact number but the direction. A pulse that did not settle, a pressure that did not rise, and a patient who could not be sat up without going grey were the findings that mattered.

There was a short period in that emergency department when everybody was tempted to be reasonable. The registrar was tired, it was late, the patient was improving on the treatment, and the path of least resistance was to give the antiemetic a little longer, to let the observations settle, and to discharge a young woman with a leaflet about gastroenteritis. He was tempted, because a young woman with vomiting and a racing pulse is one of the commonest presentations in the building, and because the numbers can be explained by the vomiting, and that explanation is not wrong, it is simply incomplete. The nurse was tempted too, in a different way. She was tempted to let the second set of observations go in the bin and rely on the first, because the first had looked like a frightened young woman, because she had a queue of other patients, and because she had been taught that her job was to observe and report. Both temptations were reasonable, and both were the product of the same design: the observations are taken at a moment, a moment that is chosen can be chosen well or badly, and a chart that opens with a reassuring set of numbers tends to keep them unless somebody looks again.

What the nurse did was go back and do the thing the nursing notes had been doing all evening, which was to sit down with the patient and take a proper history, as an equal rather than as a form to complete. And what she found, when she went through it properly, was that the period had stopped before she changed jobs, and that the patient was certain she could not be pregnant because of the intrauterine device. The nurse said the sentence that ended the story of gastroenteritis: that a coil does not prevent pregnancy, and that a pregnancy which happens with a coil in place is much more likely to be in the tube rather than in the uterus. She asked for a pregnancy test while she was still talking, and she asked for it as though she expected it to be positive, and she told the registrar what she had found and what she thought, in that order, so that he could act while she kept talking to the patient.

The test came back positive, and the diagnosis of the next few minutes was not gastroenteritis at all. An intrauterine device makes a pregnancy much less likely overall, but it does not make it impossible, and the pregnancies that do occur with one in place are far more likely to be in the fallopian tube, where there is no room for them to grow and no way for them to grow safely. So the picture of a young woman with one-sided abdominal pain, vomiting, a fast pulse, a low blood pressure and a positive pregnancy test was an ectopic pregnancy until proven otherwise, and the proof was going to be the scan and the theatre. The nurse went to the registrar and said so, and the registrar stopped what he was doing, which was writing the discharge letter.

What changed in the next little while was the ordering of priorities, and the change was almost entirely a matter of what came first on the list. The patient was no longer a woman with gastroenteritis who might need a drip and a leaflet. She was a woman who was bleeding, and the bleeding needed to be found and stopped, and the findings that would guide the treatment were an ultrasound scan, which can show a pregnancy outside the uterus and can show fluid in the abdomen, and a haemoglobin, which had come back at 8.1 grams per deciliter, well below the range for a young woman. That haemoglobin is an illustration of the reasoning and not a threshold to be acted on in the abstract, and the value that should trigger action is set out in the current guideline and the local protocol.

What the team did next was resuscitation, which is a set of decisions about what to do while the diagnosis is being confirmed, and the decisions were made in parallel rather than in sequence. She was moved to a place where she could be watched, a wide bore cannula was put into a vein in each arm, blood was taken for cross-matching and for a full blood count and an arterial blood gas, and the surgery team was called rather than informed. Blood transfusion was prepared and begun, because a woman with a haemoglobin of that level who is tachycardic and clammy has lost blood and needs volume and red cells and not only salt and water. The anticoagulation that a ruptured ectopic of this kind is treated with was given as well, and every one of these decisions rested on the finding in the nurse's observation rather than on a result that was still to come.

The operation was done without delay, because an ectopic pregnancy that has ruptured is a surgical emergency measured in minutes rather than in days, and the delay that matters is the delay before anybody decides to operate. The surgeon found blood in the abdomen and a pregnancy in the right fallopian tube that had torn, and the tube was removed and the bleeding was controlled. Her blood pressure came up on the ward as the volume was replaced, and her pulse came down, and the young woman who had looked like a stomach upset turned out to be a young woman who had nearly died. She had lost more blood than her body could replace quickly, and the observation chart that had been failing for hours was the only reason anybody knew that.

The recovery was run by the ward nursing team and it was slow in the way that recovery after emergency surgery for blood loss is slow, because the body has to make blood again and the iron has to be replaced. The pain was managed, the observations were repeated often, and she was helped to sit up in stages rather than being put upright in a chair, because the blood pressure would not hold otherwise. She asked repeatedly whether she was going to be all right and whether she was going to lose the job, and the nurses answered her honestly each time, and she slept for most of that day. The line that had been put in during the night stayed in only as long as it was needed and no longer, because a line in place is a route for infection and nothing about her treatment required it to stay.

Before she went home the nursing team taught her the things she needed to know, and the teaching was as important as the operation. She was taught to recognise the emergency signs of internal bleeding: severe abdominal pain, a shoulder that aches at the top, dizziness when she stands, a pulse that feels racing, and bleeding heavier than a period. She was taught to have a pregnancy test before taking any new medicine, and to tell any clinician that she might be pregnant before an operation or a scan, and she was taught that if she ever feels that something is wrong in the lower abdomen she is to be assessed the same day rather than waiting for a routine appointment. And she was taken through the reasoning of what had happened to her in plain words, because a woman who understands why the coil did not protect her is far more likely to use the information next time.

She was also told that the intrauterine device could be reviewed and replaced, and that if she wanted to become pregnant she should have it taken out and her contraception planned properly with the family planning service, and she was given an appointment and a telephone number for the ward she had been on. The specialist team saw her in the clinic and explained the follow-up, which is a blood test in the following weeks to be certain the pregnancy hormone is falling, a repeat pregnancy test to be certain she is not pregnant again, and a review of her abdomen. The hospital did not say much about how she had nearly died, and neither did she, and the conversation kept returning to the same two facts: the gap in her periods and the coil. What she took away was that the device had not failed, and that a pregnancy can happen with it in place, and that nobody had done anything wrong.

The general lesson is stated more sharply in this case than in most, because the teaching is not really about ectopic pregnancy at all. It is about the order of questions. No woman of reproductive age with abdominal pain and vomiting has gastroenteritis until a pregnancy test is in the notes, and that is not a rule about the diagnosis but a rule about the paperwork, because the notes are what the next person will read. The test is quick, it is cheap, it is not controversial, and it is the single question most likely to be the answer, and yet it is the one most often skipped in the rush of a busy night, because the story of vomiting in a young woman arrives already explained by everybody who has heard it before.

The second lesson is about the observation chart, which is usually the most boring document in the building and was, in this case, the most informative one. The observations said that this patient was not getting better, and the reason they could be trusted is that they were taken in a fixed way: the same measurements, in the same order, at set intervals, by people who wrote down what they saw rather than what they expected. A nurse who takes a proper history produces evidence, not paperwork. The questions she asked about the pain, and about the food, and about the bowels and the urine, and about the medicine she had taken, and about the operation she had had as a child, and about the possibility of a pregnancy, were the diagnostic instruments of this case, and the reason the case failed is that they were asked late.

None of this was a failure of care by any individual, and it is important to say so, because the case reads as a story about a woman who was careless and it is not. Dr Wetherby was reasoning correctly inside a frame that had been built by his own first question, and any team would have built that frame from the information he was given. The nurse was not slow; she was thorough in the end because she had a moment in which she was not interrupted. And the patient was not careless about her own body; she was twenty-four, she was embarrassed, she was tired, and she wanted to be told it was a stomach bug. What the service learned was not a lesson about anybody but about the shape of the pathway.

The change that came out of it was structural. Any woman of reproductive age who attends with abdominal pain and vomiting now has a pregnancy test requested before she is given a diagnosis of gastroenteritis, and the result is recorded in the notes before the discharge letter is written. The escalation triggers on the observation chart were rewritten to include the combination that mattered here: a fast pulse and a low blood pressure that do not settle with fluids in a patient with abdominal pain, and any patient who cannot be sat up without becoming grey. The nursing documentation was changed so that a long gap without menstruation is recorded as a finding rather than as a negative answer, and the paperwork no longer offers a box for a family history that will be filled in later. The interval that counts as a missed period, and the dates that should be asked about, are set out in the current guideline and the local protocol, and they should be checked rather than invented.

There is one more thing in this case that deserves to be said slowly, and it is about waiting. For several hours this woman sat on a trolley in a cubicle being treated for a stomach upset, with a line in her arm and a leaflet about rehydration, while blood was collecting in her abdomen. She was not neglected; she was attended to, the observations were taken, the staff were kind, and every one of those things was true and none of them was the thing that saved her. What saved her was a nurse who asked a question she was not required to ask, and who asked it while the rest of the team were busy with a patient who was more obviously ill. The lesson is that the questions nobody is required to ask are often the ones that change the diagnosis, and that a service is only as good as its willingness to let a nurse act on an answer nobody requested.

One more thing is worth recording about how this case ended. The nurse who asked the question was not a person who happened to be near the patient; she was a colleague whose judgment the team had learned to trust, and Dr Wetherby acted on what she said at once, without evidence, because he had watched her all evening and had seen her change her mind about this patient once already. That is how a nurse becomes part of the diagnosis rather than part of the delivery of it. The habits that made the difference were unglamorous: taking the history properly the first time, writing down what the patient said rather than what she was asked, asking the questions that were not on the form, and being willing to be the person who said the awkward thing out loud in a quiet voice.

To summarize, this case turned on a question that takes moments to ask and was not asked for hours, and on an observation chart that was telling everybody the diagnosis was wrong while everybody was reading it as dehydration. The patient presented with a story that fitted gastroenteritis almost perfectly, and the two facts that contradicted it, a long gap in her periods and an intrauterine device she believed made pregnancy impossible, were not withheld by her so much as never invited. The failure belonged to a frame and to a pathway rather than to any person, because the pathway had a well-built route for the common presentation and no route at all for the unusual one, and because the observations had no trigger that fitted what was actually in front of them. The nurse watched the treatment fail, went back and took the history properly, asked about the periods, and had the pregnancy test asked for while the patient was still talking. What followed was resuscitation, blood transfusion and emergency surgery for a ruptured ectopic pregnancy, and a recovery in which she was taught what to watch for and why the coil had not protected her. The lessons are that no woman of reproductive age with abdominal pain and vomiting has gastroenteritis until a pregnancy test is in the notes, that a coil does not prevent pregnancy, that the observations of a nurse who has taken a proper history are a diagnostic test in their own right, and that a treatment that fails without anybody being unkind is still a treatment that has failed. Every number spoken in this case is an illustration of reasoning and not a rule, and each one must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol before it influences any real decision.
