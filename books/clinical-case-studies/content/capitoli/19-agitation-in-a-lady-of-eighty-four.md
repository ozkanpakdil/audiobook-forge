# Chapter 19 — Agitation in a Lady of Eighty-Four

A woman of eighty-four who has dementia is not agitated because she has dementia, and the distance between those two statements is the whole of this case. Evelyn Hart was eighty-four, had lived for six years in a nursing home, and was admitted to an acute medical ward after a fall in the bathroom of the home. She was agitated from the moment she arrived, and the agitation had a shape that the first day of her admission was already beginning to hide. This is a constructed composite and it does not describe a real patient, and nothing in it identifies anyone living or dead. The case is built to make one failure visible: a frame in which a description of behaviour was accepted as though it were a diagnosis. Evelyn Hart had taught English for thirty-one years and had lived in the same small room at the home for six years, with a photograph of her husband on the dresser. She had vascular dementia, diagnosed some years earlier, and needed help with washing but still had long stretches when she was entirely herself. She had been continent until the previous year, and the staff spoke of that with pride, because incontinence at eighty-four is a failure in the home's eyes. Her daughter, Gillian Rooke, visited twice a week. Two objects in that room had no business being there, and the first was a dog, because Evelyn Hart had asked for the dog every day since it died six years before and the staff had learned to say that she was out in the garden. The second was the habit of picking, for she would work the edge of the sheets with her fingers, tearing a thread from the seam, and she did it when she was anxious and not when she was content.

The fall happened in the small bathroom between her bedroom and the day room, in the early hours, and Marion Attwood, the care assistant who found her, had heard a noise and found Evelyn Hart on the tiled floor with the door open. She had been unable to get up, and she was cold and wet through. She was brought in with a bruise over the right hip and a complaint of agitation. Three days before the fall, and this is the detail the case turns on, the home's medical visit had started a new blood pressure medicine for her. The interval between a new medicine and a fall in a person of her age is short enough to matter, and the interval that matters in your service should be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

She was examined in the first hour on the ward. Her temperature was 36.8 degrees Celsius, her pulse was 78 beats per minute, and her blood pressure was 168 over 88 millimeters of mercury. The observations are given as illustration only, and the values that should worry you in a patient of this age come from the current guideline and the local protocol. She knew her own name and the name of the home, and she did not know the day of the week. She moved constantly, called out and pulled at her nightdress, and she was not aggressive towards anybody. The dangerous reading of that picture was not a behavioural illness but an acute illness with an unfamiliar name, a delirium, and the finding that would have revealed it was that her state changed within the day, that she was clear in the morning and lost by the evening.

The other dangerous reading was the hip, because a fracture in a woman who cannot report pain does not announce itself, and what would have revealed it was pain when the hip was moved and a leg that would not take weight. Three further facts were on the list, recorded without weighing. Evelyn Hart had not eaten properly for several days, and the home had been pleased about it, because she had refused food for much of the previous year. She had been unsteady on her feet for most of the week and had stopped walking to the day room, and the home had put that down to the new medicine. And she had been getting up in the night more often than usual, which the home attributed to the new medicine, and nobody followed that with the question of whether she was passing urine.

The team that gathered for her was a geriatrician, Imogen Clarke, a registrar, Eli Sanderson, and the ward nurse who would look after her, Maeve Donnelly. They met at the bedside in the first hour with the intention of ranking explanations twice, once by how likely they were and once by how dangerous they were. The history from the records at the home was of a woman whose agitation had been building for weeks and whose daughter had described her mother as not being herself since the summer. Imogen Clarke asked what had changed and in what order, and the answer was that the walking had stopped a week ago, the eating five days ago, and a new tablet three days ago.

The differential they built had dementia with behavioural disturbance at the top, and it was there for reasonable reasons, because it was the label the home had been using for two years and the label the referral letter carried. Underneath it they put a delirium on a background of dementia, then pain from a fracture of the hip, then urinary retention with constipation, then an effect of the new drug, then an infection, and finally a stroke and a tumour. The dangerous end of that list was the fracture and the acute physical illness, because both were treatable within hours and both would be made more dangerous by the plan they were about to adopt. Imogen Clarke said what she thought out loud, that a person who is agitated and cannot tell you what is wrong is a person in whom something is wrong, and the agitation is the only language available to her.

What they decided was to treat the behaviour and to look for the cause in parallel. Evelyn Hart was given an antipsychotic medicine, chosen because it is the drug used for agitation of this kind in older people, and because it was what the referral expected. Her regular medicines were reviewed, which meant a list was requested from the home and a call made to her daughter, and the new blood pressure medicine was noted and left in place for the moment, because her pressure was high and stopping it in a patient who had fallen seemed the more dangerous mistake. She was given a bed in a corner of the ward, near the toilet and away from the noise of the main corridor, and the plan was to observe her and keep her out of harm's way at night.

The nursing was where the case was actually held, and Maeve Donnelly treated it that way from the first shift. She set up an observation chart that recorded more than the routine numbers, because she had a rule that anything unusual went in as words rather than as a tick, and she wrote down the call for the dog and the picking at the sheets and the fact that Evelyn Hart could not find her way to the door. She gave the medication round herself at the end of every shift, because she wanted to see what went in and how the room looked afterwards. She organised the toileting routine so that she was taken to the bathroom before she became distressed, and she put a chair by the bed so that the night staff could sit with her. The nightly fight about washing was the worst of it, and it was a fight about dignity, and the staff won it and the woman was left clean and humiliated. And she took the medication history herself, question by question, from the daughter and from the home, and wrote down what they did not know as well as what they did.

The first day went well, and the team was pleased in a way that made them careless. Evelyn Hart slept, she took fluids, she accepted the medication, and she was calm enough to be moved into a chair by the window. Her daughter came in, found her mother sitting up and holding a cup, and cried in the corridor. Eli Sanderson wrote that the plan was working and the behavioural disturbance was controlled. Nobody asked what had been treated. The question, which sounds obvious and was not asked, was why a woman with dementia had suddenly become agitated at all.

The night went wrong. Evelyn Hart was up and about after midnight, and she was looking for her dog, and she could not be settled, and at some point in the small hours she tried to reach the toilet on her own and fell. The sound she made when she hit the floor woke the whole bay, and Maeve Donnelly, who had been with her twenty minutes earlier, was there in a moment. She found her on the floor between the bed and the wall, and she could not move the left leg without the woman screaming at her, and the scream was not that of a frightened old woman but the short, sharp sound of a person in real pain. She wrote down what she had seen and heard, in words, and that the leg was not moved by the patient at all. And then she did the thing that took her the rest of the night, which was to read the notes of the day before.

The ward round the next morning found Evelyn Hart harder to read than the day before. She was asleep at nine in the morning and could not be woken for breakfast, and she was mumbling at eleven, and the impression was of a patient more confused than she had been on admission, which is a sentence that changes the direction of a case. Eli Sanderson felt the pull towards a second dose of the antipsychotic and towards a sedative for the nights, and equally the pull towards writing that she was deteriorating and telephoning the family in. Imogen Clarke asked for the whole of the nursing record rather than the summary on the front of it, and Maeve Donnelly handed her the chart, and it was in the chart that the shape of the disturbance became visible.

Because the observations had been written in words as well as numbers, the chart could be read backwards. Evelyn Hart had been clear and orientated and able to hold a cup at seven in the morning, and in the day room by ten. Then, after the lunch round, the entries changed and the language changed with them, and the nurse on the afternoon shift had written about shouting and about the sheets. The last entry of each day was always the worst and the first entry of each day was always the best. There was a pattern, and the pattern had a clock in it. Delirium is an acute disturbance of attention that develops over hours and fluctuates through the day, whereas dementia is a chronic decline measured in years. Maeve Donnelly had written the word for it once as sundowning, and that word had been the reason nobody looked further, because sundowning is a word that ends a sentence.

The reason the pattern had not been read is worth stating plainly, because it was not stupidity and it is the way the case would have gone in any ward. The referral letter said dementia with behavioural disturbance, and every entry written after it was written in the light of that phrase. A confused old woman calling for a dog is a familiar sight on any ward, and the dog was six years dead, so it had become a detail of the personality rather than a detail of the illness. The same was true of the picking at the sheets, which was in the care plan, which meant it was known, which meant it was unremarkable. And the wetness, which appeared as a single line in the nursing record as incontinence, was read as the incontinence that everybody expects in a woman of eighty-four in a ward.

What Maeve Donnelly did was go back to the home's own notes, because the home kept a careful record of continence, and the last entry in it said that Evelyn Hart had been dry and continent. The wetness in the ward was new. She asked the simplest question a nurse asks, and it turned out to be the one nobody on the team had asked, which was when had Evelyn Hart last passed urine. The answer, which came from the night staff and then from Marion Attwood, was that she had not passed urine since the morning before the fall, and that what was soaking the bed at night was not a bladder that had given up but a bladder that had filled up and overflowed. She wrote it down as a single line, and it is the line the whole case turned on, because she had asked one more question than the plan required of her.

She examined her, and the abdomen was hard and full and the bladder could be felt above the pubic bone. She passed no urine at all through the following afternoon, and the fluid balance chart she had started without being asked to start showed a total that was tiny. Her mouth was dry, her tongue was furred, and she had not eaten for the better part of a week. A bladder filled to the brim in a person of eighty-four is a finding to be acted on, and the act is a catheter, and the insertion of a catheter is painful, and pain in a confused older person is a cause of agitation. The volume that can accumulate in a bladder, and the length of time a person can go without passing urine before harm is expected, are illustrations of reasoning, and the thresholds that matter belong to the current guideline and the local protocol.

The second thread was the medicine. Maeve Donnelly had written out the whole medication history from the daughter: seven regular medicines and one new one, and three of the regular ones had effects on bladder and bowel that added up to a problem in a woman with dementia. The new blood pressure medicine had been started three days before the fall, and the home had recorded the next day that she had been dizzy on standing, and nobody joined that to the fall, because the fall had happened to a different woman in their minds. Imogen Clarke said the obvious thing: a medicine begun three days before a fall cannot be called unrelated, and if it was not the cause of the fall then it was a candidate for the confusion. Eli Sanderson answered that her pressure was high, and that in a patient who has fallen and is confused the pressure matters less than the falling, and the tablet was held.

The third thread was the leg, and it took longest because nobody examined it. Evelyn Hart had been admitted because of a fall, and the fall had produced a graze and a bruise, and the urgent service had sent her for a film of the pelvis, and the report said that no fracture was seen and that a fracture of the neck of the femur might not be visible if it was not displaced. Nobody had put a hand on the left hip, because she was agitated and could not be asked where it hurt. The nurse had asked, though, and the answer she got was a scream. On the second afternoon, when the team went back and read the notes without the frame they had brought to them, Maeve Donnelly asked for the leg to be examined, and the examination found what the fall had produced, a fracture of the neck of the femur with the leg shortened and turned outward.

When the fracture was found, the team went back through the first day with the new knowledge in their hands, and the picture rearranged itself without much argument. The agitation was pain. The agitation was a full bladder and a full bowel. The agitation was the new medicine and the dizziness and the fact that she had not eaten for a week and had not passed urine for days. The agitation was a woman of eighty-four lying in a strange bed at night in an unfamiliar room, which is the setting delirium requires and which a diagnosis of behavioural disturbance has no room for at all. Imogen Clarke wrote in the record that the differential had been ranked correctly and read incorrectly, because a diagnosis of behavioural disturbance is not an explanation of behaviour, it is a description of it.

The treatment that changed everything was the reverse of the treatment that had been given. The antipsychotic was stopped, because it was deepening the delirium, because in older people with dementia it carries real risk, and because the agitation it had suppressed was not a problem to be suppressed but a report of one. The bladder was drained with a catheter and the bowel was cleared, and a fluid plan was begun, and her mouth was looked at and treated, and the bed was moved so that she could see daylight in the morning. The new blood pressure medicine was not restarted, and her pressure was allowed to be high rather than let her fall again. And the hip was handed to the orthopaedic team, because a fracture of the neck of the femur in a woman of eighty-four is an operation and not a ward decision, and it happens quickly, because the pain of waiting is itself a cause of delirium.

Evelyn Hart went to theatre on the second day and came back with the fracture fixed, and the nursing that followed was the nursing of any older patient after hip surgery, except that it was done with the knowledge of what had nearly happened. Maeve Donnelly ran a chart that was as much about the clock as about the numbers, because the pattern was the clue and the pattern was in the timing. She recorded what Evelyn Hart knew and when she knew it, and she recorded her fluid balance, her mouth, her bowels, her skin and her pain, and she wrote the first hour of every night in detail, because the night was when this went wrong and it would be when it went wrong again. Mobilisation began the day after the operation, and the first walk of twenty metres took a quarter of an hour and was done in three pieces.

The change in Evelyn Hart was neither gradual nor subtle. On the morning after the operation she was lucid, and she asked for a cup of tea, and she wanted to know where her daughter was, and she was not asking for the dog. Her appetite came back over the next two days and her continence came back with it, because a woman who cannot reach a toilet will be wet and a woman who is dry will not be agitated about it. She was moved into the chair by the window by the fourth day and was eating with the other residents by the end of the week. The confusion did not vanish entirely and it was not expected to, because dementia does not go away, but the part of it that had been manufactured by pain, by a full bladder, by an empty bowel, by a new drug and by a dark room at night did go away.

Two things were written down after that week, and they were written as rules rather than lessons, because lessons fade and rules get argued with. The first was about the clock, and any new confusion in an older person was to be recorded with the time of day at which it appeared, and anyone who had not seen the patient in daylight was to say so before offering an opinion about her mental state. The second was about the body, and a confused older patient was to have her abdomen, her skin, her feet and her legs examined by a nurse given the time to do it, and the examination was to be written in words, and whoever found something unusual was to be thanked rather than questioned. The home was told what had happened, and the conversation was difficult, because the home had been describing behaviour for two years and had been right about it. Gillian Rooke took over the medication box at home, which had been the source of many small errors, and came in every day for the first fortnight.

What Maeve Donnelly watches for now has changed, and the change is the point of the case. She watches for the clock and writes down the time of every change in behaviour, because a fluctuation that comes and goes is a finding a treatment failing cannot explain. She watches for the wetness, because a woman who has been dry for eighty-four years and is suddenly wet has a bladder problem until somebody has proved otherwise. She watches the legs, asks a patient with dementia to stand, watches her walk, and holds the frame while she does it. She watches the medicines and asks what has changed and when, and she looks for the drug that was begun in the last week, because the week before the fall is the week she asks about.

The first lesson is that agitation in an older person is a symptom and not a diagnosis, and the two are confused because the symptom is visible and the diagnosis is invisible. The second is that a treatment which calms a patient is not thereby right, and that a patient who becomes less agitated because she has been given a drug that reduces her awareness has not been treated but made harder to examine. The third is that the word which ends a sentence, whether it is sundowning or the behaviour that comes with dementia, is the word that hides the most, because it makes everybody feel that the hard work has been done. The fourth is that in a person who cannot give a history the examination is not a formality but the only history available.

It is worth saying how much earlier this could have been caught, because the point of a teaching case is the turning point, not the post mortem. On the night of the fall, the question that would have changed everything is simple to say and hard to do, because nobody thinks it at two in the morning, and it is that an old woman who has fallen onto her hip and cannot say where it hurts has a hip that has to be examined and a film repeated if the pain continues. On the first morning on the ward, the question that would have changed everything is that a woman who has been continent and is suddenly wet has a bladder that has to be looked at, and the answer took one telephone call. And on the first afternoon, the question that would have changed everything is that a new medicine in an old woman is a precipitant until somebody has proved that it is not.

No individual in this case did anything wrong, and that is worth saying because it is what makes the case worth teaching. Imogen Clarke, Eli Sanderson and Maeve Donnelly were each reasoning correctly inside a frame built from a reasonable first answer, and the frame was reinforced every hour by a label everybody agreed on, by a family who wanted to believe that the drug had worked, and by a home that had been managing the same description for two years. Any team meeting an agitated woman of eighty-four from a nursing home would have built a version of the same frame, and any team shown the leg, or the bladder, or the chart would have changed it. What the ward changed was not the people in it but the things it wrote down.

To summarize, Evelyn Hart was admitted for agitation and was given a drug that deepened the confusion it was meant to control, because several things were happening to her at once. The decisive observation was made by a nurse who asked when a continent woman of eighty-four had last passed urine, and the answer explained the wetness, the agitation and the hardness of the abdomen, and it was in the record because the record was kept in words. The other findings, the clock, the new medicine, the legs that had stopped carrying her, and the fracture the first film did not show, were all there for anyone who went back and read. The treatment that worked was the reverse of the treatment that was given, because the agitation was never the illness.
