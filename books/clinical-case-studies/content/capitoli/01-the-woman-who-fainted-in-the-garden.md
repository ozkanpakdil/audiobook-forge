# Chapter 1 — The Woman Who Fainted in the Garden

A collapse is a short answer to a short question, and short answers are read too quickly. This chapter follows a woman who fainted in her garden, and the fact that her body had been offering an answer for six weeks while everyone around her was asking a different question. The patient is Margaret Ashby, who is 68, a retired school administrator, lives alone on the edge of a small town, and who was brought to the emergency department three times in six weeks after being found on the ground with no memory of the fall. Nothing here describes a real patient. The case is a constructed composite, assembled to make one failure visible, and it identifies no one living or dead.

This material is educational, and it says so again here because the habit it teaches is a habit of attention. It is not medical advice, it is not a protocol, and it is not a substitute for training, supervision, or the judgment of the clinician who is with the patient. Even the age and the interval quoted here are illustrations of reasoning and not thresholds to act on, so the numbers your own service uses to judge a recurrence should be taken from the current guideline and the local protocol. The failure this chapter examines is not carelessness. It is a frame: once a working diagnosis is chosen, each later finding is read as confirmation, and the one detail that would have broken the frame is the detail nobody thinks to ask about.

Margaret Ashby had been a school administrator for most of her working life, and the manner of it still showed in the way she spoke to doctors and in the way she apologised for taking up an afternoon. She lived alone in a bungalow with a small garden behind it, and the garden was the reason she was found on the ground three times, because it was where she hung the washing and stood in the afternoon to look at the hedge. The neighbours had a key for the back door and knew where she kept it. On each occasion a neighbour had found her within the hour, she had no memory of falling, and the last clear thing she remembered was standing up.

The third attendance contains a line that nobody read as anything but housekeeping. Asked what she was taking, Margaret Ashby said her blood pressure tablet and nothing else, and then added that she had started buying new eye drops for her glaucoma over the counter at the pharmacy, because the repeats had stopped being sent and the drops were not something she was willing to run out of. She said it while pulling her cardigan down over her arms, as a piece of domestic administration rather than as a symptom. The note was filed with the rest, below the blood pressure and below the end of the medication list, and it was not read by anybody again.

Each of the three attendances ended in the same way. The heart was listened to, the blood pressure was taken, and an electrocardiogram, or ECG, was recorded that showed a slow rate, a delay in the electrical conduction of the upper chambers and a broad complex in the lower chambers. The report said that nothing was acute, and the delay in conduction was attributed to her age and to the antihypertensive that had been increased at the last clinic. Margaret Ashby was given a diagnosis of fainting, advice to rise slowly, and a review appointment. On the third visit she was asked again about her tablets and gave the same answer, and the line about the eye drops sat below the end of the medication list where nobody looked.

All three attendances began with a call from a neighbour and an ambulance, and all three ended with a review appointment and a letter saying that this was a common and benign problem in a person of her age. Nothing about that was unreasonable, because she was found on her feet or on her side, she was not injured, and she could not remember the fall. The pattern that mattered was the repetition, and repetition in a person who lives alone is easy to explain away, because every time she went out she was careful, and every time she was careful she did not fall. The neighbours were well meaning and quite sure that she had stood up too quickly.

After the third attendance she was admitted for planned cardiac evaluation, and that admission is where the case can be watched properly. The team was a cardiology registrar, Simon Ridpath, a consultant cardiologist, Patricia Neve, and a nurse practitioner, Anna Crowther, who ran the falls assessment service and had been involved with Margaret Ashby since her second collapse. They met at the bedside on the afternoon of the admission with a list of possible explanations and a decision to make before the morning. Their task was not to find a rare diagnosis. It was to work out whether this was a benign problem that needed a plan and a bit of reassurance, or something that would kill her without warning.

The differential they built was, in order of likelihood, a vasovagal episode, a postural fall in blood pressure and a seizure. Below those they put a disturbance of the heart's rhythm, a narrowing of the aortic valve and a metabolic cause such as a low blood sugar. The dangerous end of that list was the rhythm disturbance, and specifically a very slow heart rate producing what are called Stokes-Adams attacks, in which the heart pauses for long enough to switch the brain off briefly and the person collapses without warning. The finding that would have revealed it was a slow pulse during a collapse, and a history in which the collapse arrives with no warning at all.

There was one fact that did not fit, and it was visible in the very first set of notes. Margaret Ashby had no prodrome. A vasovagal episode usually announces itself with warmth, sweating, nausea, a dimming of vision and a feeling that something is about to happen, and she had none of that on any of the three occasions. The registrar reasoned that the warning is often less obvious in older people, that the episode had come while she was standing in the garden, and that a vagal response to standing would explain the setting. The consultant agreed that a missing warning in a woman who was already a little slow was thin evidence against a common diagnosis, and that the evidence for it had better be in the history. The reasoning was fluent, and it accounted for the missing prodrome by making the missing prodrome less important.

What they decided was a plan for a postural problem and for a possible problem with the blood pressure tablet. Margaret Ashby was asked to drink more and to take more salt, she was asked to rise in stages from sitting and from lying, and the antihypertensive that had been increased at the last clinic was reduced. A falls assessment was carried out with an eye to the bathroom, the height of the bed, the floor and the route from the bedroom. She was reassured that this was a common and benign problem, and she was given a letter that said so, and she asked whether she could carry on going into the garden and was told that she could.

She took the reassurance and used it, and the effect of that on the case was considerable. She told the registrar that she had felt faint the week before but had not gone down, and had not mentioned it to anybody because she was sure it was the tablets, and because a person told that her faints are nothing in particular does not report feeling faint. The letter went into her handbag, she stopped telling her daughter-in-law about the garden, the family stopped offering to drive her, and the isolation that had been protective became the condition in which the fourth collapse was found by a neighbour rather than by anyone who knew her well. She had told the truth at every attendance, and the reassurance she was given was a reason to stop telling it.

The nursing was where this case was actually kept, and Anna Crowther treated it that way. She began by writing down every medicine Margaret Ashby could take, including those kept in the kitchen, and asked separately about anything bought without a prescription, because she had learned that the answer is usually in the second question. She carried out the falls assessment with the observations that belong with it, and set up telemetry and a lying and standing blood pressure chart, in which the blood pressure is taken while the patient lies flat and again after she has been standing for a set period, because a postural cause should show itself as a fall in the standing pressure. She asked what Margaret Ashby had been drinking, and wrote down that she had been drinking very little since the last clinic, because advice to drink more is only as good as what actually goes in.

The chart came back with nothing on it, in the sense that mattered. Margaret Ashby's standing blood pressure did not fall in the way a postural cause requires, and the difference between the lying and the standing pressures was small, though the size of the fall that counts as abnormal depends on the patient and the circumstances, so take the number your own service uses from the current guideline and the local protocol. Anna Crowther wrote that a normal lying and standing chart did not explain a collapse that had happened three times, and that she would raise it on the ward round. The registrar agreed that the chart made a postural cause less likely, which was true, and then said that what was left was the faint itself, which was not.

The telemetry ran overnight and recorded something the team had already half seen and not wanted to see. Margaret Ashby's heart rate fell during sleep into the low forties, and lower still, with pauses long enough to set off the recorder's alarm. A resting rate that low is worth a second look in an older adult, and the point at which it becomes dangerous depends on the person and the setting, so the number that should alarm a team should be taken from the current guideline and the local protocol. The registrar read the tracing as a slow heart rate in a relaxed older woman, and the consultant noted that sleep slows the heart and that it fitted with the slow rate on the ECG in the emergency department.

Meanwhile Margaret Ashby was having the worst night of her life without knowing it. She had not been in the garden since the third collapse, and she had begun to leave the back door unlocked so that a neighbour could get in, which she described as sensible and her daughter-in-law described as alarming. She had also begun to sit on the edge of the bed in the dark before standing, and to count to ten before she got up, which nobody had suggested and nobody had noticed. The ward round that morning described her as bright, orientated and apologetic about the disturbance, and she asked whether she could go home that afternoon, and she said she was sure it was nothing.

The collapse happened on the second morning, in the corridor outside the day case area, in front of Anna Crowther, who had come back to finish the lying and standing chart. Margaret Ashby had been walking with a frame and had stopped to turn around. She said the word "floor", and then she was on the tiles and she was not moving. She was grey rather than flushed, and there was a brief shaking of the hands that stopped on its own. Anna Crowther did not shout for help first. She put two fingers on the neck, and she looked at her watch, and she counted.

The rate she counted was very slow, in the region of forty beats per minute, with pauses in which the pulse was not there at all. A pulse that slow during a collapse, in a person whose tracing already showed a delay in conduction, is a different problem from a faint, and the rate and the pause that should call for an emergency response are set by the current guideline and the local protocol in every unit that has one. The team arrived within a minute, found the patient still unconscious but with a pulse, lifted her onto a blanket, turned her onto her side and took her to a bed. Anna Crowther said, to nobody in particular, that there had been no warning of any kind.

The temptation in the corridor was to treat the episode and move on. There is a strong pull towards putting the oxygen on, filling the bed, checking the blood sugar and writing a note that says the patient collapsed and recovered, because that note ends the event. The registrar, who had been in the department for years, felt that pull and also felt something else: the particular discomfort of a patient who has collapsed on her own ward after three collapses discharged with reassurance. He found himself hoping that the count would turn out to be something else, and he was careful afterwards to write in the record that he had hoped for it.

What Anna Crowther wrote in the nursing note that morning was short and is the reason this case exists. She recorded that there had been no prodrome and no warning, that the patient said the single word "floor" and then stopped, that the face was grey and cold and not flushed, that the shaking of the hands was brief and did not follow the pattern of a seizure, that the pulse during the collapse was very slow with pauses, that the patient was conscious again very quickly and completely oriented, and that the pulse stayed slow for a while afterwards. She also recorded that she had asked three colleagues whether they had ever seen that grey face with a slow pulse, and that they had not. It was the only note in the record that described the collapse instead of summarising it.

And then she did the thing that no protocol required of her. She went back to the notes from the third attendance and read them the way you read a note when you no longer believe the first reading, which is to say slowly, and without the frame you brought to them. The blood pressure was stable, the blood sugar was normal, and below the end of the medication list there was a line about new eye drops for glaucoma, bought over the counter at a pharmacy, added because the repeats had stopped being sent. The nurse practitioner read that line three times and then went to find the patient.

Anna Crowther asked the question she had not asked in six weeks, and asked it in a way that did not suggest there was an answer. She asked what Margaret Ashby put in her eyes, then what else she used that was not on the prescription list, and then when the drops had changed. The answer was that the eye clinic had changed the drops before the first collapse, some months earlier, and that she had not been back since because the repeats had stopped. Anna Crowther rang the eye clinic and then the community pharmacist, and both told her the same thing about the drops: they belonged to the group of drugs that block beta receptors, they were used to lower the pressure inside the eye, and enough of the drug was absorbed through the eye to slow a heart that was already slow.

A beta-blocking eye drop is absorbed through the eye into the circulation, and its effect on the heart is small in most people and considerable in people who already have a slow heart rate or a delay in conduction, and Margaret Ashby had both of those things. She had been given a stronger preparation some months before the first collapse and had taken it without interruption since. The drops had not caused her conduction problem, but they had been making a heart already conducting badly stop altogether at the wrong moment. The mechanism was not difficult to understand once it had been said out loud, and it was not difficult to explain to the family.

The team met again that afternoon with the drops in front of them, and the reasoning turned inside out inside an hour. Patricia Neve asked what would have to be true for the eye drops to be innocent, and nobody could answer it, and she asked instead what would have to be true for them to be guilty, which was easy, because it was already written in the notes from the first hour. The plan changed in three parts. The eye drops were stopped and the eye team were asked to find another way to control the pressure inside the eye. The conducting system was assessed properly, with a longer recording and a review by the electrophysiology team. And the collapse on the ward was written up as an event in its own right, so that it belonged to the record.

The assessment showed what the telemetry had hinted at: the heart was conducting badly, with pauses long enough to switch the brain off and a rate that fell too far at night. The team then faced a second question, whether the pauses were caused by the eye drops alone or whether the conducting system was permanently faulty. Stopping the drops answered the first part of that question and not the second: her resting rate improved, but the tracing still showed a fault that would not have been there in a healthy heart, and she had already had three attacks that nobody had thought to observe. Patricia Neve explained the choice in plain words: the drops had been the trigger and the wiring the underlying fault, and the pacemaker would take over the job the faulty wiring could no longer do.

Anna Crowther was part of that decision rather than a witness to it, and she argued for one specific thing. She wanted a written plan for what the ward would do if anyone collapsed again, in which the first action was to count the pulse and the second was to write down what was seen before anything was moved or given. The procedure was small, it was written on the card at the end of the bed, and it took effect that afternoon. She said that a ward full of competent people had spent six weeks being kind to a patient who was getting steadily worse, and that the least she could do was make sure the next collapse was written down.

The pacemaker was done as a planned procedure under a local anaesthetic, and Margaret Ashby was monitored overnight and then went home. The follow-up was unremarkable, and the point of the case is not that a pacemaker is a triumph but that it was the answer to a problem that had been filed under the wrong heading for six weeks. The syncope did not recur. She went back into the garden. The daughter-in-law stopped being alarmed, and Margaret Ashby went back to apologising for taking up afternoons.

What Anna Crowther watches for now is different from what she watched for before. She watches for a pulse that is slow at rest rather than for a fall in blood pressure, and for any new medicine that affects the heart rate, including anything bought without a prescription, and she asks about that at every admission and clinic visit. She asks about drops in the eyes and ears and anything taken for a headache, and what the family keeps in the kitchen cabinet. And she asks who else is in the house, because the woman who fainted alone for six weeks was found by a neighbour, and the next time it might not be. And she asks about the drops, because the drops are the medicine that nobody puts on the list.

Two things changed in the service, and both of them were about records rather than about people. Any patient admitted after a collapse now has a full medication history taken by nursing staff that includes everything bought without a prescription, and that history is read on the ward round. Her eye pressure was controlled in the end by a different preparation, and the eye clinic review asked the general practice for the same list at every annual check. Any collapse on a ward is recorded as a clinical event with the pulse counted and the observations described, so that the next person sees the episode rather than a summary of it. Nobody in this case did anything wrong: the registrar, the consultant and the nurse practitioner were each reasoning correctly inside a frame built from a reasonable first answer, and any team meeting a woman who fainted in a garden would have built the same frame.

The general lesson is about absences, because this diagnosis was made by what was not there. There was no warning before the collapse, and that absence was worth more than any of the positives, because it is what separates a falling blood pressure from a failing heart. A faint that announces itself is usually benign, and a collapse that arrives without warning in an older person, with a slow pulse, is a cardiac problem until it has been shown otherwise. The showing is done with a pulse count and a tracing, and not with reassurance.

The second lesson is about what happens in the minute the patient is on the floor. The instinct to treat is a good instinct and it should not be delayed, but it is not the only thing that should happen at the same time. Someone should be counting, someone should be watching the face, and someone should be writing. A collapse on a ward in front of a witness is one of the few gifts a diagnosis is ever given, and the price of it is that somebody has to stop moving the patient around and start describing what they see.

The third lesson is the plainest. A medication list built only from the prescription record is not a medication list. It is a record of what has been ordered, which is a different thing from what has been taken, and it will not contain the eye drops bought over the counter at a pharmacy, the painkillers, the antihistamines, the herbal remedies and the borrowed tablets that make up a great deal of what a person actually swallows. Asking is cheap. The nurse who asked was the person who had been reading the notes, and the answer had been in the notes all along.

It is worth saying how much earlier this could have been caught, because the point of a teaching case is the turning point and not the post mortem. At the second attendance, one question about what she put in her eyes would have found the drops. At the first attendance, a tracing that showed a delay in conduction in a woman who collapses without warning should have raised a slow heart rate rather than been filed as nothing acute. And at the third, the nurse who wrote the line about the eye drops could have read it aloud, because the number of times a person collapses is a reason to look for a cause rather than to repeat the same advice.

To summarize, Margaret Ashby fainted four times, and the diagnosis was wrong for six weeks because it was built on a reasonable first answer and then defended. The clue that would have broken the frame was on the page from the first hour, in a line about eye drops bought over the counter, and it went unread because nobody asks about eye drops. The nurse who found it did two things no protocol required of her: to read the old notes again, and to count the pulse during a collapse instead of managing it. The general lesson is that an absence of warning is a finding, that a collapse in front of a witness is an opportunity rather than an event to be tidied away, and that a medication list from the prescription record is a list of what was ordered rather than of what was taken. Every number in this chapter is an illustration of reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. This book is educational material, it is not medical advice, and the patient in front of you always outranks the page.
