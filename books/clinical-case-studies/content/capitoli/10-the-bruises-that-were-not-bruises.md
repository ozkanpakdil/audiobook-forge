# Chapter 10 — The Bruises That Were Not Bruises

The bruise on Lisa Nowak's shin had no story attached to it, which was the first thing the nurse noticed and the last thing the team addressed. It sat on the front of her right shin, the size of a hand, and it carried the colours a bruise goes through when it is a week old, with yellow at the edges and purple and green in the middle, and no injury on her body that could account for it. Around it, and on her forearms and in the creases of her elbows, were the small purple spots, each the size of a pinhead, that she had found in the bath a fortnight earlier and decided were the sign of a tired teacher who needed a holiday. Three days ago the headache had started, and it has not let up since.

This case is a constructed composite, put together from patterns of illness rather than from any one person's record, and it describes no real patient and identifies nobody living or dead. It is here because the illness inside it is one of the great imitators in medicine, borrowing the appearance of a far simpler condition and paying it back with interest. It is also here because the mistake it describes was not a failure of attention but a failure of organisation, with four findings belonging to a single disease divided among three specialties, each attended to competently by the people who owned it. The chapter is about what happens when a team is right about one thing and wrong about what the whole picture means.

She was thirty-four and taught in a secondary school, and she came in on a Thursday evening with her mother driving because she had been too frightened to drive herself. She was a small, pale, watchful woman, and the bruises came in two sizes. The small ones were the purple spots, each the size of a pinhead, and they were on her forearms, her elbows, her ankles and the insides of her wrists, and they did not fade when she pressed them. There was also a blood blister on the inside of her lower lip and a tender patch on the hard palate, and the nurse found both by asking her to open wide and say ah, and neither had been mentioned.

Her observations on arrival were a temperature of 36.9 degrees Celsius, a pulse of 96 beats per minute, a blood pressure of 106 over 68 millimeters of mercury and a respiratory rate of 18 breaths a minute. She was afebrile and had no infection anywhere that anyone could find, which mattered, because every one of those numbers will be read differently by a different service and a different laboratory, and the thresholds that separate a normal observation from an alarming one move with age and with the population. Take the numbers as illustrations of reasoning and verify them against the current guideline and the local protocol before you build anything on them. What the nurse wrote in the record was that she was frightened, and more frightened than her words suggested.

The history was taken by the nurse before the registrar arrived, and it took twenty minutes because she asked about the shape of the bleeding rather than the amount of it. The bruising had begun two weeks before and had spread, and she had been unusually tired for a month before that, and she had a cold with a sore throat three weeks before any of it, which she had thought nothing of. Her periods had been heavier and longer for two cycles, and she had put that down to stress at work. She also said, without being asked, that her urine had looked dark and orange in the morning and that she had assumed she was not drinking enough water, and the nurse wrote it down because it was the only thing nobody had thought to ask about.

Two things had to be named at that point, and both were named. The first was the bleed inside the head, because if her platelets are low enough to bruise her into a hand-sized bruise on her shin, then the thing that will kill her is a haemorrhage in the brain, and the findings that would reveal it are a headache that changes its character, drowsiness, confusion, a weakness in an arm or a fit. The second was that a purpuric illness with a low platelet count is not always a platelet illness at all, and if hers were a disease of the small vessels in which platelets are consumed rather than destroyed, it would show up as anaemia with red cells torn apart, as fever, as confusion and as failing kidneys. Every one of those was written down that evening as something to watch for, and every one of them appeared.

The haematologist, Dr Samira Barzani, came and saw her within the hour, and the registrar, Dr James Thornbury, had already started a list. The most likely diagnosis in a well woman with this pattern was immune thrombocytopenia, in which the body makes antibodies against its own platelets and the count falls low enough to bleed, and the recent viral illness fitted the story of a trigger. The alternatives that had to be excluded were a marrow failure or a leukaemia, which would show a very different blood count, and a disorder of the clotting system, which would show in the clotting tests. The list was sensible, the reasoning was audible, and by the end of the first hour the working diagnosis was immune thrombocytopenia.

Dr Farouk asked for a full blood count, a blood film, a direct antiglobulin test, the clotting tests, kidney and liver function, and she asked for the film to be examined by a person rather than by an automated counter, and she said why. If the red cells were being broken up by something inside the patient, she said, the film would show it, and she wanted the film read by somebody who knew what she was asking about. The count came back with the platelets profoundly reduced and the haemoglobin lower than it should have been in a woman with bruises but not otherwise striking, and the film report arrived before midnight. She wrote her reasons into the notes beside each request, which is what a request is for.

The report read, red cell film shows marked variation in size and shape with numerous fragmented red cells, occasional polychromasia, no nucleated red cells seen, no blasts seen. The registrar read the middle of that sentence and was reassured by it, because no blasts means no leukaemia, and no leukaemia means a platelet problem, and a platelet problem has a treatment. Nobody on the team that night had any reason to think that a young woman with a viral illness could have a platelet problem and something else as well, and the report was signed and filed in the usual way. It was the document in the file that held the answer, and it held it in its second sentence, and nobody read it as an answer because nobody had asked the question.

The treatment was a corticosteroid with bleeding precautions and a plan to repeat the platelet count, and the reasoning was explained to the patient, who wanted to understand it. In most patients the count rises within a few days of a corticosteroid, so the response was expected to be both the treatment and the test of the diagnosis, and if the count did not move then the diagnosis was in doubt. The bleeding precautions were taught rather than issued: a soft toothbrush, no injections into the muscle, no rectal temperatures, no contact sports, nothing that might bump her, and a padded rail at the side of the bed. She was asked to keep a diary of any bleeding, however small.

The nurse who took her admission was Tricia Mahon, and she took the treatment seriously in the sense that she took the precautions seriously and wrote down what she saw in words rather than ticks. She began a neurological observation chart, because a headache of this kind with a very low count has to be watched for changes in consciousness, and she recorded alertness, orientation, the pupils, and the power in each arm and leg. She measured and charted the urine output, because a woman with a very low platelet count can bleed into herself and because a falling urine output is the first sign of many things that matter. She took her temperature four times a day and did not like the shape of that chart, though she said nothing about it at the time.

She also spent time on the things nobody charts. She asked what Nowak had been doing in the two weeks before she came in, and found a school trip with fourteen-year-olds, boxes carried up a corridor, and an ibuprofen taken more often than it should have been for the headache, which the nurse asked her to stop at once because it makes platelets less active and it can damage the stomach lining. She told her plainly that a headache that changed, blood in the urine or the bowel, or any new weakness was to be reported at once, and that she should not decide at two in the morning that it was nothing. She asked what she had been told by the other team, and Nowak mentioned that someone had asked her about a rash, and she had no rash.

The improvement everyone was waiting for did not arrive. On the second day the platelet count came back and it had barely moved, and Dr Farouk said that was not unusual this early and that she would look again. On the third day Nowak was nauseated in the morning and her headache had gone from severe to unbearable, and she said that bright lights bothered her and that she could not bear the television on. She was sick twice, and the nurse recorded all of it, and she recorded that she was oriented to person but not to place.

By the fourth day she was febrile at 38.7 degrees Celsius, her headache was worse than it had ever been, she was intermittently confused, and she had passed four hundred millilitres of urine in the whole of the previous day. A temperature of that height, a urine output of that size and a change in consciousness are judged against thresholds that differ from service to service and from patient to patient, and the cut-offs and the actions attached to them must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol rather than carried in the memory. What the nurse wrote in the notes that afternoon was that Nowak was not simply unwell, she was different, and that the difference had a shape. The nurse also increased the frequency of the observations.

And then the case split into three, which is what it did in every ward that has ever seen this illness, and none of the three parts was wrong. The headache and the confusion went into the haematology pile, because a headache with a very low platelet count might be a bleed, and the haematologist had said so. The fever went into the pile of things that happen to people on corticosteroids, which is a real thing, and the confusion with it, because a corticosteroid can alter a person's mood and their sleep. The failing kidneys went to the renal team as a volume problem, because she had been vomiting and had not been drinking, and because a small urine output after three days of vomiting is what volume depletion looks like.

Each of those three explanations was defensible, and that was the problem, because a defensible explanation for each of three findings is not a defence of the patient. Nobody holding one piece of that picture could be blamed for the piece they were holding. From the inside, the ward round felt like an ordinary morning in which three sensible people were each doing their own job well. The temptation was to act on all three at once, to raise the corticosteroid because the count had not moved, to give an antibiotic for the fever, to increase the fluid for the kidneys, and to call the neurologist because of the confusion, and every one of those steps was reasonable and every one of them delayed the only step that mattered.

Tricia Mahon was the one who noticed the things that did not fit. The urine Nowak passed on the fourth morning was the colour of dark tea with a pink tinge in it, and when the nurse asked her to describe it she said it had been like that for a fortnight and that she had said so on the night she came in and had been told it was probably because she was not drinking enough. Nowak was paler than she had been on Friday and the colour had gone out of her lips, and she answered questions readily but lost the thread in the middle of them and then began again from the start without appearing to notice. The nurse wrote it all in words, and wrote that the patient was different from the patient of two days before.

The nurse went back to the file. The film report from the first night was a single sheet in the notes, and she read the whole of it rather than the part that had been quoted. The second half of the sentence about the red cells said that there were numerous fragmented red cells and no nucleated red cells, and the word fragments sat there in a report that nobody had read as a finding. She rang the haematologist and asked her to look at the film again, and she asked a question, which was the question the report had been written to answer and nobody had asked: were the red cells fragmented, and did anyone know why.

The answer to that question turned the case round inside an hour. A red cell that is fragmented has been pulled through a narrowing or a strand of fibrin, and a patient whose platelets are low, whose red cells are being torn, whose temperature is high, whose mind is going and whose kidneys are failing has one illness and not four, and the name of that illness is thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura. Dr Farouk came to the ward and said the words out loud, and there was a silence on the round, and then Dr Thornbury said that the film had been in the notes since the first night. That sentence was not an accusation, it was the sound of a team discovering that the answer had been available in writing since the first night.

The treatment that changes everything is plasma exchange, and the reason to begin it on the day the diagnosis was made rather than after more tests is that this illness kills quickly and the treatment works quickly. Removing the plasma and replacing it removes the antibodies that are damaging the vessel walls and replaces the missing enzyme that keeps those walls intact, and the decision was made on the combination rather than on any single number, because no single number identifies this illness and the combination identifies it well. The corticosteroid was continued, further treatment aimed at the antibodies was arranged, and the cause of the vessel damage was to be looked for rather than assumed. The decision was made at the bedside with the family present.

The work that followed was mostly nursing work, and the nurse ran it. She arranged the central line and cared for it, she kept the balance of volume going in and coming out, and she recorded both with the same attention she had given the fluid balance chart on the first night. She managed the reactions that donor plasma produces, which include shivering, a rise in temperature and a rash during the exchange, and she managed the fear, which was not irrational, since the patient could feel her blood running through a machine and had no way of knowing that this was the treatment working. She also did the small things that keep the night staff from being frightened, which was to explain before every change and to write down what she had explained.

The response was not a miracle and it was not gradual. She needed the intensive care unit, or ICU, for two nights while the exchanges were established and running, and then she went back to the ward with a plan and a schedule. The count rose over the following days, the haemoglobin rose with it, the temperature came down to normal and stayed down, the confusion lifted, and the urine output came back to something she could measure. Each of those was watched for separately, because in this illness the count can rise while the patient is still dying, and the neurological state and the urine output are the two things that say whether the patient is actually better or only looks better in a blood test.

What caused the damage to the small vessels was investigated later, because it has to be looked for, and in many people it is a medicine, an autoimmune process or an infection, and in some it cannot be found at all. The medicine history was taken again in full, because a drug is the commonest cause that can be found. The follow-up was built around the fact that this illness comes back, and the intervals and the definitions of remission differ between services and between editions of the guidance, so confirm them against the current guideline and the local protocol rather than trusting a remembered number. Nowak was given a card stating what she had had and what to do if the headache, the fever and the bruising came back together, because those three together are the warning, and she was told to telephone rather than to wait.

The teaching in this case is simple to state and hard to remember under pressure. Purpura with fever, confusion and failing kidneys is one disease and not four, and the moment those three arrive in the same patient the list of diagnoses should get shorter rather than longer. A low platelet count explains a bruise, and it does not explain a fever, and it does not explain confusion, and it does not explain a rising creatinine, and the moment a count is used to explain a finding that it cannot explain, the count has stopped being a finding and become a frame. None of the parts of this illness is dramatic on its own, and all of them together are unmistakable to anybody willing to hold them together.

The process failure was structural, and it is worth being exact about it, because it will happen again tomorrow on some ward. The platelet count belonged to the haematology pile, the fever belonged to the steroid, the kidneys belonged to the renal team, and the headache belonged to everybody and therefore to nobody in particular, and a patient with one illness is safe only while one person is holding the whole of it. Nothing here required anybody to be cleverer or more thorough than they already were. It required somebody to say out loud that these four findings had arrived together in the same person in the same week, and the structure of the ward did not invite anybody to say it.

The second lesson is about the blood film, and it is a lesson about reports rather than about laboratories. That report contained the answer in its second sentence and it also contained the sentence that made everybody relax, and the two sat a few words apart on the same line. A report is written to answer the question that was asked of it, and this one had been asked, in effect, whether there were blasts, because the patient had bruising and leukaemia was the thought that came to mind. Nobody had asked whether the red cells were fragmented, so the answer to that question was written down in a report and never read as an answer by anybody.

What the nurse did was not clever, and that is the point of including it. She read the whole of a report instead of the reassuring line inside it, she asked a colleague a specific question rather than a general one, and she said the sentence that joined the four findings together when she brought them back to the round. Each of those is available to any nurse on any ward, and none of them requires a grade or a title or a permission, and together they turned a case round in the time it takes to read one sheet of paper twice. She did not do it because she was clever, she did it because the patient was different and she had said so in writing and nobody had taken her up on it for three days.

So what should have happened on the second morning, when the count had not moved and the headache had begun? Not a bigger corticosteroid and not a conversation about adherence, but a return to the beginning: what else could produce bruising, a very low count, a fever, confusion and a falling urine output in a woman of thirty-four, and what single test would separate the answers. That question takes two minutes to ask, and it is the question the ward round did not ask, because asking it meant admitting that the diagnosis might be wrong, and admitting that in front of a patient is one of the harder things in this job. Nobody on that round had asked a wrong question, and nobody had asked the right one.

Nowak spent the best month of her year on a hospital ward, frightened and itchy and frustrated, with a rash of corticosteroid across her face and a headache she could not get rid of. She had been a teacher three weeks before and she was a patient for most of a month, and the part she remembers is not the plasma exchange but the first night, when a nurse she had only just met sat down on the end of the bed and asked her to describe the colour of her urine, and then asked what the doctors had said about it. Somebody had taken her seriously very early, and in the end that was the thing that turned the case. Most of that month was spent waiting for somebody to put four findings together.

The service changed two things afterwards. The first was that any patient admitted with an unexplained low platelet count and any other unexplained symptom now has a single senior clinician named, so that the findings have one owner rather than three. The second was that a blood film in this situation is reviewed by a person for the features the history suggests, and the report must state what was looked for, so that a report can no longer answer a question nobody asked. Neither change is dramatic and both were cheap, and both came out of a case in which the answer had been in the file the whole time.

To summarize, Lisa Nowak had one disease and the team had four, and the four were each attended to by competent people reasoning correctly inside a frame that was wrong. The failure was not a missed examination or a careless drug chart; it was the splitting of a syndrome across three specialties, so that nobody held the whole patient at once, and it was a blood film that answered a question nobody had thought to ask. A low platelet count explains a bruise and nothing else, and purpura with fever, confusion and failing kidneys is one diagnosis rather than four, and a team that reads those findings together will reach the right answer days earlier. The nurse who noticed the urine, then read the whole of a report and asked one precise question, is the reason this case turned at all, and she did it with no more equipment than a bedpan and a sheet of paper. Every number quoted here is an illustration of reasoning only and must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol of the service where you work, because the thresholds and the timings are not the same in every country, every laboratory and every edition, and this is educational material rather than a protocol.
