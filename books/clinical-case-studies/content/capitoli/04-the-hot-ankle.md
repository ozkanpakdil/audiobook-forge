# Chapter 4 — The Hot Ankle

The ankle told Denise Ryan before the man did. It came into the triage room at the end of a raised leg, held the way a leg is held when it hurts to have it touched, and the skin over it was red and shining and stretched so tightly that it caught the light along the top of the foot. I cannot put my heel on the floor, the man said, and he said it as though reporting something he had been asked to report. Barry Heslin was sixty-two years old, he had worked on the docks until six years earlier when his knees had decided to stop, and he had driven himself to the emergency department because his wife was at an appointment of her own and he had not wanted to wait for a lift.

The story took five minutes and it had a shape. Three days earlier, without any injury he could remember, the ankle had begun to ache in the evening after a day on his feet. By the second day it was hot and swollen enough that his sock had left a red band around it, and by the third day he had not slept for more than an hour at a time. He had taken the anti-inflammatory that a neighbour had given him and had not been much the better for it. He had soaked the foot twice a day in water as hot as he could stand, with salt in it, because that was what his mother had done to his father's feet, and he had walked on it as much as he could because he had been told that if you keep a joint still it will set like cement.

Two things about him did not fit the story everyone was about to tell. He had never had gout, not once in his life, and he had never had a kidney stone either, which the history took and put down because it did not fit anything anybody was thinking of. He had had type 2 diabetes for some years, managed with tablets and one injection, and he had been due for a review of it for eleven months and had not gone, because the tablets worked well enough and because a man who has felt well for a year does not think of himself as unwell. His wife had mentioned twice that his feet looked wrong, and he had told her that his feet had always looked wrong.

Denise Ryan took the observations herself, and they are the part of this story that mattered most. His temperature was 38.9 degrees Celsius, and the threshold that counts as a fever is set in the current guideline and the local protocol for your own service, and it is not the same number in every place. His pulse was 112 beats per minute, and the rate at which a pulse should concern you is a matter for the current guideline and the local protocol, and it shifts with age and with whatever illness is behind it. His blood pressure was 104 over 68 millimeters of mercury, and a blood pressure means nothing at all except against the reading your own service works to, which is set in the current guideline and the local protocol. His respiratory rate was 24 per minute, which is the number at which most services begin to watch closely, and the threshold for watching is written in the current guideline and the local protocol.

A hot joint in a man of sixty-two has a short list of causes, and one of them is an emergency measured in hours rather than days, which is an infection inside the joint. The findings that reveal it are the ones this man already had, and they are easy to write down and easy to file under something more comfortable: a temperature above the level your service calls a fever, a pulse faster than the pain alone would explain, and a joint that hurts more when it is moved than when it is left alone. A hot joint is a joint that needs a needle in it, and the finding that says so is the fluid, which nobody had looked at. Nothing in the history ruled it out.

This is a constructed composite rather than a record of any real patient, and no detail of it identifies anyone living or dead, and the case has been put together to make one particular piece of reasoning visible. What follows is what the team did, what they felt while they were doing it, and what was written down at the time. The reason it is worth telling is that the mistake here was not an unusual one, and the evidence that would have prevented it was sitting in the notes from the first hour. It is written to be argued with rather than obeyed.

Dr Aiden Fowler, the registrar on that shift, took the history again with the nurse sitting in, and then they built the list properly, which is what a team should do. Ranked by likelihood came gout, because the story fitted it almost too well: one joint, hot and red and swollen, a beginning over a day or two, no injury to explain it, a middle-aged man, a weekend of shellfish and more red wine than he usually drinks, and a joint that had gone from nothing to agony in the space of two days. Nothing about the presentation was wrong for gout, and a presentation that is entirely correct for a disease still has to be weighed against the cost of being wrong about it. Alongside gout on that list sat cellulitis of the foot, a fracture nobody had seen, and a hot joint of another cause.

Ranked by danger the list was different, and infection in the joint was second on it. Fowler said the dangerous possibility out loud in front of Denise, because that is the correct habit and because he had been burned once before by a man he had sent home with a hot ankle. The findings that would have supported it were all present: the fever, the fast pulse, the diabetes, and the whole foot being involved rather than the joint alone, because infection in the soft tissues of a foot that cannot feel itself does not stay tidily inside one joint. He wrote that down in the notes. He then wrote that a first attack of gout in a man of sixty-two with a dietary trigger was the most likely diagnosis, and the second line of the note absorbed the first. He also wrote down the observation that would have separated the two, which was to move the ankle and to see where the pain was.

The treatment began the way the treatment for gout begins. An anti-inflammatory, and rest, and ice, and the leg raised above the level of the heart, and a review in a couple of days with a safety net if things got worse. The reasoning was audible and reasonable on the evidence as it stood: if this is gout, then the anti-inflammatory will take the inflammation down, the joint will settle inside a day or two, and the swelling will go with it, and if it does not, then the diagnosis is wrong. There was a nursing plan as well, and it was a good one, with observations, a temperature chart, and a measurement of the limb at the same point on the leg every day.

What the nurse did in the first hour is where this case is decided, and it is worth describing in the order she did it. She marked a point on the skin of each leg just above the ankle with a pen, so that the measurement would be taken in the same place by whoever came on next, and she wrote the first measurements down herself: thirty-three centimeters on the affected side and thirty on the other. How a change in a limb measurement should be judged is set out in the current guideline and the local protocol, and in most services the direction of the change matters more than the number itself. Then she asked him the question that nobody had asked him, which was what have you been doing to your foot.

The answer was that he had been doing what people do, which is a great deal. He had soaked the foot twice a day in water as hot as he could stand, with salt in it. He had been taking the anti-inflammatory from the neighbour. His wife had been rubbing a cream from the pharmacy into the ankle twice a day. He had been walking on it constantly, because he had been told that if you keep a joint still it will set like cement, and because there was a job he had to do in the garden. Denise wrote all of it down, and what she had not yet understood was what she was looking at: the skin was taut and shining because it had been stretched, because the water had been hot enough to scald, and because he could not feel it being hot.

She understood it twenty minutes later, while she was looking at the sole of the foot with a good light. The skin had been scalded by water that he had not known was scalding, because the feeling in his feet had gone years ago. Years of diabetes had taken the feeling out of the bottom of his feet, which is one reason a person with diabetes can walk on a broken foot without stopping, and another reason a hot foot in such a man is far more serious than it looks. She wrote that down as well. Both observations were in the notes for the rest of the day, and both were read as evidence of a severe attack of gout rather than of a foot burned by a man who could not feel it happen.

The night shift took the observations four times, and the numbers did not do what the treatment had promised. The temperature did not come down. The measurement of the leg went up by two centimeters, and the skin at the top of the foot became more tense, and at two in the morning he asked for more pain relief and said that he could feel his heart beating in the ankle. Denise came in and found him sitting on the edge of the bed with the leg held out again, in exactly the position she had first seen him in, and she thought that he did not look any better, and she went back to the chart and looked at the numbers instead of at the man.

By the second morning he was worse, and the failure was visible in the observations rather than in the complaint. His temperature was higher. The swelling had crossed the ankle and was now up the lower leg, and it was not the shape of a joint any more but the shape of a limb. He could not bear weight on the foot at all, and when she took the ankle between her hands and moved it, even a little, he shouted, which is not what a man with gout does when his ankle is moved. He had not slept. He asked, in the tone of a man apologising for the inconvenience, whether this was normal for gout.

Then Denise asked him to turn the foot over. She wanted to look at the sole, because that is what you do with a foot, and because she had asked him about the garden and he had said that he had been out there barefoot at the weekend. On the heel of the foot there was a small puncture no longer than a nail head, with a red rim around it and a tender spot in the middle, and the skin around it was the same red as the rest of the foot. He looked at it and said that yes, he had stood on a thorn, and that he had forgotten about it, and that he had not looked at the sole of his foot in three days because there was no reason to.

The failure was felt by everyone in that room, and it was felt in a particular way, because the treatment had been reasonable and the notes were tidy and nobody had done anything careless. Fowler's first thought was that the man had ruined it by walking on the foot, and his second was that the anti-inflammatory had not had long enough, and his third was the one he was least proud of, which was that this was what gout looked like when it was severe, and people did get very unwell with it. He wrote that the foot was to be raised, the anti-inflammatory continued, and the patient reviewed in forty-eight hours. Denise's first thought was that something in the chart was not adding up, and her second was that she had been reading the chart instead of the man.

She sat down on the end of the bed and did the thing that nurses do and doctors do not always do, which was to put the observations in a row and read across them. A man with diabetes and a hot foot and a temperature of 38.9 degrees Celsius and a pulse of 112 beats per minute and a foot that is hot from the toes to the calf and a joint that hurts more when it is moved than when it is left alone and a hole in the sole of the foot. That is not gout. Gout is a loud and dramatic inflammation of one joint, and it has no portal of entry, and it does not make a man look like that. This is an infection, and it has been sitting in that foot for three days while everybody was looking at the ankle.

So the clues were these, and every one of them was in the notes on the first day. The temperature of 38.9 degrees Celsius was written down as a fever caused by the pain of the attack, and pain does raise a temperature a little, which is exactly why it was such a good explanation. The fast pulse was the pain. The swelling of the whole foot was the inflammation of a gouty ankle spreading into the soft tissues. The exquisite pain on the slightest passive movement was what a gouty ankle does. The inability to bear weight was what a gouty ankle does. The diabetes was in the notes as a background condition, in the third line, in the way that diabetes is always in a set of notes.

And the hole in the sole of the foot was in the notes too, because nobody had looked for it. The reason all of this was missed is not that any of it was hidden. It is that the diagnosis was chosen from the story and then the observations were read through it, so that anything which fitted gout counted as evidence for gout and anything which did not was explained away, one at a time, with a reason that was individually reasonable. The frame was a man with a hot ankle. The frame that should have been in place was a man who is systemically unwell, and that frame was never adopted, because nobody ever wrote down what would have made the team wrong.

What happened next was fast, and it was fast because of the nurse. The joint was aspirated that morning, and the fluid was sent for culture and examined in the laboratory, and antibiotics were begun. The orthopaedic team was called, and the registrar rang the consultant to say, in words that were unusual for him, that he was sending a man to theatre rather than sending him home. The nurse practitioner took the blood the team wanted, cleaned and dressed the foot, and then did the thing that kept the case safe while the arrangements were made, which was to keep him still, keep the leg raised, and keep telling him what was happening.

The interval between the first day and the moment of the operation was short, and it was the interval that mattered. An infected joint in a foot that belongs to a man with diabetes is not a condition that waits for a clinic appointment or for a week of anti-inflammatory tablets; the window in which the joint can be drained and saved is small, and it has narrowed as the surgery has become more reliable. How long that window is in your own service is written in the current guideline and the local protocol, and it is the kind of number to check before deciding that a hot ankle can look again on Monday. There is no way of making that window longer by waiting.

At the operation the joint was opened and washed out, because infection in a joint of a diabetic foot does not settle on antibiotics alone, and the fluid that came out was cloudy with cells in it rather than the clear fluid of a gouty joint. The surgeon found the track of the infection running up through the soft tissues from the heel to the joint, and the hole in the sole was the way in. The culture grew the organism, and the organism was one that lives on skin and in soil, which was the last piece of the story and the piece that fitted the thorn in the garden. The material removed was sent on too, so that the laboratory could name what they were dealing with.

He recovered, and the recovery was slow in the way that recoveries are slow after a joint has been opened and washed. The fever settled within a day or two of the operation and never came back. The swelling went down over the following weeks, and the ankle became a joint again rather than a balloon. The hole in the sole healed, and it was the one that took longest, because the skin of the sole of a foot that cannot feel its own heat takes its own time about anything. He walked out on crutches in the end, and he said that walking back through the dock gate without thinking about his feet was the strangest pleasure he had had in years.

Then physiotherapy, and the slow business of teaching a foot to take weight again, and the flat-footed shuffle that a person who has been off a leg for six weeks learns to walk with. He was seen again in the clinic at the end of that period, walking without crutches and without limping. The ankle kept a small restriction of movement that would improve over the following months. His diabetes was reviewed properly in the aftermath, because a foot that got infected in the first place is a foot that belongs to a man whose sugar was running high, and his blood glucose was 13 millimoles per liter when he came in, which is a number to be read against the target set in the current guideline and the local protocol for a man of his age rather than against a number remembered from an old clinic letter.

What Denise Ryan watches for now, and teaches, is written into the discharge teaching and into the note that went back to his general practice. A man with diabetes should have his feet looked at, not occasionally but routinely, by somebody who is looking for the skin and not only for the pulses. He should never soak a foot in hot water, because he cannot tell how hot it is, and the scalding that produced the shiny red skin of this foot would have happened again. He should never walk barefoot in a garden or on a beach, and he should be taught to look at the soles of his own feet with a mirror once a week. And he was told again, and told again, that a foot which becomes hot, red or swollen is a reason to be seen the same day.

The general practice received a note saying that the man who had presented with what was assumed to be gout had in fact had an infected joint, and asking whether his feet were examined at his reviews, and the answer was that they were not, because nobody examines a foot that is not the complaint. The emergency department changed its own practice in a small way and a useful way: every hot joint now has its temperature, its pulse and the presence or absence of pain on passive movement recorded in the notes, so that the next person to read the file can see the pattern without having to reconstruct it. The review of his diabetes was arranged at the same time, with a foot check that happens whether or not the foot complains. One unlucky attendance had become a list of things that would be done differently.

There are three lessons here, and the first of them is short enough to be a rule of thumb. Gout does not make a person systemically unwell. A man with a hot ankle and a temperature of 38.9 degrees Celsius and a pulse of 112 beats per minute does not have gout until somebody has proved that he does, and the way to prove it is a needle in the joint and the fluid in the laboratory. The second lesson is that a hot joint is a joint that needs a needle in it, and that the needle is not an escalation of the treatment but part of the assessment.

The third lesson is that the diabetes is the reason it went wrong, not once but three times over, because diabetes is why the foot could be scalded without him feeling it, why a small puncture could become an infection unnoticed, and why an infection in that foot was far more dangerous than in a man whose feeling and blood supply were intact. Underneath those three there is a fourth thing, which is that the dangerous alternative was named on the first day, in the notes, and then never revisited, and a team that has written down what would make it wrong has already done most of the work of being safe. It also decides how long the question can be left open, because a man with diabetes and a hot joint cannot be sent home with a promise to come back if it gets worse. Diabetes is also why the injuries of a hot foot are silent and the signs of a serious one are easy to explain away.

Denise Ryan was left with a lesson that had nothing to do with the surgery and everything to do with twenty minutes spent with a man and a bowl of water. She had asked him what he was doing to his foot, and she had written down what he said, and she had then read what he said as evidence of a severe attack of gout rather than as evidence of a man with no feeling in his foot trying to treat himself with hot water. The habit she took from it was to read what a patient does to a limb as a symptom in its own right, because the things that people do to a painful foot are often more informative than the things that can be measured from it. She had noticed too that nobody had examined the sole of the foot, and that the reason was assumption rather than oversight. A nurse who asks the question nobody asked can be the reason a diagnosis is made in time to act on it.

To summarize, Barry Heslin's ankle was not gout, and the reason was written in the nursing observations from the first hour, in a fever, a fast pulse, a whole foot rather than one joint, and a small hole in the sole of a foot that could not feel a thorn. The nurse practitioner who watched the treatment fail found the decisive observation and put the findings in a row, and what she read across them was an infection in a joint rather than a crystal disease. The approach changed within hours, the joint was aspirated and drained, antibiotics and surgery followed, and the leg was saved with physiotherapy afterwards. The lessons are that gout does not make a person systemically unwell, that a hot joint is a joint that needs a needle in it, and that the diabetes was the reason this went wrong rather than an incidental detail of the history. This case is a constructed composite and describes no real patient, and it is educational material rather than medical advice or a substitute for the judgment of the clinician who is with the patient.
