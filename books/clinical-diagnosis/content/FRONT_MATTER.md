# Clinical Diagnosis — A Practical Manual for History, Examination and Differential Reasoning

This manual is about the part of medicine that no machine performs: working out what is
wrong with the patient in front of you. It begins with how a history is taken and ends
with how a diagnosis is reviewed, taught and, when it turns out to be wrong, disclosed.
On the way it describes how each part of the body is examined and what each sign means,
what the common complaints of clinical practice are, how the sick patient is recognized
in the first minute, and how all of it is done when the laboratory is closed, the
radiograph is a day's journey away, and the only instruments are a stethoscope, a
thermometer, a torch and a pair of hands.

Every subject is treated at two levels. First the principle: why the question is asked,
why the sign appears, what the finding does to the probability of disease, and what the
listener should be thinking while the patient is talking or the hand is on the abdomen.
Then the practice: what is actually said, done, looked for and written down, in a
well-resourced hospital and again in a clinic that has almost nothing. The comparison is
deliberate, because the same diagnostic problem is solved with very different tools
depending on where the patient lies, and the reasoning is what travels between those two
places.

It is written to be listened to rather than read. Nothing can be looked up or underlined,
and no diagram can be consulted. Where a chart, a table or a photograph would help on a
page, this book describes the thing in words precisely enough that the listener can see
it: the shape of a rash, the sound of a murmur, the feel of a liver edge, the look of the
patient who is about to deteriorate. Numbers appear as illustrations of reasoning, and
every one of them must be checked against the current guideline and the protocol of the
institution where the patient is being seen.

The seventy-seven chapters are organized in ten parts, followed by five appendices: the
foundations of clinical reasoning, the clinical encounter, examination skills by system,
diagnosis by presenting complaint, the recognition of the sick patient, the common
diseases of each system, special populations, investigations, decision tools, and the
practice of clinical judgment over a working life. The appendices are examination
checklists by system, differential diagnosis tables by symptom, normal values and
reference ranges, red-flag summary cards, and a glossary. The book is meant to be heard
in order once and returned to chapter by chapter afterwards.

This is educational material. It is not medical advice, it is not a protocol, and it is
not a substitute for training, supervision, or the judgment of the clinician who is
actually with the patient. Every appendix repeats that caution, and so does the first
chapter. What the book offers is the reasoning that lets a student, a house officer, a
clinical officer in a district hospital or a nurse practitioner in a clinic ask the right
questions in the right order, notice what the examination is showing, and know which of
the possibilities on the list will kill the patient first if it is missed.
