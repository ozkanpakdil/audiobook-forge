# Chapter 12 — Cardiovascular Examination

The cardiovascular examination is an inquiry into a pump, the pipes it feeds, and the pressures that keep fluid moving in the right direction. The reasoning that organizes it is a pair of questions asked in order: is the circulation delivering enough blood to the brain, the kidneys, and the working muscle, and is the cause of this illness one of the few that kill within hours. Those urgent causes deserve to be named before anything is touched. They are infection that has destroyed a heart valve, a tear in the wall of the aorta, fluid or blood compressing the heart so that it cannot fill, and the flooded lung of acute pulmonary edema. This chapter is educational material and nothing more; it is not medical advice, it is not a protocol, and it is not a substitute for training, supervision, or the judgment of the clinician who is with the patient. Every number spoken here is an illustration of reasoning, and each must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between countries, editions, and laboratories.

The examination is a series of small experiments rather than a fixed procession of rituals. Each maneuver is worth performing only if the clinician knows in advance what its result would change, and a sign sought without a question behind it is a sign that will be missed. The order can be rearranged around the leading hypothesis without losing anything essential, so a clinician who fears compression of the heart goes first to the neck veins and the blood pressure, while one who suspects a damaged valve listens first and then looks at the hands, the eyes, and the temperature chart. What must not be rearranged is the principle that the reason precedes the maneuver, because the reason is what makes the finding visible.

Observation begins at the end of the bed, before the patient is touched, and it costs only seconds. The clinician notes whether the patient lies flat in comfort or is propped upright and fighting for air, whether the lips and fingertips carry a blue tinge, whether the skin is pale, sweaty, or mottled, and whether the ankles are swollen. Posture is itself a finding, because a patient who cannot lie flat without gasping is telling the clinician that the heart is failing to clear the lungs. In a clinic with a torch and no monitor, this inspection is not a preliminary; it is a large part of the examination.

The hands and the face carry signs that point to the heart long before the heart is heard. The nails may be clubbed, curved and spongy at the base, which speaks of long-standing low oxygen or of infection inside the heart, and small painless red streaks may lie lengthwise under the nail plate, while the pads of the fingers may bear tender nodules that appear and fade within days. The skin may be pale, cool, and clammy, and the tongue and lips may be dark rather than pink. Fever with a new murmur and these peripheral marks is the combination that must raise the possibility of infection on a valve, which is one reason a fever is never only a fever.

The pulse is the pressure wave the heart generates, felt at the wall of an artery, and its character carries information that a number cannot. The rate, the rhythm, the volume, and the shape of the wave each answer a different question, so the pulse is examined in parts even when it is felt in a single gesture. The rate is counted for a full minute rather than a fraction of one, because an irregular rhythm makes a short count unreliable in both directions. A resting rate above 100 beats per minute in an adult may mean fever, fear, pain, anemia, or a failing heart, and the figure that should cause alarm varies with age, pregnancy, and fitness, so confirm the threshold that applies in your setting against the current guideline and the local protocol. A rate that is unusually slow may be normal in a trained athlete and dangerous in someone taking a drug that slows the heart.

Rhythm is assessed by whether the interval between beats is even and whether each beat has the same force. A regularly irregular rhythm points to a repeating pattern, such as a beat that arrives early and is followed by a pause, while an irregularly irregular rhythm with no pattern at all points to the chaotic electrical activity of atrial fibrillation. When the rhythm is irregular, the clinician compares the rate felt at the wrist with the rate heard at the heart, because some beats are too weak to reach the wrist and the difference measures how much the heart is wasting. An irregular rhythm that is also fast may reduce filling enough to drop the blood pressure and confuse the patient, so it is never a curiosity to be noted and set aside. The two wrists are compared with each other whenever an artery is suspected of being narrowed.

Volume and character are judged together, and they are learned by comparing many healthy pulses until the normal feel is stored in the hands. A pulse that is full and then collapses away from the fingers suggests that a great volume of blood is escaping backward with each beat, and a pulse that rises slowly and plateaus suggests that the outflow from the heart is obstructed. A pulse that alternates in strength from beat to beat is a serious sign that the heart muscle is failing to empty consistently, and it often accompanies a low output state. A pulse barely perceptible at the wrist while the patient is awake and talking warns that the blood pressure is being held by constricted vessels and may fall at any moment.

The pulse is sought at several places, because a pulse missing in one place and present in another localizes the problem. The carotid pulse in the neck, the brachial pulse in the arm, the radial pulse at the wrist, and the femoral pulse in the groin each represent a different segment of the arterial tree, and an absent or diminished pulse at one of them, in a patient with pain, is an emergency rather than a finding for the next clinic. A difference in the pulse or in the blood pressure between the two arms raises the possibility of a tear in the wall of the aorta, one of the lethal causes named at the start of this chapter. The finding that would reveal that tear is exactly this asymmetry, together with pain of sudden onset, and it must be sought on purpose because the patient may look deceptively stable at first. An unequal pulse in the two arms is not a variation to be attributed to technique until the dangerous explanation has been excluded.

The blood pressure converts the feel of the pulse into a number, and it is the number most often trusted more than it deserves. The cuff must fit the arm, because one that is too small reads falsely high and one that is too large reads falsely low, and the arm must be supported at the level of the heart, because an arm held low reads high and one held high reads low. The reading should be taken in both arms at least once, and repeated after the patient has stood, because a pressure that falls on standing points to a loss of the reflex that maintains it. A systolic pressure of 90 millimeters of mercury in a patient who was previously well is a warning to search for bleeding, infection, or a failing heart, but the value that should trigger action depends on the patient's usual pressure, on age, and on pregnancy, so confirm the threshold in your own service against the current guideline and the local protocol.

One pattern of blood pressure deserves separate attention because it points to a specific emergency. When the heart is surrounded by fluid or blood inside the fibrous sac that holds it, it cannot fill properly, and the pressure falls during inspiration more than it should, because the blood returning to the right side competes for space with the left side. The clinician detects this by inflating the cuff above the systolic pressure and lowering it slowly while watching the first sounds appear and disappear with breathing, and a fall of more than about 10 millimeters of mercury during inspiration is the pattern usually described, though that precise figure is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. The same patient is likely to have a raised jugular venous pressure, muffled heart sounds, a low blood pressure, and a rapid pulse, and the combination is the picture of tamponade. The finding that reveals it is the falling pressure on inspiration together with the distended neck veins.

The jugular venous pressure is the height at which blood stands in the great veins of the neck, and it is a direct manometer of the pressure in the right side of the heart. The vein is observed rather than felt, because it has no valve and its level rises and falls with the pulse and with breathing, while the carotid artery beside it is felt as a single brisk thrust. The patient is propped up at an angle with the head turned slightly away, and the light is directed across the neck so that the flicker of the vein can be seen. The height is described in relation to the sternal angle, and a level more than 3 centimeters above that landmark is usually taken as raised, but that figure is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the reference point and the normal range differ between services. A raised jugular venous pressure points to the right side of the heart while the clinician is still at the foot of the bed.

The waveform of the jugular venous pulse carries more information than its height alone. The vein normally shows two gentle rises for each heartbeat, one produced when the atrium contracts against a closed valve and one produced as blood fills the atrium while the heart contracts, and a loss of that pulsation, so that the vein is full and still, is itself a serious finding. A very large rise in the neck with each beat suggests that blood is leaking backward through the valve between the right atrium and the right ventricle, and a rise that increases when the patient breathes in points to a heart that cannot accept the extra returning blood. Pressing gently on the right upper abdomen for a short time normally raises the venous level only slightly and briefly, and a sustained rise during that maneuver is a sign that the right side of the heart is failing to cope with the added return.

Auscultation of the heart is an exercise in timing and location, and it should never be a random tour of the chest. The first heart sound is produced by the closing of the valves between the atria and the ventricles, and it marks the beginning of the contraction that ejects blood. The second heart sound is produced by the closing of the valves at the exits of the ventricles, and the way its two components separate during breathing tells the clinician about the pressures on the two sides of the heart. The first sound is best heard at the lower left edge of the breastbone, where the valve between the left atrium and the left ventricle sits, and the second sound is best heard at the upper right edge, where the aortic valve sits. The clinician who knows which sound belongs to which event can date every other sound in the cycle, and that dating is the whole of auscultation.

Extra sounds are the small additions that change the meaning of the cycle. A third heart sound, arriving a short time after the second, is a low soft thud that suggests a ventricle filling too quickly, and in an older patient it is a strong clue to a failing heart, while in a young person it may be entirely normal. A fourth heart sound, arriving just before the first, is a soft sound that suggests a stiff ventricle resisting the push of the atrium, and it is common when the blood pressure has been high for years or the heart muscle has thickened. A sharp early click after the first sound can mark a valve that is narrowed but still mobile, and the opening snap of a narrowed left-sided valve has its own early position in the cycle. Each of these sounds is meaningful only in its place in the cycle.

A murmur is the noise of turbulent blood, and it is described by four properties that must all be reported together. The timing says whether the noise occurs while the heart is ejecting, between the first and second sounds, or while it is filling, after the second sound and before the next first sound. The site says where the noise is loudest, because each valve projects its murmur to a characteristic place on the chest wall. The radiation says where the noise travels, and a murmur that travels into the neck or the armpit means something different from one that stays where it began. The response to posture and breathing says whether the turbulence changes when the heart is filled differently, because a murmur that becomes louder when the patient stands or breathes out narrows the possibilities.

The changes that posture and breathing produce are as informative as the murmur itself. When a patient stands from sitting, the heart holds less blood, and the noise of most obstructions to outflow becomes quieter, while the noise of a thickened, leaky valve may become louder. When a patient squats, the heart holds more blood, and the opposite happens. When a patient breathes in, more blood returns to the right side of the heart, which increases the noise of murmurs arising there, while a maneuver that raises the pressure in the chest reduces the return to the left side and quietens the murmurs that arise there. These are the clinician's substitute for a pressure measurement when no laboratory is available. A murmur is a product of flow, not a fixed property of a valve, and a description that omits these changes is incomplete.

The peripheral signs of heart failure are the visible consequences of a heart that cannot keep up with the blood returning to it. Swelling of the ankles and the lower legs, worse in the evening and less after a night in bed, is the commonest, and it is caused by the raised venous pressure pushing fluid into the tissues. The same pressure raises the jugular venous level, enlarges the liver so that it may be tender under the right rib margin, and can fill the abdomen with fluid. When the left side fails, the lungs fill with fluid, so the patient becomes breathless on lying flat and wakes at night fighting for air, and the clinician may hear fine crackles at the bases. Weight is the simplest measure of the fluid that accumulates, so a patient who has gained weight without eating more is retaining fluid.

Acute pulmonary edema is one of the lethal presentations named at the start, and it can be deceptive. The patient is desperately breathless, sitting bolt upright, often unable to speak in full sentences, with cool and clammy skin, and yet the chest may be surprisingly quiet, because the air spaces are so filled with fluid that little air movement remains to generate sound. The quiet chest in a breathless patient is more alarming than a loud one, and it should move the clinician to act rather than to wait for confirmation. In hospital the diagnosis is supported by the picture and by a chest radiograph, and in a clinic with only a stethoscope and a torch the same picture must carry the decision. The clinician who waits for a test before treating a patient who is drowning is making the one error that diagnosis cannot afford.

Infection on a heart valve is the second lethal possibility, and its danger lies in how ordinary it can look at first. A patient with fever, fatigue, and a murmur that has changed or appeared for the first time may have infection growing on a valve, and that infection can destroy the valve, throw infected material into the circulation, or cause an abscess in the heart. The findings that would reveal it are a new or changing murmur, a fever that does not declare its source, tender nodules on the fingers, painless streaks under the nails, small broken vessels in the eye, and an enlarged spleen. A clinician who hears a new murmur in a febrile patient should not simply record it and move on. The decision to treat rests on the combination of fever, a changing murmur, and evidence of infection in the blood, while the decision to withhold treatment rests on a clear alternative explanation for the fever and an unchanged, old murmur.

Compression of the heart and a tear in the aorta are the other lethal possibilities, and each announces itself through the pulse. In tamponade the blood pressure is low, the jugular venous pressure is high, the heart sounds are muffled, and the pulse is rapid and weak, and any one of these alone may be attributed to something benign, which is why all four must be sought together. In a tear of the aorta the pain is often described as tearing and moving, the pulses in the two arms may differ, a murmur of leaking back through the aortic valve may appear, and the patient may lose blood into the chest with very few external signs. A ruptured aneurysm of the abdominal aorta presents with pain and a pulsatile mass, and it can bleed enough to cause collapse before any laboratory result returns. In each case the absent or unequal pulse, or the pulsatile mass, is the finding that turns a vague story into an emergency.

The cardiovascular examination is also the examination of shock, and it often separates its causes. A patient with cool hands, a rapid weak pulse, a low blood pressure, and collapsed neck veins is likely to be losing volume, whether through bleeding, vomiting, or diarrhea, while a patient with cool hands, a rapid pulse, a low pressure, and distended neck veins is likely to have a heart that cannot pump or cannot fill. Warm hands with a bounding pulse and a low pressure suggest that the vessels themselves have opened wide, as they do in severe infection. The distinction is made at the bedside, with the hands and the eyes, and it changes what the clinician does next, because a failing pump and an empty circulation are treated in opposite ways. The clinician who examines the veins as carefully as the pressure will make that distinction in a minute.

These patterns were described in particular populations, and their meaning can shift with age, pregnancy, and the presence of other disease. The examination changes its shape when resources are plentiful, but not its logic. In a quiet cardiology room the clinician can listen without competing noise, repeat the blood pressure with an electronic device, ask a colleague to confirm a murmur, and send the patient for an electrocardiogram, for an echocardiogram, and for imaging of the chest. The electrocardiogram, or ECG, adds the electrical story to the mechanical one, and the echocardiogram shows the valves and the muscle directly, which is the closest thing to looking inside the heart. Even here the physical findings come first, because they decide what the test is being asked to answer, and because a normal result in a patient with a strong physical sign should prompt a repeat rather than relief.

In a noisy ward or a clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, the same examination is performed more slowly and more carefully. The torch is used to look at the eyes for small broken vessels and at the mouth for the state of the tongue, the thermometer confirms the fever the hand suspected, and the stethoscope is used in a quiet corner because background noise hides the soft sounds that matter most. The clinician counts the pulse for a full minute, compares both arms, looks at the neck with the patient propped at an angle, and examines the ankles and the abdomen for fluid. Without an echocardiogram, the decision rests on the combination of findings, and that combination is often enough to identify the dangerous case and to arrange a referral that cannot wait. A clinic that cannot image the heart can still detect the patient who needs to be moved.

The findings must then be written so that the next clinician can use them. A pulse should be recorded with its rate, rhythm, volume, and character, and a murmur with its timing, site, radiation, and response to posture and breathing. The jugular venous pressure should be recorded with the position of the patient and the reference point used, because a bare number means nothing without them, and the blood pressure with the arm used and the position of the patient. The peripheral findings should be recorded with the side and the extent, so that a later reader can tell whether the picture is improving or worsening. The note should also show what was considered and set aside, not only what was found, because reasoning that is not written down cannot be checked by the next person.

Uncertainty is a normal finding in the cardiovascular examination, and it must be managed rather than hidden. A patient with a soft murmur, no fever, and a normal pulse may be safely watched, but the clinician must say what to watch for and when to return. A patient with chest pain, an unequal pulse, or a murmur that appeared with a fever should not be sent home with reassurance, because the dangerous causes named here progress quickly and the window for treatment is short. Where the clinician cannot be certain, the safest course is often a review in a few hours or a referral, and the reasoning for that decision should be recorded as carefully as the findings. Safety-netting protects the patient when the first decision turns out to be wrong.

A constructed illustration may make the sequence visible, and it is a teaching device rather than a record of any real person. A middle-aged patient arrives with fever and a new murmur, and the clinician, having taken the temperature and felt the pulse, looks at the hands and finds the small streaks under the nails, then examines the eyes and the spleen. Nothing about the story is dramatic, and the dangerous diagnosis is found not because the clinician was clever but because a fixed set of questions was asked in a fixed order. In another constructed illustration, a patient with sudden severe chest pain has a pressure in one arm that is 20 millimeters of mercury lower than in the other, and that single comparison moves the patient from the waiting area to the resuscitation room, though that figure is an illustration and the difference that should matter in your setting should be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

The examination improves with practice, and the practice is not the memorizing of lists but the repeated comparison of what is felt and heard with what is later found to be true. Some bodies are harder to examine than others, and the difficulty should be acknowledged rather than ignored. A thick chest wall, breast tissue, or a noisy ventilator can hide a murmur and an extra sound, and in those situations the clinician must rely more openly on the history, the pulse, the blood pressure, and the visible signs. Older patients often have stiff arteries, so a pulse may feel strong while the true pressure is high, and a very fast heart rate may make murmurs impossible to time by ear alone, in which case the honest course is to note the uncertainty and arrange the test that can answer it. Describing what could not be heard is as useful as describing what was.

To summarize, the cardiovascular examination asks first whether the circulation is adequate and second whether the cause is one of the few that kill quickly, and it answers both with the hands, the eyes, and the ears before it reaches for a machine. The pulse is read in rate, rhythm, volume, and character, the jugular venous pressure is read as a manometer of the right heart, the heart sounds are dated within the cycle, and every murmur is described by its timing, its site, its radiation, and its response to posture and breathing. The peripheral signs of heart failure, the swollen ankles, the raised venous pressure, the enlarged liver, and the fine crackles at the lung bases, are the visible consequence of a heart that cannot keep up. The dangerous possibilities, infection on a valve, a tear in the aorta, compression of the heart, and the flooded lung, are sought early and deliberately, because they are the findings that change the plan within minutes. Every rate, pressure, height, and cut-off mentioned here is an illustration only, and each must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol before it influences a real decision. This is educational material, it is not medical advice, it is not a protocol, and it is not a substitute for training, supervision, or the judgment of the clinician who is with the patient, and where the patient is deteriorating the examination stops and the patient is treated.
