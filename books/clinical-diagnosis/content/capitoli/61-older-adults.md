# Chapter 61 — Older Adults

The older patient is the one in whom the textbook picture is most often missing, and this chapter is arranged around that fact. The familiar presentation of an illness belongs to a younger body with reserve to spare, while the same disease in an older body may announce itself only as a fall, a confusion, a refusal to eat, or a quiet loss of the ability to dress and walk. A clinician who waits for the expected symptom will wait past the moment when the diagnosis was still simple, and a clinician who treats the change in function as the presenting complaint will usually find the disease behind it. Age by itself is not a diagnosis, and the years a patient carries say less than the state of that patient's reserve, muscle, memory, and medicines. This chapter follows the older patient through the four changes that most often bring the illness into view, which are a fall, a confusion, a loss of appetite, and a decline in what the patient could formerly do. It is educational material, not medical advice, not a protocol, and not a substitute for training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the rules of the service in which the patient is seen.

The dangers that hide inside these quiet presentations should be named before a comfortable explanation is accepted, because each is revealed by a finding that can be sought in the first minutes. An older patient with a new confusion and an ordinary temperature may have an infection in the bloodstream, and the finding that betrays it is often a climb in the breathing rate or a sudden loss of the ability to walk rather than any fever at all. The patient who has fallen and cannot put weight on a leg may have a fracture of the hip, and the finding is a limb that lies shortened and turned outward, even though the patient may deny pain. The patient whose confusion comes and goes, who has a headache, or who has grown weak on one side may have blood collecting over the surface of the brain, and the finding that confirms it is seen on a computed tomography, or CT, scan of the head after an injury so slight that nobody remembers it. The patient who becomes breathless, clammy, or faint without chest pain may be having a myocardial infarction, and the finding is the tracing on an electrocardiogram, or ECG, together with the substances released by damaged heart muscle. A new symptom that began after a medicine was added or increased may be caused by that medicine, and dehydration, the easiest danger to overlook, is revealed by a dry mouth, a rising pulse, and urine that has become scanty and dark.

Why older patients present this way lies in reserve rather than in any single organ. With age the reserve of every system shrinks, so the heart, the kidney, the lung, and the brain have less room to absorb a new demand before they fail visibly. The immune response also changes, so fever may be blunted or absent even in severe infection, and pain is perceived and reported differently, so a patient with long-standing arthritis may not separate a new injury from the pain that is always there. The senses fade as well, so a hearing loss or a failing eye changes the history that can be taken and the examination that can be trusted. The age at which a person is counted old is conventionally placed at 65 years, and that line is an administrative convenience rather than a biological fact, so any such figure must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because services and studies draw it in different places. What matters at the bedside is not the birth date but how much reserve this particular patient has left.

Because the usual symptoms are unreliable, the clinician measures the older patient against the patient's own recent self, and function becomes the most informative vital sign. The questions that matter are practical: whether the patient could walk to the toilet unaided last month, cook a meal, manage the tablets, and climb the stairs, and whether any of that was lost, and over what period. A decline over days points to a new acute illness, while a decline that has crept over months points toward something chronic, progressive, or social. Dressing, washing, feeding, walking, and using the toilet are the ordinary acts that reveal reserve, and the clinician who records them has a baseline against which every later change can be judged. A patient whose function is stable is reassuring even when one number is abnormal, and a patient whose function has dropped is worrying even when every number looks acceptable.

Taking the history from an older patient is a skill that must be adapted rather than abandoned. The clinician sits at eye level, faces the patient in good light, speaks slowly and in a lower voice, and checks that the hearing aid is in place and working before deciding that an answer was confused. A relative or a neighbor who sees the patient daily is a legitimate witness, and the account should be gathered from both without setting one against the other. The Oldcarts framework still organizes the present illness, but the questions are asked in simpler language and repeated without impatience, because fatigue and deafness shorten attention. The review of systems must ask directly about falls, dizziness, incontinence, constipation, sleep, appetite, weight, mood, and memory, since each is often accepted as a normal part of age instead of a symptom.

Frailty is the clinical state that explains much of this, and it changes both the diagnosis and the decision. It is not the same as age and not the same as disability, but a state of reduced reserve in which a small insult produces a large collapse, so that a mild infection becomes a fall, a fall becomes a fracture, and a fracture becomes a permanent loss of independence. The frail patient tolerates illness badly, recovers slowly, and is at greater risk from the investigations and treatments offered than a robust patient of the same age, so recognizing frailty changes the threshold for admission, the intensity of treatment, and the honesty of the conversation about what is likely to happen. A walking speed, a grip, a weight that has fallen, a report of exhaustion, and a low level of activity are the observations from which the state is judged, and several scores have been built from them. No score replaces the clinician's eyes, and any figure attached to such a score is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the versions in use do not set the same line and were derived in populations that may not resemble the patient before you.

A fall is a symptom and never a diagnosis, and the reasoning that follows it asks what made this patient fall today. The clinician looks for a cardiac rhythm disturbance, a stroke, an injury or a painful joint, an infection or a low blood sugar, and a sedative or a medicine that lowers the pressure. The circumstances are more useful than the injury: what the patient was doing, whether there was warning, whether consciousness was lost, how long the patient lay on the floor, and whether anyone saw it. A fall with no warning and no memory of falling suggests a rhythm disturbance, while a trip over a rug suggests something about the environment, and the pressure is measured lying and then standing. A fall of 20 millimeters of mercury on standing is often taught as a meaningful drop, though that figure and the way it is measured must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the technique and the accepted line vary between services. A fracture may hide here without the pain and deformity a younger patient would show, and an unexplained inability to walk must be treated as a fracture until an image proves otherwise.

Confusion is the second great atypical presentation, and the first task is to separate a delirium from a dementia. A delirium is a disturbance of attention and awareness that develops over a short period, fluctuates through the day, and is caused by something else, whether an infection, a medicine, a metabolic upset, pain, or a change in surroundings. A dementia is a chronic and progressive decline in several areas of thinking that leaves attention relatively intact until late and does not fluctuate in the same way. The two are not alternatives, because a patient with dementia is the patient most likely to become delirious, and the delirium settles on top of the dementia like an acute illness on a chronic one. The most useful bedside test of delirium is attention, examined by asking the patient to repeat a short list of words, to count backward, or to name the days of the week in reverse. A confusion assumed to be dementia and left unexamined is one of the commonest ways a treatable illness in an older patient is missed, and a sudden worsening of a known dementia is a delirium until proved otherwise.

The search for the cause of a delirium is a systematic hunt and not a guess. Infection is the commonest trigger, and it may be in the chest, the urine, the skin, the abdomen, or a line in the vein, and it may produce no fever at all. Medicines come next, especially sedatives, medicines for the bladder, strong painkillers, steroids, and anything recently added or increased. Metabolic causes include a low blood sugar, a disturbed salt balance, a failing kidney or liver, a low oxygen level, and a thyroid disorder, and each leaves a trace that can be sought. The clinician should also consider alcohol or sedative withdrawal, a stroke, and an infection of the brain or its covering, and should look for a focal weakness, a headache, a stiff neck, or a seizure. The finding that must not be missed is a treatable cause, so an older patient with a new confusion deserves a full examination, a set of observations, and a review of the medicine list before any label is written.

Medicines are the third great theme of the older patient, because they produce symptoms that imitate disease and because the patient is usually taking several at once. The list a patient reports from memory is rarely complete, so the clinician asks to see every container, including those from another doctor, those bought without a prescription, and those stopped but never discarded. A single new symptom that appears within days or weeks of a new medicine is that medicine until proved otherwise, and the reasoning rests on the timing rather than on the shape of the symptom. Dizziness, drowsiness, confusion, a dry mouth, constipation, a fall, an irregular heartbeat, and a loss of bladder control are all common effects of ordinary medicines, and each is easily mistaken for a new disease. The clinician who reads the labels, counts the containers, and asks what each one is for will often find the answer in the bag.

When several medicines act together, the effects may be greater than any one of them alone, and the combination rather than the single drug becomes the cause. The clinician looks for two medicines that do the same thing, two that interact, and a medicine prescribed to treat the side effect of another, which is the pattern sometimes called a prescribing cascade. The decision to stop a medicine is itself a diagnostic test, and it is made by the clinician who knows the patient and the indication, because some medicines must never be stopped suddenly and withdrawal can produce its own illness. The finding that makes withdrawal of a suspected medicine right is a symptom that began with the drug and settles when it is removed, while the finding that makes it wrong is a strong indication that the medicine is protecting the patient from something worse. The reason for every change should be written down and explained to the patient and the family, because a list adjusted in silence will be restored at the next visit.

Dehydration and poor nutrition arrive quietly and produce a picture that is mistaken for disease. The sensation of thirst fades with age, so a patient who does not feel thirsty may still be short of water, especially during an illness, in hot weather, or when a diuretic or a laxative is being taken. The finding that suggests it is a dry mouth and tongue, a pulse that has risen, a pressure that falls on standing, a scanty and dark passage of urine, and a weight that has dropped without intent, though skin and eyes are less reliable in an old body than the textbook suggests. Poor intake has many causes, among them a painful mouth, bad teeth, difficulty swallowing, nausea, constipation, depression, a medicine, and a loss of smell and taste, and each must be looked for rather than assumed. Swallowing is examined directly, because a patient who coughs or changes voice after drinking is at risk of inhaling, which can lead to an infection of the lung. The decision about fluid is clinical rather than numerical, and the finding that makes it right is a dry patient who cannot take enough by mouth, while the finding that makes it wrong is a circulation already overloaded.

Sepsis in the older patient is the lethal cause that most often presents without its classic signs, and it deserves its own warning. A temperature of 38 degrees Celsius is traditionally taught as the line above which fever is present, and a temperature below 36 degrees Celsius is sometimes just as important here, but both figures are illustrations that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the accepted thresholds differ between countries, editions, and the sites at which temperature is measured. A breathing rate above 20 breaths per minute or a systolic pressure below 90 millimeters of mercury may be the only abnormality, and those numbers likewise belong to the local guideline rather than to this book and should be checked against the current protocol where the patient is seen. The change that matters most is often a new confusion or a new inability to stand, and the family may describe the patient as simply not himself. The clinician must move quickly, because an older patient with an infection can pass from vague illness to organ failure in a short time, and that is exactly why early treatment on suspicion is often the right decision. The finding that would make treatment the wrong answer is a clear and different cause that fully explains the picture, and even then the patient must be watched.

A myocardial infarction in an older patient may occur with no chest pain at all, so the possibility must stay in view in any sudden change of condition. The presentation may be a new breathlessness, a faint, a confusion, a weakness that came on suddenly, a feeling of indigestion, or a decline that nobody can explain, and the danger is that these are attributed to age or to a chest infection. The finding that reveals it is the ECG, taken early and repeated, together with the substances released by damaged heart muscle, whose reference ranges are set by the laboratory that measures them and must be read from the local report and the current guideline rather than from a textbook. Where there is no ECG, a sudden deterioration with sweating, a fall in the pressure, or a new irregularity of the pulse is a reason to arrange urgent transfer rather than to wait, because waiting for confirmation can cost the heart muscle that is still salvageable.

A subdural hematoma, which is blood collecting over the surface of the brain, is a danger of the older patient because the brain shrinks with age and the veins that cross the space become stretched and fragile, so a trivial injury can tear one. The patient may not recall any injury, and the bleeding may declare itself days or weeks later, so a clear history of trauma is not required to consider it. The presentation is typically a fluctuating confusion, a headache that is worse in the morning or on lying down, a slowing of the pulse, a weakness on one side, a difficulty with speech, or a drowsiness that deepens. The finding that confirms it is a CT of the head, and where that is not available the clinician should treat a fluctuating confusion after any fall as a possible bleed and arrange transfer. The patient must be asked about any blood-thinning medicine, including one bought without a prescription, because that changes both the probability and the urgency. A confusion that no cause explains should always return the clinician to the question of a head injury, however slight or distant it seemed.

Other lethal causes belong in the same early list, because each can present as a general decline rather than as a localized complaint. A clot in the lung may appear as sudden breathlessness, fainting, or a decline in exercise tolerance rather than as chest pain, and the finding that supports it is a swollen or tender calf on one side, although the leg may look normal. A tear in the aorta may present as pain that moves between the chest and the back, or as a sudden collapse, and the finding that suggests it is a difference in the pulses or the pressures between the two arms. An inability to pass urine may produce agitation and confusion rather than a complaint about the bladder, and the finding is a tender, distended lower abdomen. A stroke may present as a confusion, a fall, or a sudden neglect of one side of the body, and the finding is a weakness or a change in speech that was not there before.

The examination of an older patient must be adapted, because the standard sequence assumes a body that can cooperate and a room that is warm. The clinician ensures privacy, warmth, good light, and enough time, and asks the patient to undress only as far as the examination requires, because cold provokes shivering, raises the pressure, and makes the pulse harder to count. Hearing and vision are assessed early, since a patient who cannot see or hear will appear confused, uncooperative, or demented when the true problem is sensory. The pulse is counted for a full interval rather than guessed, the pressure is taken in both arms and then with the patient standing, and the breathing rate is counted while the patient is unaware of being watched. The older patient tires quickly, so the examination is performed in stages with rest between, and a gentle technique matters more here than elsewhere, because the skin tears, the bones break, and the joints hurt under a hand that has not adjusted. Certain regions must be examined in every older patient, among them the pressure areas over the heels, hips, spine, and shoulders, the feet for a wound that a patient with diabetes may not feel, the mouth for a dry tongue or an ill-fitting denture, and the abdomen for a full bladder or a loaded bowel.

In a hospital with imaging and a specialist team, the older patient still needs the reasoning before the tests, because the tests are chosen to answer the questions the examination has raised. A CT of the head answers the question of bleeding or stroke after a fall or a new confusion, an image of the hip or the spine answers the question of a fracture, and a chest radiograph answers the question of infection or fluid in the lung. An ECG answers the question of rhythm and injury, and blood tests answer the questions of infection, salts, kidney function, blood count, and thyroid, each of which may explain a decline. The specialist team in the care of older people adds an assessment of mobility, thinking, mood, continence, nutrition, and the home, and its members are often the ones who notice that the patient cannot return to the place he came from without help. The ward environment itself can produce a delirium, so sleep, light, noise, orientation, hydration, and the presence of a familiar person are part of the treatment as much as any medicine.

At home, or in a clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, the reasoning is the same, and the house becomes part of the examination. The carpet that slides, the loose stair rod, the low chair that the patient cannot rise from, the poor light on the way to the toilet, the distance to the bathroom at night, and the pill organizer filled by several different hands are clinical findings rather than domestic details. The temperature is taken with a thermometer and recorded together with the route used, the pulse and breathing are counted with a watch, and the torch makes the throat, the ears, the mouth, the feet, and the skin visible. Where a cuff is available, the pressure is measured sitting and standing, and a walking test along the corridor or the yard, with the patient observed and asked how it feels, is one of the most informative examinations in medicine. The decision that follows is often not a diagnosis but a plan about whether the patient can be safely watched at home, and the finding that makes that decision wrong is an inability to eat, drink, walk, or be safe alone.

The capacity of the older patient to make decisions deserves an explicit assessment rather than an assumption drawn from age or from a diagnosis of dementia. Capacity is specific to the decision at hand, so a patient may be able to choose what to eat and not to consent to an operation, and it is judged by whether the patient understands the information, retains it, weighs it, and can communicate a choice. Communication must be made as easy as possible before capacity is judged, with the hearing aid, the glasses, an interpreter, a quiet room, and enough time, because a failure to communicate is not the same as an inability to decide. The clinician should ask what matters to the patient, what the patient expects from treatment, and what the patient would not want, and should record those answers for the next clinician. The family may speak for a patient who cannot, but the clinician must check that the family is expressing the patient's wishes rather than its own. These conversations are part of diagnosis, because the goals they reveal change which investigations and treatments are worth pursuing.

A constructed illustration may make these habits concrete, and it is offered as a teaching device rather than as a portrait of any real person. Imagine an older woman brought to a clinic because she has stopped eating and has become vague over a few days, and whose family says she was walking and cooking a week ago. She is not hot to the touch, her breathing is a little quick, and she cannot say where she is though she knows her name. The clinician does not settle for the word confusion, and finds instead a small wound on the shin that the family had not noticed and a passage of urine that has become small and dark. The wound is the likely source, the dehydration is a treatable burden on top of it, and the record now names the baseline function against which the next visit can be judged. Nothing here is real, and the story should not be applied to any living patient without a full assessment.

The older patient who is sent home must leave with a plan written in ordinary language and understood by the patient and the family. The clinician should say what has been found, what remains uncertain, what has been changed in the medicines and why, and which signs should bring the patient back sooner, among them a new confusion, a fall, a fever, a refusal to drink, a breathlessness, or a return of something the patient had recovered from. A visit arranged in advance is worth more than a promise to return if things worsen, because the patient and the family may not judge a slow decline as a reason to call. The record should show the baseline function, the medicine list as actually taken, and the plan for review, so that the next clinician inherits a story rather than a set of numbers. Where the patient's goals are comfort rather than cure, that decision belongs in the same record and should be honored.

To summarize, the older patient most often presents through a change in function rather than a classic symptom, so a fall, a confusion, a loss of appetite, and a decline in what the patient could formerly do are treated as the beginning of the reasoning and not the whole of it. The lethal causes are named at the bedside and matched to the finding that reveals them: sepsis without fever, a hip fracture that does not hurt, a bleed over the brain after an injury nobody remembers, a myocardial infarction without chest pain, a medicine that has become the disease, and dehydration that has gone unnoticed. Frailty changes both the diagnosis and the decision, delirium must be separated from the dementia it so often accompanies, and every medicine the patient actually takes is part of the assessment. Every score, cut-off, pressure, temperature, rate, and reference range in this chapter is an illustration of reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between countries, editions, and laboratories. This is educational material and does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service. The same reasoning works in the hospital with its imaging and its specialist team and in the home where the carpet, the stairs, and the pill organizer are examined alongside the patient.
