# Chapter 23 — Dyspnea and Cough

Breathlessness and cough are taught apart and met together, because a patient who is short of breath usually coughs and a patient who has coughed for weeks usually notices that he can no longer do what he used to do. The pairing is not a convenience of the syllabus, since the two symptoms arise from the same apparatus and often from the same disease, and a cough that has changed may be the only early evidence of a lung that is losing function. This chapter organizes them around four questions rather than around a list of diseases: what the sensation of breathlessness actually is, how its pace narrows the possibilities, how the character of a cough separates the causes, and which findings at the bedside identify the patient who cannot wait. This chapter is educational material and cannot take the place of training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of the service in which you work. What follows is reasoning to be practiced under supervision, never a set of instructions to be applied alone.

Certain presentations are dangerous enough to be named before the reasoning that surrounds them, and each is recognized by a specific finding. The patient who cannot finish a sentence and who sits upright using the muscles of the neck is in ventilatory difficulty now, and if he is also blue, drowsy, or exhausted then failure is already advanced. The patient with a silent chest is in greater danger still, because a chest that has stopped making sound during a severe attack may mean airflow too low to generate any, and the examiner who mistakes that silence for improvement has made a lethal error. A new swelling of one leg in a breathless patient points to a clot in the deep veins and a clot that has traveled to the lungs, and it is found only when the patient is undressed and both calves are compared. Coughing blood is never trivial, and it demands an explanation that includes tuberculosis, a tumor, an infection, and a clot in the lung until one of them has been excluded by the evidence rather than by hope.

Breathlessness is a sensation rather than a measurement, and it is produced by a mismatch rather than by a single sensor. The brain issues a command to breathe and receives back information about how the chest and lungs responded, and when the effort demanded and the movement achieved do not match, the patient feels short of breath. Receptors in the airways, the lung tissue, the chest wall, and the muscles of respiration all contribute, together with the chemical drive from carbon dioxide and oxygen, which is why the sensation is compounded of effort, tightness, and the feeling of not getting enough air. The practical consequence is that breathlessness and oxygen level are not the same thing, so a patient may be deeply breathless with a normal oxygen saturation while another whose carbon dioxide has risen slowly over months feels almost nothing. Anxiety, fever, anemia, and poor fitness each lower the threshold at which ordinary activity produces the sensation, and none of those is imaginary. The examiner therefore records both what the patient feels and what the body shows, and treats a disagreement between the two as information rather than as error.

The history of breathlessness is built from comparison rather than from description, because the words patients choose for the sensation vary enormously while pointing at the same thing. The useful questions are about what the patient could do before and what he can do now: how far he walks on the flat, how many stairs he climbs before stopping, whether he can carry shopping, and whether he has quietly given up something he used to enjoy. A change in exercise tolerance that the patient noticed and explained away is often the most important fact in the room, and it should be dated as precisely as he can manage. The pace of onset sorts the possibilities more sharply than any other single feature, because a collapse in the doorway and a decline across a season admit very different explanations. What the patient believes is wrong, what he has already taken, and what he fears are part of the history too, because they shape what he will accept and what he will conceal. The clinician who asks about function will learn more than the one who asks the patient to grade his breathlessness on a scale.

Two questions deserve their own place because they move the ranking so decisively. Orthopnea is breathlessness that appears when the patient lies flat and eases when he sits up, and it is often reported not as a symptom but as a habit, in the pillows that have been added over the months and the chair he now sleeps in. Paroxysmal nocturnal breathlessness is the patient waking after a period of sleep with a terrifying sense of suffocation, sitting up, and taking many minutes to recover, and it is among the most specific symptoms of a failing left ventricle. Both arise from a shared mechanism, in which lying flat returns fluid from the legs and the abdomen to the chest and a heart that cannot keep up allows it to collect in the lungs. Neither is exclusive to heart failure, since severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, obesity, an airway that collapses during sleep, and reflux can each produce a version of the story. The clinician therefore asks how the patient sleeps and not merely whether he sleeps, and listens for the pattern of the change rather than for a single detail.

Cough is first divided by how long it has been present, because duration moves the list of causes more than any other feature. A cough of less than 3 weeks is usually an infection of the upper or lower airway, and most of those settle without any diagnosis being made. A cough that lasts between 3 weeks and 8 weeks is called subacute, and its common explanations are a lingering airway infection, a cough that persists after the infection has gone, and a cough driven by the upper airway or by reflux. A cough beyond 8 weeks is chronic, and it brings in asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, a drug effect, an allergy, and tuberculosis, along with a tumor in a patient whose story includes weight loss or blood. Those boundaries are a teaching convention and not a natural law, so confirm the intervals against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the divisions between acute, subacute, and chronic are drawn differently in different countries and revised between editions. What matters more than the number of weeks is whether the cough has changed, because a cough stable for years that is suddenly different deserves the same attention as a new one.

The character of the cough is the second sorting tool, and it is gathered by watching the patient cough rather than by asking him to describe the sound. A dry cough suggests irritation of the airway, an allergic trigger, a drug, or the early phase of a viral illness, while a productive cough suggests infection, chronic bronchitis, or a cavity in the lung. The sputum is examined for color, quantity, and blood, and a change from clear to yellow, green, or rusty is a fact that can be followed over time even where no laboratory exists. The sound carries information as well, since a barking cough, a whooping cough in a child, a brassy cough that suggests pressure on the windpipe, and a cough that ends in a vomit or a faint each have a short list of their own. Timing is as useful as sound, so the clinician asks about cough at night, on waking, after meals, on exertion, in cold air, and on lying down. The company the cough keeps matters too, because fever, weight loss, night sweats, chest pain, or a swelling in the neck turns a common symptom into a different problem.

Asthma is the first explanation to consider when breathlessness and cough come and go, and its defining feature is variability rather than severity. The symptoms are episodic, often worse at night and in the early morning, and they are provoked by cold air, exercise, dust, smoke, pollen, and infection in a patient who often has an allergic history. Between episodes the chest may sound entirely normal, which is why a quiet examination does not remove the diagnosis and why the history must be trusted over a single listening. During an attack the examiner finds a widespread wheeze heard on expiration, a prolonged expiratory phase, and a patient who is working hard to move air. The paradox of the silent chest belongs here, because when the attack becomes severe enough that airflow falls very low the wheeze disappears and the chest goes quiet, so a chest that has fallen silent in a deteriorating asthmatic is a sign of impending respiratory arrest rather than of recovery. A response to treatment that is repeated each time it is given, with the variability returning when treatment stops, is the pattern that supports the diagnosis, and the decision to start and to stop treatment should rest on that pattern rather than on one measurement.

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease presents the opposite shape, with breathlessness that is persistent and slowly progressive rather than episodic. The history usually contains many years of tobacco smoke, or of smoke from cooking and heating fires in a poorly ventilated room, and the cough is productive and chronic. The examiner sees a patient who may be barrel-chested, who breathes through pursed lips, who uses the muscles of the neck and the spaces between the ribs, and whose expiration is prolonged and quiet. Air entry is reduced everywhere, and added sounds such as wheeze or crackles come and go with exacerbations rather than defining the condition. The practical difficulty is that a patient with this disease can also develop pneumonia, a collapsed lung, a pneumothorax, or heart failure, and a sudden change in a previously stable pattern is a new problem until it is proved otherwise. The distinction from asthma rests on the history of variability, the age of onset, the exposure history, and the response to treatment over time, and no single finding at the bedside settles it alone.

Pneumonia is the most common acute explanation of fever with cough and breathlessness, and it is recognized by a pattern of focal signs. The patient is often flushed and sweating, the breathing is rapid and shallow, and the affected side of the chest moves less than the other. On the sick side the examiner finds crackles, a dull note on percussion, a change in the transmitted voice sounds, and bronchial breathing when the lung is consolidated. A temperature of 38 degrees Celsius or higher supports the diagnosis in a younger patient, but that figure is a teaching illustration and must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, since fever is defined differently in different countries, revised between editions, and measured differently by every laboratory. The older patient is the trap, because pneumonia there may present with confusion, a fall, or a general decline and almost no fever or cough, while the chest may be quiet rather than noisy. A patient who is breathing rapidly, whose oxygen saturation has fallen below 90 per cent, or who looks exhausted should be treated as seriously ill while the cause is being confirmed, and that saturation is an illustration as well, so establish the number your service acts on from the current guideline and the local protocol.

Disease of the pleura changes the examination in a way that is easy to recognize and easy to reason from. When fluid collects between the lung and the chest wall, the note over it is dull, the breath sounds are reduced or absent, the voice sounds are reduced, and the trachea and heart may be pushed away from the affected side if the collection is large. When air collects instead, as in a pneumothorax, the note is hyperresonant, the breath sounds are absent, and a large collection under pressure pushes the trachea and the heart toward the opposite side while the patient becomes shocked. The distinction between an effusion and consolidation is made at the bedside by the voice sounds, which are increased over solid lung and decreased over fluid, and by the character of the breath sounds rather than by their mere presence. A patient with sudden pleural pain and breathlessness, especially a tall thin young man or someone with known lung disease, must be examined for pneumothorax before any other explanation is accepted. Where no radiograph exists, a hyperresonant silent side of the chest with a displaced trachea in a deteriorating patient is enough to act on.

A clot that has traveled to the lungs is the diagnosis that most often hides behind a normal examination, and it must be considered in every patient with unexplained breathlessness. The presentation ranges from a sudden breathlessness with a clear chest to a collapse with a falling pressure, distended neck veins, and a heart beating very fast. Pleuritic pain, a cough, and blood in the sputum are common, and a fainting episode may be the first event. The finding that reveals it is often in the leg rather than the chest, namely a new unilateral swelling, warmth, or tenderness over the deep veins, which is why both legs are examined in every breathless patient. A heart rate greater than 110 beats per minute and a systolic pressure less than 90 millimeters of mercury mark a large clot and a patient who needs help immediately, and those two figures are illustrations of the reasoning, so establish the values your own service treats as critical from the current guideline and the local protocol, because the thresholds differ between countries and editions and are set locally by each service and laboratory. A normal electrocardiogram, or ECG, and a normal chest radiograph do not exclude it, and treating either as reassurance is a recognized cause of preventable death.

Heart failure explains breathlessness, cough, and a wheeze that is easily mistaken for asthma, and the discriminating findings are those of a circulation that is congested. The patient describes orthopnea and waking at night gasping, and reports swelling of the ankles, weight gain, and a need to sleep propped up. The examiner finds a raised jugular venous pressure, a third heart sound that suggests a failing ventricle, crackles at both lung bases that do not clear with coughing, and swelling of the ankles or the sacrum. The apex beat may be displaced, the pulse may be irregular, and the liver may be enlarged and tender. A cough that is worse on lying down and a wheeze that accompanies it are common, which is why heart failure is one of the classic imitators of lung disease and why the jugular venous pressure must be looked for rather than assumed. Where a laboratory exists, a blood test for the peptide released by a stretched heart muscle helps to separate the two, and the cut-off used for that test is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the value depends on age, kidney function, and the assay the laboratory uses.

Tuberculosis is the chronic infection the clinician must keep in view, because it is common in many settings this book serves and because its early symptoms are ordinary. The cough has usually lasted more than 3 weeks, it may be dry at first and productive later, and it is accompanied by blood in the sputum in a substantial minority. Night sweats, fever, loss of appetite, and weight loss of more than 5 kilograms complete the picture, and there may be a history of contact with someone already treated for the disease. The chest may sound normal, which is why the history and not the stethoscope is the instrument that raises suspicion. A patient infected with the human immunodeficiency virus, or HIV, is at much higher risk, and the combination of that infection with a chronic cough and weight loss should lower the threshold for investigation sharply. Where a laboratory exists, sputum is examined for the organism; where none exists, the same combination of history and findings justifies referral, because investigation is decided on the clinical picture while treatment is decided on the confirmed result. The interval of 3 weeks and the weight of 5 kilograms are illustrations of the reasoning, so confirm both against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the thresholds that trigger investigation differ between countries and editions and between programs.

Assembling these explanations into a ranked list is the central work of the chapter, and it is ordered twice, once by likelihood and once by how much harm a delay would do. The pace of the illness does most of the sorting, and the boundaries between an abrupt onset, a rapid course, and a gradual decline are conventions that must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, because they are drawn differently in different countries and revised between editions. An abrupt onset suggests a clot in the lung, a pneumothorax, a foreign body, an allergic reaction, or sudden flooding of the lungs, and each of those is an emergency. A rapid course suggests pneumonia, an exacerbation of asthma or of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, heart failure, or a large pleural effusion. A gradual decline suggests anemia, a tumor, tuberculosis, a slowly enlarging effusion, or a neuromuscular cause, and the same long time course applies to a cough that has never settled. The setting adjusts the order again, because a young person in a rural clinic and an older person in a referral hospital do not carry the same probabilities. The ranking should be written down so that the next clinician can see what was considered and what was set aside.

Examination of the breathless patient begins before the stethoscope is lifted, because the work of breathing is visible from the door. The examiner notes whether the patient is comfortable, sitting up, or leaning forward with the arms braced, and whether he is using the muscles of the neck, the spaces between the ribs, or the abdomen to breathe. The rate is counted rather than estimated, because a rising respiratory rate is often the earliest sign that a patient is deteriorating, and the rhythm, depth, and symmetry of the movement are noted as well. The lips, tongue, and nail beds are inspected for a blue tinge, the neck veins are examined for a raised pressure, and both legs are compared for swelling. The patient is asked to speak a full sentence, and the length of that sentence is among the most useful observations in the whole encounter. Only then is the chest examined, with the examiner listening to both sides in a fixed order and describing what is heard rather than what is expected.

The chest examination is directed at four questions: whether air is moving, whether it is moving equally on both sides, whether there are added sounds, and whether the signs point to consolidation, fluid, or collapse. Air entry is compared side to side at the same level, because the difference between the two sides carries more information than the absolute quality on either. Wheezes are widespread and musical and suggest narrowed airways, crackles are discontinuous and suggest fluid or inflammation in the small airways and air sacs, and a pleural rub is a grating sound that suggests inflamed pleural surfaces. Percussion maps dullness and hyperresonance, and the voice sounds tell whether the lung beneath is solid or compressed. Where a peak flow meter or a pulse oximeter exists, the measurement adds an objective number that can be followed over time, and where neither exists the counted respiratory rate and the patient's ability to speak are the equivalents. No single one of these findings is decisive alone, and the pattern they form together is what moves the ranking.

It is as important to know what the examination can exclude as what it can confirm, because overconfidence at the bedside costs lives. A chest that sounds clear does not exclude a clot in the lung, an early pneumonia, or a small pneumothorax, and a normal oxygen saturation does not exclude serious disease. A normal chest examination in a patient with a long smoking history and a changed cough does not exclude a tumor, and a normal examination in a patient with fever and breathlessness does not exclude pneumonia in its first hours. What a careful examination can do is identify the patient who is working hard to breathe, the one with focal signs that demand a radiograph, the one with a swollen leg, and the one whose oxygen is falling despite treatment. Those findings change the pace and the place of care even when the diagnosis is still uncertain. The examination is therefore used to sort urgency first and to name a disease second, and a patient who is deteriorating is treated before the label is settled.

Where a radiograph is available, the examination becomes a set of questions that the image is asked to answer. The clinician predicts what the radiograph should show before it is taken, so that an unexpected finding is recognized as a surprise rather than accepted without thought. A plain chest radiograph separates consolidation, a pleural effusion, a pneumothorax, a collapsed lung, a mass, and an enlarged heart, and it is the single most useful image in this presentation. An electrocardiogram is recorded in almost every patient with acute breathlessness, not because it diagnoses the lung but because it identifies the rhythm and the strain on the heart that change the ranking. Imaging of the veins of the legs, an ultrasound of the heart, and a computed tomography, or CT, scan of the arteries of the lungs answer specific questions that the history has already raised. A normal radiograph in a breathless patient is not a normal patient, and it should send the clinician back to the history and to the leg examination rather than to a discharge letter.

In a clinic with a stethoscope, a thermometer, and a torch, the same reasoning is performed with fewer inputs and with a greater reliance on repeated observation. The thermometer turns a vague impression of fever into a number that can be followed, the stethoscope supplies the four questions of the chest examination, and the torch allows the mouth, the throat, and the nail beds to be examined properly. A watch allows the respiratory rate and the pulse to be counted rather than guessed, which is often the most valuable information available. The decision in this setting is rarely a final diagnosis, since it is whether the patient can be watched safely where he is, whether treatment can begin on the clinical picture, and whether a journey must start and how urgently. Where a trial of treatment is used as a diagnostic test, the clinician must decide in advance what improvement would confirm the diagnosis and what failure would overturn it, and must arrange a review at a stated point rather than leaving the patient to return on his own judgment. The examination here cannot exclude a clot in the lung, a small pneumothorax, a tumor, or early tuberculosis, and that honest limit is itself a finding that shapes the plan.

A constructed illustration may make the sorting visible, and neither of the patients in it is real. Imagine a young man who has had episodes of wheeze and dry cough since childhood, worse at night and after running in cold air, whose chest is quiet between episodes and who improves when the pattern is recognized and treated as asthma. Now imagine a woman in middle age who becomes breathless across a single day, whose chest sounds clear, whose heart is fast, and whose left calf is swollen and tender. The first patient is separated from the second not by the loudness of the wheeze but by the pace of the illness, the triggers, and the state of the leg, and the second patient's clear chest is the finding that makes her situation more dangerous rather than less. A third constructed patient, an older man with a cough for months, weight loss, and blood in the sputum, is included to show how a story that sounds chronic can still demand urgent investigation. These illustrations exist only to fix a habit of thought, and real patients are more variable than any of them.

To summarize, breathlessness and cough are read together, first through the sensation and its mechanism, then through the pace of the illness, then through the character of the cough, and finally through the findings that separate a patient who can wait from one who cannot. The dangerous presentations are named where the symptom is described rather than deferred, among them the patient who cannot finish a sentence, the silent chest in a severe attack, the new unilateral leg swelling that points to a clot in the lung, and the patient coughing blood, each with the finding that reveals it. Asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, pneumonia, pleural disease, pulmonary embolism, heart failure, and tuberculosis are carried as competing explanations and separated by the pattern of history and examination rather than by any single sign. Every number in this chapter, whether a temperature, a rate, a pressure, a saturation, an interval, or a weight, is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between countries, between editions, and between laboratories. A patient who is working hard to breathe is treated while the diagnosis is still being assembled, because no label is worth the delay of someone who is getting worse.
