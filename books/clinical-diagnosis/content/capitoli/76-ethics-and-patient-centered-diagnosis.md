# Chapter 76 — Ethics and Patient-Centered Diagnosis

A diagnosis lands on a person's life as much as on a disease, and the two are never separated in the room where the news is given. This book reasons from findings and probabilities, but those findings come from someone with a body, a family, a language, a fear, and a right to be told what is happening. This chapter places that person at the center and asks three questions of every consultation: what does this patient understand, what has this patient agreed to, and what does this patient want done. The subject is not politeness added to diagnosis, but the same reasoning carried out with the patient as a participant rather than a specimen. This is educational material, and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of the service in which the patient is seen.

Several dangers belong at the beginning, because each grows out of how a diagnosis is conducted rather than out of the disease itself. The first is the treatable illness missed because the consultation closed politely and quickly, before the patient's real concern was spoken aloud. The finding that reveals it is the answer to one open question about what the patient feared most, together with a vital sign that was never repeated. The second danger is the error known and not disclosed, since a decision made on a hidden mistake rests on false information. The third is the test accepted without understanding, which leaves the patient unable to recognize the harm it causes. Each is exposed by something that costs nothing: an open question, a pause, and a note that records the reasoning.

Patient-centered diagnosis is a method rather than a manner, and it can be described as moves another clinician could repeat. The clinician asks for the patient's agenda before imposing a structure, listens for what the symptom means to this person, explains the reasoning in words the patient can hold, and then checks that the explanation has landed. Every patient also arrives with an explanation of what has happened, and that explanation belongs in the history rather than standing in its way. The patient may blame a fall, a curse, a food, a stress, a medicine, or a spiritual cause, and each belief shapes what is reported and withheld. Asking what the patient thinks is wrong, and what the patient fears, is a diagnostic act with a real yield.

The consultation opens with an invitation rather than an interrogation, and the first minute belongs to the patient. A question such as what made you come today, asked without a list attached, lets the patient begin where the alarm is greatest. The clinician lets the story run and notes the structure to be imposed later, because the Oldcarts framework is a discipline applied after the account has been heard. Silence is an instrument here, since the sentence added after a pause often carries the diagnosis. The clinician who cannot tolerate a pause collects the ordinary parts of the story and loses the dangerous one.

A patient can look well and be dying, and the ethics of diagnosis include the duty to look again when the appearance does not match the numbers. The febrile patient who has sat upright all night, the patient whose breathing rate has climbed without complaint, and the older patient who has grown quiet and stopped eating may all be released too early. The finding that reveals each is a set of vital signs taken together, repeated after an interval, and compared with what they were before. The clinician who notices the mismatch owes the patient an explanation of why the plan is changing, because a patient who does not understand may refuse and leave. Naming the dangerous possibility aloud, to the patient and to the next clinician, is part of treating the patient.

The examination is an intrusion the patient permits, and its manner teaches the patient whether to trust the clinician with the body as well as the history. The clinician says what is about to be done before touching, asks permission at each step, and keeps the patient covered except for the part being examined. A chaperone should be offered where the examination may feel exposing, and the order of the examination should be explained, because a patient who knows what comes next can relax against it. Teaching uses the same body as a place of learning, and that use requires a consent of its own. A patient who agrees to be seen has not agreed to be examined by a student or watched by learners, and a patient who refuses loses nothing in care.

Consent is a conversation that ends in a decision, not a signature that ends a conversation, and the form only records that the conversation happened. For consent to be real, the patient must understand and weigh the information, must be given what a reasonable person would want to know, and must be free to refuse without punishment or abandonment. The information that matters is the purpose of the test, what it involves, what it may find, what would happen if nothing were done, and what the alternatives are. The clinician should describe the uncertainty honestly, including the chance that a test settles nothing and leads only to another test. Consent taken as a formality produces a patient who has signed and has not agreed.

Capacity is the ability to make this decision at this moment, and it is assumed rather than withdrawn until there is reason to question it. The clinician tests it by asking the patient to say back what has been explained, to weigh the options in the patient's own terms, and to show that a choice follows from reasons the patient can give. A patient who refuses treatment the clinician believes is necessary has not thereby proved incapacity, and a patient who agrees with everything has not thereby proved capacity. Capacity can fluctuate with delirium, with pain, with exhaustion, and with a medicine given shortly before, so the assessment may need to be repeated. Where capacity is absent, the decision passes to the person whom the law and the local protocol designate, and that framework must be confirmed against the law of the place of practice.

The patient may also refuse, and the clinician's duty then shifts from persuading to protecting. A refusal is a decision to be understood rather than a problem to be overcome, so the clinician asks what the patient fears, corrects any misunderstanding, and records what was offered and what was decided. A patient with capacity who refuses admission for a condition that may kill has the right to do so, and the clinician's obligation is to make that refusal informed and to arrange the best possible follow-up rather than abandon the patient at the door. Safety-netting must then be explicit: the signs that should bring the patient back, the person who will check, and the time by which the check will happen. Any such interval must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because expectations differ between services and editions. A refusal is not a discharge, and the patient who refuses remains the clinician's patient.

Some consent is implicit in the arrangement, and the clinician should tell the difference between what the setting implies and what must be asked in words. A patient who joins a queue at a clinic has agreed to be seen, and a patient who rolls up a sleeve has agreed to have a pressure taken, but neither has agreed to an examination of the body. A queue is a place where patients are often examined in view of others, and the fact that everyone else is examined the same way is not a reason to skip the question. A brief sentence, spoken quietly, asking whether the clinician may look and naming what will be looked at, turns a public examination into a permitted one. Explicit consent belongs to everything invasive and everything uncertain, and a lumbar puncture, a biopsy, a sedated procedure, a photograph, a recording, or the storage of tissue for research each require their own conversation.

A test is an intervention, and ordering one is a clinical decision as serious as any other. Every investigation has a purpose, a burden, a chance of being wrong in either direction, and a chance of finding something that was never asked about. The clinician should be able to say what would be done differently for each possible result. An incidental finding can set off a chain of further tests whose harms may exceed the illness being sought, so the conversation before the test should mention the possibility of the unexpected result. A test ordered because it is available, because the clinician is uneasy, or because the patient expects something to be done is a test whose result is unlikely to be used well.

Telling a patient the diagnosis and explaining it are different acts, and both are owed. Telling is the short sentence that names the condition in words the patient already knows or can learn, with nothing hidden behind a phrase only a clinician understands. Explaining connects the name to what the patient will experience, what is known and unknown about the cause, what the options are, and what happens next. The order matters, because news delivered before the patient has somewhere to put it will not be retained, and a flood of detail delivered with the news is heard as noise. The clinician should therefore tell, stop, and let the patient respond before explaining, since the response shows how much can be carried at this visit.

The setting in which news is given is part of the news itself, because a patient told in a corridor hears the words as a verdict delivered in passing. A short warning sentence before the news allows the patient a moment to prepare, and the pause that follows belongs to the patient rather than to the clinician's need to fill it. The diagnosis should be repeated at the end of the visit and again at the next, because shock interferes with memory. Asking the patient to say the plan back in their own words is worth more than asking whether everything is clear. Where the diagnosis is uncertain, that uncertainty is part of the news and is better told plainly than buried under a comfortable guess.

Language is not a detail of the encounter but the encounter itself, and a diagnosis delivered through a relative who edits it is a diagnosis the patient may not receive at all. The clinician should use an interpreter where one is available and should speak to the patient rather than about the patient, because a person described in the third person becomes an object in their own consultation. A family's request to keep the diagnosis from the patient is common and should be handled as a question about the patient's own preference, since many patients said to be too fragile to know already know or wish to be told. Confidentiality within a family is not one rule but a series of decisions, and the patient's consent decides who may be told what. A relative who asks for information about a patient who has not agreed to share it should receive an explanation rather than the information itself.

Honesty and hope are not opposites, and the clinician who withholds the truth to protect hope usually damages both. Hope is best understood as a direction rather than a forecast, since hope for comfort, for time, for a particular event, or for a dignified death remains available even when a cure does not. The clinician therefore tells what is known, states plainly what is not known, and then turns to what can still be done, which is never nothing at all. A promise not to abandon the patient is the sentence most often remembered from a difficult consultation. The patient also has the right not to be told, and that right is respected without abandoning the duty of care, though the limits appear where the patient must know something in order to consent or to act.

Shared decision-making belongs where the reasonable options are genuinely balanced and the choice turns on what the patient values. When one option is clearly better on the evidence, the clinician owes the patient a recommendation rather than a menu, and offering a false balance abandons the patient to a decision the clinician is better placed to frame. When the options are balanced, the clinician lays out what each offers and what each costs in terms the patient lives with, including work, family, sleep, pain, travel, and the chance of being present for a particular event. The patient's values then decide, and the clinician's task is to make sure those values are informed rather than to make the choice instead of the patient. Recording who was present, what was explained, and what the patient chose turns a conversation into a plan.

Uncertainty is the ordinary condition of diagnosis, and admitting it is a skill rather than a confession. A clinician who says plainly that the evidence does not yet separate the possibilities, and then states what will be done to narrow them, gives the patient something solid to hold. A confident answer that later collapses destroys the trust that honesty would have protected. A working diagnosis should be named as working, and its replacement should be explained when the evidence moves rather than quietly abandoned. Uncertainty that is communicated becomes a shared plan, while uncertainty that is hidden becomes a private worry for the clinician and a surprise for the patient.

Diagnostic error is not an unusual event but a predictable feature of a process that depends on incomplete information, limited time, and human attention. Errors arise from a missed finding, a wrong weighting of evidence, an interrupted handover, a test that was never followed up, or a shortcut in reasoning that closed the question too early. The clinician's own mind contributes through the biases that make a first impression sticky, and the system contributes through fatigue, understaffing, and a queue that allows no second look. Naming the source honestly matters, because a purely personal account produces shame and concealment while a purely systemic account produces an excuse. Both are true at once, and both must be addressed if the same error is not to reach the next patient.

When an error is recognized, the duty to disclose it is owed to the patient, and it does not depend on whether the patient would otherwise find out. Disclosure means telling the patient in plain words what happened, what is known about the consequences, what will be done to correct the situation, and what the patient can expect next. The conversation should include what changes in the patient's care as a result, because a disclosure that ends with nothing is heard as an apology for a problem that is still present. An apology is owed as well, and it names the harm, accepts responsibility for the part that belongs to the clinician or the service, and expresses regret. Honesty after an error is an obligation rather than a technique, and it is owed whether or not it repairs the relationship.

The duty created by an error reaches past the patient who suffered it, because the next patient has not yet been harmed and can still be protected. The clinician records what happened in terms that make the system visible, reports it through the channel the service provides, and takes part in the review that asks how the same error could happen again. A review that ends with a name and a warning accomplishes little, while a review that ends with a change in the handover, the follow-up, or the way results are tracked protects patients who will never know it. The record itself is an ethical instrument rather than a diary of impressions, and it should show what was found, what was considered and set aside, what was explained, what the patient chose, and the plan for the next review. Statements that judge the patient's character or record a colleague's error in a way that invites blame should not enter the record, because the record follows the patient and may be read by the patient.

Confidentiality protects more than privacy, because it protects the patient's willingness to return and to speak. A diagnosis of infection with the human immunodeficiency virus, known as HIV, of a mental illness, of a sexually transmitted infection, or of an addiction carries a social cost the clinician must anticipate. The corridor, the shared waiting area, and the open ward are places where that cost is imposed, so the clinician should ask before speaking in front of a relative and should avoid naming a diagnosis where others may hear. An infectious disease that must be reported, a partner who may be at risk, or a driver whose condition may endanger others raises a conflict between confidentiality and the protection of third parties. The clinician should not resolve that conflict alone and silently, and the requirement differs between countries and must be confirmed against the current law and the local protocol.

A consultation with neither time nor privacy is not excused from the ethical essentials, and the essentials are fewer than they appear. The patient must be greeted and given the chance to say what brought them, must be told what is being done and asked before it is done, must be told what has been found and what the plan is, and must be told what should bring them back. A short consultation can still have a structure, in which the clinician sits if there is a seat, opens with an invitation, and asks permission before touching. After the examination, the clinician says what has been found, what remains uncertain, and which symptoms should bring the patient back sooner, and then asks the patient to repeat the plan. The clinician who preserves these essentials in the hardest setting is practicing the same medicine as the clinician in the private room.

A constructed illustration may make the contrast concrete, and it is offered as a teaching device rather than as a portrait of any real person. Imagine a hospital clinic where a man is told that the shadow on his chest radiograph is a tumor, in a room with a door, a chair for his wife, and a clinician who has put the telephone aside. The clinician gives the warning sentence, waits, names the diagnosis in plain words, and then explains what is known and what will be tested. Now imagine the same conversation in a district clinic whose waiting area is a bench under a tree, where the clinician calls the patient to the far end of the veranda and sits beside him rather than across from him. The news is given in the same order, shortened to what the patient can hold, and the warning sentence, the pause, the name of the illness, and the promise not to abandon him are all preserved. Both patients are invented, and the point of placing them together is that the ethical content of the two consultations is the same while the resources are not.

Children require a different arrangement, since the decision usually rests with a parent while the child remains a person with a view. The clinician should explain at the child's level, should ask the child's assent before an examination or a test where the child can give it, and should treat distress as information rather than as an obstacle. A patient who cannot speak, hear, see, or remember is owed more effort rather than less, because the usual route to the history is closed. The clinician should use whatever channel remains, whether writing, gesture, a familiar person, or a quiet room where speech can be heard, and a patient with aphasia should be given simple questions answerable with yes or no. The age at which a young person may consent alone differs between jurisdictions and must be confirmed against the law and the protocol of the place of practice.

A test that will not change comfort, that requires a journey the patient cannot make, or that consumes time the patient could spend with family should not be ordered simply because it exists. The clinician should explain why the question is being closed and what will take its place, because a patient who hears only that nothing more will be done may hear abandonment. A diagnosis is transferred between clinicians many times, and each transfer is an ethical moment as well as a clinical one. The clinician who hands over a patient should say what is known, what is suspected, what has been excluded, what is awaited, and what would change the plan, because a handover that lists only the diagnosis transfers the conclusion and loses the reasoning. A result that is awaited must be tracked by a named person, because the result nobody owns is the one that is missed.

Safety-netting belongs to the same duty, and the patient should be told which symptoms would mean the working diagnosis was wrong or the illness had changed, when to return, whom to contact, and what to do if that person cannot be reached. The clinician should check that the patient can repeat the plan and has the means to act on it, which may include money for travel or a telephone that works. Where the risk is high the net should be tighter, with an interval confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because services set their expectations differently. Treating a colleague, a friend, or a relative brings pressures that damage judgment, and the clinician may skip the examination that would be embarrassing or order a test to be reassuring rather than informative. The best arrangement is usually to hand the care to someone else while remaining a support, and to say clearly why that is being done.

The record belongs to the patient in a moral sense even where the law assigns it to the institution, and a patient who asks to see it should be met with help rather than resistance. Reading it can correct a misunderstanding, reveal an error, and allow the patient to take part in the next decision, so the record should be written in the knowledge that the patient may read it. The ethical failures of diagnosis are often produced by the conditions in which diagnosis is done, and a queue that allows no second look, a shift that runs past the point of safe attention, and a handover in a corridor all shape the decisions that follow. The clinician should use whatever authority exists to protect the time a difficult conversation requires, and where the system cannot be changed the honest response is to say what it prevented in the record. Patient-centered diagnosis is not a separate subject from good diagnosis but the condition under which good diagnosis happens.

The clinician who holds both the reasoning and the person in view makes fewer errors, is told more, and is trusted with the truth when the truth is bad. A diagnosis that is technically correct and delivered without consent, without explanation, or without a plan has failed at the level that matters most to the person who received it. The whole of this book, from the first look at the patient to the last decision about follow-up, is carried out on someone whose life the diagnosis will change. The discipline is to remember that in every consultation, including the ones that are too short, too public, and too sad. To summarize, a diagnosis belongs to the person who receives it as much as to the clinician who makes it, and the ethical work is part of the diagnostic work rather than an addition to it. The dangerous failures of the process are named early: the treatable illness missed because the consultation closed before the patient's concern was spoken, the error known and not disclosed, the test done without understanding, and the patient sent home without a net. Telling and explaining are distinct acts, consent is a conversation rather than a signature, and the consent implied by a queue does not cover an examination of the body, a procedure, or a use of the patient for teaching. Where the options are balanced the patient's values decide, where they are not the clinician owes a recommendation, and uncertainty is stated plainly because hidden uncertainty becomes a private worry and a public surprise. The duty created by an error reaches past the patient who suffered it, through disclosure and apology to the record, the review, and the next patient. Every score, cut-off, reference range, pressure, temperature, rate, interval, or duration mentioned in this chapter is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between countries, between editions, and between laboratories. This chapter is educational material, and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service. The essentials of dignity, consent, disclosure, and follow-up can be preserved in a private room and in a clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, and the clinician who preserves them in the harder setting has understood the point.
