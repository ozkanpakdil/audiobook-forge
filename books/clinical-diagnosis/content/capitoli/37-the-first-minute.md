# Chapter 37 — The First Minute

Every chapter in this book has asked the clinician to reason toward a diagnosis, but this one inverts the order, because the first act is not to name the disease but to recognize that the patient is dangerously ill at all. The airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure sequence organizes that recognition, and its logic is simple: some threats kill quickly and must be answered before any history is taken. A patient who cannot move air, whose breathing has stopped or become agonal, whose pulse cannot be felt, whose seizure is still running, or whose blood glucose is dangerously low is a patient in whom the diagnosis can wait and the treatment cannot. The phrase the first minute is a figure of speech, and any clock time in this chapter is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the intervals that define urgency differ between services. This chapter is educational material and it cannot replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service.

The recognition itself is the hardest skill here, because it is a judgment about the whole person rather than a measurement of one part. The clinician looks for the patient who is gray or mottled, who sweats without exertion, who sits bolt upright because lying flat is unbearable, who cannot finish a sentence, or who is restless in a way that does not fit the story. Any of these may mean nothing alone, but several together, or one of them in a patient whose family says he is not himself, is a reason to act before the explanation is complete. The clinician's own alarm is a piece of data, because the sense that something is badly wrong is the accumulated pattern recognition of every sick patient seen before. Recognition and the sequence happen together, and a patient who looks seriously unwell is treated as seriously unwell until a reason is found.

The lethal causes of a collapsed or deteriorating patient deserve to be named at the start, because each announces itself through a finding that can be sought in the first minute. Hemorrhage hides in the abdomen, the chest, the gut, the pelvis, and the tissues around a broken bone, and it is revealed by a climbing pulse, cool clammy skin, a pressure that falls on sitting, and a belly or limb that is swelling. Hypoxia is revealed by agitation or drowsiness with a risen breathing rate, a chest that moves unevenly, and lips or tongue that have lost their color, though no single sign is reliable. Sepsis is revealed by fever or an abnormally low temperature with a fast pulse, a raised breathing rate, confusion, and a falling pressure, and the quick sequential organ failure assessment is one way of formalizing that combination. Cardiac arrest is revealed by a patient who does not respond, does not breathe normally, and has no pulse that can be felt. Stroke is revealed by a face that droops, an arm that drifts downward when both are held out, and speech that has become slurred or lost, and any figure quoted here must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such values vary between countries and editions.

The sequence has an order because each step depends on the one before it. Air must move before oxygen can reach the blood, the blood must circulate before it can carry that oxygen to the brain, and the brain must respond before the clinician can judge how much of it is working. Exposure comes last because undressing a patient costs warmth and dignity, and it is worth that cost only when a hidden injury or rash cannot be found any other way. When the clinician works alone the steps blur into one continuous act, and the only rule that survives is that treatment precedes diagnosis.

The airway is assessed first because a complete obstruction kills quickly, and the assessment takes only seconds. The clinician looks at the chest and abdomen for movement, listens at the mouth for air, and feels for the movement of air against the cheek, and silence with a chest that is not rising demands action at once. A partial obstruction makes its own sounds, among them a harsh noise on breathing in, a gurgle, a snore in a deeply unconscious patient, or a voice that has changed. The patient who drools, whose neck is swelling, whose throat is burned, or who makes a harsh noise after a sting may be losing the airway even while talking. In the hospital the call for help goes out before the airway closes, while in the clinic the clinician tilts the head, lifts the chin, removes what can be seen, and supports the airway with a bag and a mask.

Breathing is assessed by counting rather than glancing, and the count is the cheapest and most sensitive measurement in the first minute. The clinician watches the rise of the chest, the use of the muscles between the ribs and above the collarbones, the flaring of the nostrils, and the position the patient has chosen, then counts the breaths over a full interval with a watch. Absent or agonal breathing, in which gasps come at long and irregular intervals, means cardiac arrest and compressions until help arrives, and it is easily mistaken for normal breathing. Any figure quoted for the rate at which breathing should cause alarm is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because normal ranges differ with age, pregnancy, altitude, and the reference in use.

Circulation is assessed by feeling for a pulse, looking at the skin, and asking what the pressure is doing. The clinician feels the wrist and, when no pulse is found there, the neck or the groin, and no central pulse in an unresponsive patient who is not breathing defines cardiac arrest for the purpose of action. The quality of the pulse matters too, since a thin, fast pulse suggests a circulation that is emptying while a bounding pulse suggests one that is dilated and losing its tone. The skin is the window the clinician already has, and cool, clammy, mottled skin with a slow refill after pressure tells of blood being sent to the center and away from the limbs. The figure of 90 millimeters of mercury often quoted for the systolic pressure is an illustration and must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the value that should prompt action varies with the patient's usual pressure, with age, and with pregnancy.

Disability means the brain, and it is assessed by the level of response, the pupils, the movement of the limbs on each side, and the blood glucose. The Glasgow coma scale records the response to voice, to pain, and to command, and the reasoning it formalizes is a graded search for how much of the brain is still answering; its contents and the score at which alarm is raised must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the version in use varies between services. A patient who was alert and is now drowsy, or drowsy and now unrousable, has declared a deterioration that no laboratory result is needed to confirm. The pupils are compared for size and for the speed with which they narrow to light, and a difference between the two sides demands urgent explanation. The blood glucose is measured whenever the equipment exists, because a low glucose can imitate a stroke, a seizure, a drunken state, and a psychiatric emergency, and it is the most reversible of the lethal causes.

Exposure closes the sequence because the answer is often under the clothing, and a clinician who does not look will not find the wound, the rash, the distended abdomen, or the swollen limb. The patient is undressed enough to be examined, kept covered where possible, and warmed actively, because cold impairs clotting and worsens the shock the clinician is trying to treat. The examination is quick and deliberate, so the back is rolled to be seen, the perineum is inspected when bleeding or trauma is possible, the limbs are compared for swelling, and every line and dressing is looked at directly. The skin is searched for a rash that does not fade when pressed, for puncture marks, and for the pale, cold, mottled pattern that speaks of a circulation in retreat. The dignity of the patient is part of the task, and in a clinic with only a torch the light turns an impression into a finding.

In a hospital the first minute is a performance by several people at once, and the reasoning lies in directing them. One person calls for the resuscitation team, one opens the airway and supports breathing, one attaches the monitor and the pressure cuff, one finds a vein and takes the samples that will answer later questions, and one writes the times and findings. The monitor is an instrument of the first minute and not a substitute for the examination, because a tracing can be believed while the patient in front of it is deteriorating. The trolley is prepared before it is needed, and the rule that treatment precedes diagnosis means the team acts on the findings it has while the cause is pursued once the immediate threats are answered.

Alone in a clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, the same reasoning is compressed into what two hands can do. The clinician puts a hand on the chest to feel whether it rises, bends an ear to the mouth to hear whether air moves, feels the neck for a pulse, and holds a finger on the wrist to count. A bag and a mask allow breathing to be supported, a watch allows the rate to be counted rather than guessed, and the torch makes the pupils, the throat, and the skin visible. The clinician alone must also decide when to call for help, and that call is part of the treatment, because the patient who must travel should begin that journey early. What the clinic lacks in equipment it partly replaces with continuity, since the same clinician who looks again shortly may see the change a monitor would have shown.

Hemorrhage is the lethal cause most easily hidden, because blood can be lost into spaces the clinician cannot see. Trauma conceals blood in the chest, the abdomen, the pelvis, and around a fractured thigh, and the signs are a climbing pulse, cool clammy skin, a pressure that falls on sitting, and a swelling or belly that grows between examinations. A gastrointestinal bleed may present first as weakness, sweating, and a black stool rather than as visible blood, and a patient who has taken a medicine that reduces stomach acid may bleed without pain. A ruptured ectopic pregnancy in a woman of childbearing age can kill quickly, and the findings that reveal it are lower abdominal pain, a missed or unusual period, shoulder tip pain from blood under the diaphragm, and the signs of internal bleeding. The decision is whether the loss is continuing and whether the patient must go where it can be stopped, and a pulse and a pressure moving in the wrong direction make observation the wrong answer.

Hypoxia is the quiet death, and the patient who is not obviously struggling may be the closest to it. Its causes in the first minute include a blocked airway, a chest that cannot expand, fluid in the lungs, a clot in the lung, a collapsed lung under pressure, a severe asthma attack, and a depression of breathing by a medicine or a poison. The findings are a risen breathing rate, restlessness or drowsiness, a chest that moves uneasily on one side, a windpipe that has shifted, and lips or tongue that have changed color, but skin color is a poor guide in dark skin, in anemia, and in a failing circulation. A pulse oximeter is useful when it exists, and it can be wrong when the circulation to the finger is poor, the skin is cold, or the pigment in the blood is abnormal. Any figure given for an oxygen saturation below which action is required is an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the accepted value differs with age, altitude, pregnancy, and the purpose for which it is used.

Sepsis is named early because it kills through the response to infection rather than through the infection alone, and it can present looking like almost anything. The findings that should raise it are fever or an abnormally low temperature, a fast pulse, a raised breathing rate, confusion or drowsiness, cool or mottled skin, and a pressure that has fallen or falls on sitting. The quick sequential organ failure assessment formalizes that combination at the bedside, and the reasoning it captures is a search for the failing organ rather than for the organism; its contents and its trigger must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the thresholds vary between services. The patient who is compensating well deserves particular care, because a normal pressure with a fast pulse, a raised breathing rate, and cold extremities is a patient spending the last of the reserve. A patient with a suspected infection and any sign of organ failure needs help and treatment now, and a clinic without a laboratory reaches the same decision from the pulse, the breathing rate, the temperature, the skin, and the passage of urine.

Cardiac arrest is recognized rather than predicted, and recognition depends on two questions asked in the right order, which are whether the patient responds and whether the breathing is normal. A patient who does not respond and is not breathing normally, with occasional irregular gasps, is treated as being in cardiac arrest, and the pulse check must be brief because a long search delays the compressions that matter. A patient with agonal breathing and no pulse that can be found has already answered the question, and the answer is to begin cardiopulmonary resuscitation, or CPR, and to call for the team and the equipment. In the clinic the clinician begins compressions, calls for help, and continues until the patient recovers or help arrives, because compressions need no equipment at all. Any figure quoted for how long the pulse check may take, or for the rate at which compressions are delivered, is an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since such figures are revised and differ between training bodies.

Stroke is the emergency in which time is brain, and the first minute is when it is usually made or missed. The findings are a face that has fallen on one side, an arm that drifts downward when both are held out with the eyes closed, and speech that has become slurred, jumbled, or absent, and any of these appearing suddenly with a normal blood glucose is a reason to move quickly. The blood glucose is measured because low and very high values can each imitate a stroke, and a seizure that has just ended can leave a weakness that fades over the following minutes, so the examination is repeated. A stroke in the back of the brain may present without weakness, with a sudden loss of balance, double vision, difficulty swallowing, or confusion mistaken for drunkenness, so a strong arm does not exclude it. The decision is where the patient should be taken, and any figure quoted for the interval in which clot-directed treatment is still possible is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because it differs between services and editions.

A low blood glucose deserves its own place because it is the treatable imitation of every other emergency. It can present as confusion, agitation, drowsiness, a seizure, a weakness on one side, strange behavior, or a patient who appears drunk, and in a patient who takes a medicine for diabetes the history alone should raise it. The measurement is made with a meter when one exists, and a low reading in a patient without symptoms may need confirmation by a laboratory method, because meters vary. A figure such as 3 millimoles per liter is often spoken of as the level below which the brain begins to suffer, but that value is an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the accepted threshold differs with age, pregnancy, and the reference in use. The decision is whether to correct the glucose at once, and the finding that makes it right is a low reading with a patient who is not fully awake, while the finding that makes it wrong is a normal reading with another explanation for the behavior.

A seizure that is still running when the patient arrives demands action in the first minute, because a brain that seizes without pause suffers injury that continues after the movement stops. The clinician notes the time the seizure began, protects the patient from injury without restraining the limbs or putting anything in the mouth, and looks at once for the treatable causes, among them a low blood glucose, a low sodium, an infection, a poison, and a cause related to pregnancy. A seizure that has stopped still leaves the patient at risk, because the breathing may be poor, the airway soiled, and a second seizure may follow. The distinction between a continuing seizure and a patient who is shaking from another cause is made by watching the pattern, the eyes, and the response, and by asking a witness what happened before the movement began. Any figure given for the duration at which a seizure becomes an emergency is an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the definition is drawn differently between services.

Anaphylaxis is named here because it can kill within the first minute through the airway, the breathing, or the circulation, and the first decision is made on the clinical picture rather than on a test. The findings are a sudden illness after a sting, a food, or a medicine, with swelling of the lips or tongue, a hoarse voice, a harsh noise on breathing in, a wheeze, raised itching welts, vomiting, and a pressure that is falling. The patient may describe a sense of doom or a tightness in the throat, and a clinician who waits for the rash may lose the airway while waiting, since the skin may be spared. The decision is whether to treat at once on the picture, and the finding that makes treatment right is any of those features appearing suddenly in a patient who was well, while the finding that makes it wrong is a different explanation that accounts for all of them.

The clinician is also part of the scene, and the first minute includes the judgment of whether the patient can be approached safely. A patient who has collapsed in a confined space, beside a fire, after a chemical exposure, or with a history suggesting an infectious hazard may put the clinician at risk, and a rescuer who becomes the second casualty has failed the patient. Personal protective equipment, or PPE, is put on before contact when the history allows, and the hands are cleaned before and after every patient, because the same hands carry infection from the patient who is bleeding to the patient who is not. Where a needlestick or a splash has occurred, the local protocol for reporting and assessment begins immediately, and the decision about treatment for the human immunodeficiency virus, known as HIV, or for another infection carried in blood belongs to the service that manages such exposures.

The sequence is harder to read in the patients whose signs are muted, and those are exactly the patients most likely to be missed. A frail older patient may present with confusion, a fall, or a refusal to eat, with no fever and no pain, and the first minute may reveal only that the patient is not the person the family knows. A child may compensate for a long time and then deteriorate suddenly, and poor perfusion in a child is best read from behavior, color, feeding, and alertness rather than from a single pressure. A pregnant woman has a raised pulse and a lower pressure as part of her normal state, so a value that would reassure in another patient may already represent a loss of reserve. A patient whose immune defenses are suppressed, whether by HIV infection, a cancer treatment, or a long course of steroids, may carry a severe infection with almost no signs, so the threshold for acting must be lower.

The first minute is also where the reasoning can go wrong, and the commonest error is to fall in love with a label before the findings have earned it. A patient labeled as drunk, as anxious, as having had a seizure, or as having had a simple faint may then be examined in a way that confirms the label and misses the stroke, the low glucose, the sepsis, or the bleed beneath it. The clinician must hold several possibilities at once and name what would change the mind, because that discipline is the protection against anchoring. Soap notes, which organize the record into subjective, objective, assessment and plan, offer a structure for this, and the record should say what the next observer is to look for rather than only what has been concluded, so that the next reader can continue the thought.

The patient and the family are part of the first minute even when the patient cannot speak, because the person who knows the patient best is often in the room. The clinician asks what happened, what the patient's usual state is, what medicines are taken, and what allergies exist, since those answers direct the reasoning while the examination continues. Consent for examination and treatment in an emergency is presumed where the patient cannot give it and delay would cause harm, and the local protocol governs how that presumption is recorded. The family should be told what is being done and why, in words they can follow, and where a language or cultural difference stands between them and the clinician, an interpreter is found early rather than late.

The rule that treatment precedes diagnosis is the heart of this chapter, and it is a statement about the order of decisions rather than about the value of diagnosis. Treatment is the right answer in the first minute when the airway is obstructed, when breathing has stopped or become agonal, when no pulse can be felt, when a seizure continues, when the blood glucose is low, or when the signs of shock or severe hypoxia are present, because waiting for certainty then carries a cost that cannot be recovered. Treatment is the wrong answer when the patient is stable, when the picture is explained by a cause that needs no emergency treatment, and when treating would destroy the evidence the diagnosis depends on, as when samples must be taken before an antibiotic is given. The clinician should say which finding would justify acting and which would forbid it, because that habit transfers between settings even when the available treatments do not. Naming the decision rather than a specific medicine is deliberate in a book about diagnosis, and the choice of what to give belongs with the team, the guideline, and the patient in front of you.

Two constructed illustrations may fix the habit, and neither is a portrait of a real person. Imagine a man brought to a clinic after a collapse, cool and clammy, with no pulse felt at the wrist, fast breathing, and an abdomen that is beginning to distend, and the clinician does not wait to learn which organ is bleeding but begins the journey to surgical help while supporting the airway and the circulation. Now imagine a woman who is drowsy and has been labeled as having had too much to drink, in whom a glucose measurement is low and whose alertness returns once the glucose is corrected, so the label dissolves in the first minute. The findings in these stories are illustrations and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the values that trigger action differ between services. The sequence is not the whole of resuscitation, and it has succeeded when the patient is alive to receive a proper history and examination.

To summarize, the first minute is not a diagnosis but a recognition, and the airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure sequence exists to find the threats that kill before any cause is named. The findings that demand action are the obstructed airway, the absent or agonal breathing, the pulse that cannot be felt, the seizure that is continuing, and the blood glucose that is low, and each is met with treatment rather than a further question. The lethal causes named at the bedside are hemorrhage, hypoxia, sepsis, cardiac arrest, and stroke, and each carries a revealing finding, among them a climbing pulse with a swelling belly, a risen breathing rate with drowsiness, a fever with confusion and cold skin, absent response with abnormal breathing, and a face that droops with an arm that drifts. The same reasoning is used by a team with a monitor and a trolley and by a clinician alone with a bag and a mask, a torch, a watch, and a hand on the chest. Every number in this chapter, whether a rate, a pressure, a temperature, an oxygen saturation, a score, or a duration, is an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between countries, editions, and laboratories. This is educational material and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service. A patient who looks critically ill is treated as critically ill first, and the diagnosis is settled afterwards.
