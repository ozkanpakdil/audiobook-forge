# Chapter 39 — Sepsis Recognition

Sepsis is the name clinicians give to the moment when the body's response to an infection stops protecting the patient and begins to injure the organs instead. It is not a single value that can be read from a thermometer or a laboratory report, and no one sign proves it, because the same findings that announce it in one patient are that patient's ordinary state in another. Sepsis is recognized by its trajectory, by the direction in which the patient is moving over hours rather than by the position of any one measurement. A patient whose breathing rate has climbed at two successive reviews, whose pressure has fallen since the last observation, and whose family says he is not himself is a different patient from one whose single reading happens to sit at the same number. The reasoning is built on comparison, against the patient's own baseline, against the previous set of observations, and against the story of how the illness began. This chapter is educational material; it is not medical advice, it is not a protocol, and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service.

The lethal possibility must be named before the comfortable one, and here it is the patient who is dying of infection while the examination still looks unremarkable. Septic shock kills in hours, and the finding that reveals it is not a dramatic fever but a pressure that will not hold, a mind that has wandered, and skin that looks mottled and cool at the edges. The patient whose immune defenses have been stripped by disease or treatment may carry a bloodstream infection with no fever and no localizing sign at all, so that a change in behavior is the only evidence available before the pressure falls. The patient with a new murmur and a fever may be developing an infection on a heart valve, and that murmur heard for the first time turns a vague febrile illness into a specific hunt with a specific deadline. The patient with a rigid, board-like abdomen has a perforated organ until proven otherwise, and no amount of fever reasoning substitutes for the surgical decision this finding demands. The patient whose skin is already mottled has moved past the earliest stage, and the mottling signals that blood is being directed away from the periphery toward the vital centers, a compensation that cannot continue.

Infection and sepsis are not the same thing, and the difference is the whole point of the distinction. Infection describes organisms in the body, in a wound, in the urine, in the lung, or in the blood, while sepsis describes a response to that infection which has become dysregulated and which is damaging organs the organisms themselves have not reached. The lungs stiffen and the patient breathes faster, the vessels dilate and the pressure falls, the kidneys slow, the brain clouds, and the clotting system becomes unstable, and these features make sepsis a different problem from the pneumonia or the urinary infection that started it. A patient can therefore have a serious infection without sepsis, and sepsis can be declared before any organism has been found, because the recognition rests on the response and on evidence of organ injury rather than on a positive culture. The definition has been redrawn more than once, and each revision has moved the boundary, so the current definition must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol before it is used to classify a real patient. What has not changed is the clinical task, which is to notice the patient whose response to infection has become dangerous, and to notice it early enough that the organs can still recover.

The systemic inflammatory response syndrome was an attempt to make the impression that a patient looks infected into something countable. It formalizes a small set of observations made at the bedside anyway, the temperature, the pulse, the breathing rate, and the white cell count, and it proposes that when enough of them are abnormal the patient is mounting a systemic response. Its limitation matters more than its virtue, because the syndrome does not require an infection and is met by burns, trauma, pancreatitis, autoimmune disease, a recent operation, and the inflammation that follows childbirth, so it labels many people as infected who are not. It is equally poor at the reverse, since a patient can be dying of infection without meeting its criteria, particularly an older patient, a patient taking steroids, or a patient whose immune defenses are suppressed. A temperature above 38 degrees Celsius is the figure most often taught as the line at which fever is said to be present, and that number is offered as an illustration of the reasoning rather than as a rule; it must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because accepted values differ between countries, editions, and laboratories. The lesson it leaves behind is that a group of ordinary observations taken together can raise a suspicion, but the suspicion still has to be tested against the patient in front of you.

The quick sequential organ failure assessment is a newer attempt to solve a different problem, which is how to recognize the patient with a suspected infection who is at risk of dying before any blood test has returned. It formalizes three things any clinician anywhere can observe, namely how fast the patient is breathing, whether the mind has changed from its usual state, and how low the systolic pressure has fallen. Its chief limitation is that it is not sensitive, so a patient who does not meet its criteria may still be severely ill, and a clinician who lets a low count close the question will miss the patient it was meant to catch. It was derived in a particular population and not for children, pregnant women, or patients whose immune defenses are suppressed, so its meaning shifts in those groups. Many services have adapted it by adding the oxygen saturation, the urine output, or a laboratory measure, and a breathing rate above 22 breaths per minute and a systolic pressure below 90 millimeters of mercury are the figures most often quoted within it; because the items and the values differ between services and editions, the version in use and its figures must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

The signs that precede the obvious fever are the ones that save patients, and they are found by watching the patient rather than by reading a result. A new confusion is often the earliest, and it is the one most easily attributed to age, to the hour, or to the strange surroundings of a hospital. The person who knows the patient best, the spouse, the son, the daughter, or the carer, can say whether this is new, and that question is worth more than any screening test. A breathing rate that has risen is the second sign, and it is the vital sign most often guessed rather than counted, which is why a ward that counts it discovers deterioration and a ward that estimates it does not. A pressure that has fallen, whether or not it has crossed any line, is the third, and the direction of the fall matters more than the absolute figure. A passage of urine that has dwindled to a trickle is the fourth, noticed only when the urine is measured and written down, because a patient who is not passing urine does not complain of it.

Because sepsis is recognized by its trajectory, the repeated set of observations is the most important investigation in this chapter and the one that costs nothing. The same pressure that looks reassuring at the first reading can look ominous beside the reading taken earlier, and the same breathing rate can look ordinary until it is compared with what the patient was doing at home. This is the reasoning that the early warning scores formalize, by combining routine observations into a single number that rises as the patient deteriorates and by prompting a response when it does. The Glasgow coma scale is the way the level of consciousness is recorded, and the reasoning it formalizes is the response to voice, to command, and to pain. A change of a few points in a patient whose baseline is known carries more information than the absolute number, and the version in use and the value at which it should cause alarm must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. An early warning score is only a summary of what has been recorded, so a chart with a blank column cannot warn anyone, and a patient whose score is unchanged but whose family says something is wrong deserves a second look, because the clinician's unease is itself an observation that no score contains.

The fever that everyone expects may be absent, and a clinician who waits for it will arrive late. An older patient whose infection is severe may have a temperature that is barely raised, or a temperature that has fallen below the usual range, and that low temperature is a warning rather than a comfort. The same is true of the patient whose immune defenses are suppressed by illness or treatment, and of the patient with poorly controlled diabetes, in whom the response to infection is blunted and the ordinary signs are muted. In these patients the presentation may be a fall, a refusal to eat, a new drowsiness, a loss of continence, or a conversation that no longer makes sense, so the infection announces itself through function rather than through temperature. A temperature of 36 degrees Celsius in a frail older patient who was at 37 degrees Celsius the day before may carry more information than a fever in a healthy young adult, and both figures are illustrative and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the usual range is drawn differently between laboratories and age groups. The absence of fever therefore never excludes a serious infection, and the threshold for asking for help in these patients is lower rather than higher.

The hunt for the source begins with the history, before the patient is touched, because the answer is often in what the patient or the family says rather than in what the examination finds. The clinician uses the Oldcarts framework to keep the account of the present illness complete, then broadens the questions to the exposures that change the list of likely organisms. Where has the patient traveled, what animals are kept at home, what water is drunk, what food has been eaten away from home, and has there been an injection, a dental procedure, an operation, or a device placed in the body. Whether there is a risk of a sexually transmitted infection, whether the patient takes steroids or treatment that suppresses the immune system, whether there has been a recent course of antibiotics, and whether the patient is pregnant all belong in the same conversation. The clinician should also ask what the patient is normally like, how much they normally eat, drink, and pass urine, because a change from the baseline is the earliest evidence available.

The examination of a patient with suspected sepsis is a deliberate search for a focus, performed in a fixed order so that nothing is forgotten while the patient is deteriorating. It begins with the general appearance, the work of breathing, the color of the skin, and the level of consciousness, then moves to the skin from the scalp to the soles in search of a rash, a puncture mark, an ulcer, a bite, or a red and warm area. The throat, the ears, the nose, the mouth, and the teeth are inspected with a torch, because an abscess in a tooth or a hidden infection in a sinus can sustain a bloodstream infection for a long time. The chest is examined for crackles and dullness, the heart for a new murmur, the abdomen for tenderness, rigidity, an enlarged organ, or a mass, and the joints for swelling and warmth. The examiner then looks at the places that are easy to forget, namely the back, the spine, the groins, the perineum, the pressure areas over the heels and the sacrum, and every wound, line, tube, and device in the body, since an infection hidden in a line or a drain is invisible to a clinician who does not look at it directly.

The patient whose immune defenses are suppressed is the one in whom the usual reasoning fails most completely, and the patient with a low neutrophil count is its clearest example. A low neutrophil count, produced by a cancer of the blood, by treatment that suppresses the marrow, or by a transplant, removes the cells that make pus and inflammation, so an infection can spread without producing a single localizing sign. In such a patient a fever may be the only abnormal finding, and sometimes even the fever is absent, so the clinician is left with a patient who simply looks unwell and a history of treatment that suppressed the marrow. Advanced infection with the human immunodeficiency virus, known as HIV, long courses of steroids, and some treatments for autoimmune disease produce a similar blurring of the picture, though usually to a lesser degree. The decision that follows is that a new fever, or a new deterioration, is treated as an emergency rather than watched, and the threshold for seeking senior help is lowered rather than raised. The finding that would make treatment the wrong answer is a clear non-infectious explanation, such as a reaction to a medicine, a clot, or the underlying disease itself, and even then the patient must be reviewed closely.

A fever with a murmur that is new, or a murmur that has changed, is a specific problem with a specific deadline, and it is the infection of the lining of the heart. The patients most at risk are those with a damaged or prosthetic valve, a pacemaker or another device inside the heart, a history of injecting drugs, or a recent procedure on the teeth or the gut, and in these people the fever may be low and persistent rather than dramatic. The finding that reveals the diagnosis is the murmur itself, heard for the first time or heard to be different from the last description, and an examiner who listens carefully in every febrile patient is the one who finds it. The other clues are small and easily missed, being tiny bleeding spots under the nails or in the eye, tender red nodules on the fingers or toes, an enlarged spleen, and a patient who is anemic or whose urine contains blood. The decision that follows is whether to take blood cultures before any treatment begins, because cultures drawn after the first treatment are far less likely to name the organism. The finding that would make the diagnosis the wrong answer is another focus that fully explains the fever, though a patient with a prosthetic valve and a fever should be discussed with a senior colleague before that alternative is accepted.

A rigid, board-like abdomen in a patient with fever is a surgical emergency and not a problem for the treatment decision alone. Rigidity is the abdominal wall held tight in a way the patient cannot relax on request, and it usually means that the peritoneum has been irritated by a perforated organ, by pus, or by blood. The supporting signs are pain on the slightest movement or cough, rebound tenderness, quiet bowel sounds, a rising pulse, and a pressure that is falling. The abdomen can lie, because an older patient, a patient taking steroids, and a patient whose immune defenses are suppressed may have a perforated organ with only mild tenderness and a vague story of not feeling well. The decision it forces is about surgery and senior help rather than about which treatment to choose, and it should be made at the bedside without waiting for an image that may delay the operating room. The finding that would make the catastrophe less likely is a soft abdomen that stays soft on repeated examination, while a patient who is getting worse with each review confirms it.

The skin is the organ that can be examined most quickly at the bedside, and what it shows in sepsis is the redistribution of blood away from the surface. The findings are cool hands and feet, a capillary refill that is visibly delayed when the clinician presses the fingertip and counts, mottling over the knees and the thighs, and a dusky or gray color that the family notices before the clinician does. A rash that does not fade when a clear glass is pressed against it is a different and even more urgent finding, because it may mean that organisms are in the bloodstream and that the clotting system has begun to fail. One presentation on the skin deserves to be named separately because it kills quickly and is easily dismissed as a bruise, and that is a necrotizing infection of the soft tissue. The findings that reveal it are pain far worse than the appearance suggests, an area that is swollen and discolored, a numbness or loss of sensation over that area, blisters or fluid seeping from the skin, and sometimes a crackling sensation when the skin is pressed. The decision in that situation is surgical and immediate, because the treatment is the removal of the dead tissue, and the finding that would make it wrong is a superficial infection clearly confined to the surface in a patient who is stable and improving.

In a hospital with a laboratory, the recognition of sepsis is supported by tests that sharpen the picture but do not replace the examination. A blood count, a measure of inflammation such as C-reactive protein, a measure of kidney function, a measure of the acidity of the blood, and a measure of lactate are the ordinary set, and each answers a particular question about which organs are already involved. A lactate above 2 millimoles per liter is often quoted as evidence that the tissues are not receiving enough oxygen, and that figure must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the value depends on the method and on the laboratory. Blood cultures should be taken before treatment whenever that is possible without delay, because the organism is the one piece of information that treatment cannot recover once it has begun. Imaging by chest radiograph, by ultrasound, by computed tomography, or CT, and by magnetic resonance imaging, or MRI, is chosen by the question it has to answer, and an electrocardiogram, or ECG, belongs in the assessment of a patient whose picture could also be explained by a heart attack. A lumbar puncture belongs in the patient with signs of meningitis once the questions of safety have been answered, and the transfer of a patient to an intensive care unit, or ICU, rests on the trajectory rather than on a single number.

In a clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, a thermometer, and a watch, the recognition of sepsis is made with the same reasoning and a smaller set of instruments. The breathing rate is counted rather than guessed, the pulse is counted in the same way, and the temperature is taken with the same thermometer each time so that the readings can be compared. The torch makes the throat, the ears, the skin, the eyes, and the perineum visible, and the watch allows the observations to be placed in time, so a set of observations with times beside them is the closest thing to a laboratory that a clinic possesses. The urine is measured where that can be done, the abdomen is pressed gently and then pressed again later, and the patient is asked to return, or to be brought back, at a stated interval rather than whenever it is convenient. The decision the clinic faces is not usually a diagnosis but a plan, which is whether the patient can be watched here, whether treatment should begin on the clinical picture, and whether the journey must begin now. A patient who is deteriorating in the clinic must travel, and the clinician who sends that patient with a written summary of what has been observed gives the receiving team information that cannot be reconstructed later.

The decision about treatment is part of diagnosis and not a separate step, and in sepsis it is often made before any organism has been named. Treatment is the right answer when the signs of organ failure are already present, when the pressure is low and does not improve when the patient is laid flat, when the mind has changed from its baseline or the breathing rate is climbing, and when a focus is strongly suspected that would respond. Treatment is the wrong answer when the picture is explained by something that is not infection, such as a clot in the lung, bleeding inside the body, a heart that is failing, or a reaction to a medicine, and when treating before the samples are taken would destroy the evidence on which diagnosis depends. This book gives no doses, regimens, rates, or durations for any drug, because it is a book about diagnosis and because those decisions belong to the current guideline, the local protocol, the patient, and the organism. Fluid given into a vein is often part of the immediate response, and whether it is given, and how much, turns on whether the pressure and the perfusion improve, while the most useful thing a diagnosing clinician can say is what finding would make treatment right and what finding would make it wrong, because that reasoning transfers between settings even when the medicines do not.

A constructed illustration may make the reasoning concrete, and it is offered as a teaching device rather than as a portrait of any real person. Imagine an older woman brought to a clinic because her family says she is confused and not herself, with a temperature only slightly raised, a breathing rate of 24 breaths per minute, and a blood pressure of 100 millimeters of mercury. Those figures are illustrations of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the values that should prompt action differ with age and with the usual blood pressure. The clinician counts the breathing rate again, looks at the skin with the torch, finds a red and warm area over the hip, and arranges the journey to hospital on the basis of the whole picture rather than the temperature alone. Now imagine a young man receiving treatment for a cancer of the blood, who feels shivery and unwell but looks entirely ordinary, and whose only abnormal finding is a fever that has appeared since the last review. His immune defenses have been suppressed by the treatment, so the ordinary signs are not available, and the decision to treat him as an emergency is made from the history and the trajectory rather than from a focus that can be found. Neither patient is real, and neither story should be applied to a real person without a full assessment.

The record of a patient with suspected sepsis should show the thinking and not only the numbers, including the time of each set of observations, the breathing rate as counted, the direction of the pressure, and what the family said about the baseline. The clinician should write down what was considered and what was set aside, so that the next reader can pick up the thread rather than start again. Safety netting is part of the decision, because a patient who is sent home or watched must be told which signs should bring him back, how quickly to return, and where to go. The patient and the family should be told that a new confusion, a difficulty in breathing, a passage of urine that stops, a rash that does not fade when pressed, or a shivering attack are reasons to return at once. A follow-up visit arranged in advance is worth more than a promise to return if things worsen, because a patient who is becoming septic may not be able to judge that for himself. The clinician who is uncertain should say so, and should convert that uncertainty into a plan for review rather than into a confident answer that the evidence does not support.

To summarize, sepsis is a dysregulated response to infection that is recognized by its trajectory and by the evidence of organ injury rather than by any single value, and the signs that precede the obvious fever are a new confusion, a raised breathing rate, a falling pressure, and a shrinking passage of urine. The systemic inflammatory response syndrome and the quick sequential organ failure assessment are best understood as attempts to formalize the observations a clinician makes anyway, each with real limitations and each adapted locally, so the version in use must always be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. The dangerous presentations are named at the bedside and not deferred, among them the patient whose immune defenses are suppressed and whose signs are silent, the patient with a new murmur, the patient with a rigid abdomen, and the patient whose skin is already mottled or whose soft tissue is dying. Treatment is a decision about the patient rather than about a drug, and this chapter gives no doses, regimens, rates, or durations, because those belong to the protocol of your own service. Every figure in this chapter is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such numbers differ between countries, editions, and laboratories. This is educational material and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service, and a patient who looks unwell is treated and helped first while the exact cause is settled afterwards.
