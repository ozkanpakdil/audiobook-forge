# Chapter 72 — Red Flags and Referral Criteria

Every clinician carries two questions through a consultation, one about what the patient probably has and one about what the patient must not be allowed to have, and this chapter is about the second. The findings that answer it are called red flags, and the name misleads if it suggests a list to memorize, because a red flag is simply a finding that changes the next decision. When a symptom, a sign, or a number raises the probability of a cause that will injure or kill the patient if it is not dealt with soon, the plan stops being one of watching and becomes one of acting. The action may be treatment, a test at once, a telephone call, or a journey to another town, and the choice among them is the subject of referral. This chapter is educational material; it is not medical advice, it is not a protocol, and it is not a substitute for training, supervision, clinical judgment, or local protocol.

A red flag can come from any of the three instruments of the encounter, and a clinician who watches only one will miss the others. From the history come a sudden onset, the worst pain of a life, a pain that arrives at rest or wakes the patient from sleep, an unintended loss of weight, a bleed from a place that should not bleed, or a change in awareness the family noticed first. From the examination come a neck that resists bending, a rash that does not disappear under pressure from a clear glass, a cold mottled limb, a tender testis that has risen, a mass in the abdomen or breast, and a weakness on one side. From the vital signs, the cheapest measurements in medicine, come the numbers that describe a body losing its fight, and their direction over repeated observations matters more than any single reading. A red flag is therefore not a diagnosis; it is a change in the probability of a dangerous diagnosis large enough to change what happens next.

Because the dangerous causes must be named where the flags belong to them, this chapter names them now, each with the finding that reveals it. A fever with a stiff neck, a severe headache, a dislike of light, or a clouded consciousness may be meningitis, revealed by resistance when the neck is flexed and sometimes by a rash that does not fade under pressure. A headache that reaches full severity almost at once may be bleeding around the brain, revealed by neck stiffness, vomiting, and a brief loss of consciousness, while a normal examination is the trap that delays the diagnosis. A sudden scrotal pain may be a twisted testis, which destroys the organ quickly, revealed by a testis sitting high and crosswise with an absent reflex when the inner thigh is stroked. Blood in the urine without pain may be a tumor of the bladder or kidney, an unintended loss of weight may be tuberculosis or a cancer, and a new confusion in an older patient may be infection, a low sugar, or a stroke.

The logic of the red flag is a logic of costs rather than certainties, which is why a common harmless finding can be a flag while a rare frightening one is not. A finding deserves the name when the harm of missing the dangerous cause behind it is greater than the harm of acting on it, and that comparison depends on what acting costs where the patient lies. In a hospital with a scanner and a laboratory, acting may mean a test that settles the question within an hour, so the threshold is low. In a clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, acting may mean a journey of hours and the loss of a day's work, so the threshold must still be set by the danger rather than by the inconvenience.

Three dispositions follow from the reasoning, and naming them plainly helps clinician and patient alike. The first is that the patient must go now, meaning the danger is present or cannot be excluded and the next hour changes the outcome; this covers the fever with a stiff neck, the headache that arrived like a blow, the sudden scrotal pain, the crushing chest pain, and the patient who is pale and cold after bleeding. The second is that the patient must be referred, meaning the danger is possible rather than immediate and the assessment needed lies elsewhere, so the journey may wait until transport is arranged. The third is that the patient may be watched with a safety net, meaning the dangerous causes have been considered and can be excluded by observation, and the patient or family can recognize the findings that should bring the patient back. The three are not degrees of one decision but different decisions, and a clinician who cannot say which applies has not finished the assessment.

The vital signs deserve their own place, because they are the red flags no history or examination can replace and because they can be obtained almost anywhere. The breathing rate is the measurement most often skipped and the one that most often reveals a deteriorating patient, since the body raises it before the pressure falls. A rate above 20 breaths per minute in an adult is often treated as the point of concern, though that figure is an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. A pulse above 100 beats per minute at rest carries the same caution, and the figure that should prompt action is stated differently by different services, so it must be checked locally rather than recalled. A pressure below 90 millimeters of mercury, or a temperature above 38 degrees Celsius, belongs to the same family, and each must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the accepted value depends on the usual reading and the site of measurement. What matters most is the direction of travel, because a pulse that climbs, a pressure that falls, or a patient harder to rouse than an hour ago tells the clinician what absolute numbers cannot.

The history contributes red flags of a different kind, because they are descriptions rather than measurements, and the description that matters most is the shape of the symptom in time. A pain that reached full intensity at once is a different illness from one that grew over weeks, and a weakness that appeared while the patient watched differs from one that crept forward for months. The clinician therefore asks whether the onset was sudden, what the patient was doing, whether the symptom is the worst of the patient's life, and whether it has happened before. Other history flags include unintended weight loss, drenching night sweats, a cough bringing blood, bleeding after the menopause, a pregnancy with pain or bleeding, a recent operation, and a medicine or illness that suppresses the immune defenses. Each raises the probability of a dangerous cause and is worth writing down even when the examination that follows is normal.

A normal examination never removes a red flag from the history, and the clinician who allows it to do so makes the error that fills the case reviews. The sudden severe headache has often eased by the time the patient is seen, and the patient with a twisted testis may have no swelling in the first hour. A patient with bleeding around the brain may have a neck that bends freely, and a patient with a clot in the lung may have a quiet chest. The examination and the tests describe the present moment, while the history describes an event that has already happened, and an event is not undone by a quiet interval. That is why the clinician records what was found and what was absent, and decides on the history, the vital signs, and the trajectory together.

Fever with a stiff neck is the clearest flag in this chapter and the one most often softened by a reassuring explanation. The patient may have a headache, a dislike of light, vomiting, and a level of consciousness that is not quite right, and the examination looks for resistance when the chin is brought to the chest, for a rash that does not fade under pressure, and for any change in awareness. In a hospital the next steps are blood cultures drawn before treatment where that does not delay it, a lumbar puncture when it is safe, and help from the most senior clinician available. In a clinic with a thermometer and a torch, the same reasoning leads to treating on the clinical suspicion and arranging immediate transfer, because the illness can kill within hours and the journey must not wait for a test that does not exist. An older patient, a patient with diabetes, and a patient whose immune defenses are suppressed may have a neck that bends easily, and the absence of stiffness proves nothing in them.

Headache of sudden onset is the second flag, and the word sudden is the whole of it. The patient often describes a blow at the back of the head, a pain that reached its worst in seconds, vomiting, and sometimes a moment of lost consciousness or a strange sensation down the back of the neck. The interval within which the pain reaches its peak is a description rather than a measured threshold, so a figure a listener may have heard, whether one minute or five minutes, must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. A normal examination after such an event is common and is not reassuring, since the bleeding may have stopped, and where a computed tomography scan, known as CT, exists it is performed early. In a clinic with nothing but a torch and a thermometer this description means an immediate journey, because a pain that has settled does not make the patient safe.

Sudden scrotal pain is a flag of a special kind, because the danger is not death but the loss of an organ, and the window in which it can be saved is short. The twisted testis turns on its cord and its blood supply is squeezed, and the patient is usually a boy or young man with a pain that came on quickly, sometimes waking him from sleep, often with nausea. The findings that support it are a testis sitting higher than its fellow and lying crosswise, a tender swollen scrotum, and an absent reflex when the skin of the inner thigh is stroked, though swelling may hide all of these early. In a hospital the diagnosis is pursued with an ultrasound where that can be obtained without delay, and with a surgical opinion regardless of the image, while in a clinic the decision is taken at once, because there is no test and no time.

Blood in the urine that causes no pain is the quietest flag in this chapter, because it does not hurt, it may come and go, and it is easy to attribute to an infection already treated. Visible blood, or blood found on testing in a patient with no symptoms, may be the first evidence of a tumor of the bladder, kidney, or prostate, and the risk is higher in an older patient, a smoker, or a person exposed to certain industrial substances. Infection and stones are far more common causes and are the ones a clinician considers first, which is exactly why the flag is missed, since treating an infection will often make the blood disappear while the tumor continues to grow. In a hospital the examination includes the microscopy of the urine, imaging of the urinary tract, and inspection of the inside of the bladder with a camera. In a clinic with a torch and no laboratory the finding is made by looking at the urine in good light, and the action is an urgent referral with a written note of what was seen. Only a proven and fully treated infection, with the blood gone on repeat examination, makes the referral unnecessary, and that proof must be obtained rather than assumed.

An unintended loss of weight is a flag that develops slowly and is often noticed by the family before the patient mentions it. The clinician asks how much weight has been lost, over what interval, whether the appetite has changed, and whether the loss was intended, and a loss the patient cannot explain deserves investigation whatever the patient weighs now. The causes that must not be missed are tuberculosis, a cancer, advanced infection with the human immunodeficiency virus, known as HIV, unrecognized diabetes, an overactive thyroid gland, and a disease of the bowel that prevents absorption of food, and night sweats, a cough lasting weeks, enlarged lymph nodes, and a mass felt anywhere all belong to the same search. The interval over which a loss becomes concerning cannot be fixed, since a figure such as 5 per cent of body weight over 6 months appears in some sources and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

A new confusion in an older patient is a flag frequently attributed to age, to a change of surroundings, or to temperament, and each of those explanations can be right while a lethal cause is present. The important word is new, and the person who knows the patient's usual state, often a relative or caregiver, is the most reliable witness. Causes that can kill or maim include infection anywhere in the body, a low level of sugar, a low level of oxygen, a disturbance of the salts, a stroke, bleeding inside the head after a fall, and the effect of a medicine or of alcohol withdrawal. The quiet form, in which the patient is drowsy and slow rather than loud and restless, is the form most often missed and is just as dangerous, and the Glasgow coma scale is one way of recording the level of consciousness, though the figure at which it should cause alarm must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. In a clinic the measurement of sugar where a meter exists is the single most useful act, since a low sugar is reversible within minutes and imitates every other cause, and a patient with no meter and a new confusion must travel.

The examination contributes flags that must be sought deliberately, because a clinician who waits for them to announce themselves will miss them. A rash that does not fade under pressure may mean bleeding into the skin from an infection in the bloodstream, and a limb that is cold, pale, mottled, or pulseless may be losing its blood supply. A pulsating abdominal mass with back pain raises the possibility of a swelling of the aorta about to tear, and a rigid silent abdomen with severe pain, vomiting of blood, or black stool points to a problem needing surgical or endoscopic help. A chest pain that is new, comes at rest, or wakes the patient from sleep is treated as a possible heart attack until an electrocardiogram, known as ECG, and the history say otherwise, and a new weakness, a drooping face, or a sudden loss of vision is a stroke until proved otherwise, with the level of sugar measured at once because a low sugar imitates it. A child who is drowsy, cannot be roused, breathes with effort, drinks poorly, or has mottled skin needs immediate assessment, since serious illness in a child is often quiet rather than dramatic.

Referral is not a single act but a decision with criteria, and the criteria are clinical rather than administrative. The first is the nature of the suspected danger and the speed with which it can cause harm, since an illness that kills within hours and one that progresses over months call for different journeys. The second is the trajectory, because a finding that is stable, one that is improving, and one that is worsening are three different findings. The third is what can be done where the patient is now, and the fourth is the reliability of the observation that would follow if the patient stayed. The fifth is the distance and cost of the journey, not because they change the danger but because they change what must be done before the patient leaves.

The patient who must go now is the one in whom the danger is present or cannot be excluded and an hour or two changes the outcome. This covers the fever with a stiff neck, the headache that arrived like a blow, the sudden scrotal pain, the chest pain that is new or comes at rest, the sudden breathlessness, the stroke, the severe abdominal pain with a rigid abdomen, and bleeding that will not stop. The clinician does not require a completed diagnosis before sending such a patient and does not wait for a test hours away, because the delay is the harm. Help is summoned by the fastest means available, the receiving service is told what is coming and why, a note is written with the findings, times, reasoning, and any treatment given, and the patient is not sent alone. Where an unresponsive patient is not breathing, cardiopulmonary resuscitation, known as CPR, is begun at once, and where the receiving service is a telephone call away the clinician calls before the patient leaves and records who gave the advice and what was said. Only a demonstrated alternative cause that accounts for every abnormal finding and cannot itself deteriorate would change this disposition.

The patient who must be referred is the one in whom the danger is possible rather than immediate and the assessment that would settle it lies elsewhere. The flags that lead here are quieter: the painless blood in the urine, the unintended loss of weight, the lump in the breast or neck, the lymph node that has not settled after several weeks, the change in bowel habit in an older patient, and the heart murmur that was not there before. The interval within which such a referral should be seen depends on the suspected diagnosis and the service, and a figure such as 2 weeks must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since priority categories differ between systems. The finding that moves the patient into the group that must go now is any sign that the danger is no longer theoretical, such as severe pain, new bleeding, a rapidly growing mass, or a general deterioration.

Watching is a legitimate disposition and not a confession of uncertainty, but it requires that the dangerous causes have been considered and can be excluded by observation where the patient is. A patient with a mild fever, a clear source, stable vital signs, and a reliable person at home may be watched with a plan, and a patient with a twisted testis may not, whatever the hour. The safety net must be specific, because a patient cannot act on the advice to return if things worsen, which transfers no information and creates no plan, so the clinician names the findings that should bring the patient back and asks the patient or family to repeat them. A safety net that depends on a telephone that does not work, a journey the family cannot afford, or a literacy the patient does not have is not a safety net. The interval for review is decided by the illness, and a figure such as 24 hours is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, while any stated interval of days carries the same caution.

When referral is a telephone call, the clinician has a short structured conversation with a colleague who has not seen the patient, and the structure is what makes the call work. The opening states who is calling, from where, and whether the patient is in front of the caller, because a colleague who knows the patient is being described listens differently. The next part states the problem in one sentence, with the suspected danger and the finding that raised it, and it is better to say that a stiff neck and a fever are the reason for the call than to recite the whole history in order. The third part gives the observations, the times, and the treatment already given, and the fourth states plainly what is asked for, whether advice, a bed, an operating room, a scan, or a specialist review. Where the call cannot be completed, the clinician does not treat that as an answer and uses the next available route, which may be a second number, a radio, a message carried by a relative, or a journey begun without confirmation.

Where the referral is a journey of some hours, the reasoning changes, because the patient will be without a clinician for a long stretch and the danger will have that time to act. The patient should travel with a person who can watch and who carries a written note stating the diagnosis suspected, the findings and times, the treatment given, the results of any test, and a number that can be called. A patient who is bleeding, confused, unable to drink, or losing pressure should not travel unattended, and a patient whose condition is worsening may need the nearest facility even when that is not the intended destination. When no vehicle exists, the obligation does not end, and the reasoning is to find the nearest thing that can help, which may be a health post, a community health worker, a radio, or a neighbor with transport, and to keep responsibility until someone else has accepted it. In this setting the threshold for referral is lower rather than higher, because the clinician is trading the risk of an unnecessary journey against the certainty of being unable to observe the patient.

There will be occasions when the receiving service declines the patient, when the bed is full, when transport does not come, or when the family refuses to travel, and the duty of the clinician continues through each of them. The first response is to make the clinical case again, in the terms the receiving service needs, and to record what was said and by whom, because a refusal that is not documented cannot be reviewed. The second is to seek another route to the same help, and the third is to make the patient as safe as the local setting allows while the search continues, which may mean treatment begun on the clinical picture, observation at shorter intervals, or the involvement of a relative who can watch. If no help can be reached, the clinician says so in plain words, explains what is likely to happen and what can still be done, and continues whatever care is possible, because the absence of a facility does not create the option of abandonment.

The documentation of a red flag is part of the diagnosis rather than an administrative afterthought, because a clinician who does not write down why the patient was sent cannot explain it later, and the next clinician must begin again. The reasoning should be visible in a sentence such as the fact that a fever with a headache and a dislike of light was treated as possible meningitis and the patient sent at once, because that carries more than a list of numbers. The record should also show what was absent, since a neck that bends freely at one visit and resists a few hours later are two different observations, and the time of each should be given. A referral note should be legible, dated, timed, signed, and free of abbreviations only the writer understands, and where the record is electronic the same discipline applies, because a template describes a patient who may not be the one in the bed.

A constructed illustration may show how a red flag decides the plan, and it is offered as a teaching device rather than as a portrait of any real person. Imagine a young man who arrives at a village clinic with a sudden scrotal pain that woke him in the night, who is nauseated, and whose right testis sits higher than the left. The clinician has no ultrasound and no surgeon within a day's walk, and the reasoning is not to watch, not to treat for infection, and not to wait for the pain to settle, but to arrange the fastest possible journey with a note of what was found and when. This patient is not real, and the story should not be applied to a real person without a full assessment. What it shows is the move that matters, which is that the clinician recognized the finding that had to decide the plan and let it decide before knowing the answer.

To summarize, a red flag is any finding from the history, the examination, or the vital signs that raises the probability of a cause which will injure or kill the patient soon enough to change what is done next, and the discipline is to let that finding govern the plan before the diagnosis is complete. The dangerous situations are named where the flags belong to them: the fever with a stiff neck, the headache that arrived like a blow, the sudden scrotal pain, the painless blood in the urine, the unintended loss of weight, and the new confusion in an older patient. The disposition is one of three decisions, whether the patient must go now, must be referred, or may be watched with a safety net that is specific, understood, and possible. The same criteria apply whether the referral is a telephone call or a journey of hours, and the journey itself is part of the care. Every figure in this chapter, whether a rate, a temperature, a pressure, a duration, a cut-off, or a score, is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such numbers differ between countries, between editions, and between laboratories. A normal examination never cancels an alarming story, and a clinician who recognizes the flag, acts on it, and documents the reasoning has done the most important thing this book can teach.
