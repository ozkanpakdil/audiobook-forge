# Chapter 55 — ENT and Ophthalmology

Vision and hearing are the two senses through which a person stays in contact with the world, and the structures that carry them are packed into the front of the head, where an illness in one can imitate an illness in another. A patient comes with a red eye, a blurred view, a lost field of vision, a blocked or ringing ear, a discharge, a blocked nose, or a sore throat, and the first task is not to name the disease but to decide what it threatens. The reasoning that organizes this chapter is therefore a question of urgency and of place: whether the problem threatens sight or hearing within hours, and where along the pathway the fault lies. A patient whose retina has lifted, whose cornea is infected, whose eye has closed its drainage angle, or whose optic nerve is being starved by inflamed arteries may lose the function permanently while waiting for a routine appointment. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of the service in which the patient is seen.

The dangers deserve to be named before any comfortable explanation is entertained, because each announces itself through a finding that can be sought in the first minutes. A painful red eye with a hazy cornea, a partly dilated pupil that does not react, and halos around lights may be acute angle closure glaucoma, in which the drainage angle has shut and the pressure is rising against the optic nerve. A sudden shower of flashes and floating spots followed by a curtain across the vision suggests a detached retina. A painful red eye in a contact lens wearer, with a white spot on the cornea that takes up fluorescein dye, is a corneal ulcer until proved otherwise. An older patient with a new headache, tenderness over the scalp, and pain in the jaw on chewing may have giant cell arteritis, and the finding that supports it is a pale and swollen optic disc with a raised marker of inflammation in the blood. A hearing loss that arrives suddenly in one ear, without pain or discharge, is an emergency of the inner ear, and a sore throat with one-sided swelling, a muffled voice, or drooling may be a collection of pus beside the tonsil or behind the throat. A firm, painless lump in the neck of an adult, hoarseness that does not settle, or an ulcer in the mouth that will not heal belongs on the same list, because a tumor of the head and neck can present in any of those ways.

The eye can be understood as a camera with a lens in front, a film behind, and a fluid-filled space between them whose pressure must stay within a narrow range. The cornea and the lens bend light onto the retina, the retina turns light into a signal, and the optic nerve carries that signal to the brain, so a failure anywhere along the chain produces the same complaint of poor vision while demanding a different answer. The clinician therefore asks where the fault lies: on the surface, in the transparent media, in the retina, in the nerve, or in the brain. The ear follows a similar logic, because sound travels through the canal, strikes the drum, and is carried by three small bones into a fluid-filled inner ear where hair cells turn the vibration into a nerve signal. A problem before the inner ear is a conductive loss, and a problem in the inner ear or the nerve is a sensorineural loss, and the two produce the same report of deafness while pointing to entirely different causes. In both systems the most useful bedside question is the question of place.

A patient who has lost vision is asked to describe it in time, and the shape of that description often names the cause. Loss that arrives in an instant and without pain may be a blockage of an artery feeding the retina, a bleed into the jelly of the eye, or a detachment, while loss that builds over hours or days with pain suggests inflammation of the cornea, the iris, or the optic nerve. Loss that is transient, lasting seconds to minutes and then recovering, may be a warning from a narrowed artery in the neck, and it deserves the same seriousness as a stroke, because the same narrowing can produce a permanent one. Loss that comes with flashes of light, new floating shapes, and a dark curtain drawn from the side is the classic story of a detached retina, and it is a reason to send the patient for expert examination at once rather than to watch. A gradual painless loss in both eyes over years is the ordinary cataract or the ordinary effect of age, and it is the least urgent of these, which is exactly why the urgent ones must be excluded first.

Vision is measured before it is explained, and the measurement can be made without equipment. A patient who can read the smallest line of a chart, or any ordinary printed page held at a fixed distance, has useful central vision, and a patient who cannot read but who can count fingers, see a hand move, or tell light from dark still has an eye that can be assessed. A pinhole made in a card sharpens the vision of an eye whose problem is a refractive error and does nothing for an eye whose problem is in the retina, the nerve, or the media, so the pinhole is a small test with a large answer. The visual fields are examined by confrontation, with the examiner's face level with the patient's and a moving finger brought in from each side, and a defect that stops at the midline points to the eye or its nerve while a defect that crosses it points to the brain. The pupils are examined with a torch for their size, their shape, and their reaction, and the light is swung from one eye to the other to find a pupil that dilates rather than constricts when the light moves to it, which means the retina or the optic nerve of that eye is not working.

The red eye is the commonest eye complaint and the one most often dismissed, so the work is to separate the many benign causes from the few dangerous ones. Infection of the conjunctiva produces a diffuse redness that is worst at the edges, a discharge that may be watery or thick, itching in the allergic form, and vision that stays normal once the discharge is wiped away. A corneal ulcer is different, because the pain is severe, light is unbearable, there is a feeling of something in the eye, the redness is concentrated around the cornea, and vision is blurred. The finding that reveals it is a defect on the cornea that stains with fluorescein and glows under blue light, and a contact lens wearer with a red and painful eye must be assumed to have one, because such an infection can destroy the cornea within a short time. Inflammation inside the eye, in the iris and the tissues around it, gives a deep aching pain, a dislike of light, a small pupil, and a rim of redness around the cornea, and it is often linked to a disease of the joints, the bowel, or the spine.

Acute angle closure glaucoma is the red eye that blinds, and it happens when the drainage angle at the edge of the iris closes and the fluid inside the eye can no longer leave. The pressure then rises quickly, the cornea becomes cloudy, and the optic nerve, which has no defense against pressure, begins to lose fibers. The patient is usually older and often farsighted, and describes severe pain in and around the eye, a headache, nausea and vomiting, and colored halos around lights. The examination finds a partly dilated pupil that does not react, a cornea that looks steamy, and an eye that feels harder than the other one through the closed lid. The finding that would make a calm diagnosis of simple conjunctivitis wrong is any of these together with a red eye that hurts deeply and disturbs vision. A figure such as 21 millimeters of mercury is often quoted as the upper limit of normal pressure inside the eye, but it is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the instruments, the technique, and the accepted range differ between services. The decision that follows is urgent referral for treatment that lowers the pressure, and the finding that would change that decision is a clear alternative such as a superficial infection with discharge, itching, and normal vision.

Giant cell arteritis is an inflammation of the large and medium arteries of the head and neck, and it matters because it can close the artery that supplies the eye and leave the patient blind in one eye and then in the other. The patient is usually older than 50 years, and though that age is offered as a guide and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, the reasoning behind it is that the disease becomes common only in later life. The symptoms before the visual loss are a new headache, tenderness of the scalp when the hair is brushed, pain in the jaw or the tongue when the patient chews or talks, pain in the shoulders and hips, and a general sense of illness. The visual loss may be sudden and painless, or it may come in brief spells that recover, and either pattern demands immediate action. The finding that supports the diagnosis is a pale and swollen optic disc when the back of the eye is examined, together with a raised sedimentation rate or another marker of inflammation in the blood, and a sample of the temporal artery examined under the microscope confirms it, though the result may take days. Because the loss can become permanent while those results are awaited, the decision to begin treatment is made on suspicion, and the finding that would make that decision wrong is a different cause that fully explains the picture.

In a well-resourced hospital the eye is examined with a slit lamp, which brings a magnified beam of light onto the surface and allows the cornea, the front chamber, the iris, and the lens to be seen in detail. Fluorescein is used with a blue light to show a defect in the cornea, and the depth of the front chamber is judged to decide whether the angle is narrow and whether the pupil can be safely dilated. The pressure inside the eye is measured with an instrument that rests gently on the cornea, and the back of the eye is examined through a dilated pupil to see the retina, the optic disc, and the vessels. Where a detachment is suspected, an ultrasound of the eye can show the retina when blood or a cataract blocks the view. Each test answers a question that the bedside examination has already raised, and a test ordered without such a question produces a result that no one can use. The rule that protects the patient is simple: the eye that hurts deeply and sees halos is not an eye to dilate until the angle has been shown to be open.

The patient who cannot hear is asked whether the loss came suddenly or slowly, whether one ear or both are affected, and what accompanies it. A slowly progressive loss in both ears, worse in noisy places and in conversation against background sound, suggests the ordinary loss of age or of years of noise. A loss with a discharge, a perforated drum, or a change in pressure suggests disease of the canal, the drum, or the middle ear. Sudden loss in one ear with ringing, and sometimes with dizziness, is a different matter, because it may be an injury of the inner ear and it must be treated as an emergency of hearing, since the chance of recovery falls when the patient is seen late. The finding that reveals it is a loss that goes to the better ear when a vibrating fork is placed on the middle of the forehead, together with a normal canal and a normal drum, because a conductive loss sends the sound to the affected side while a sensorineural loss sends it to the healthy one. A tumor of the nerve that carries hearing and balance can produce a loss that is gradual and one-sided, often with ringing and unsteadiness, and it is a reason to image the pathway where imaging exists.

Sudden sensorineural hearing loss deserves its own place because it is the ear's equivalent of a stroke and because it is so easily attributed to wax. The loss usually appears over hours, and a figure such as 3 days is often quoted as the interval within which the diagnosis is made, but that interval is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the definitions in use differ between services and editions. The patient may wake with the loss, or notice it while using the telephone, and there is often a loud ringing and a sense of fullness rather than pain. The examination of the canal and the drum is normal, and the fork placed on the forehead is heard in the better ear, which places the fault beyond the middle ear. The decision to treat is made on the clinical picture and does not wait for a hearing test where access to one is delayed, and the finding that would make that decision wrong is an obvious cause in the canal or the middle ear that explains the loss completely. An audiometer measures the softest tone the patient can hear at each frequency, and the values it reports are set by the equipment and the reference tables in use and must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol.

A discharging ear has a short list of causes and one dangerous direction of spread. Infection of the canal, common after swimming or after scratching with a finger or a stick, gives itching, pain when the ear is pulled, and a scanty discharge, and the drum is usually intact and visible once the debris is cleared. Infection of the middle ear that has burst through the drum gives a discharge that follows pain and fever, and the patient often feels better once the drum has opened, though the infection continues behind it. A discharge that has lasted for weeks or months, with an unpleasant smell and material that looks like skin debris in the canal, may be a cholesteatoma, a growing collection of skin in the middle ear that destroys bone and can reach the brain. The spread of infection to the bone behind the ear produces mastoiditis, and its findings are swelling and redness over the bone, an ear pushed forward, and a fever, and a temperature such as 38 degrees Celsius supports it, though the line at which fever is called present varies with the site and the method of measurement and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. A facial weakness, a severe headache, a stiff neck, or a change in consciousness in a patient with an ear infection means that the infection has reached the nerve or the brain, and that patient needs urgent help. A discharging ear in a patient with diabetes or with immune defenses suppressed, especially with severe pain at night and fleshy tissue in the canal, may be a destructive infection of the base of the skull and is a diagnosis not to miss.

The blocked nose is usually a nuisance and occasionally a warning. A blockage that shifts from side to side with the position of the head, with sneezing, itching, and a clear discharge, is the allergic nose, and a blockage with a thick discharge, facial pain, and a poor sense of smell follows a cold or an infection of the sinuses. A blockage that is fixed to one side and does not clear, especially with a blood-stained discharge, a loss of smell, an ache in the face or the ear, and a lump in the neck, may be a tumor of the nose or the space behind it, and that possibility must be named because it is easily treated as a simple cold for months. Bleeding from the nose is anterior in most patients and comes from a visible vessel on the front part of the septum, but bleeding that pours backward into the throat, that comes from an older patient with a high pressure, or that follows an injury or a medicine that thins the blood is a different problem and may need expert packing. A torch and a spoon handle are enough to see the front of the nose and to decide whether the bleeding can be stopped simply or whether the patient must travel.

Sore throat is the commonest reason for a visit and the place where a dangerous cause hides inside a familiar one. Most sore throats are viral, with a cough, a runny nose, a hoarse voice, and a fever that settles, and they need no specific treatment beyond comfort. Infection with the streptococcus bacterium is more likely when there is fever, pus on the tonsils, tender lymph nodes in the neck, and no cough, and the Centor score is one attempt to formalize that reasoning; its contents and any cut-off attached to it are an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the thresholds differ between editions and populations. The finding that changes everything is asymmetry: a swelling on one side of the throat that pushes the uvula to the other side, a voice that sounds muffled, drooling, and difficulty opening the mouth mean a collection of pus beside the tonsil. A collection behind the throat is more common in children, and it produces a stiff neck, a refusal to swallow, a muffled voice, and difficulty breathing, and the neck may look full on one side. Neither collection responds to tablets alone, and both require expert drainage, so the decision is where the patient should be examined next rather than which medicine to use.

The sore throat that threatens the airway is the one that must be recognized without touching the patient. Inflammation of the flap that covers the windpipe, which is rare but lethal, produces a sudden severe sore throat, a muffled voice, drooling, a high fever, and a posture in which the patient sits forward with the chin up and the arms braced, and the breathing is noisy and labored. The patient looks frightened and still, and the throat should not be examined with a tongue depressor in this setting, because the act can close the airway completely. The finding that reveals the danger is stridor, a harsh noise heard on inspiration, together with drooling and a refusal to swallow, and any of these in a patient who is febrile and unwell must bring senior help and equipment to the bedside rather than a walk to another room.

A tumor of the head and neck announces itself in ordinary ways, and the clinician who waits for a dramatic sign will miss it. The risk is concentrated in people who use tobacco and drink alcohol heavily, and it is also seen in people with a long-standing infection of the throat or with a poor diet. The symptoms to take seriously are hoarseness or a change in the voice that has lasted more than a few weeks, a sore throat on one side that does not settle, blood in the saliva, a lump in the neck, an ulcer in the mouth that will not heal, pain in one ear with a normal ear, and a blockage of one side of the nose. The interval that turns an ordinary hoarseness into a concern is commonly given as a few weeks, and that interval is offered as an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the recommended period differs between services and editions. The finding that matters most is a neck lump in an adult that is firm, painless, and fixed, which is a cancer until proved otherwise, and a single enlarged node on one side of the neck deserves examination of the whole upper airway and food passage rather than a course of antibiotics. The decision is referral to a center where the airway can be examined directly and a sample taken, and the finding that would make that decision less urgent is a clear infection that resolves completely with the expected time course.

In the hospital the examination of the ear, nose and throat is extended by instruments that answer precise questions. An audiometer measures hearing thresholds across frequencies and separates a conductive loss from a sensorineural one, and a test of the movement of the drum and the small bones shows whether the middle ear is working. A flexible instrument passed through the nose allows the space behind the nose, the base of the tongue, and the voice box to be seen directly, which is the decisive step in a patient with hoarseness or a neck lump. A computed tomography, or CT, scan of the sinuses, the ear, or the neck shows bone and the spread of infection, and a magnetic resonance imaging, or MRI, scan shows the soft tissues, the nerves, and the brain more clearly, and each is chosen only when its result would change the plan. A biopsy of a lump, an ulcer, or the lining of the nose gives the diagnosis that no image can give. The clinician who knows which question is unanswered will use the hospital well, and the clinician who orders everything will be left with findings that no one can interpret.

Where the clinic has only a torch, a spoon, and a thermometer, the same reasoning is carried out with fewer instruments and no less care. The torch examines the pupil and its reaction, and the light swung from one eye to the other will show a pupil that fails to constrict, which points to the retina or the nerve behind it. The back of the eye can be seen only with an instrument, but the red glow reflected from the pupil through the torch shows that the media are clear, and the bowl of a polished spoon held close to the eye and lit from the side can give a crude magnified view of the front of the eye. A torch held at the outer side of the eye and shone across the front of the eye casts a shadow on the inner part of the iris when the angle is narrow, which is a warning not to dilate the pupil. Hearing is tested with a whispered voice at arm's length in a quiet room, with the other ear masked by rubbing a finger against it if a second person is available to help. The spoon handle depresses the tongue to see the throat and the tonsils, but never in the patient who is drooling and struggling to breathe. The thermometer, the pulse, and the breathing rate turn the impression of illness into something recordable, and the decision is again about the journey rather than the label.

The decision that follows any of these assessments is usually not a diagnosis but a plan, and the plan should say what finding would change it. Referral now is the right answer when sight or hearing is threatened, when the airway is in question, when a collection of pus is suspected, when a lump is firm and fixed, or when the patient is febrile and unwell. Watching at home is the right answer when the findings are those of a simple illness that will settle, when the patient understands what to return for, and when a review has been arranged rather than merely promised. The clinician should name the signs that must bring the patient back: a curtain across the vision, a sudden loss of hearing, a red eye that hurts and blurs, a swelling in the neck that grows, difficulty breathing or swallowing, and a fever that will not settle.

To summarize, the eye and the ear, nose and throat are best approached by asking first what the symptom threatens and where the fault lies, since the same complaint of poor vision or poor hearing may come from a surface that will heal or from a nerve that will not. The dangerous causes are named where their symptoms appear: acute angle closure glaucoma with a painful red eye, a hazy cornea, and halos; a detached retina with flashes, floaters, and a curtain; a corneal ulcer in a contact lens wearer; giant cell arteritis in an older patient with headache, scalp tenderness, and jaw pain; sudden sensorineural hearing loss in a normal-looking ear; a collection of pus beside or behind the throat; an airway threatened by inflammation of the flap over the windpipe; and a tumor of the head and neck presenting as hoarseness, an ulcer, or a firm neck lump. The bedside examination, done with a torch, a spoon, and a whispered voice, or with a slit lamp, an audiometer, and a scanner, is chosen to answer the question of place, and the reasoning transfers between the two settings even when the instruments do not. Every figure in this chapter, whether a temperature, an age, an interval, a threshold, or a reference range, is an illustration of that reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such numbers differ between countries, editions, and laboratories. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service. A patient whose sight, hearing, or breathing is at risk is helped first, and the clinician who arranges the next step clearly has done the most important part of the work.
