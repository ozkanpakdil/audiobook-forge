# Chapter 19 — Genitourinary and Breast Examination

Some regions of the body are examined only when a question demands it, and the genitalia and the breast are the clearest examples. This examination is not a tour performed because a form has a space for it, but a small set of specific questions put to the body in an order chosen by the history. The reasoning begins with the symptom that brought the patient in, moves to the structures that could produce it, and stops once the question has an answer. What separates this examination from every other in this book is not the anatomy but the meaning it holds for the person on the couch, and that meaning changes how it must be conducted before a hand touches the skin. This is educational material, and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the local protocol of your own setting.

Consent is the first instrument of this examination, and it is a conversation rather than a signature. The patient should be told what will be examined, why, what it may feel like, how long it is likely to take, and that any part may be refused without losing your care. Consent to a plan is not consent to each step, because a patient may accept the conversation and decline the speculum, and that refusal is complete and legitimate. Consent is not permanent either, and a patient who stiffens, withdraws, or asks you to stop during the examination has withdrawn it at that moment. In an emergency the urgency may change how much can be explained in advance, but it never removes the duty to explain as soon as the patient can hear it. A clinician who explains plainly before touching usually finds the patient relaxes, and the examination becomes both easier and more accurate.

The chaperone is offered rather than imposed, and the offer is part of the examination rather than an administrative extra. A chaperone witnesses what was done, assists with positioning and instruments, reassures the patient, and protects the clinician from a misunderstanding that neither party intended. In many services the chaperone is a colleague of the same sex as the patient, and the sex of the examiner and of the chaperone may matter greatly to the person being examined. Where a chaperone is offered and declined, that offer belongs in the record, and where none can be found at all, an urgent examination is still performed with a clear explanation of why. A non-urgent examination that cannot be chaperoned may reasonably wait for another time or another clinician, and that decision is a clinical one. The same courtesy applies when a student or visitor wishes to observe, because the patient may refuse an audience without refusing care.

Privacy is not a curtain pulled halfway, and it is often the resource that is scarcest. The examination should take place in a room, behind a screen, or at least at a distance from other patients, with exposure limited to the part being examined and everything else kept covered. The clinician should describe each step before it happens, use a sheet or a gown to preserve dignity, and avoid the casual exposure that a busy ward makes tempting. Where beds are separated only by curtains, the examination can often be timed for a quieter moment, and the conversation that surrounds it can be held in a lower voice. Where a relative or a neighbour insists on staying, the patient should be asked privately whether that is really what the patient wants, because in small communities the person in the next chair is often known to everyone. A private space improvised well is worth more here than an elegant room used badly.

The history decides what the examination must answer. The clinician asks about discharge and its color, amount, and smell; about ulcers, lumps, and warts; about pain and its relation to the menstrual cycle, to intercourse, and to passing urine; about bleeding between periods, after intercourse, or after the menopause; and about the stream of urine, its frequency, and whether it hurts. The sexual history is taken without judgement and with enough detail to make the examination and the tests meaningful, including the number and sex of partners, the use of protection, and any previous sexually transmitted infection. The possibility of pregnancy is established in every woman of reproductive age who has abdominal pain or bleeding, because that single question reorders everything that follows. Previous surgery, previous childbirth, contraception, and the date of the last menstrual period are recorded for the same reason, since each changes what the examiner expects to feel. This is also the point at which the examiner decides whether the examination is needed at all, and what its smallest useful form would be.

The dangerous possibility in this region must be named at the outset, and the first is a pregnancy implanted outside the uterus. Any woman of reproductive age with lower abdominal pain, vaginal bleeding, fainting, or shoulder tip pain may have a ruptured ectopic pregnancy, which can bleed into the abdomen and kill within hours, and since the speed of that deterioration cannot be known in advance, the time frame that should set your urgency must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. The findings that reveal it are tenderness with guarding across the lower abdomen, shoulder tip pain from blood irritating the diaphragm, and signs of shock such as a pulse above 100 beats per minute with a falling blood pressure; the rate that should alarm you must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol because it shifts with age, pregnancy, and fitness. A pregnancy test is the single answer that must be obtained, and where no test exists the reasoning is unchanged and the patient is treated as though the diagnosis were possible until a safer explanation is proved. A negative test makes the diagnosis unlikely but not impossible, and a woman who is unstable is resuscitated and referred before any pelvic examination is completed.

The second dangerous possibility is a twisted testis, and it is a diagnosis that must be reached before the patient leaves the room. A boy or young man with sudden severe pain in one side of the scrotum, often waking him at night and often with nausea or vomiting, may have torsion of the testis, in which the blood supply is strangulated. The findings that point to it are a testis that sits high and lies across rather than lengthwise, an absent cremasteric reflex on that side, and pain that does not ease when the scrotum is lifted. Torsion is a surgical emergency with a window measured in hours, and the exact interval that matters must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol because it varies between centers and editions. A tender swollen scrotum in a young patient is torsion until proved otherwise, because the cost of waiting is the loss of a testis while the cost of exploring unnecessarily is small.

The third is infection ascending from the cervix into the uterus and the fallopian tubes, which is pelvic inflammatory disease. A woman with lower abdominal pain, fever, discharge, pain on deep intercourse, and bleeding between periods may have this condition, and the findings that reveal it are tenderness when the cervix is moved, tenderness beside the uterus, and a fever with discharge visible at the cervix. The immediate danger is an abscess beside the uterus, and the later danger is the scar it leaves, which can block a tube and lead to infertility or to a future ectopic pregnancy. The diagnosis is made clinically, and although several scores and thresholds exist, their values differ between populations and editions, so confirm what your service uses against the current guideline and the local protocol. Treatment is offered when the clinical picture and the risk that the patient will not return make the diagnosis likely, and it is deferred only when an alternative explanation is firm and follow-up is certain.

The fourth is a breast mass that is fixed, and fixation is the finding that changes the direction of the consultation. A hard, irregular lump that cannot be moved over the chest wall or over the skin, especially with dimpling of the skin, retraction of the nipple, a bloody nipple discharge, or a firm node in the armpit, must be treated as malignant until proved otherwise. Fixation means the tumor has invaded the tissues around it, and a fixed mass together with skin change and an enlarged regional node is the constellation that requires urgent referral rather than observation. Most breast lumps are not cancer, and the examiner must not turn every benign thickening into a sentence of doom, but the decision to reassure rests on a full examination and on the tools available. A mass in a man, a mass with a bloody discharge, and a mass in an older woman all raise the same question and take the same path, and the current guideline and the local protocol should be consulted for where that path leads in your setting.

A fifth danger belongs to the skin rather than to one organ, and it is the necrotizing infection of the perineum. A rapidly spreading, exquisitely painful swelling of the scrotum or the vulva, with dusky or blistered skin, a crackling sensation under the fingers, and a patient who looks systemically unwell, is a surgical emergency that kills quickly when it is mistaken for a simple abscess. The finding that reveals it is pain far greater than the visible change, together with skin that has lost its normal color and gas in the tissues felt as crepitus. This diagnosis is named early because the reasoning that saves the patient is a habit of asking, whenever a swelling in this region is unusually painful and unusually fast, what infection is spreading beneath the skin. The well-resourced hospital and the bare clinic differ in how quickly they can reach a surgeon, but not in the need to recognize this picture.

The examination of the external genitalia begins with inspection in good light and in a position the patient has agreed to. The patient lies on the back with the knees bent and the thighs apart, covered so that only the area being examined is visible, and the clinician asks before touching. The skin is examined for redness, ulceration, warts, vesicles, scratching marks, wasting, swelling, and asymmetry, and the groin is inspected and palpated for enlarged or tender nodes. Even in a well-lit room a torch is still useful, because a small ulcer or a subtle color change can be missed in shadow. The patient is told what is seen and what it may mean, in words that are honest and not alarming, because silence during this examination is experienced as judgement. What the examiner looks for is set by the history, so a woman with discharge is examined differently from one with an ulcer or a lump.

The speculum converts the vagina and the cervix from a history into a visible surface. With consent and with the smallest comfortable instrument, the cervix is inspected for discharge, for an outward turning of its surface, for bleeding on contact, for ulceration, for a polyp, and for any change in appearance, and the walls of the vagina are examined at the same time. Swabs or samples are taken according to what is available, and what is taken is determined by the question the clinician is asking rather than by habit. The bimanual examination follows, in which 2 fingers are placed in the vagina and the other hand presses on the lower abdomen, so that the uterus and the tissues beside it can be felt between the hands. The examiner asks about the size, position, mobility, and tenderness of the uterus, and about any fullness or mass beside it. A cervix that hurts when moved, an enlarged or irregular uterus, and a tender fullness beside the uterus are the findings that carry the reasoning forward.

What the pelvic examination reveals is rarely a diagnosis by itself, and it is always a set of findings to be assembled. An enlarged uterus may mean pregnancy, a fibroid, or a tumor, and the history together with a pregnancy test separates them. A uterus that is fixed and tender may indicate infection or endometriosis, while a painless fixed mass may indicate cancer. A fullness beside the uterus may be an ovary, a cyst, an abscess, an ectopic pregnancy, or a normal structure felt differently because of the position of the patient. A cervix that bleeds on contact may be an outward turning of its surface, an infection, a polyp, or a cancer, and the hand alone cannot make that distinction. This is why the examination is described as part of a chain, in which each finding narrows the list and none of them closes it.

The prostate is reached through the back passage, and the examination is brief, informative, and usually feared more than it deserves. With consent, with lubrication, and with the patient lying on the side or leaning forward, 1 finger is passed gently into the rectum, and the prostate is felt through the wall in front. The examiner describes the size, consistency, the presence of the groove, the mobility of the surface, and any hard nodule or area of tenderness. A smooth enlarged rubbery gland suggests benign enlargement, a hard irregular nodule suggests cancer, and an exquisitely tender gland suggests acute inflammation, in which case vigorous pressure is avoided. The same examination detects blood, a rectal mass, and other pelvic disease, so it is not wasted when the prostate feels normal. Where a blood test for prostate specific antigen is available, its result is interpreted with the examination and the history, and the value that prompts referral must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol because the threshold differs with age and between services.

The male genitalia are examined with the patient standing or lying, in privacy and with a chaperone if the patient wishes, and inspection begins before the hand. The penis is inspected for ulcers, warts, vesicles, discharge, scars, and for a foreskin that cannot be drawn back or, once back, cannot be returned, which is a painful emergency of its own. The scrotum is inspected for asymmetry, redness, swelling, and visible veins, and each testis is palpated gently between the fingers, with the epididymis felt behind it. A swelling that is separate from the testis and glows when a torch is held against it is likely to be fluid, while a swelling that replaces the testis must be regarded with suspicion. A cough impulse at the groin, a soft fullness that disappears when the patient lies down, and a bluish cluster of veins that enlarges on standing each point to a different structural answer.

The breast is examined by inspection first, because a change in the skin and the nipple is often visible before anything is felt. The patient sits with the arms at the sides, then raises them above the head, then presses the hands onto the hips to tighten the muscle beneath, and the examiner compares the 2 sides for asymmetry, dimpling, retraction, and change in the nipple or the skin. Palpation then uses the flat pads of the fingers rather than the tips, with the patient first sitting and then lying with the arm raised on the side being examined, so the breast spreads over the chest wall. The examiner works systematically through every part of the breast, including the tail that reaches into the armpit, and then examines the armpit and the area above the collarbone for nodes. A gentle squeeze of the nipple checks for discharge, and the other breast is examined in the same way, because comparison is itself an instrument. What the examiner seeks is a dominant lump distinct from the general texture of the breast, and that texture changes with age, with the cycle, and with hormonal state.

Breast self-awareness is taught as knowledge rather than as a ritual of fixed strokes in a fixed order. The patient is encouraged to know how her breasts normally look and feel, so that a change becomes noticeable, and to report a new lump, a change in the skin, a change in the nipple, or a discharge that is new or bloody. The message is not that a particular method of self-examination has been proved to save lives, because the evidence for formal self-examination is not what it was once claimed to be, but that a woman who knows her own body is a better observer of change than a clinician who sees her once. The clinician should say plainly that most changes are not cancer and that reporting them is not an overreaction. Where mammography or another imaging service exists, the starting age and the interval between examinations are matters for the current guideline and the local protocol, and both must be confirmed there because they differ between countries and editions.

Discharge is described before it is interpreted, because the description carries most of what a laboratory would add. The examiner records the color, amount, consistency, smell, and whether it comes with itching, burning, pain on intercourse, bleeding, or lower abdominal pain. A thin gray discharge with an unpleasant smell suggests an overgrowth of the normal vaginal bacteria, a thick white discharge with itching suggests a yeast infection, and a yellow or green discharge with burning suggests a sexually transmitted infection of the cervix. Several infections commonly coexist, so a single label found in one sample should not close the search. Where microscopy exists, the salt solution preparation and the measurement of the acidity of the vaginal fluid add precision, and the cut-off separating normal from abnormal must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol because laboratories and populations differ. In the clinic with no laboratory, the syndromic approach treats the likely group of causes together and asks the patient to return if the picture does not settle.

Ulceration of the genitalia is read from its pain, its number, its edge, and its nodes. A single painless ulcer with a clean base and firm, non-tender nodes suggests syphilis, while multiple painful ulcers with a red base and tender nodes suggest herpes, and a ragged painful ulcer with swollen matted nodes suggests other infections. The examiner must remember that these patterns overlap, that several infections may be present at once, and that a genital ulcer is a marker of risk for the human immunodeficiency virus, or HIV, so testing for HIV should be offered wherever it is available. The examination includes the whole genital area, the anus, the mouth, and the skin, because the same infection can appear in several places at once. The patient is asked about partners, because an infection treated in one person and not in another will return. Where no laboratory exists the reasoning is unchanged, and the decision to treat rests on the most likely cause while the other possibilities are kept in mind.

Pain in this region is interrogated with the discipline used for pain anywhere else, and its timing is often the whole answer. Pain that comes with the period suggests endometriosis or an ordinary cramp, pain with intercourse may be at the entrance or deep in the pelvis, and pain on passing urine points to the bladder or the urethra. Sudden severe pain in a testis is a different emergency from a dull ache that has grown over months, and the sudden version must be acted on first. Pain that is cyclical and improving, pain that is constant and worsening, and pain brought on by movement each suggest a different mechanism. The examiner also asks what the pain does to sleep and to daily function, because the answer reveals how much the patient is suffering and what the patient hopes to have explained.

Structural disease of the urinary and genital tract declares itself through obstruction, leakage, or descent. A bladder that cannot empty produces a swelling above the pubic bone that is dull to percussion and uncomfortable to press, and it is a relief to find because a catheter or a referral changes the patient's night. A stream that has become thin suggests a narrowing of the urethra, blood in the urine with pain suggests a stone, and blood without pain raises the question of a tumor and requires investigation. Prolapse of the uterus or the vaginal wall is seen when the patient is examined standing or straining, and the examiner should look for it rather than assume that a patient's description of a lump is inaccurate. A channel between the bladder or the rectum and the vagina produces continuous leakage, and it is easy to miss and devastating to live with.

Pregnancy changes what the examination looks for and what it must not disturb. The uterus enlarges, the cervix becomes softer and bluish, and the structures beside it are pushed away, so the examiner reads every finding against the stage of the pregnancy. Bleeding in early pregnancy raises the same ectopic question as before, and pain with bleeding in later pregnancy raises the question of separation of the placenta, which is an emergency for 2 patients at once. Where the pregnancy is beyond the first months, the examination is gentler and the decision to examine at all is made with the safety of the pregnancy in mind. The period after childbirth brings its own findings, including infection of the uterus, clots, and tears, and the examiner should not assume that a fever after childbirth comes from the breast alone. Each of these situations has its own threshold for referral, and those thresholds must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol for the stage of pregnancy and for the setting.

In a well-resourced hospital the examination is extended by machines that answer precise questions. Ultrasound at the bedside can show a pregnancy inside the uterus or outside it, can measure a mass, and can show fluid in the pelvis, and it is used to answer the question the hands have raised rather than to replace them. A laboratory can identify an infection, measure a hormone, or count cells, and imaging can map a tumor before surgery. Each result is interpreted together with the examination, because a normal scan does not remove a diagnosis that the clinical picture strongly suggests, and an abnormal scan does not by itself explain the patient's pain. The availability of these tools also changes the pace of the encounter, because a question that would wait a long time in a clinic may be answered within a single visit. The examiner must still decide what each result would change before ordering it.

In a clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, the same examination is performed with fewer confirmations and more careful reasoning. The torch replaces the bright lamp, the thermometer replaces the laboratory for fever, and the clinician's hands replace the scanner for the size and position of a mass. The syndromic approach is used for discharge and for ulcers, treating the most likely group of causes together and asking the patient to return if the picture does not resolve. A urine dipstick, where it exists, adds information about infection and blood, and a pregnancy test, where it exists, settles the question that matters most in a woman with abdominal pain. Where neither exists, the clinician refers or treats on the strength of the history and the examination, and says plainly what is uncertain. The limited clinic is not a lesser clinic; it is the same reasoning with fewer places to hide.

A normal examination of this region does not exclude serious disease, and the clinician must resist the comfort it offers. Early cancer may be a thickening rather than a lump, an ectopic pregnancy may be present before it ruptures, and an infection may sit deep in the pelvis without a visible discharge. The safeguard is the same as everywhere else in this book, which is to tell the patient what to watch for, where to return, and when the picture should be reviewed. A patient who leaves with a plan for what to do if things change is safer than one who leaves with reassurance alone. Where the examination was limited by pain, by refusal, or by the setting, that limitation is recorded and addressed at the next opportunity. The examination ends with words, and those words decide what the patient understands and what the patient does next.

To summarize, the genitourinary and breast examination is governed as much by manner as by technique, and the manner is part of the diagnosis rather than a courtesy around it. Consent is explained, a chaperone is offered, privacy is protected, and the examination is limited to what the question requires, in the hospital and in the clinic where privacy itself is scarce. The dangerous possibilities are named where the presentation is described, among them ectopic pregnancy, torsion of the testis, pelvic inflammatory disease, a breast mass fixed to the chest wall, and the spreading infection of the perineum, and each is sought through the finding that reveals it. Every score, cut-off, reference range, pressure, temperature, rate, and duration in this chapter is an illustration only, and each must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such numbers differ between countries, editions, and laboratories. This book is educational material; it is not medical advice, it is not a protocol, and it is not a substitute for training, supervision, or the judgment of the clinician who is with the patient.
