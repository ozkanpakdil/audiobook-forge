# Chapter 71 — Flowcharts and Algorithms

An algorithm is a route rather than an answer, and a flowchart is that route written down so that two different clinicians can walk it the same way. The subject of this chapter is the flowchart heard instead of seen, because nothing in this book can be shown and the listener has to follow the path by ear. The reasoning is straightforward, since a clinician under pressure does not need a list of every possible diagnosis but the next question, the finding that answers it, and the decision that follows, in the order in which time allows them to be asked. An algorithm supplies that order, and it does so best when the person using it understands why each step sits where it does. The dangerous possibilities are named first in every path worth following, which is the whole reason the path is written down at all. This chapter is educational material and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of the service in which the patient is seen.

Before any path is walked, the lethal possibilities deserve to be named, because each is revealed by a finding that can be sought within the first minutes. The febrile child who is drowsy, whose hands are cold, and who develops a rash that does not fade when a clear glass is pressed upon it may have an infection of the bloodstream, and that rash turns a routine fever into an emergency. The breathless adult who cannot finish a sentence, whose chest sounds silent when listened to, or who is visibly tiring may be close to failure of the breathing, and the silence of the chest and the effort of the shoulders reveal it. The patient with chest discomfort who sweats, vomits, and feels the pain move into the jaw or the arm may be having an acute myocardial infarction, and a normal examination does not exclude it, so the history and the electrocardiogram, or ECG, carry the decision together. The patient who collapses with a tearing pain that moves between the chest and the back, and whose pulses or pressures differ between the two arms, may have a dissection of the aorta. The patient who is pale, clammy, confused, and passing very little urine is in shock, whatever the cause, and shock is a decision to act rather than a diagnosis to complete.

Altered consciousness belongs in the same opening list, because several of its causes are reversible and one of them is fatal within hours if it is not recognized. A patient who is confused or unrousable may have a low level of sugar in the blood, and that is a question a meter answers in seconds, while a clinic without one must still treat the possibility as the first item on its path. The same patient may have a slow bleed inside the head, an infection of the brain or its coverings, a stroke, a poisoning, or a failure of the liver or the kidney, and the examination of the pupils, the limbs, the neck, and the smell of the breath narrows the field. The finding that most often changes the immediate plan is a difference between the two sides of the body, which speaks of a stroke, or a fever with a stiff neck, which speaks of infection. These are the patients for whom an algorithm is at its most useful and at its most dangerous, because the path has to be entered at the right door.

Algorithms exist because attention is finite and because the same mistake is made at the same point by one clinician after another. A well-made path puts the dangerous question before the comfortable one, forces the observations to be recorded rather than remembered, and makes the next step independent of the mood of the moment or the hour of the shift. It also makes care consistent, so that two clinicians handed the same patient would at least begin in the same place. The reasoning it formalizes is not new, because it is the sequence an experienced clinician follows without noticing, set down so that a tired one can follow it too. The cost of that clarity is rigidity, and much of this chapter is about holding the clarity without accepting the rigidity.

To hear a flowchart is to hear a series of questions with two or three exits, and the listener can hold the shape in mind if the structure is spoken plainly. Each step is a question followed by the answers that are possible and the place each answer leads, so the path unfolds as a route rather than as a picture. The walk ends when a step names an action instead of another question, and that action is almost always to treat now, to test, to watch, or to send the patient onward. A path learned this way is remembered as a journey through a building, with turns that can be pictured and repeated at the bedside. A listener who can say the path back to a colleague has learned it, while a listener who can only recognize it on a page has not.

Every algorithm assumes something about the patient in front of the clinician, and asking what it assumes is the discipline that keeps it safe. It assumes that the patient resembles the people in whom the path was studied, so a rule built for adults may mislead in a child and a rule built in one climate may mislead in another. It assumes that the first question can be answered, which is false when the patient cannot speak, when the family is absent, or when the record is missing. It assumes that every branch is available, so a path that forks toward a laboratory test is not the same path in a clinic that has none. It assumes that the finding it asks for means the same thing in every pair of hands, which is untrue of a murmur, a rash, or the feel of an abdomen. The clinician who names those assumptions aloud before starting has already improved on the chart.

The febrile child is the clearest example of a path that saves lives when it is followed and harms when it is abandoned halfway. The first question is the age of the child, because a fever in a newborn is handled as a serious illness until it is proved otherwise. The next is the general appearance, because a child who is alert, feeding, and playing is a different problem from a child who is limp, irritable, or inconsolable. The path then asks how the child is breathing and how the circulation is behaving, judged by the color of the skin, the warmth of the hands, and the speed with which the color returns after a finger is pressed. Any point that reaches a red exit sends the child for urgent help and treatment rather than for a period of waiting, and a rash that does not fade under pressure is one of those exits. Where the child looks well and no red exit is found, the path turns to the search for a focus, which is the examination of the throat, the ears, the chest, the abdomen, the skin, and the urine.

The edges of that path are where it misleads, and the clinician should know them before following it. A child in a region where malaria is transmitted may need the question about travel and exposure before any other, because the disease can kill within days and the examination can be deceptively calm. A child whose immune defenses are suppressed may never produce the appearance the path expects, and a child who has already received an antibiotic may look better than the illness warrants. Age shifts every line, since a fever that might be watched in a sturdy older child is treated at once in a baby of a few weeks, and the exact age at which that changes must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because services draw the boundary in different places. In a clinic with no laboratory, the branches that ask for a blood count or a culture are replaced by the decision to treat on the clinical picture and to arrange the journey. A child who cannot be watched at home must travel even when the examination is reassuring, and that decision is part of the algorithm rather than a departure from it.

The breathless adult asks a different first question, which is whether the patient is about to fail. The path begins with the ability to speak, the rate and the effort of breathing, the use of the muscles of the neck and the spaces between the ribs, and the level of consciousness, because a patient who is tiring is treated before any cause is named. It then asks whether the breathlessness came suddenly or over days, because sudden onset sends the reasoning toward a clot in the lung, a collapsed lung, a rhythm that has failed, or fluid flooding the lungs. It asks about fever and cough, about chest pain, about swelling of the legs, and about the medicines the patient takes, since a common cause is a heart that has been pushed into failure. The examination listens to both lungs and to the heart, feels the pulse and the pressure, looks at the veins in the neck, and presses the legs. A patient who cannot lie flat is a patient whose lungs are congested until it is proved otherwise, and that single finding reorganizes the whole path.

Here the hospital and the clinic follow the same logic with different instruments. The hospital branch that asks for a chest radiograph, an ultrasound of the heart, a blood count, or a measurement of oxygen in the blood turns a good question into a picture, and the clinician must know which question each test is answering. The clinic branch cannot ask those questions, so it asks the same thing with the stethoscope, the thermometer, the watch, and the hands, and it reaches a decision about travel rather than a decision about a diagnosis. The error at the edge is to treat an unavailable test as though it had been performed and found normal, which is the same as declaring the clot absent because the scanner is far away. The correct move is to say plainly that a question could not be answered, to act as though the dangerous answer were possible, and to write down why.

Chest pain is the presentation where an algorithm earns its place most quickly, because the lethal causes are few and the time is short. The path asks first whether the patient is unstable, judged by consciousness, breathing, pulse, pressure, and the color of the skin, and an unstable patient is treated and moved rather than questioned further. It then asks what the discomfort is like, where it sits, where it travels, what brings it on, what relieves it, and how the pattern has changed, and the Socrates framework is a way of keeping that account complete. It asks about a tearing pain that moves to the back, about breathlessness and a swollen leg, about fever and a new murmur, and about the risk factors for a clot. Where an ECG exists it is performed early and repeated, because a single tracing can look innocent at the wrong moment, and where a laboratory exists the substances released by damaged heart muscle are measured in sequence. In the clinic with no ECG, the only safe exit for a new or changing chest discomfort is a journey to a place where the tracing can be done.

The edge of the chest pain path is the patient who does not look ill. An acute myocardial infarction can produce a normal examination, a normal pressure, and a normal pulse, and a clinician who waits for the picture to declare itself has already lost the time that mattered. The same is true of the young patient, in whom the path is often abandoned because the risk seems low, and of the older patient or the patient with diabetes, in whom the pain may be slight or absent and the presentation may be breathlessness, sweating, or vomiting instead. A dissection of the aorta is missed because the pain is attributed to something ordinary, and the finding that should stop the walk is a difference between the two arms in pulse or in pressure. The lesson is that the path must be entered on suspicion rather than on certainty, because the purpose of putting the dangerous question first is to ask it before the evidence is complete.

Shock is the clearest example of a presentation in which the algorithm is a sequence of actions rather than a sequence of thoughts. The path begins with the recognition that the circulation is failing, which is seen in a rapid pulse, a rapid breathing rate, cool or mottled skin, confusion, and a passage of urine that has shrunk, and every one of those lines shifts with age and with the usual state of the patient, so it must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol rather than carried in the head. The path then asks which broad pattern is present, whether blood loss, infection, failure of the pump, or loss of fluid through vomiting, diarrhea, or burns. It looks for the source of bleeding in the abdomen, the chest, the gut, and the pelvis, and for the signs of infection that make treatment urgent. It examines the heart for a murmur or a muffled sound, the lungs for crackles, the veins of the neck for their height, and the legs for swelling. The decision at the end is to restore the circulation and to call for senior help, and the diagnosis is settled while that is already happening.

Altered consciousness is the presentation where the algorithm must be entered at the fastest door, because several of its causes are reversible within minutes and one of them, a low level of sugar in the blood, is the reason every such patient is tested or treated for it first. The path then checks the airway, the breathing, and the circulation, because a patient who is not moving air or not moving blood will not improve whatever else is done. It asks about the speed of onset, about a fall or a blow to the head, about fever, about medicines and poisons, about alcohol, and about the illnesses the patient already carries, and it looks for a medicine list, a medical alert card, or a witness who can describe the hours before. The examination searches for a difference between the two sides of the body, for the size and the reaction of the pupils, for the stiffness of the neck, for the smell of the breath, and for any injury that does not fit the story. The Glasgow coma scale is one way of recording the level of consciousness by the response to voice, to pain, and to command, and the exact figure at which it should cause alarm belongs to the current source and the local protocol rather than to memory. A clinic with no meter must still treat the possibility of low sugar on the clinical picture, and a patient who does not return to normal must travel.

The danger at the edge of this path is the patient who is assumed to be drunk, tired, or simply old. A slow bleed inside the head, an infection, a stroke, and a disturbance of the body chemistry all present at the beginning as sleepiness, and the finding that should stop the walk is any asymmetry, any fever, any injury that does not fit the story, or any deterioration across successive observations. The clinician should also ask whether the usual state of this patient is known, because a family member's account of what is normal for this person is worth more than one examination. Where a medicine is the cause, the path turns toward reviewing what was prescribed and what was actually taken, and the decision that follows is a diagnostic one rather than a question of amounts. Where no cause is found and the patient is not returning to normal, the honest exit is referral, made before the period of observation has been stretched past what the setting can safely provide.

In a hospital with every option available, the algorithm grows longer, and the danger changes from missing a branch to losing the patient inside the machinery. Each test added to the path costs time, and a path that waits for a result before acting on an obvious emergency has replaced judgment with sequence. The hospital also rewards a habit the clinic cannot afford, which is to investigate an interesting question that would not change what is done, and the clinician must keep asking which decision each result would alter. The strength of the hospital is the ability to answer the questions the clinic had to leave open, so the path there should be read as a set of questions awaiting answers rather than as a single verdict. A clinician who carries the discipline of the clinic into the hospital, and who treats every test as a question rather than as an answer, uses both settings well.

The clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer cannot shorten the path, but it can translate it. The translation is made by asking what question each unavailable test was meant to answer and then finding another way to ask it. A test for anemia is replaced by looking at the color of the conjunctiva and the palms and by asking about bleeding. A test for dehydration is replaced by the state of the mouth, the skin, the pulse, and the urine, and a picture of the chest is replaced by the work of breathing, the symmetry of the breath sounds, and the history of what has changed. What cannot be translated at all becomes a referral decision, and that decision should be made explicitly rather than left to the judgment of the patient about how ill he or she is. The clinic also holds an instrument the hospital often forgets, which is time, because the same patient can be seen again and the comparison between visits is itself a test of great value.

An algorithm that has been translated in this way is no longer the published path, and the clinician should say so in the record rather than pretend otherwise. The record should show where the path was entered, which branch was taken, which branch could not be followed, and what was done instead, and the Soap notes format gives that account a familiar shape. That habit has three uses, because it makes the reasoning visible to the next clinician, it exposes the assumptions that were made, and it allows the path to be improved the next time the same problem is seen. It also protects the patient, since a documented plan for review is a promise that someone will look again. A path followed without any of this becomes a ritual, and a ritual is the form an algorithm takes when its reasoning has been forgotten.

An algorithm and a differential diagnosis are different tools, and confusing them is a common error. The algorithm orders the next few minutes, while the differential list widens the field so that nothing important is left out, and the Vindicate framework belongs to that second task. A clinician who uses only the path will handle the common presentation well and the unusual patient badly, because the path contains only the branches its authors thought of. A clinician who uses only the list will generate possibilities without deciding what to do first. The two work together when the list is written down and then narrowed by the path, and the reasoning is complete only when both have had their turn.

A constructed illustration may show the difference, and it is offered as a teaching device rather than as a portrait of any real person. Imagine an older patient brought to a clinic with a cough and a fever, breathing a little quickly, whose hands are warm, and in whom the path stops at the work of breathing and sends the patient onward instead of home with a diagnosis of a chest infection. Now imagine a child with a fever and a rash, whose mother says the rash does not fade when a glass is pressed on it, and in whom the path reaches its red exit in the first minute rather than after the throat has been examined. Neither patient is real, and neither story should be applied to a living person without a full assessment. What the two share is that the algorithm did not make the decision; it made sure the decision was faced in the right order.

An algorithm is also a language, and much of its practical value lies in the handover between clinicians. When the night team is told which branch was taken and which question remains unanswered, the next shift begins where the last one stopped instead of starting again. The same clarity serves the patient, who should be told what would bring them back rather than be given a diagnosis that may yet prove wrong. Safety-netting is the branch at the end of every path that says what to do if the patient does not improve, and a path without it is incomplete. The clinician should name the findings that would mean the plan was wrong and should write them where the patient or the family can see them. The algorithm does not remove uncertainty and should not pretend to, because its real gift is making uncertainty explicit enough to be managed.

To summarize, a flowchart is reasoning made audible, a route of questions whose exits lead to treatment, to testing, to watching, or to referral, and it is learned by walking it aloud rather than by looking at it. The dangerous causes are named at the entrance and not at the end, so the febrile child with a rash that does not fade, the breathless adult who is tiring, the patient with tearing chest pain and unequal pulses, the patient in shock, and the patient whose consciousness has changed are each met by the finding that reveals them. An algorithm clarifies the common path and misleads at the edges, and the discipline that keeps it safe is asking what it assumes about the patient in front of the clinician, which population it was built in, and which of its branches cannot be followed here. The same path is walked in a hospital with every option and in a clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, where an unavailable test becomes a question asked another way or a referral made deliberately rather than a result assumed to be normal. Every figure in this chapter, whether a rate, a pressure, a temperature, an age, or a duration, is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such numbers differ between countries, editions, and laboratories. This chapter is educational material and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service, and a patient who does not fit the path is a reason to leave it and think again rather than to force the patient into it.
