# Chapter 18 — Skin, Hair and Nails

The skin is the only organ a clinician can inspect in its entirety, and that abundance of information is exactly why it is so often examined badly. The eye arrives at the bedside with a name already prepared, and the moment a lesion resembles something seen before, description stops and recognition takes over. This chapter asks for the opposite order: describe first, in a fixed and shared vocabulary, and then let the description build the differential diagnosis instead of merely decorating it. The stakes are highest at the start, because a small number of skin findings announce disease that can kill within hours. The rash that does not fade when pressed, in a patient who has a fever, is the clearest of these, and it must be sought deliberately in every febrile patient rather than noticed by chance. This book is educational material, and nothing in it replaces training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of the place where you work.

The description begins with where the eruption is, not with what it looks like. Site means the exact anatomical region, while distribution means whether the lesions are scattered, grouped, linear, arranged along a nerve or a vessel, or confined to skin exposed to light. Symmetry matters because symmetric eruptions usually reflect an internal or systemic process, whereas one-sided eruptions more often reflect an external cause such as an injury, an infection, or a contact. The pattern on a limb can be read as a map of what touches the skin, since a rash that spares covered areas and involves exposed ones suggests something in the environment rather than something inside the patient. Recording distribution before morphology prevents the common error of describing one memorable lesion while forgetting the hundred around it, and a single lesion is a curiosity whereas a distribution is a diagnosis.

Color should be named plainly, as red, purple, brown, black, yellow, white, or flesh colored, and never as angry or suspicious, because those words tell the listener about the observer rather than the patient. The border is the next property, and the useful choices are sharp or ill defined, regular or irregular, raised or flat at the edge, and whether it is surrounded by a halo of redness. The surface may be smooth, scaly, greasy, crusted, weeping, pustular, or covered with a fine white scale that comes away on scratching. Size is measured rather than estimated, and the helpful habit is to compare the lesion with a familiar object such as the patient's fingertip or a coin, so that the number means the same thing to the next listener. Each property is a question with a small number of answers, and their combination narrows the field far more than any single impression, which is precision rather than pedantry.

The most important mechanical question is whether the lesion is flat or raised, and the second is whether it blanches when pressed. A flat lesion is a change in color without a change in level, while a raised one has substance that can be felt and often a border that can be traced with the finger. Blanching means the redness disappears briefly under pressure and returns when the pressure is released, which tells the clinician that the color comes from dilated blood vessels rather than from blood that has escaped them. A lesion that does not blanch, or that remains visible through the side of a clear glass tumbler pressed against the skin, indicates bleeding into the tissue and carries a different and more urgent meaning. The pressure test costs nothing and takes a second, and in a clinic with no laboratory the glass tumbler may be the most important instrument in the room.

The vocabulary of the surface carries information about depth that an untrained eye reads only as texture. Scale suggests the process lies in the outer layer of the skin and is shedding, while crust suggests dried fluid that may be serum, pus, or blood. Vesicles and bullae mean fluid has collected within or beneath the surface, and therefore that the process is deeper than scale alone would imply. Ulceration means the surface has been lost and the defect extends into the dermis, and it should be described by its edge, its base, and its discharge rather than by its size alone. Scarring is the permanent trace of damage deep enough to destroy the structures that normally let the skin regenerate. A vesicle that ruptures and leaves an erosion, a crust, or a scar has told the listener about the destructive power of the disease, which a photograph often cannot convey.

Once the description is complete, the differential diagnosis is built from three axes at once, namely morphology, distribution, and time course. A scaly rash on the elbows and knees that has been stable for years suggests a chronic inflammatory condition, while the same scale appearing suddenly over the trunk after a sore throat suggests a reaction to infection. A blistering eruption confined to one band of skin has one set of causes, and a blistering eruption scattered over the trunk and limbs has another, even when the individual blisters look identical. The time course is the axis most often neglected, because it must be asked for rather than seen, and whether a rash appeared over hours, days, or weeks changes the ranking more than any single feature of the lesion. A constructed illustration makes the point: a patient with a few red spots on the ankles, a normal temperature, and no other symptom may be reassured after a careful description, while another patient with the same spots, a fever, and a rash that does not blanch needs urgent help, and the difference lies in the whole picture rather than in the spots. Description, distribution, and time are the grammar of this subject, learnable in an afternoon and useful for a career.

The history that accompanies the skin is as important as the history that accompanies any other organ, and it is usually taken too quickly. The clinician should ask when the eruption began and how it has changed, what the patient has applied to it, what medicines have been taken in the preceding weeks, and whether anyone else in the household or the workplace has a similar problem. Occupation, travel, contact with animals, sun exposure, new cosmetics, and sexual history belong to this examination when the pattern suggests them. The patient's own words about itching, pain, tenderness, and whether the lesions are changing are evidence, and they should be recorded rather than translated into a diagnostic label. A medicine started weeks earlier is still a candidate cause, and a remedy bought without a prescription is still a medicine. The most common reason a skin diagnosis is missed is not that the lesion was difficult but that the history that would have explained it was never taken.

The examination itself is simple, but it must be complete, because the skin rewards the clinician who looks at all of it. The patient should be undressed enough to allow inspection of the trunk and limbs, with privacy protected and a chaperone offered wherever the local rule requires one. Daylight is the best light for judging color, and a torch is the next best, though a torch held close and at an angle flattens the surface and hides the relief that shows whether a lesion is raised. The scalp, the nails, the palms, the soles, the webs between the fingers and toes, the mouth, and the genital area are all part of the examination and are all commonly skipped. A rash described on the trunk alone, when the mouth and the palms were never inspected, is an incomplete description whatever its elegance. The clinician who examines systematically finds the second finding that changes the first impression, and that second finding is often the one that matters.

The non-blanching rash in a febrile patient is the finding that must be named early, because it can mark invasion of the bloodstream by bacteria and the start of a collapse that proceeds over hours. The organism most feared is the one that causes meningococcal disease, and the finding that reveals the danger is a purpuric or petechial lesion that does not fade under pressure in a patient who looks more unwell than the fever alone would explain. Early in the illness the rash may be sparse and may fade, so a single negative pressure test does not close the question, and the patient must be undressed fully and looked at again after an interval. Other causes, including viral infection, a low platelet count, and disease of the small blood vessels, can produce similar lesions, and the clinician must judge the whole patient rather than the rash in isolation. The decision to seek urgent help rests on the combination of fever, a rash that does not blanch, and a patient who appears to be deteriorating, and it must never wait for a laboratory result. The finding that would make simple observation the wrong answer is any non-blanching lesion in a febrile patient, whatever the patient's age and whatever the rest of the examination shows.

The blistering reaction to a medicine is the second eruption that can kill, and its earliest sign is often not a blister but a tender, spreading redness beginning on the trunk and face with soreness of the mouth or the eyes. When the surface begins to lift in sheets and the skin can be slid sideways with light pressure, the process has reached the deeper layers and the situation has become an emergency. Mucosal involvement, meaning erosions on the lips, inside the mouth, in the eyes, or in the genital area, is the finding that separates this reaction from the far more common and harmless drug rash. The clinician must ask what the patient has taken, including medicines bought without a prescription, herbal preparations, and remedies begun for another problem, because the culprit may have been started weeks earlier. Decisions here turn on how much surface is involved, how quickly it is spreading, and whether the patient can maintain their own airway and fluid balance, and those judgements belong in a facility that can support them. The finding that would make local treatment alone the wrong answer is any mucosal erosion, any skin that slides under pressure, or any patient who is becoming systemically unwell.

The spreading infection of the soft tissues is the third emergency, and it is missed because its early appearance resembles ordinary cellulitis. The finding that should raise alarm is pain far more severe than the visible redness would suggest, with skin that is tense, dusky, or numb, and a patient whose general condition is worsening despite the apparently limited local problem. Crepitus, a crackling felt under the fingers, means gas is present in the tissues and points strongly towards a destructive infection with organisms that thrive without oxygen, and it demands immediate surgical assessment. A blister filled with dark fluid, or skin that has lost its sensation, is a late sign and must not be waited for. In the well-resourced hospital the diagnosis is supported by imaging and an urgent surgical opinion, while in the clinic with a torch the same conclusion is reached through the mismatch between the patient's distress and the visible extent of the redness. The decision to treat is driven by that mismatch, and the finding that would make a plan of watching and waiting the wrong answer is any of these signs, because such an infection can outrun treatment within hours.

The pigmented lesion that has changed is the fourth finding that must be named early, and it is the one most often dismissed because the patient feels well. The features that matter are asymmetry of shape, a border that is irregular or notched, color that is not uniform, a size that is increasing, and any change in elevation, bleeding, itching, or crusting. A diameter of 6 millimeters is often taught as a threshold for concern, and it is an illustration only, to be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, because small lesions can also be dangerous and this figure differs between editions and authorities. The clinician should ask whether the lesion is new or has changed, and should record the answer, because the patient's own observation of change is among the most useful pieces of evidence available. Any lesion that has changed in a way the patient can describe should be examined in good light, and a decision made about whether it needs to be seen by someone with the training and the tools to remove it. The finding that would make simple reassurance the wrong answer is a history of change, whatever the lesion measures today.

A rash that involves the mucous membranes is never an ordinary rash, and that combination should be treated as a warning until the cause is clear. Mucosal involvement appears as erosions, blisters, white plaques that cannot be wiped away, or raw areas on the lips, inside the mouth, in the eyes, or on the genitalia, and it may precede the skin changes by a day or two. The conditions that produce it range from a self-limiting viral illness to the severe drug reactions and to the blistering diseases in which the immune system attacks the skin, and the skin changes alone may not distinguish them. The patient's general condition, the speed of onset, and the medicines taken in the preceding weeks are the discriminators that matter most. Any patient with fever, a spreading rash, and mucosal erosions should be discussed urgently with a clinician who can admit and support them, because the illness can progress quickly and the decision cannot wait for a definitive diagnosis. The same reasoning applies where no dermatologist is within a day's travel, and there the correct action is early referral rather than repeated observation.

Hair is a window on systemic disease, because its growth is sensitive to illness, nutrition, hormones, and medicines, and its changes are slow enough to record a history of the recent past. Loss of hair that is diffuse and does not scar usually points towards a systemic disturbance such as a severe illness some months earlier, a disorder of the thyroid gland, a nutritional deficiency, or a reaction to a medicine. Loss that is patchy and confined to smooth round areas, with hair broken off and skin that looks normal, is the pattern of a disease that attacks the hair follicle itself. Loss accompanied by redness, scaling, or scarring of the scalp is different again, because scarring means the follicle has been destroyed and the loss may be permanent, which raises the urgency of an early diagnosis. The clinician should examine the scalp in good light, note the pattern and the condition of the skin, and ask about recent illness, weight change, childbirth, and every medicine being taken.

The nails record systemic disease in their shape, their surface, their color, and their attachment to the nail bed, and they should be inspected in every patient who is unwell for more than a short time. Clubbing, the painless enlargement of the fingertip with loss of the normal angle between the nail and the nail fold, is associated with disease of the lungs, the heart, the liver, and the bowel, and it can be confirmed by pressing the nails of the two index fingers together to see whether a diamond shaped gap remains. Pitting of the nail surface, small depressions like the surface of a thimble, is characteristic of psoriasis and can appear before the skin rash is obvious. Separation of the nail from the bed beneath it, and the small brown streaks under the nail called splinter hemorrhages, each have their own lists of causes, and the clinician should resist the temptation to diagnose one disease from one nail sign. The nails should be examined in good light and compared one hand with the other, because one-sided changes point towards a local cause while changes on both sides point towards a systemic one.

In the clinic with a stethoscope, a thermometer, and a torch, the description itself becomes the diagnostic instrument, and it must be performed with the same care as in a dermatology clinic. The torch should be used from the side to show relief and from the front to show color, and daylight should be used whenever it is available, because artificial light distorts the distinction between red, purple, and brown. The clinician can measure lesions against a ruler, a coin, or the patient's own fingertip, and can draw a simple sketch with the distribution marked, which preserves information that a written note alone may lose. The essential decisions, namely whether this is a harmless rash or a dangerous one, whether it needs referral, and how soon, can all be reached from the history and the description.

When the only view of a rash is a poor telephone image sent from a remote post, the reasoning changes but does not disappear. The image should be requested together with a description rather than instead of one, and the description should include the distribution, the colors, the surface, whether the lesions blanch, and how the rash has evolved. A photograph taken in daylight, at a fixed distance, with something of known size in the frame, is far more useful than a close-up taken in the dark with a flash, and asking for a second image of another region is often more informative than asking for a better version of the first. The clinician must accept that color is unreliable in a digital image and that subtle relief and texture are usually lost, so the decision should lean on the history, the distribution, and the patient's general condition. Where the image shows fever with a rash that does not blanch, mucosal involvement, blistering, or a spreading tender infection, the correct action is to arrange urgent transfer rather than to study the picture further. Where the image is uninterpretable, the honest answer is that it is uninterpretable, and the patient should be brought to a place where they can be examined in person.

Infection of the skin is a large part of what presents in any clinic, and the reasoning is about depth and spread rather than about the name of the organism. Infection limited to the surface presents as impetigo, with golden crusts and little systemic upset, and it can usually be managed where the patient sits. Infection of the deeper dermis and the fat presents as spreading redness with warmth, tenderness, and a raised edge, and the boundary should be marked and timed so that its movement can be judged later. A collection of pus under the skin is a different problem again, because it requires drainage rather than medicine alone, and the finding that points to it is a tense, tender, fluctuant swelling. Infection tracking along the lymph vessels shows as a red line with tender nodes upstream, and it should raise the question of a deeper source. A temperature of 38 degrees Celsius or higher is treated as important in many settings, but the figure that should prompt action must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because it varies with age and with the clinical situation.

Purpura, which is bleeding into the skin, may be caused by a low platelet count, by a disorder of the platelets themselves, by damaged blood vessels, or by an infection, and the pattern helps to sort these apart. Petechiae are small and pinhead sized, while ecchymoses are larger and confluent, and both should be pressed to confirm that they do not blanch. The distribution matters as much as the lesion, since purpura confined to the legs of a person who is up and walking suggests inflammation of the small vessels, while widespread purpura in a febrile patient suggests infection in the bloodstream. The clinician should look for other evidence of bleeding, such as bleeding gums, blood in the urine or the stool, and bruising in unusual sites, and should ask about medicines that interfere with clotting. Any patient with fever and a rash that does not blanch needs urgent assessment, and that rule holds in every setting, however few the tools.

The skin is a signboard for disease elsewhere, and the clinician who reads it can sometimes reach a systemic diagnosis at the bedside. A dark velvety thickening of the skin in the folds of the neck and the armpits suggests a problem of insulin resistance, while a diffuse bronze darkening with weight loss and low blood pressure suggests a failing adrenal gland. Yellowing of the skin and the whites of the eyes points towards the liver or towards the breakdown of red blood cells, and it should prompt an examination of the abdomen. Small yellow deposits around the eyes and on the elbows suggest a disorder of fat metabolism, and a violet colored rash over the upper eyelids with muscle weakness suggests inflammation of the muscles. Recurrent boils, persistent thrush, and shingles at a young age should raise the question of an immune deficiency and prompt testing for the human immunodeficiency virus, or HIV, with consent and counseling. A rash that appears after a new medicine, a blistering reaction, and a widespread redness with scaling are all patterns that should make the clinician review every medicine the patient is taking.

The description must be written down in a form that a colleague can picture, and this is a skill as learnable as the examination itself. A note that says only that the rash is maculopapular tells almost nothing, whereas a note naming the site, the distribution, the color, the border, the surface, the size, and the time course allows another clinician to see the patient through the page. Where a diagram is permitted, a simple body outline with the distribution shaded is worth a paragraph of prose and is quicker to draw than to describe. The note should record the pressure test, the state of the mouth and the nails, and what the patient has been taking, and it should also record what the clinician considered and why, so that the next reader can weigh the reasoning rather than merely trust the conclusion. In a referral letter, the description and the specific question being asked are more valuable than a list of possible diagnoses, because they tell the receiving clinician what to look for first.

The decision about treatment for a skin problem usually turns on a small number of findings, and the clinician should be able to say which finding would change the plan. Where the process is confined, is not spreading, causes no systemic upset, and does not involve a mucous membrane, local measures with review may be the right answer. Where there is fever, rapid spread, blistering, mucosal involvement, severe pain, or a patient who is deteriorating, the decision moves towards urgent treatment and referral, and any of these findings would make simple local treatment the wrong answer. Where the diagnosis is a reaction to a medicine, the decision is to identify and stop the suspected agent, and the finding that would make continuing it the wrong answer is the close link in time between the medicine and the rash together with any mucosal or systemic feature. This book gives no doses and no regimens, and it should not, because the choice and the amount belong to the current guideline, the local protocol, and the clinician who knows the patient.

The same disease can look different in different people, and the clinician must adjust the description rather than the reasoning. In darker skin, redness is often seen as a darker brown or purple change rather than as bright red, and the pressure test may be harder to interpret because the natural color does not change under pressure in the same way. In infants, a rash may be the only sign of a serious infection, and a child who is irritable, feeding poorly, or floppy must be assessed for danger regardless of the appearance of the skin. In older adults the skin is thinner, the immune response is blunted, and an infection may spread with less redness and less fever than expected, so a change in function or alertness may be the first sign. Pregnancy changes the skin in many harmless ways, but itching without a rash late in pregnancy is a finding that must be taken seriously. A skin lesion in a person who has received a transplanted organ, or who takes treatment that suppresses immunity, may be a serious infection or a tumor that would be harmless in another patient, so the clinician should describe the appearance and then ask what it means in this particular person.

To summarize, the skin, the hair, and the nails are examined by describing before naming, using a shared vocabulary of site, distribution, symmetry, color, border, surface, size, and time, and then letting that description build a differential diagnosis ranked by danger as well as by likelihood. The findings that must never be missed are the rash that does not blanch in a febrile patient, the blistering reaction with mucosal involvement, the spreading soft tissue infection with pain out of proportion to the redness, the pigmented lesion that has changed, and any rash that involves a mucous membrane, and each of these demands action rather than observation. Every threshold, measurement, and interval mentioned in this chapter is an illustration only, and each must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol before it shapes a real decision, because such figures differ between countries, between editions, and between one laboratory and the next. This is educational material, it is not medical advice, it is not a protocol, and it is not a substitute for training, supervision, or the judgment of the clinician who is actually with the patient. In a hospital with every resource, and in a clinic with a torch and a thermometer, the method is the same, and the clinician who can describe what is in front of them will always reason better than the one who reaches for a name too soon.
