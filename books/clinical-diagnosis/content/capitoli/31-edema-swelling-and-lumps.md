# Chapter 31 — Edema, Swelling and Lumps

Swelling is a sign a patient can often see before any clinician looks, and because it is visible it is easy to accept as a fact rather than read as a sign. A swollen leg, a puffy face, and a lump in the neck look alike in a waiting room and mean different things at the bedside, and what separates them is pattern rather than size. The organizing question is not how large the swelling has become but whether it is generalized or local, whether it pits under the finger, whether it is tender, and how it behaves with posture and time. Those four observations, made with the eyes and the hands, divide the problem more usefully than any laboratory result, because each points to a different mechanism and a different list of causes. This chapter is educational material; it is not medical advice, it is not a protocol, and it is not a substitute for training, supervision, or the judgment of the clinician who is with the patient.

Certain swellings can kill, and they deserve to be named before the ordinary reasoning begins. The first is the leg that swells on one side after a long journey, an operation, a cast, or any stretch of immobility, because such a leg may hold a clot in its deep veins. The danger is not the leg but the lung, since a fragment of the clot can travel and block the circulation there, and what reveals that complication is sudden breathlessness, chest pain, a racing pulse, or faintness that arrives without warning. The finding that points to the clot is a calf warmer, more tender, and larger than its partner, often with pitting that stops below the knee. A calf that measures 3 centimeters more in circumference than the other is one commonly taught marker, and that figure is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such boundaries are drawn differently in different countries and revised between editions. A swollen leg with any chest symptom needs urgent help, because waiting for the leg to declare itself may lose the moment when treatment would have worked.

The lump that beats is the second danger, and it is an artery that has widened and thinned rather than a growth. It may lie behind the knee, in the groin, or deep in the abdomen, and it expands in every direction with each pulse, felt as a push rather than a flick transmitted from elsewhere. That expansile movement, sometimes with a vibration or a sound heard through the stethoscope, betrays an aneurysm, and a clinician who mistakes it for an ordinary node and presses hard on it has risked a rupture. The third danger is the lymph node that is hard, irregular, and fixed, because such a node is a cancer until the assessment says otherwise, and what reveals it is fixation to the skin or the tissues beneath. The fourth is the warm, red, severely tender swelling that spreads through a limb while the patient is feverish, and the greatest warning is pain out of proportion to the appearance, blistering, or a patch of skin that has gone numb.

The movement of fluid explains why edema appears where it does, and it can be understood without a single formula. Fluid is pushed out of the smallest blood vessels by the pressure inside them, drawn back by the proteins held in the blood, and whatever remains is collected by the lymphatic channels. Edema appears when one of those four elements fails, namely when the pressure inside the vessel rises, the protein in the blood falls, the vessel wall becomes leaky, or the drainage is blocked. Each failure produces a different distribution, a different feel under the hand, and a different behavior over the day, so the mechanism is the first branch of the differential diagnosis. A clinician who reasons from the mechanism will examine the heart, the liver, the kidney, and the veins in the same patient instead of choosing among them by habit.

When the pressure inside the vessels is too high, fluid gathers in the parts of the body that lie lowest, because gravity decides where the extra pressure is greatest. That is why the legs swell toward evening in a person with a failing heart or an obstructed vein, and why the ankles are noticed first. The swelling pits easily under a finger, it is usually the same on both sides when the heart is the cause, and it may climb higher as the day goes on. A raised pressure in the veins of the neck points to the heart rather than the kidney, and crackles at the lung bases, a heart murmur, or an impulse displaced to the left belong to the same story. The abdomen may fill with fluid as well, so a swollen belly with swollen legs should suggest one engine rather than two separate diseases.

When the protein in the blood falls, fluid leaves the vessels all over the body rather than only in the dependent parts, and the face is often puffy on waking because the fluid pooled there overnight. The kidney can lose large amounts of protein in the urine, the liver can fail to make it, and chronic poor nutrition can do the same, so a generalized pitting swelling with frothy urine or a wasted appearance sends the clinician to those organs. A leaky vessel wall produces a local and often tender swelling, which is the mechanism of inflammation and severe infection, and it develops quickly and may be warm or red. A blocked lymphatic channel produces a swelling that is firm, heavy, and does not pit, because the fluid is rich in protein and the tissues thicken with time. An arm that swells years after an operation or radiotherapy, or a leg swollen where a parasitic worm blocks the lymphatics, follows the same logic.

Pitting is tested by pressing a finger firmly over the swelling against a bone for several seconds, then watching whether a dent remains and how long it takes to fill. A dent that persists shows that fluid is free to move in the tissues, and its depth and duration are recorded in words so that the next examiner can compare. The behavior of the swelling with posture and time is the most informative observation the patient can supply, because a face puffy on waking and normal by noon describes a different mechanism from a leg thin in the morning and tight by evening. The clinician asks when the swelling is worst, what makes it better, whether raising the limb reduces it, and whether it has changed over weeks or hours, and whether it follows gravity and settles with rest, since a swelling that ignores both is usually not fluid.

A swelling that does not pit is a different problem, and the hand should say so at once, because fat, tumor, and thickened lymphatic tissue resist the finger. The skin over it may be dimpled, shiny, or fixed, and each tells the clinician more than the measurement of the lump. Tenderness changes the list of causes sharply, because an inflamed swelling is usually hot, red, and painful, while a painless one is more often a tumor, a chronic venous change, or a lymphatic problem. A swelling that is severely painful out of proportion to its appearance, that is spreading quickly, and that has lost sensation over part of its surface may be a deep infection destroying tissue, and the finding of numbness or of skin that does not bleed demands an immediate surgical opinion.

The history is taken like any other history, but with particular attention to time and to the organs that govern fluid. The clinician asks when the swelling began, whether it started on one side, whether it has spread, and what the patient was doing, including travel, surgery, immobility, or a long period in bed. Questions about breathlessness on lying flat, waking at night short of breath, chest pain, and the passage of urine belong in the first minutes, because they name the heart and the kidney without any test. The patient is asked about weight change, alcohol, the medicines already taken, and any previous clot, cancer, or operation, since each moves a diagnosis up or down the list.

Edema is a final common pathway, so the examination is organized to find which organ is failing rather than to confirm a single guess. The clinician begins with the general look, noting the distribution of the swelling, the pitting, the color of the skin, and the state of the veins, and then turns to the heart. The jugular venous pressure, which is the height of the pulse seen in the veins of the neck, is the most useful single sign, because it separates a heart that cannot cope from a kidney or a liver that has failed. A pressure raised to about 10 millimeters of mercury above the level of the right atrium is one commonly taught boundary, and it is offered here only to show the shape of the reasoning, so confirm what your own service accepts against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the reference differs between editions and between one ward and another. The finding of a raised jugular venous pressure with swollen legs and crackles in the lungs makes the heart the engine of the problem, while the same finding in a patient whose legs are thin suggests that the veins themselves are obstructed.

The kidney announces itself through the urine, the blood pressure, and the face, and all three can be assessed almost anywhere. The clinician asks whether the patient passes less urine than usual, whether it is frothy, whether it has changed color, and whether blood has been visible, and then looks at the face and the eyelids in the morning light. The blood pressure is measured with the patient seated and the arm supported, and a systolic pressure of 140 millimeters of mercury with a diastolic pressure of 90 millimeters of mercury is a widely quoted limit that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the boundary depends on age, pregnancy, and the setting in which it is applied. In a clinic without a laboratory the urine can be inspected in a clear container and the patient asked to collect it over a full day, which turns a vague complaint into a quantity that can be recorded. The finding of blood in the urine together with a generalized swelling makes the kidney the leading organ, and that patient needs assessment for a disease of the filtering units that may be treatable.

The liver produces the proteins that hold fluid in the vessels and also governs the pressure in the veins that drain the gut, so it can produce swelling in two separate ways. The clinician looks for yellow color in the eyes, for small branching red marks on the upper body, and for redness of the palms, and feels for a liver that is enlarged, hard, or irregular. The abdomen is examined for fluid, because a patient with a failing liver often has a swollen belly and thin limbs rather than swollen legs alone. The history of alcohol, of previous jaundice, of injections or blood products, and of a family illness belongs here, and because a patient may be reluctant to give it, the questions are asked plainly and without reproach. Poor nutrition must also be considered, because a poor diet can lower the proteins without any organ being inflamed, and the finding that points there is a wasted appearance with a generalized and painless swelling.

The veins of the legs are examined by looking at both limbs from the groin to the ankle, with the patient standing if possible, because a vein that is invisible lying down may be obvious when the blood has to rise against gravity. The clinician looks for dilated twisting veins, for brown staining of the skin above the ankle, for eczema, for hardening of the fat beneath the skin, and for an ulcer at the inner ankle. The swelling of chronic venous disease is usually present on both sides, worse after standing, better after a night in bed, and it pits at first and becomes firm with time. The finding that separates it from an acute clot is that it has been present for months or years and is neither warm nor tender. A clinician should still ask about a recent clot, because the two conditions can coexist and old venous disease does not protect a patient from a new one.

A leg that has swollen suddenly and on one side must be treated as a clot until that is excluded, and the examination is designed to answer that question carefully. The clinician inspects both legs in good light, notes the color and warmth, feels the calf gently rather than deeply, and measures the same point on each limb with a tape. The finding that supports the diagnosis is a swollen, tender, warm calf with pitting on the affected side, sometimes with discomfort when the foot is bent upward, although none of these signs is reliable enough to settle the matter alone. The interval that matters most is the recent one, because the risk is greatest in the first 3 months after an operation, a long journey, or a period of immobility, and that interval is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since different services draw the window differently. In a hospital the veins can be examined with ultrasound, and a blood test that measures the breakdown products of a clot can help to set the diagnosis aside in a patient whose probability is low, while a clinic with none of these should not attempt to exclude the diagnosis by examination alone and should refer instead.

A lump is described before it is interpreted, and the description follows a fixed order so that nothing is left out. The clinician records the site, the size, the shape, the consistency, the mobility, whether it is attached to the skin or to the tissues beneath, whether it is tender, whether it pulses, and what the skin over it looks like. The neighboring lymph nodes and the rest of the drainage region are examined in the same session, because the lump may be the visible part of a disease that has already spread. The clinician then decides from which layer the lump arises, whether the skin, the fat beneath it, the fibrous sheet that covers the muscle, the muscle itself, the bone, a gland, a lymph node, or a blood vessel. Each layer has its own list of causes, and naming the layer usually removes more of the uncertainty than any other single step.

The site of a lump carries information because the drainage of each region of the body is known. A node in the neck drains the skin, the mouth, the throat, and the ear, a node in the armpit drains the arm, the breast, and the chest wall, and a node in the groin drains the leg, the perineum, and the skin below the waist. The node that sits just above the collarbone deserves particular respect, because disease in the chest or the abdomen can announce itself there while the patient feels entirely well. In the neck the clinician also watches the thyroid gland, because a swelling in the front of the neck that rises when the patient swallows arises from that gland, and a single hard nodule, a fixed gland, or a hoarse voice raises the question of cancer. A node enlarged in a region where the skin is broken or inflamed is usually reacting to that local problem, and the finding that would make reaction the right answer is a node that shrinks as the infection settles.

Consistency and mobility do most of the work when a lump is examined. A soft, tender, freely mobile lump that has appeared over a few days is usually an inflamed node or a small collection of pus, while a firm, rubbery, mobile lump is more often a tumor of the lymph glands and a hard, irregular, fixed lump most strongly suggests a cancer. The clinician tests mobility by moving the lump over the tissues beneath it with the flat of the fingers, and tests attachment to the skin by trying to lift the skin away from it. The finding of skin that dimples or retracts when the lump is lifted, or of a lump that will not move at all, should stop the examiner and trigger a referral. A node that stays enlarged for more than 3 weeks after the infection that seemed to explain it deserves its own assessment, and that interval is a teaching figure that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because follow-up periods differ between services and editions.

The lump that pulsates is examined with great care because the hand can do harm. The clinician places the fingers on either side of the swelling and asks whether it widens in every direction with each beat, which is the behavior of an artery that has dilated, or whether the movement is merely transmitted from a vessel lying beneath an ordinary mass. The finding of an expansile pulsation, sometimes with a vibration or a sound heard through the stethoscope, identifies the swelling as an aneurysm, and the examiner should then stop pressing and look for signs that it is leaking or about to rupture. Pain that is new and severe in the back or the abdomen, a falling blood pressure, a racing pulse, and a pulsatile mass together describe a vascular emergency that needs help immediately. In the abdomen the mass may be felt only when the patient relaxes the wall and the clinician uses both hands at the level of the navel, and behind the knee the same swelling may be mistaken for a joint or a cyst until the pulse is felt.

Lumps in the groin and the scrotum are common and are separated by a few simple observations. The clinician asks the patient to cough and watches whether the swelling expands with the cough, because a hernia does and a lymph node does not, and then asks whether the swelling can be pushed back. A swelling in the scrotum that can be made to disappear by gentle pressure and that returns on standing is likely a hernia, while one that surrounds the testis and transmits light from a torch held against it is likely a collection of fluid. The finding that must never be missed is a firm lump within the testis itself, because a lump of that kind is a cancer until the assessment says otherwise. A swollen abdomen is a related problem, decided by whether the swelling is fluid, gas, fat, an enlarged organ, or a mass. The finding of an irregular, hard abdominal mass together with weight loss and a poor appetite, or of dullness in the flanks that moves when the patient turns onto one side, is the one that demands urgent assessment.

In a well-resourced hospital the assessment is extended by imaging and by the laboratory, but the reasoning that directs them is unchanged. Ultrasound can show whether a vein is blocked, whether the liver is smooth or irregular, whether the kidney is swollen, whether a node is solid or fluid, and whether the abdomen contains free fluid, and it does all of this at the bedside. Computed tomography, or CT, and magnetic resonance imaging, or MRI, answer questions about deep structures the hand cannot reach, and a clinician who has formed a hypothesis already knows which image to ask for. The laboratory adds a blood count, an assessment of the proteins in the blood, a test of kidney function, and the examination of the urine, each of which answers a specific question in the reasoning. A test is worth ordering only when its result would change what is done next, and a fever of 38 degrees Celsius in a patient with a tender swelling is one widely used marker of infection, though that figure must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the threshold for fever shifts with the site of measurement and the age of the patient.

Where there is no imaging, the same assessment is made with a stethoscope, a torch, a thermometer, and a tape, and it is not a lesser form of reasoning. The tape measures the two limbs at the same point so that a difference can be recorded and compared at the next visit, and the torch makes a scrotal swelling transmit light and a lesion visible in the mouth or the throat. The clinician examines the drainage region of every lump, looks at the urine in a clear container, and measures the blood pressure, and then decides whether the patient can be watched or must travel. A period of about 24 hours of elevation and observation is sometimes enough to separate a venous swelling from an inflammatory one, and that interval is offered as an illustration, so check the current guideline and the local protocol, because the acceptable period of watching depends on the patient and the setting. The finding that makes watching the wrong answer is any chest symptom, a spreading redness, a rapidly growing lump, or a patient who looks unwell, and in those cases the journey begins now.

Treatment is part of diagnosis, and in swelling it is usually a decision about the mechanism rather than about a medicine. Treatment is the right answer when a clot is confirmed or strongly suspected and the risk of bleeding does not outweigh the risk of the clot, and it is the wrong answer when the leg swelling is clearly venous and long-standing, because treating that patient as though a clot were present exposes them to harm for no benefit. A medicine that removes fluid is the right answer when the swelling comes from a heart that is overloaded, and the wrong answer when the swelling comes from a blocked lymphatic channel, which does not respond to it at all. A support stocking is the right answer for a swollen leg whose cause is venous and whose arterial supply is sound, and the wrong answer when the pulses in the foot are poor, because compression can then damage the limb. An antibiotic is the right answer when the swelling is infected and the patient is systemically unwell, and the wrong answer when the appearance is inflammation from another cause and the patient is stable.

A clinician who sends a patient with a swelling home must say clearly what should bring that patient back sooner. The patient or the family should be told to return for breathlessness, chest pain, a swelling that grows quickly, a redness that spreads, a fever, a lump that becomes fixed, or any change in the color of a limb. A review arranged in advance is worth more than a promise to return if things worsen, because many patients cannot judge that for themselves. The record should show the distribution of the swelling, whether it pitted, whether it was tender, the measurements of both limbs, the nodes examined, and the reasoning that was considered and set aside, and a record that lists findings without the thinking forces the next reader to begin again.

A constructed illustration may fix these habits, and it is a teaching device rather than a portrait of any real person. Imagine a patient who arrives with a left leg that has swollen over two days after a long journey, with a calf that is warm and tender and larger than its partner, and no chest symptoms at all. The immediate reasoning names the clot and asks about the lung, and the examination records the measurements, looks for a raised jugular venous pressure, and listens to the chest. Now imagine another patient whose face is puffy in the morning and whose ankles swell by evening, with frothy urine and a blood pressure higher than expected, in whom the kidney is the leading organ from the first minutes. What the two share is the move that matters, which is that the clinician asked the organizing question first and let the pattern choose the next examination.

To summarize, a swelling is interpreted by its distribution, by whether it pits, by whether it is tender, and by how it behaves with posture and time, and those four observations organize everything that follows. The mechanisms are the movement of fluid described in words, namely pressure that pushes it out, protein that holds it in, a wall that lets it through, and drainage that fails, and each mechanism predicts a different picture. The dangers are named at the bedside and not deferred: the unilateral swollen leg with a clot and the lung behind it, the pulsatile mass that is an aneurysm, the hard fixed node that is a cancer until proven otherwise, and the rapidly spreading painful swelling that is a surgical emergency. The same patient is examined through the heart, the liver, the kidney, and the veins, and a lump is described by site, size, consistency, mobility, attachment, and the state of the surrounding skin, with the drainage region examined in the same session. In a hospital the assessment is extended by ultrasound, by computed tomography, and by the laboratory, and in a clinic it is made with a stethoscope, a torch, a thermometer, and a tape, and the reasoning is the same in both rooms. Every score, cut-off, reference range, pressure, temperature, rate, or interval mentioned in this chapter is an illustration of reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such numbers differ between countries, editions, and laboratories. This is educational material and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service. A patient whose swelling is spreading, whose breathing is changing, or whose lump is hard and fixed is helped first, and the exact cause is settled afterwards.
