# Chapter 13 — Respiratory Examination

The respiratory examination is best understood as an interrogation of a mechanical system rather than a tour of a list of diseases. The chest is a bellows, the airways are a branching set of tubes, the lungs are a pair of elastic sacs, and the whole arrangement is sealed inside a pleural space that holds each lung against the chest wall. When a patient is breathless, the fault may lie in any of those parts, and each part produces a somewhat different set of physical findings. The examination is organized to separate them, and the reasoning that drives it is the subject of this chapter.

Long before the stethoscope touches the chest, the work of breathing has already told the examiner most of what matters. The clinician who watches from the end of the bed notices the rate, the depth, the effort, and the position the body has chosen, and those observations are made in seconds. A respiratory rate that looks fast is counted rather than estimated, because the eye underestimates it consistently. The patient who sits forward with the arms braced on the knees, who uses the muscles of the neck with every breath, and who cannot finish a sentence is telling the examiner that the ventilatory reserve is nearly gone. This is the moment to look for the lethal cause rather than to continue a leisurely examination.

The lethal cause that must be named at this point is tension pneumothorax, in which air enters the pleural space and cannot leave, so that pressure builds and the lung on that side collapses while the heart and the great veins are compressed. The findings that reveal it are a sudden onset of breathlessness and chest pain, one side of the chest that moves less than the other, breath sounds that have disappeared on that side, a percussion note that is louder than the healthy side rather than dull, neck veins that are distended, and a blood pressure that is falling. The trachea may shift away from the affected side, but that sign appears late and its absence proves nothing. In this condition the decision does not wait for a chest radiograph, because the pressure is the emergency and the film is a delay.

The examination that follows has a fixed order for a reason. Inspection comes first because it is free and because it directs everything that follows. Palpation then tests the movement of the chest wall and the transmission of sound through the tissues. Percussion tests the density of what lies beneath the hand, and auscultation listens to the air itself. Each step is a question about a different layer of the system, and each finding is read against the same finding on the other side, since the patient is his own best control. A clinician who performs the steps in this order is building an argument rather than collecting sensations.

Access matters more than technique at the start. The patient should be undressed to the waist, because a vest or a gown hides asymmetry, scars, and the pattern of movement, and dignity is preserved with a blanket and a screen rather than with clothing. The examination is done in a good light, which in a well-resourced hospital means a bright room and in a remote clinic means a torch held at an angle across the chest so that the shadows reveal the shape. The back must be examined as carefully as the front, and the axillae must not be skipped, because a lesion at the apex of a lung announces itself there. The patient is asked to breathe through the mouth, since breathing through the nose makes the sounds quieter and harder to interpret.

Counting the respiratory rate is among the most useful measurements in respiratory medicine. It is counted for a full minute while the patient is unaware of being watched, because a patient who knows that the rate is being timed will alter it. A rate of 12 to 20 breaths per minute is often quoted for a resting adult, but the figure that should concern you must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the range differs with age, with pregnancy, with fever, and with the reference used by each service. The rate is least useful as a single value and most useful as a trend, so a rate that has climbed since the last set of observations carries more weight than the same number found once.

The pattern of breathing matters as much as the rate. A deep and regular pattern without distress suggests a metabolic cause rather than a mechanical one, while a shallow pattern with a fast rate suggests that the effort of each breath has become too costly. An irregular rhythm that waxes and wanes, with pauses followed by deeper breaths, points to a disturbance of the control of breathing rather than of the lung, and it is common in advanced illness and with some medicines. In an infant the signs are different and must be looked for deliberately: flaring of the nostrils, drawing in of the ribs and of the space above the sternum, and a grunting sound at the end of each breath, which is the child trying to keep the small airways open. An older patient may show almost none of this and still be dangerously short of breath, so the absence of visible distress is not reassurance.

The muscles that should be silent during quiet breathing are the ones to watch. The sternocleidomastoid muscles of the neck, the muscles between the ribs, and the muscles of the abdominal wall are recruited only when the diaphragm and the ordinary effort are insufficient. When the diaphragm is exhausted, the abdomen moves inward as the chest moves outward during inspiration, which is the reverse of the normal pattern and a sign of a failing pump. The clinician should also note whether the patient can lie flat, since a patient who must sit upright to breathe is describing a problem with the mechanics of the chest or with the heart behind it. These observations cost nothing, and they are available in a clinic with a thermometer and a torch just as they are in an intensive care unit.

Palpation begins with the movement of the chest wall. The examiner places both hands flat on the back with the thumbs meeting in the midline, asks the patient to take a deep breath, and watches how far the thumbs separate and whether they separate equally. The same maneuver can be performed on the front of the chest, and either version answers a simple question: does this side of the chest fill as well as the other. Reduced expansion on one side means that something is preventing that lung from inflating, whether pain, a collection in the pleural space, a collapsed lung, or a blocked airway. Expansion is a coarse measure and it will not detect a small lesion, but a clear asymmetry is a strong clue that directs the rest of the examination.

Tactile fremitus is the next question, and it is best understood through the physics of sound. Sound travels poorly through air and well through solid or fluid, so the vibration felt on the chest wall tells the examiner what the lung beneath the hand is made of. The examiner places the flat of the hand on the chest and asks the patient to say a short phrase in a normal voice, then compares the two sides at the same level. The vibration is increased over an area of consolidated lung, because solid tissue transmits the voice better than air does, and it is reduced over a pleural effusion, a pneumothorax, or a lung whose airway is blocked, because none of those transmits sound well. The sign is a comparison rather than a measurement, and it is more reliable in a thin chest than in a muscular one.

Percussion translates density into sound. The examiner places one finger firmly on the chest wall, strikes it with the tip of the finger of the other hand, and listens to the note that results, moving from the apex to the base and comparing the two sides at every level. Air beneath the finger gives a low, hollow, resonant note; solid lung gives a dull note; and fluid gives a note that is flatter still, often described as stony dull. The reasoning is direct, because the note reflects how much air is present in the tissue under the hand, and it therefore changes with consolidation, with collapse, with fluid, and with air in the pleural space. Percussion is difficult in a thick chest wall, it is unreliable for small collections, and its agreement between two examiners is modest, so a normal percussion note does not exclude a serious problem.

Auscultation should be performed systematically and with the patient breathing through the mouth. The examiner listens at the apex, the axilla, and the base on each side, then repeats the sequence on the back, comparing side with side at each level. The stethoscope is pressed firmly enough to make a seal but not so firmly that it hurts the chest wall, since pain alters the breathing pattern and defeats the purpose. The examiner should listen through several full breaths at each site rather than snatching one and moving on, because many abnormal sounds are intermittent. The patient should be asked to breathe a little more deeply than usual but not to the point of dizziness, since over-breathing produces misleading sounds and distress.

The normal breath sound is generated by air moving through the branching airways, and its character changes as the airways become smaller and the distance from the chest wall shortens. At the periphery, where the examiner listens over the lung, the sound is soft, low in pitch, and only slightly shorter in expiration than in inspiration, and it is described as vesicular. Over the trachea and the large airways, the sound is louder, harsher, and higher, with a clear gap between the two phases. The examiner who knows where the normal sound belongs will notice immediately when the wrong sound is heard in the wrong place. Much of respiratory auscultation is the detection of sound where it should not be.

Bronchial breathing is the name for the harsh, high-pitched sound of the large airways heard where the soft peripheral sound belongs. It appears because consolidated lung transmits sound from the airways with unusual efficiency, so the examiner hears the tube rather than the surrounding air spaces. The same quality can be heard over the top of a large pleural effusion, where the lung has been compressed against the airway, and it can be heard over the trachea in a healthy person, which is why the site matters as much as the sound. The phenomenon extends to the voice, because when the patient speaks the syllables are heard with unexpected clarity through the stethoscope over the same area, and whispered words that should be inaudible become distinct. These are among the more specific findings in the examination of the chest, and they point to solid tissue where air should be.

Crackles are the sounds produced when small airways that have closed are pulled open again as the lung inflates, or when air moves through a film of fluid in the small airways. The mechanism explains their timing. When the cause is a stiff, deflated lung that needs a high pressure to open, the crackles cluster at the end of inspiration, are fine and close together, and are heard at the bases. When the cause is fluid or secretions in larger airways, the crackles are coarser, earlier, and may shift after a cough. Crackles are therefore not a disease but a behavior of the airway, and the same sound may accompany very different processes. Their value comes from the company they keep, the history, the percussion note, and the other findings taken together.

A wheeze is the sound of air forced through a narrowed airway at speed, and its mechanism tells the examiner what it means. The narrowing may come from spasm of the muscle in the airway wall, from swelling of that wall, from secretions, or from a mass pressing on the tube, and the examination alone cannot distinguish them. A wheeze is therefore evidence that airflow is obstructed, not evidence of a particular cause. A wheeze heard without a stethoscope across a room is a loud one, and its disappearance may be alarming rather than reassuring, because it can mean that the patient is moving too little air to make a sound at all. Stridor is different, since it arises in the upper airway, is heard on inspiration, and signals a threat to the airway itself rather than to the lung.

The pleural rub is the sound of the two inflamed pleural surfaces moving against each other. It is heard in both phases of the breath, it is not altered by coughing, and it is often described as the sound of walking on fresh snow, a texture rather than a tone. The examiner finds it over a localized area, sometimes only under firm pressure of the stethoscope, and it may be the earliest physical sign of an inflamed pleura. It appears in infections of the pleura, after a clot reaches the lung, in inflammatory conditions, and in some cancers, but it is not sensitive enough that its absence excludes any of them. Its importance lies in the fact that it is a sign of inflammation rather than of fluid, and it therefore directs the reasoning differently from dullness or crackles.

Consolidation is the first of the three great patterns, and it means that the air spaces of part of the lung have filled with something that is not air, usually fluid or inflammatory material. The findings follow from the physics. The patient is often febrile and breathless, the rate is raised, and the cough may bring up sputum. On the affected side the chest expands less, tactile fremitus is increased, percussion is dull, and bronchial breathing is heard with crackles. The same combination is produced by pneumonia, by a part of the lung that has collapsed, and by other processes, so the pattern must be interpreted with the history. The findings are typically unilateral and localized rather than diffuse, and that distribution is itself part of the reasoning.

A pleural effusion is a collection of fluid between the lung and the chest wall, and its signs depend on how much fluid is present. A small collection may produce nothing at all, which is why the examination cannot exclude one. A larger collection reduces expansion on that side, reduces tactile fremitus, is dull to percussion, and abolishes the breath sounds below the fluid, while the lung above it may be compressed and transmit bronchial breathing with a rub or crackles. The percussion note is the most useful single sign, but its reliability at the bedside depends on the size of the collection. Fever, weight loss, and a history of exposure to tuberculosis in a patient with an effusion change the balance of causes, and the fluid must be sampled where that is possible rather than assumed.

A pneumothorax is air in the pleural space. When it is not under tension, the signs are a sudden onset of one-sided pain and breathlessness, reduced expansion on that side, a resonant or hyper-resonant percussion note where the lung should be dull or normal, and absent or greatly reduced breath sounds. The combination of hyper-resonance with absent breath sounds on the same side is the signature of air rather than fluid, and it is what separates a pneumothorax from an effusion at the bedside. The size of a pneumothorax does not map neatly onto the intensity of the signs, so a patient may be uncomfortable with little to find. In a patient with known lung disease, a small leak can become a large problem quickly, and the examination should be repeated rather than trusted once.

Tension pneumothorax is the same process with pressure behind it, and it is the emergency that this chapter names at the start. The findings that reveal it are the ones already described, and the clinician should look for them in any patient who becomes suddenly breathless and hypotensive, particularly if the patient is receiving positive pressure ventilation or has sustained a chest injury. The decision to treat does not wait for imaging, because the film takes time and the pressure is compressing the heart. The finding that would make treatment the wrong answer is a patient who is stable with a small collection and no sign of pressure, in whom the same procedure would be unnecessary. That distinction is a bedside judgment, and it must be made against the current guideline and the local protocol, which vary in how they define the unstable patient.

A large effusion that compresses the lung is dangerous for a different reason. The fluid occupies space, pushes the lung aside, and may shift the mediastinum, so that the trachea and the apex beat are displaced away from the affected side. The patient becomes breathless at rest and cannot lie flat, and the percussion note becomes stony dull over a wide area. If the fluid is blood the cause may be trauma or a cancer, and if it is infected the patient is septic, and either possibility changes the urgency rather than the physical findings. The examination cannot tell the nature of the fluid, and the reasoning must therefore include the history, the temperature, and the local capacity to sample it.

The silent chest and the exhausted respiratory effort are the third dangerous presentation, and they are recognized by an absence of noise rather than by any sound. In severe obstruction the airways are so narrow that air moves too slowly to generate a wheeze, so the examiner hears a quiet chest in a patient who is clearly working hard. The patient who is tiring may become quiet, drowsy, or confused, and the rate may fall as the effort fails, which is why a falling rate in a breathless patient is a warning rather than an improvement. The findings that demand immediate help are a diminishing level of consciousness, a weakening effort, and a chest that has gone quiet while the distress continues. This is a situation in which the examination is interrupted and help is summoned, because the next event may be respiratory arrest.

The most important limitation of the respiratory examination is that a normal result excludes very little. A clot in the lung may leave the chest entirely normal, with no crackles, no rub, and no change in the percussion note, and a patient whose only findings are breathlessness and a fast pulse may still have a dangerous obstruction of the pulmonary artery. Early pneumonia may be inaudible, and tuberculosis in its early stages may produce no findings at all. A small pneumothorax and a small effusion may both be missed by careful hands. The clinician who understands this examines the chest for the findings that are present rather than to obtain reassurance, and he uses the history, the vital signs, and the risk factors to decide how much weight a normal examination can carry.

When imaging is immediately available, the examination does not become unnecessary; it becomes the frame in which the image is read. A chest radiograph answers questions about consolidation, effusion, pneumothorax, and the size of the heart, and computed tomography, or CT, answers finer questions about the lung substance and the vessels. Ultrasound imaging at the bedside is increasingly used to find fluid and to guide its sampling, and it is often more sensitive than the radiograph for a small collection. Each of these tools was studied in a particular population and has a limit to its resolution, and none of them replaces the observation of the patient. The clinician who examines first knows what to look for in the image, and notices when the image and the patient disagree, which is the moment that needs explaining rather than dismissing.

In a clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, the examination is the imaging. The clinician can identify a consolidation with reasonable confidence when the findings are unilateral and consistent, recognize a large effusion by the stony dullness and the displaced apex, and identify a pneumothorax by the hyper-resonance and the absent breath sounds. What cannot be identified at the bedside must be treated as possible and referred, and the question is not whether the patient has a disease but whether the patient can safely wait. The thermometer identifies fever, and its pattern over time is itself a diagnostic instrument, while the torch examines the throat, the tongue, and the mouth for the signs that a chest examination would otherwise miss. A clinician working this way is not practising a reduced medicine; he is performing the same reasoning with a smaller set of tools.

Pulse oximetry, where it is available, adds a number that must be interpreted rather than obeyed. A saturation of 94 per cent is often used as a threshold for concern in a healthy adult breathing room air, but the value that matters must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the target differs for a patient with chronic lung disease, for a child, and for a pregnant woman. The device is unreliable in poor perfusion, in motion, in severe anemia, and in some patients with deeply pigmented skin, in whom several devices read falsely high. A normal saturation does not exclude a clot in the lung, since that patient may maintain oxygenation while ventilation and circulation are compromised. The number is a useful trend and a poor verdict.

A constructed illustration may make the reasoning visible. Suppose an adult arrives with a fever that has lasted some days, a cough, and breathlessness that has worsened, and the examination finds a rate of 24 breaths per minute, reduced expansion at the right base, dull percussion there, increased tactile fremitus, and bronchial breathing with fine crackles over the same area. A rate of 24 breaths per minute is above the usual resting range and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, as must the local definition of a fever. The pattern is coherent, because the findings all point to the same piece of lung and the mechanism of each is explained by solid tissue where air should be. No single sign is decisive, but the constellation is strong, and the reasoning then asks whether this patient is well enough to be managed where he is.

A second constructed illustration concerns the emergency. Suppose a patient develops sudden breathlessness after a procedure on the chest, becomes restless, and has a falling blood pressure with distended neck veins and no breath sounds on one side, where percussion is louder than on the other. The finding that makes this a tension pneumothorax rather than a simple one is the combination of pressure and compromise, and the finding that would change the decision is a patient who is stable with the same physical signs and no evidence of obstruction to the return of blood to the heart. The clinician who waits for a radiograph in the first patient has misunderstood which of the two problems is urgent. The procedure is named here as a decision rather than a technique, and that decision belongs to the setting and to the local protocol.

A patient can deteriorate during the examination itself, and the clinician must be willing to stop. If the rate climbs while the chest is being examined, if the patient becomes drowsy, or if the effort visibly weakens, the examination is over and the response begins. The findings gathered so far are still useful and should be recorded, but they are no longer the priority. The clinician who completes a leisurely sequence on a patient who is failing has confused thoroughness with care, and the principle that governs every chapter of this book applies here: when the patient is becoming more ill, the reasoning pauses and the patient is treated.

To summarize, the respiratory examination is a structured interrogation of the mechanics of breathing, beginning with the observation of effort and rate before the stethoscope is applied. Inspection, palpation, percussion, and auscultation each ask about a different layer of the system, and the findings are read as patterns: consolidation with its dullness and bronchial breathing, effusion with its stony dullness and reduced expansion, and pneumothorax with its hyper-resonance and absent breath sounds. The tension pneumothorax, the large effusion that compresses the lung, and the silent chest of the exhausted patient are named as emergencies, and each is recognized by findings rather than by a radiograph. Every number, threshold, and reference value mentioned here is an illustration only and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between countries, editions, and laboratories. The examination is performed with a full service behind it and again with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, and it is the same reasoning in both places. It does not replace training, supervision, or the judgment of the clinician who is with the patient, and a normal chest never excludes a dangerous cause on its own.
