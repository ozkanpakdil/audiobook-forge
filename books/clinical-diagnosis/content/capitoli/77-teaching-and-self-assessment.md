# Chapter 77 — Teaching and Self-Assessment

The reasoning this book has described is not absorbed by hearing it once, and it does not stay with the clinician who learned it quickly and then stopped. What keeps it is a set of habits deliberately built: practice on real patients, a commitment to a diagnosis made before the result arrives, feedback that is sought rather than awaited, errors reviewed without shame, and a practice audited against what happened to its patients. A manual can describe reasoning, but only practice keeps it alive, which is why this chapter is about learning and teaching rather than about one more disease. The material is educational and does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of the service in which the patient is seen. It is offered to the student at the bedside, to the clinician who works alone, and to the teacher who must draw the reasoning out of someone else.

The dangerous possibility this chapter keeps in view is not one disease but the missed diagnosis of anything that kills within hours, and the finding that most often reveals it is the direction of the observations across time rather than any single reading. One constructed patient arrives with a new chest discomfort and a fast pulse, the discomfort settles with rest, and the clinician sends the patient home after recording the improvement, while the electrocardiogram, or ECG, that the danger required was never obtained. Another constructed patient has a breathing rate that climbs from one recording to the next while the plan in the notes stays the same, and that mismatch betrays a deterioration nobody noticed. A breathing rate of 20 breaths per minute is often quoted as the line above which concern should rise, but that figure is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the accepted line moves with age, with pregnancy, and with the edition of the reference in use. Such misses come not from ignorance of a disease but from a reasoning habit never examined, which is why this subject belongs to safety rather than to academic polish.

Learning to reason begins when the learner stops reading about diagnosis and starts making diagnoses that can be checked. Deliberate practice has three parts: work at the edge of present ability, immediate feedback on how the work turned out, and repetition of the same kind of problem until the reasoning becomes reliable. In diagnosis the edge of ability is a real patient whose presentation is not quite clear, the feedback is the result of a test or the later course of the illness, and the repetition is the next patient with a similar story. The teacher's task is therefore not to supply information but to arrange the conditions in which reasoning is practiced and corrected.

Every chapter read and every lecture heard leaves a comfortable illusion of having understood something that has not yet been used. Recognition is not recall, and recall is not reasoning, so a clinician may recognize a disease on a page and still fail to consider it while the patient is talking. The remedy is to make the reasoning visible while it happens, by saying it aloud, writing it in the note, or answering a question before the answer is known. A learner who says that this looks like an infection of the lungs because the fever is high and the breathing is fast has produced something that can be examined and corrected, while a learner who nods at the same facts has produced nothing.

The most useful discipline in self-assessment is to commit to a diagnosis before the result that will settle it arrives. The commitment should be written down with the reasoning behind it and a rough statement of confidence, because a judgment kept only in the mind is revised by memory to fit whatever happens next. When the result arrives, the clinician compares it with the commitment, and the comparison teaches what no result can teach alone: whether the reasoning was sound, sound but unlucky, or whether a finding was available and ignored. In a hospital the commitment can be a line before the scan, and in a place with no scan it can be a line before the referral, stating what is expected and why.

In a clinic where no test will arrive, the commitment takes the form of the plan written before the patient leaves. The clinician states the working diagnosis, the finding that supports it, the findings that would overturn it, and the signs that should bring the patient back. That plan is tested by the follow-up visit, which is the only laboratory the clinic possesses, and a plan that names what would change it is a hypothesis rather than a habit. The clinician who reviews the plans of the past period against what happened to those patients is doing audit at the smallest scale, and it costs nothing but attention.

Reviewing one's own reasoning is hard because the mind rewrites the past to make the present seem inevitable. Once a diagnosis is known, every earlier finding appears to have pointed toward it, and the moment when several possibilities were equally open is forgotten. This is why the original record matters, and why a note containing only a conclusion cannot be reviewed at all; the clinician who wrote that the illness was probably viral but that the neck was stiff has left a trace that a bare diagnosis destroys. Reconstructing what was known at the time, rather than what is known now, is what turns a mistake into a lesson.

The review of one's own errors must be conducted without humiliation, because humiliation produces concealment and concealment produces the next error. An error is information about the system that produced it, which includes the clinician, the team, the record, the equipment, and the time available. A bad outcome is not the same as an error, and a sound decision made with the information at hand can end badly, so confusing the two destroys careful clinicians and flatters the lucky ones. The useful question is not who is at fault but what made the wrong path look reasonable and what would have made the right path visible.

A method helps, and it can be a few questions asked in order. What did I think was happening, and which finding supported that thought? What else could have explained the picture, and on what grounds did I set it aside? What would have changed my plan, and at what moment could that change still have been made? The answers belong in a private register of the cases that surprised the clinician, which becomes the most personal textbook the clinician will ever own and reveals, across many cases, the presentations habitually dismissed and the findings habitually not sought.

Feedback on reasoning rarely arrives by itself, because the patient who recovers does not return to say the diagnosis was right, and the patient who does badly often goes elsewhere. The silence after a consultation is therefore not evidence of success, and the clinician who waits for feedback will wait a long time. What works is to ask for it directly, at the moment when the person who can answer is still present. A question that asks what the other person would have done differently produces more than one that asks whether everything was all right, because the first invites a description and the second a reassurance.

In a hospital the colleague is close at hand, and the habit is the informal second opinion asked before a decision hardens rather than after it fails. In a clinic with one clinician, the colleague may be a distant voice on a telephone, a periodic supervisor, or a group of peers who meet over cases they have each seen. What matters is not the seniority of the person asked but the freshness of the eye, because a clinician who has already decided is least able to see the alternative. The question to ask is narrow: here is the story, here is what I think, and here is what I may be missing.

The record makes all of this possible, and it must be written for the reviewer rather than for the bill. A note showing the reasoning, the alternatives considered, the findings sought and not found, and the plan with its review conditions can be audited by the clinician who wrote it and by anyone who follows. A note showing only a diagnosis cannot be audited, taught from, or used to protect the patient at the next visit. The Soap notes framework, which separates the subjective account, the objective findings, the assessment, and the plan, keeps those elements from dissolving into one another, and it is a scaffold for thought as much as a format for writing.

Audit compares what a practice does with what happens to the patients it serves, and it can be done anywhere. The clinician who keeps a register of every patient sent home with chest pain, referred with a fever, or returned unwell soon afterwards, and who then looks at what became of them, is conducting an audit. The purpose is not a report for someone else but a view of one's own patterns, and the most valuable finding is usually a group of patients who were not followed up at all. The standard a service adopts for follow-up, and the interval at which it is applied, must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because what counts as adequate follow-up differs between settings and editions.

Many services hold a meeting that reviews a death or a serious complication, and its value depends on how it is conducted. A meeting that searches for a person to blame will receive fewer and less honest cases each time, until it receives none, while a meeting that asks what the team knew, what the record shows, and what would have made the outcome different will produce lasting change. The clinician whose patient died should speak first and at length, without being interrupted by a defense. The conclusions should name both the reasoning that could improve and the resources that were missing, because a broken thermometer and a missed diagnosis can belong to the same story.

The teacher's central skill is the question that draws reasoning out rather than the question that tests recall. A question asking for a fact can be answered with a word or a number and teaches nothing about how the answer was reached, while a question asking why this finding matters, what else might produce it, what would change the plan, and what the clinician would do without the test forces a chain of thought into the open. The teacher should wait after asking, because the silence that follows a good question is where thinking happens, and filling it destroys the exercise. The learner should commit before the teacher comments, since a comment made first only replaces the learner's reasoning with the teacher's.

Questioning must not become an interrogation, and the difference is whether the learner is helped to think or shown to be wrong. A chain of questions designed to arrive at a predetermined answer is recall testing in disguise, and the learner only guesses what the teacher wants. A better pattern is to ask for the working diagnosis and its reasoning, then for the finding that would overturn it, then for what the learner will actually do. When the reasoning is faulty, the correction should aim at the step that failed rather than at the person, which is the same discipline the clinician owes his or her own errors.

The examination is taught by demonstration, then supervised repetition, then independent performance with a report. The teacher should watch the learner rather than the patient for part of every encounter, because the commonest fault is not a missed sign but a step never performed at all. The learner should say what is being sought before the hand moves, so the maneuver is connected to a question rather than performed as a ritual. Where the examination is normal, the teacher should ask what that normality excludes and what it does not, because the meaning of a normal finding is the part most often left unlearned.

Frameworks such as Socrates for the pain story, Oldcarts for the history of the present illness, and Vindicate for the differential diagnosis are taught as scaffolds, and they should be presented that way. They prevent the omission that comes from enthusiasm, and they are most useful to the beginner and to the clinician who is tired or interrupted. They diagnose nothing, and a clinician who fills in every letter and then names the first disease that fits has used the framework as a substitute for thought. The teacher should show where the framework ends, which is where this particular patient differs from the general pattern.

In a well-resourced hospital the learner has instruments that a book can only describe, and each should test reasoning rather than replace it. The ECG library allows tracings to be read against the clinical story, and the learner should predict the tracing before it is shown, while imaging by ultrasound, computed tomography, or magnetic resonance imaging lets anatomical reasoning be checked against what the image shows. The intensive care unit, or ICU, records trajectories of pulse, pressure, breathing, and urine output continuously, which makes it the best classroom for learning what a trend means. A systolic pressure of 90 millimeters of mercury is often quoted as the point at which shock must be assumed, and that figure is likewise an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the pressure that matters depends on the patient's usual value, age, and pregnancy.

Simulation is valuable and has a limit that should be stated to learners. A mannequin does not look at the clinician with fear, does not answer a question in a way that changes the story, and does not have a family in the corridor. Communication and the reading of a person's face are practiced only on people, and they are as diagnostic as any test, so a learner comfortable in a simulated emergency and uncomfortable beside a frightened patient has practiced half the task. The teacher should therefore pair every simulated exercise with a real encounter in which the learner speaks to the patient and then reports what was said.

The clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer is not a lesser school, and some of its lessons cannot be learned elsewhere. There the reasoning is fully exposed, because nothing can be ordered and the decision must come from the history and the examination alone, and a patient with a fever and no laboratory teaches the value of the repeated temperature and the honest naming of uncertainty. A temperature of 38 degrees Celsius is often taught as the line above which fever is present, and that figure too is an illustration whose exact value must be verified against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the threshold depends on where the temperature is taken and on which edition of the reference is followed. The torch makes the throat, the ear, the skin, and the eye visible, the thermometer turns an impression into a record, and a watch turns a guess about the pulse into a measurement.

In a clinic where patients wait and time is short, teaching still happens, though it must be small. The teacher can ask one question after the consultation about the finding that decided the plan or the possibility set aside, and the learner can commit to a diagnosis in writing before the patient leaves. A case can be discussed with the patient's agreement in a few sentences, without identifying anyone, as educational material rather than advice about that person. The teacher should not let the teaching replace the care, because a patient waiting while two clinicians reason is still waiting.

Self-assessment becomes real when given a form, and several are available to the clinician who works alone. A logbook of cases, with the diagnosis made and the outcome where it is known, is the simplest and most durable, while a personal list of the cases that surprised the clinician shows the patterns of thought that need attention. A plan naming two or three specific weaknesses and the cases that will address them is more useful than a general intention to read more. A review of the referrals made, and of the patients who returned unwell, is an audit in miniature and needs only a notebook.

Calibration is the match between a clinician's confidence and that clinician's accuracy, and it can be assessed without statistics. A clinician certain of every diagnosis and often wrong is dangerous, and so is one uncertain of every diagnosis who treats nobody. Stating confidence in words and then comparing it with what happened is the practical way to correct either fault, because the clinician who notices that confidence is always high in one kind of presentation that proves innocent has found a bias worth knowing. Neither correction requires a course, only attention and a record.

Expertise in diagnosis is maintained rather than acquired, and the clinician who stops examining his own judgment is the one who will be surprised by it. Knowledge learned well enough to pass an examination fades unless it is used, and habits learned at the bedside drift unless they are watched. A clinician who has not changed a diagnosis in years has either an unusual practice or a closed mind, and the second is more common. The protection is to remain a learner deliberately, to ask for feedback when it is uncomfortable, and to keep teaching, because nothing exposes a gap in one's own reasoning as quickly as explaining it to someone else.

Teaching is therefore not a duty that follows expertise but one of the practices that preserves it. A clinician who must explain why a finding matters, and who must answer a learner's question about the alternative, revisits ground that habit had made automatic. The teacher also learns the shape of his or her own reasoning, including the parts that were never conscious and the parts that do not survive being stated. A team that teaches together develops a shared vocabulary for uncertainty, which lets one member say that something does not fit without being thought incompetent, and the patient benefits because a concern that can be spoken can be acted upon.

The two settings differ in their instruments and not in their reasoning, and a teaching program should say so. In the hospital the learner can check judgment against an ECG, an image, and a laboratory result, so the feedback loop closes quickly, while in the clinic it may close only at the follow-up visit or when the patient returns worse. The hospital teaches the natural history of severe disease, and the clinic teaches the early and the ordinary, which is where most diagnoses are actually made. The clinician who moves between them should expect the instruments to change and should not expect the reasoning to change with them.

A constructed illustration may make the shape of a teaching encounter concrete, and it is offered as a teaching device rather than as a portrait of any real person. Imagine a learner who presents a patient with a shortness of breath and says the diagnosis is an infection of the lungs. The teacher does not correct the diagnosis but asks what finding supports it, what else could produce the same picture, and what the learner would do if the fever were absent. The learner then notices that the heart rate has climbed to 110 beats per minute and that the pressure has fallen, and the diagnosis changes to a failing circulation, with the lung findings explained differently; the rate that should alarm a particular patient is not fixed, and this figure is a teaching illustration to be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, since fitness, age, and medicines all shift the line. That patient is not real, and the story should not be applied to any real person without a full assessment.

What the clinician writes about his or her own learning matters as much as what is written about patients. A brief entry after a difficult case, recording what was thought, what was found, and what would be done differently, is enough. The entry should be honest about emotion as well as reasoning, because fear, hurry, and irritation cause errors that a purely clinical note will never show. The register should be reviewed deliberately, and its patterns turned into the two or three specific aims already mentioned.

The patient is the ultimate source of feedback, and the clinician who asks can receive it directly. A patient who understood the plan, who knows what to watch for, and who knows when to return has become a partner in the diagnosis rather than a passive recipient of it. A patient who did not understand, who was not asked about the medicine already being taken, or who left without knowing when to come back has revealed a gap that no test result will show. Asking the patient to repeat the plan back is a form of assessment that costs nothing and catches errors before they leave the room.

A culture that supports this work is one in which uncertainty can be spoken and error reported without punishment. That culture is built by small acts: a senior who admits not knowing, a team that thanks the person who raises a concern, a meeting that changes a process rather than a reputation. It is destroyed by the same acts in reverse, and the destruction is faster than the building. The learner watches how errors are handled more closely than he or she listens to any lecture about safety.

Senior clinicians therefore teach most by what they do with uncertainty in front of others. A consultant who says the diagnosis is not yet clear, and who states what would settle it, teaches more than one who produces a confident label to end the discussion. A trainee who sees a senior ask for help learns that asking is a professional act rather than a failure, and a trainee who sees a mistake concealed learns to conceal the next one. The teaching of diagnosis is thus inseparable from the conduct of the team, and the two improve or decay together.

Some of what a clinician must review is not reasoning at all but the conditions in which the reasoning was done. A torch with a failing battery, a thermometer that has not been checked, a queue that does not allow a patient to be undressed, and a record system that loses the last note are sources of diagnostic error in their own right. A practice that audits itself will find these causes beside the cognitive ones, and will often find them easier to fix. Naming the system cause does not excuse the reasoning error, and naming the reasoning error does not excuse the broken equipment; both are examined and both are addressed.

If reasoning is what matters, then the assessment of learners should measure reasoning rather than recall. A question that presents a short story and asks for the next step, the dangerous possibility, and the finding that would change the plan tests something a factual question cannot. A commitment recorded before a result is known, and reviewed afterwards, tests calibration rather than knowledge, and a simulated deterioration in which the learner must notice a trend tests the habit of repeated observation. The examination that rewards the recitation of a list will produce clinicians who recite lists.

The loop closes when what was learned is put back into the next patient's care. The clinician who has identified a habitual omission asks a different question at the next consultation and watches whether the answer changes anything, the teacher who has found that a question works refines it, and the practice that has found a gap in follow-up changes how it arranges the next appointment. Without that return, self-assessment becomes introspection, which is pleasant and useless. The measure of whether the learning was real is not what the clinician felt during the review but what the next patient experienced. The habits described here are meant to be practiced within training, supervision, clinical judgment, and the protocol of the service, and not in place of them.

To summarize, the reasoning of this book is kept by practice on real cases, by committing to a diagnosis before the result arrives, by seeking feedback rather than waiting for it, by reviewing one's own errors without humiliation, and by auditing the practice against what happened to the patients it served. The lethal possibility kept in view is the missed diagnosis of a condition that kills within hours, and the finding that most often reveals it is the direction of the observations across time rather than any single reading. Every figure in this chapter, whether a rate, a pressure, a temperature, a score, or an interval, is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between countries, between editions, and between laboratories. The teacher's work is to ask questions that draw reasoning out rather than questions that test recall, and the clinician's work is to keep asking those questions of himself or herself. Expertise is maintained rather than acquired, and the clinician who stops examining his own judgment is the one who will be surprised by it. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service.
