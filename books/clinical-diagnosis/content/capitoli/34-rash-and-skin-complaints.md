# Chapter 34 — Rash and Skin Complaints

A rash is a sign the patient can see and the clinician can also see, yet it is one of the hardest problems to reason about, because the eye recognizes a pattern long before it can say what the pattern means. The skin reflects what is happening within it and beneath it, so the same red eruption may be a local irritation, a reaction to a medicine, or the first sign of an infection in the bloodstream. The reasoning here is a sequence of questions rather than a list of diseases: what shape is the lesion, how are the lesions arranged, and is the patient systemically well or ill. That last question overrides the others, because a comfortable patient with an itchy rash sits in a different position from one with the same rash who has a fever, a rapid pulse, and the look of serious illness. This chapter is educational material; it is not medical advice, it is not a protocol, and it is not a substitute for training, supervision, or the judgment of the clinician who is with the patient.

Before the ordinary patterns are described, the eruptions that kill must be named where the rash is first seen, since each is revealed by a finding that can be sought at once. A rash of small spots that do not fade when a clear glass is pressed against it is bleeding into the skin, and it may be the purpura of meningococcal disease, a bloodstream infection, or a failure of the clotting system, so it is treated as an emergency until explained. Red skin that then peels in sheets, especially after a new medicine and with involvement of the mouth, the eyes, or the genitals, raises toxic epidermal necrolysis, in which the skin detaches and the barrier against infection is gone. Pain far worse than the skin suggests, with spreading dusky redness, a hard feel to the tissue, or crackling under the fingers, points to necrotizing fasciitis, which is a surgical emergency. Fever with a widespread rash, a swollen face, enlarged lymph nodes, and a rising eosinophil count after a new medicine raises the drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms, in which organs as well as skin are attacked.

The first skill is description, performed in words that another clinician can picture without seeing the patient. A macule is a flat change in color, a papule is a small raised solid lesion, and a plaque is a larger raised area with a flat top, while a vesicle is a small fluid-filled blister, a bulla is a larger one, and a pustule is filled with pus. A wheal is a raised, pale, itchy swelling that comes and goes, a nodule is a solid lesion deeper in the skin, and a scale is a flake of dead surface, while a crust is dried fluid and an erosion is a shallow break in the surface. A clinician who says only that a patient has a red rash has said almost nothing, while one who describes raised, scaly plaques with sharply defined edges on both elbows has begun to make a diagnosis. Description also slows the mind, since finding the right word forces the eye to look a second time, and the second look is where the useful findings live.

Morphology is only half of the description, and distribution is the other half. The examiner notes whether the lesions are scattered or grouped, whether they follow a line, a band, or a circle, whether they cluster around hair follicles, and whether they are symmetric between the two sides of the body. Distribution may follow the territory of a single nerve, as it does when the chickenpox virus wakes in one band of skin, it may favor surfaces that face the sun, it may collect in the creases of the elbows and the knees, or it may begin on the trunk and spread toward the limbs. The palms, the soles, the scalp, the nails, and the mouth are part of the distribution and are examined every time, because a rash that involves them belongs to a different list of causes from one that spares them. Two patients described in the same words can have entirely different diseases, and the distribution is often what separates them.

The single most useful maneuver in this chapter is a press on the skin, and it takes nothing beyond a clear glass, a lens, or a finger. Firm pressure on a red lesion that fades and then refills means the redness comes from dilated blood vessels, which is the ordinary behavior of inflammation, while pressure that changes nothing means blood has left the vessels and lies in the tissue. The smallest bleeding spots are called petechiae and larger ones purpura, and the measurement commonly taught to separate them is a diameter of 2 millimeters, but that figure illustrates the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because texts and departments draw the boundary differently. The distinction matters far less than the non-blanching itself, which raises the possibilities of infection in the bloodstream, a disorder of the platelets, a failure of the clotting system, or an inflammation of the blood vessels. The examiner should also look for the same change in the mouth, the conjunctivae, and the nail beds, since bleeding in several places is more alarming than bleeding confined to one.

Fever with a rash changes the whole approach, because a systemic infection can be present before any other sign is visible. The clinician asks which came first, the fever or the rash, how long separates them, and whether the patient has been where a particular infection is common, and these questions narrow the field before the skin is examined. A rash that follows fever, cough, and red eyes, beginning on the face and spreading downward, suggests a viral exanthem. Fever also raises the possibility that the rash is a reaction to a medicine given for the fever, which is why everything the patient has taken, including anything bought without a prescription, belongs in the history. A temperature of 38 degrees Celsius is treated as significant in some services and ignored in others, so the threshold used where you work must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because it varies with the site of measurement, the age of the patient, and the edition of the reference. Mucosal involvement is the finding that turns a skin problem into a systemic one, and it is the most commonly missed part of the examination. The examiner looks inside the mouth, at the eyes, and at the genitals and the anal area, since eroded mucous membranes with peeling skin need urgent assessment.

The examination of the skin is systematic, unhurried, and performed in the best light available. The patient should be undressed enough for the whole surface to be seen, with a gown or a sheet for warmth and dignity, and the examination moves from the scalp to the soles rather than jumping to the most obvious lesion. Daylight is the best illuminant, because it shows subtle differences in color that an electric bulb flattens, and the examiner should look from the side as well as from above, since a raised edge and a fine scale are easier to see when light grazes the surface. Gloves, a clean surface, and hand hygiene before and after contact belong to the examination of every patient with a rash, because some eruptions are infectious and some skin is broken. The appearance of a rash depends on the color of the skin beneath it, so erythema is harder to see against brown or black skin, and the examiner relies there on warmth, swelling, tenderness, and a change in texture rather than redness alone, comparing the two sides and asking whether a patch feels different.

The symptoms that accompany a rash are as informative as its appearance, and they are obtained before the skin is touched. The clinician asks whether it itches, hurts, burns, or is numb, and whether the feeling is constant or comes and goes, because an itchy rash and a painful rash belong to different lists of causes; an itch that is worst at night suggests scabies or eczema, while burning or stinging may be a reaction to a medicine or a sign that nerves are involved. Some parts of the body hide a rash unless deliberately examined. The scalp must be parted and looked at directly, the area behind the ears inspected, and the spaces between the toes, the soles, the palms, and the nails examined, since these are frequent sites of fungal infection and psoriasis. A patient who is immobile should be turned and the pressure areas over the sacrum, the heels, and the hips examined, because a change there may be the first sign of an injury that will deepen unless relieved.

Eczema, also called atopic dermatitis, is the most common itchy rash of childhood, and it continues into adult life in many people. It favors the creases of the elbows and the knees in children and the hands, the neck, and the eyelids in adults, and it usually begins as small itchy blisters on red skin. The itch dominates the symptom and is often worse at night, and long continued scratching thickens the skin into a leathery texture while the scratches break the surface and allow bacteria to enter, which is why a sudden increase in redness, weeping, and pain may mean infection rather than a flare. The diagnosis is made from the pattern, the itch, the age at onset, and the personal or family history of asthma, hay fever, or eczema, and no test is needed in the ordinary case. A finding that would make the diagnosis wrong is a rash respecting a boundary the disease does not, such as an edge as sharp as a line or an area limited to one part of the body.

Psoriasis is the other great scaly eruption, and it is separated from eczema by the sharpness of its edges and the thickness of its scale. The typical lesion is a raised, red plaque covered by silvery flakes, with a clearly defined border, and it prefers the outer surfaces of the elbows and the knees, the lower back, the scalp, and the nails. The scales are dry rather than weeping, and the nails may show small pits, a separation of the nail from its bed, or a yellow-brown discoloration, while the joints may be painful and swollen in a patient whose skin disease has been long-standing. New lesions can appear where the skin has been injured, which is one reason the patient's account of a scratch, a burn, or a surgical scar matters. Psoriasis affects the whole body rather than the skin alone, so the joints, the mood, and the general health of a patient who has carried it a long time deserve attention.

Fungal infection of the skin is recognized by its edge, because the fungus is most active at the boundary of a lesion and has usually cleared the middle. The result is a ring with a raised, scaly, sometimes red border and a paler center, which is why the old name for it describes a worm even though no worm is involved. The infection may affect the feet and the spaces between the toes, the groin, the trunk, the scalp, or the nails, and each site has its own appearance and its own list of alternatives. On the scalp the hair may break and fall, and in a child with patches of hair loss and scaling the possibility of fungal infection must be considered before it is attributed to habit or stress. A scale scraped from the active edge and examined under the microscope after preparation with potassium hydroxide, or grown in a culture, settles the question where such a service exists, while a rash already treated with a steroid cream can deceive because the steroid flattens the active edge.

Scabies is a small mite that burrows into the surface of the skin, and it produces an itch famous for being worse at night and for affecting several members of the same household. The burrow is a thin, gray, slightly raised line a few millimeters long, found most often in the webs of the fingers, the wrists, the waist, the genital area, and the breasts, while the face and the neck are usually spared in adults. The diagnosis is clinical in most settings, and the finding that supports it is the itch in a family member or a sexual partner together with the distribution of the rash. Any figure used to describe the burrow is only an illustration, so the length that matters in your own practice should be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since teaching texts state such measurements differently. The decision to treat the whole household at the same time is a diagnostic act as much as a therapeutic one, and the finding that would make that decision wrong is a rash sparing the typical sites and having another explanation.

Urticaria is the rash of raised, pale, intensely itchy wheals that appear quickly and move from place to place, and the individual lesion is said to fade without leaving a mark. The speed and the transience are the diagnostic features, and the interval usually quoted for a single lesion illustrates rather than fixes the rule, so confirm it against the current guideline and the local protocol, since textbooks draw that boundary differently. A rash whose individual spots stay fixed in one place is probably not ordinary urticaria, so the question the clinician asks is not what the rash looks like now but what it looked like earlier. Angiedema is the same process deeper in the tissue, and it swells the eyelids, the lips, the tongue, and sometimes the throat, and that swelling is the dangerous part because it can obstruct the airway. The finding that would make urgent treatment unnecessary is a rash that is mild, stable, and free of swelling of the mouth or throat, though a patient whose voice is changing is treated without waiting for certainty.

A drug eruption is one of the most common reasons for a rash to appear in a patient who is already unwell, and its timing is the clue that points to it. The usual pattern is a red, blotchy rash that begins on the trunk and spreads, sometimes with fever and itch, and it can be difficult to separate from a viral exanthem. The history must include everything the patient has taken, including medicines bought without a prescription, traditional remedies, and anything given by injection, together with the date each started, and it should also ask about work, hobbies, new products, and plants. The decision to stop a suspected medicine is a diagnostic act as well as a treatment, and the finding that would make a drug the wrong answer is a rash that began before the medicine was started or one with a pattern the drug does not explain.

A rash is sometimes the most accessible evidence of a disease happening elsewhere, and the examiner who thinks only about the skin will miss it. A rash with painful joints, mouth ulcers, hair loss, and a change in the color of the fingers in the cold suggests an inflammatory disease of the connective tissue, and the same conditions may inflame the kidney, the lung, and the nerves. A rash with fever, a murmur, and a history of valve disease or of injecting drugs suggests an infection of the lining of the heart, and the skin signs may be small and easy to overlook. A rash with a rise in the eosinophil count, a swollen face, and an enlarged liver or spleen suggests a systemic drug reaction rather than a simple eruption. In each of these situations the skin is the door and not the room, and the decision is how much of the body to investigate before the picture declares itself.

The patient whose skin is blistering and peeling is among the most urgent in this chapter, because loss of the skin barrier threatens life. The clinician looks for skin that slides away when pressed or rubbed, for large blisters that join together, for raw surfaces where the top layer has separated, and for involvement of the mouth, the eyes, and the genitals. The cause may be a severe drug reaction, an infection, a burn, or an immune disease that splits the skin, and the treatment and the isolation required differ with the cause, so the history of medicines and the speed of the spread are essential. The extent of peeling matters, but it must be judged by someone who can see the whole patient, and any figure quoted for the proportion of skin involved is only an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since grading systems differ between services. The finding that would make this presentation less urgent is a small, stable blister in a patient who is otherwise well, which is a different problem with a different pace.

Necrotizing fasciitis is the infection that destroys tissue beneath the skin, and it is named again here because the skin at the beginning can look almost innocent. The earliest complaint is pain out of proportion to what is visible, because the process follows the layers of tissue rather than the surface, and as it advances the skin becomes dusky, then purple, then gray, while blisters, a crackling sensation under the fingers, and numbness may appear as nerves are destroyed. Fever, a rapid pulse, and the look of serious illness are common, but their absence does not exclude the diagnosis, and the patient with diabetes, liver disease, or a suppressed immune defense is at particular risk. The decision that matters is whether to take the patient to surgery, and the finding that supports it is a spread that is visibly advancing or a wound that does not behave as a simple infection should. Where imaging exists it may show the depth of the process, but it must not delay the call to the surgeon, because this diagnosis is made by the eye and confirmed in the operating room.

Any rash in a patient who is becoming ill is assessed for the signs of organ failure, because the skin may be the visible part of an infection that is already systemic. The clinician looks at the color and warmth of the hands and feet, the time for color to return after pressure, the rate of breathing, the pulse, the level of alertness, and the passage of urine. A patient who is confused, breathing quickly, and whose hands are cold despite a warm trunk needs treatment and senior help immediately, and the rash is the least important finding in that assessment. The decision about treatment is made on the whole picture rather than the appearance of the skin, because a patient with a non-blanching rash and a fever may need treatment before any result returns. The finding that would make treatment the wrong answer is a clear, benign explanation for every abnormal sign together with a patient who looks well, and even then the patient should be watched rather than sent away without a plan.

In the dermatology clinic the description is made with better light, more time, and tools the field clinic does not have. A magnifying instrument with its own light reveals the fine structure of a lesion, a camera records it so that change over time becomes visible, and a biopsy takes a small piece of skin for examination under the microscope, which is decisive for many tumors and several inflammatory diseases. Scrapings and swabs can identify a fungus, a bacterium, or a virus, blood tests can detect the antibodies and organ involvement that point to a connective tissue disease, and patch testing can identify a contact allergy. The reasoning behind each test is the same as everywhere else, because the clinician should know what a positive result and a negative result would each change before the test is ordered. None of these tools replaces the history and the examination, and many experienced clinicians reach the correct diagnosis before the first test is requested.

In a clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, the same reasoning is applied with fewer instruments and more care. The torch is held to the side rather than straight on, because a side light reveals the raise and the scale that a direct light hides, and the examiner uses the brightest natural light available. The whole skin is still examined, and the patient is asked to point to every place that itches, hurts, or looks different, while a mirror or the help of a family member allows parts the patient cannot see to be examined. Where the light is poor and the lesions are subtle, the honest answer may be that the diagnosis cannot be made today, and the right decision is to ask the patient to return in daylight rather than to guess. A return visit, with the reason explained and the journey made worthwhile, is a diagnostic instrument in its own right, and it should be arranged deliberately rather than promised vaguely.

The treatment of a skin problem is part of the diagnostic reasoning, and the decision is often made at the same visit. Treatment is the right answer when the picture fits a condition that responds to it and the patient is not systemically ill, and it is also right when a serious infection is suspected and waiting for confirmation would put the patient at risk. Treatment is the wrong answer when the rash signals a systemic disease that has not been assessed, when a medicine is the likely cause and stopping it is the safer test, and when the diagnosis is uncertain enough that treating now would hide the very finding that would settle it. A clinician who applies a cream to a rash that is part of a drug reaction, or who treats a spreading infection as a local problem, may delay the decision that matters most. Naming the finding that would justify treatment, and the finding that would forbid it, is more useful than naming a product.

A rash should be written down in words that allow another clinician to picture it without seeing the patient. The record should describe the individual lesion, the arrangement, the distribution, the extent, the color, the texture, and the state of the mucous membranes, together with what the patient says about the itch, the pain, and the timing, while the general condition, the temperature, the pulse, the breathing, and the medicines taken belong in the same note. When the diagnosis is uncertain, the clinician should say so plainly and record what was considered and what was set aside, so that the next reader can continue the reasoning rather than begin it again. The patient should leave with clear instructions about what should bring them back, including a rash that spreads quickly, a fever, a sore mouth or eyes, a blister, a growing area of pain, or any difficulty breathing. A return visit arranged in advance is worth more than a warning to come back if things get worse, because a frightened or unwell patient may not judge that for themselves.

A constructed illustration may make the method concrete, and it is offered as a teaching device rather than a portrait of any real person. Imagine a traveler with a fever, a headache, and a rash of small spots that do not fade when a clear glass is pressed against them, in whom the non-blanching rash is noticed at once and changes the whole plan because the clinician was taught to press before deciding. Now imagine a child with an itchy rash in the creases of the elbows whose family also itches at night, in whom the household history turns a vague eczema into a clear diagnosis that can be made locally. Now imagine a patient who started a new medicine recently and who now has a fever, a swollen face, and a widespread rash, in whom the combination is recognized as a systemic drug reaction rather than a simple eruption.

It is worth saying plainly that a pattern is not a patient. The pictures and the names in this chapter were assembled from groups of people, usually in a particular climate, age group, and setting, so a description that fits most of them will mislead in the patient who does not fit. A teaching framework tells the clinician which questions to ask and which dangers to exclude, not what the answer must be. The same eruption on a fair arm and on a dark arm, or in a newborn and in an older person, may belong to a different list of causes.

To summarize, a rash is a visible sign whose meaning depends on the shape of the lesion, its distribution, the speed of its appearance, and above all on whether the patient is systemically well or ill. The non-blanching rash, the peeling skin with mucosal involvement, the spreading area of pain worse than it looks, and the fever with a widespread eruption after a new medicine are named at the bedside and not deferred, and each demands senior help. Eczema, psoriasis, fungal infection, scabies, urticaria, viral exanthem, and drug eruption are separated by pattern, distribution, itch, timing, and the general state of the patient rather than by any single sign. Every figure in this chapter, whether a temperature, a size, an interval, or a proportion, is an illustration of reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between countries, editions, and laboratories. This is educational material and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service, and a patient with a rash who looks unwell is assessed and helped first, with the exact name of the eruption settled afterward.
