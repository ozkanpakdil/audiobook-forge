# Chapter 41 — Acute Neurological Emergencies

Acute neurological emergencies are the presentations in which a mistake is measured in minutes rather than in days, and they are organized here by the decision each one forces rather than by the anatomy each one damages. A stroke forces a decision about time, because brain tissue that is not perfused is lost and the chance to restore flow closes as the clock runs. Meningitis forces a decision about treatment before proof, because the organism is not yet known and the patient may worsen while the laboratory works. A seizure that will not stop forces a decision about the failure of the mechanism that normally terminates it. Raised pressure inside the skull forces a decision about the threat of herniation, in which the brain is pushed through the openings that confine it and the patient dies of compression rather than of the original disease. Each of these forces a different set of findings, yet all are assessed by the same short examination performed in the same order. This chapter is educational material; it is not medical advice, it is not a protocol, and it cannot replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your service.

Danger is named before the ordinary reasoning begins, because each of these killers announces itself through a finding that can be sought in the first minutes. A sudden severe headache that reached its worst in an instant may mean bleeding around the brain, and the finding that supports it is a neck that resists flexion with a dislike of light in a patient whose consciousness is otherwise preserved. Fever, headache, and a rash that does not fade when a clear glass is pressed on it may mean infection of the membranes around the brain and of the bloodstream at once, and that rash is the finding that ends the debate about whether to act. A seizure that continues without recovery between attacks means the brain's own stopping mechanism has failed. A consciousness that is falling, pupils that have become unequal, and breathing that has become irregular mean raised pressure inside the skull that is threatening herniation, and the pupil that dilates and no longer reacts is the finding that turns a headache into an emergency.

The first minute is spent on the things that kill before the diagnosis is made, in the same order in every setting. The airway is looked at and listened to, because a patient who cannot protect it or who breathes noisily needs help before any imaging is discussed. Breathing is assessed by counting the respiratory rate and watching the chest, and the oxygen saturation is measured where an oximeter exists. The circulation is assessed by the pulse, the pressure, and the color and warmth of the skin, since a brain that is not perfused cannot be assessed properly whatever its disease. The blood glucose is measured at once, because a low blood sugar imitates every emergency in this chapter and is reversed far more easily than any of them. The temperature is recorded, because fever points toward infection and a very high temperature can itself drive a seizure.

The level of consciousness is the most useful observation in a neurological emergency, because it is the first thing to change and the easiest to record. The Glasgow coma scale is the way this book records it, and the reasoning it formalizes is simple: how the eyes open, what the voice produces, and what the limbs do in response to a command or to pain. Its value is not the exact figure but the fact that the same observer, or a different one, can compare one hour with the next and see a direction, and it is the direction rather than the single reading that should change the plan. The contents of the scale, the way the parts are added, and the figure at which a service becomes alarmed must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the version in use differs between hospitals and editions. A patient who is drowsy but rousable is a different problem from one who responds only to pain, and a patient who has become less responsive since the last observation is deteriorating whatever the figure says. Where the scale is not used, the clinician should still describe the behavior in words, because a clear sentence about what the patient did is worth more than a figure no one can interpret.

Lateralizing signs are the findings that suggest a lesion on one side of the brain, and they are sought deliberately rather than noticed by accident. The face is examined for a droop of the mouth and a failure to close one eye, and the patient is asked to show the teeth and to squeeze the examiner's fingers with each hand. The arms are compared by asking the patient to hold both out with the eyes closed, because an arm that drifts downward and inward reveals a weakness the patient may not report. The legs are compared in the same way, and where the patient cannot cooperate the response to pain on each side is observed. Speech is tested by asking the patient to name ordinary objects and to repeat a sentence, since difficulty in finding or producing words points to the language areas of the dominant side. The visual fields are checked by asking the patient to look at the examiner's nose and to say when a moving finger appears in each part of the view, because a field missing on one side is easily missed.

The neck and the back of the eye are examined next, because they answer two different dangers at once. The examiner asks the patient to touch the chin to the chest and watches for resistance, then lifts the head gently to see whether the hips and knees flex, another sign of irritation of the membranes. Resistance in a patient with fever and headache supports infection of the membranes, while resistance in a patient with a sudden severe headache supports bleeding around the brain. The fundus is examined with an ophthalmoscope where one exists, and the finding sought is a swollen optic disc with blurred margins, which suggests that pressure inside the skull has been raised for some time. The pupils are compared for size and reaction to light, and eye movements are tested in all directions to look for a palsy of the nerves that serve them. The corneal reflex, the gag reflex, and the strength of the cough are noted in the patient who is not fully conscious, because they describe how the brainstem is working and whether the airway is at risk. Papilledema takes time to develop, so its absence in a patient who became ill very recently does not exclude raised pressure, and a normal fundus must never be used to dismiss it.

The pattern of the seizure carries as much information as the event itself, and the account must come from someone who watched it, not only from the patient. The clinician asks where the movement began, whether it stayed on one side, whether the eyes turned, whether the patient was aware, and how long the event lasted. A seizure that begins in one hand and spreads up the arm, or that begins with a smell, a flash of light, or an unpleasant feeling in the stomach, is focal in onset and points to a lesion in a particular region. A seizure that involves both sides from the first moment is more often generalized, though a focal onset can be missed when no one saw the beginning. The state afterwards matters, because weakness of one arm or one side that clears over the following hours, known as a Todd paresis, is strong evidence that the event was a seizure rather than a stroke. A return to full consciousness between events is reassuring, while a patient who does not wake between seizures has entered the dangerous state in which the brain cannot stop. A first seizure in an adult, a seizure with fever, and a seizure after a head injury each raise different possibilities and each needs its own assessment.

Stroke is the emergency in which time is the substance of the decision, because brain tissue deprived of blood is lost at a rate that can be measured, and the chance to restore flow closes with every minute. The clinician must establish when the patient was last known to be well, which is not the same as when the symptoms were noticed, and must write that time down where everyone can see it. The decision that follows is whether the patient is a candidate for treatment that dissolves a clot, and that decision belongs to a team with imaging and a protocol rather than to a single pair of hands. The interval within which such treatment can be considered is often quoted as a number of hours from the onset, but that figure is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because it has been revised repeatedly and differs between services and countries. Treatment is the right answer when the patient arrives inside the accepted interval, when the symptoms are disabling, and when imaging has excluded bleeding. Treatment is the wrong answer when the scan shows hemorrhage, when the patient is already improving steadily, or when the time of onset cannot be established and the risk cannot be judged. The clinician who cannot obtain imaging must still decide whether to move the patient, and that decision rests on the same time of onset.

No examination can separate a clot from a bleed with certainty, and the clinician who pretends otherwise will eventually harm a patient. The findings that raise the probability of bleeding are a sudden onset with a headache that peaked at once, vomiting, a falling level of consciousness, and a pressure that climbs while the pulse slows. The findings that raise the probability of a clot are a stepwise or gradual onset, a clear time of onset, and a deficit that matches the territory of one artery without early loss of consciousness. The most important step, however, is to look for the conditions that imitate a stroke and are treated differently, because several are common and reversible. A low blood sugar is the first, and its threshold is defined by the laboratory that measures it, so that figure must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the level at which symptoms appear varies with the patient. A seizure that has left a weakness behind, an infection of the brain, a migraine with a prolonged deficit, and a metabolic disturbance can each present exactly as a stroke. The clinician who treats the first computed tomography, or CT, as the end of the assessment rather than the beginning of it will miss the mimic that was never vascular.

Meningitis is the emergency in which treatment begins before the diagnosis is confirmed, because the organism is not yet known and the delay spent waiting for proof can cost the patient his life. The classical findings are fever, a severe headache, a dislike of light, vomiting, and a neck that resists flexion, and later the level of consciousness falls. A temperature above 38 degrees Celsius is often taught as the line at which fever is present, but that figure is an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the threshold shifts with the site of measurement, the age of the patient, and the edition of the reference. A rash that does not fade when pressed is the finding that should end all hesitation, because it points to infection in the bloodstream as well as in the membranes, and that combination can progress within hours. The clinician must also weigh the age and the immune state of the patient, since an older person, a person taking treatment that suppresses the immune response, and a person living with the human immunodeficiency virus, known as HIV, may show few of these signs and may present only with confusion. The decision to treat before confirmation is right when the picture is convincing, when the patient is deteriorating, or when a lumbar puncture cannot be performed safely at once. The decision is wrong when a clear alternative explains the picture completely and the patient is stable enough for the diagnosis to be completed first.

A seizure that does not stop is a failure of the mechanism that normally ends it, and that failure is the emergency rather than the seizure itself. The definition rests on a duration, and the duration usually quoted is about 5 minutes of continuous seizure activity or of repeated seizures without recovery, a figure offered here as an illustration of the reasoning and to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because services draw the line at different points. The finding that reveals the danger is a patient who is not waking between events, whose movements may become subtle or hidden while the brain continues to discharge. A patient who has stopped moving but remains unconscious and unresponsive is not necessarily improving, and the clinician who assumes recovery at that moment may miss activity that is still going on. The cause must be sought at the same time as the seizure is brought under control, because a low blood sugar, a low sodium level, an infection of the brain, a medicine that was missed or withdrawn, and a new structural lesion each demand a different response. The pattern of the movements should be described in words, including which side moved first and whether the eyes turned, because those details decide what happens after the event. Treatment is the right answer when the seizure is continuing or recurring, and the wrong answer when the movement has a cause that is not epileptic, such as a faint or a shaking attack from which the patient can be distracted.

Raised pressure inside the skull is dangerous not because of the pressure itself but because of what it does to the brain, which is pushed through the rigid openings at the base of the skull in a process called herniation. The findings that suggest it are a headache worse on waking or on lying flat, vomiting without nausea, a level of consciousness that is falling, and a pressure that is high while the pulse is slow and the breathing irregular. The pupil that becomes fixed and dilated on one side is the classic sign of part of the temporal lobe being pushed downward, and it may appear before the consciousness changes. The clinician should look for a recent head injury, a fever that suggests a collection of pus, a history of cancer that suggests a secondary deposit, and the use of treatment that thins the blood, because each changes the speed of the deterioration. The patient should be positioned with the head of the bed raised where that is tolerated, the neck should not be obstructed, and the blood pressure should not be allowed to fall, since the brain depends on that pressure. A lumbar puncture in this patient can remove the pressure that is holding the herniation back, and it is the wrong decision when any of these findings is present until imaging and advice have been obtained.

Bleeding around the brain from a ruptured aneurysm announces itself through the history rather than through the examination. The patient describes a headache that reached its worst within an instant, often with vomiting, neck stiffness, and a dislike of light, and some patients report a smaller warning headache in the days before. The examination may be entirely normal, which is why the description of the onset carries the whole diagnosis, and a patient sent home with a diagnosis of migraine after such a description is a patient at risk. Clotting in the veins that drain the brain is a less familiar danger that produces headache, seizures, weakness on one side, and a level of consciousness that fluctuates, and it is more common in pregnancy, after childbirth, in dehydration, and in conditions that make the blood clot more easily. An infection confined to a collection of pus inside the skull, an inflammation of the brain itself, and a rise in blood pressure severe enough to damage the brain each imitate the emergencies already described. A tumor that bleeds, a disorder that makes the blood too thick, and a poison such as carbon monoxide can also present this way, and the history is the only place where they are found.

The most important group after the structural emergencies is the group of systemic causes that present as neurological emergencies and are corrected by treating the body rather than the brain. A low blood sugar, a low sodium level, a high calcium level, a failing kidney, a failing liver, an infection, and the withdrawal of alcohol or of sedative medicine can each produce confusion, seizures, or coma that looks exactly like a primary disease of the brain. The clinician must therefore measure what can be measured, and where nothing can be measured must ask what the patient has been drinking, eating, and taking, and what has changed in the days before the illness. An infection of the brain can follow an infection elsewhere, and in a patient living with HIV the list of causes is wider and the usual signs are less reliable. Tuberculosis of the membranes around the brain, a fungal infection, and a parasitic infection of the brain each occur in particular places and in particular patients, and the travel history and the immune history decide whether they belong on the list. These causes are named because they are treatable, and because a clinician who searches only for stroke and meningitis will send away the patient whose confusion was metabolic. The finding that changes the plan is a measurement that was not expected to be abnormal, so the tests that would reveal these causes belong early.

In a hospital the assessment is the same, but the questions can be answered within minutes and the order in which they are answered changes. Computed tomography, or CT, of the head is performed first in most patients with a suspected stroke, because it separates bleeding from a clot, and that separation decides whether clot-dissolving treatment is possible. Computed tomography of the blood vessels, magnetic resonance imaging, or MRI, and a study of the vessels in the neck answer further questions when the first image is not enough, and the clinician should know which question each test is meant to settle before it is ordered. An electrocardiogram, or ECG, is recorded in every patient with a new neurological deficit, because a clot formed in the heart is a common cause of stroke and the rhythm of the heart may be the source. Blood is sent for sugar, sodium, calcium, and the tests of kidney, liver, and clotting function, since each changes the ranking of the differential. A lumbar puncture is performed when infection or bleeding around the brain is suspected and the safety questions have been answered, and the fluid is examined for cells, protein, sugar, and organisms.

In a clinic that has a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, the task is not the same diagnosis but the same reasoning pushed to a single decision. That decision is whether the patient can be watched where he is or must begin a journey, and the journey itself may take hours and may worsen the patient on the way. Some diagnoses can be made without any image at all: a fever with a rash that does not fade, a seizure that will not stop, a pupil that has changed, and a limb that has become weak are each recognizable at the bedside, and each demands action rather than investigation. The torch makes the pupils visible and the thermometer turns fever into a number. The clinician should record the time of onset, the level of consciousness, the weakness, the neck, and the pupils, and should repeat the observation at short intervals, because a patient who is worse on the second look must travel. A patient with a seizure that has stopped, who has woken fully, and whose examination is normal can sometimes be watched, provided that someone can observe him and a plan for review exists. A patient with fever and a rash that does not fade, with a falling level of consciousness, with a new weakness, or with a pupil that has changed must be moved, and the decision should be made early rather than after a second examination confirms what the first suggested. A normal examination in a patient who worries the clinician is a reason to look again and to discuss, never a reason to relax, and the honesty of that uncertainty is part of the reasoning.

Treatment enters this chapter only as a decision, because the doses and the regimens belong to the protocol of the service and not to a book about diagnosis. Treatment is the right answer when a treatable cause is strongly suspected and the patient is deteriorating, when the findings point to infection of the membranes and waiting for confirmation would cost more than acting, or when a seizure has not stopped and the brain is being damaged with every minute. Treatment is the wrong answer when the scan has shown bleeding and the treatment designed for a clot would make it worse, when a metabolic cause such as a low blood sugar explains the whole picture and is corrected directly, or when an attack that is not epileptic is mistaken for a seizure and the patient is quietened for it. The finding that usually decides is not the dramatic one but the one that appeared on the second examination, the pupil that changed, the arm that drifted further, the fever that climbed, or the glucose that was never measured. The clinician should ask what finding would make the treatment right and what finding would make it wrong, and should write both down before the treatment is given, because that habit protects the patient from the momentum of the clinician. Naming the decision rather than the medicine is what transfers between settings.

A constructed illustration may fix these habits, and it is offered as a teaching device rather than as a description of any real person. Imagine a man of about 60 who was last seen well at breakfast, who now cannot lift his right arm and cannot find words for ordinary objects, and whose blood sugar is normal when it is measured at once. The time of onset is written down, the weakness is recorded, and the scan shows no bleeding, so the decision about clot-dissolving treatment can be made by the team that owns that protocol. Now imagine a young woman brought to a clinic with fever, a severe headache, and a rash that does not fade when a clear glass is pressed on it, whose neck resists flexion and who is becoming drowsy. Neither patient is real, and neither story should be applied to a real person without a full assessment. What the two share is the move that matters: the clinician looked for the finding that would change the decision rather than waiting for the diagnosis to become obvious.

The record of a neurological emergency should show the reasoning and not only the findings, because the next reader must see what was considered and what was set aside. The time of onset belongs at the top, together with the level of consciousness at each observation, the weakness on each side, the pupils, the neck, the temperature, and the blood sugar. The patient or the family should be told which signs must bring him back or must bring him to a hospital, and that advice must be specific enough to be followed, including a new weakness, a headache that reached its worst in an instant, a seizure that will not stop, a fever with a rash that does not fade, and a fall in the level of consciousness. A follow-up visit arranged in advance is worth more than a promise to return if things worsen, because many patients cannot judge that for themselves. No patient in this chapter is real, and any resemblance to a person you have met is a coincidence of the disease rather than a portrait.

To summarize, the acute neurological emergencies are grouped by the decision each one forces, so that stroke is read against the clock, meningitis is treated before it is confirmed, a seizure that will not stop is recognized as a failure of the usual termination, and raised pressure inside the skull is feared for the herniation it threatens. The examination is short and always the same, moving from the level of consciousness to the lateralizing signs, the neck, the fundi, the pupils, and the pattern of the event, and the findings that separate bleeding from a clot from an infection from a metabolic cause are found in the history and in a few measurements rather than in a single image. Every score, interval, pressure, temperature, and rate named in this chapter is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between countries, editions, and laboratories. The hospital answers the same questions with imaging, laboratory tests, and a team, while the clinic answers them with a torch, a thermometer, and the decision about whether the patient can be moved at all. The dangers are named at the bedside and not deferred: a headache that reached its worst in an instant, a rash that does not fade when pressed, a pupil that has changed, and a patient who does not wake between seizures. This is educational material and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service. A patient who is deteriorating is helped and moved first, and the exact cause is settled afterwards.
