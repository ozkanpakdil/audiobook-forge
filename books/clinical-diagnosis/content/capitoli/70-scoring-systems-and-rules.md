# Chapter 70 — Scoring Systems and Rules

A score asks several questions in a fixed order and adds the answers, and a rule turns those answers into a decision. Both exist because judgment varies, memory is unreliable, and two competent clinicians can assess the same patient differently on the same afternoon. The reasoning that organizes this chapter is that a score is a formalization of somebody else's thinking rather than a substitute for the listener's own, fixing which findings were thought to matter, how heavily each was weighted, and what its authors feared most when they chose a line. The instruments used here as examples are the Wells score for the probability of a clot, the Curb sixty-five score for the severity of pneumonia, the Cha two D S two Vasc score for the risk of stroke in atrial fibrillation, the Ottawa rules for the decision about a radiograph after an injury, and the Centor criteria for the probability of streptococcal pharyngitis, and each is described by the reasoning it organizes rather than by its arithmetic. The arithmetic behind all of them changes with time and must be read from the current source and the local protocol rather than remembered from a lecture.

Before the mechanics of any rule, the causes that kill must be named where they appear, because a score never feels the patient. The patient whose probability of a clot is scored as low may still be dying of a clot that has traveled to the lung, and the finding that betrays it is a sudden dyspnea with a rapid pulse, sometimes with a swollen tender calf and sometimes with no leg signs at all. The patient whose pneumonia is scored as mild may be deteriorating while the form is completed, and the finding is a breathing rate that has climbed or a new confusion in an older person. The patient whose sore throat scores as likely streptococcal may instead have a deep infection of the neck that is closing the airway, and the findings are drooling, a muffled voice, difficulty swallowing, and a posture propped forward. The child who is scored as low risk may have meningitis or an infection of the bloodstream, and the finding is a rash that does not fade when a clear glass is pressed on it. A hemorrhage into the brain, a clot that travels to the brain, and an infection spreading through the blood outrank every number on a form.

Three questions should be asked before any rule is applied. The first is what decision the rule is meant to inform, because one built to decide whether to order a test behaves differently from one built to decide where a patient should be cared for. The second is the population in which it was derived and the one in which it has since been checked, since a rule tested in teaching hospitals may perform poorly in a rural clinic where the disease is rarer. The third is what the rule leaves out, which is usually the patient's own account, the ability to return if things worsen, the home circumstances, and the appearance that worries the clinician. Some scores predict, some stratify severity, some decide a test, and some merely record a state, and the Glasgow coma scale belongs to that last family because it summarizes consciousness without claiming to know the future. A rule not checked in a population like the one in front of the clinician is a hypothesis rather than a tool, and anyone using it should confirm the current figures against the current guideline and the local protocol.

A score produces a probability, and the same probability means different things in different rooms. A positive result that indicates serious illness on a ward where that illness is common may indicate a false alarm in a clinic where it is rare, because the meaning of a result depends on how many people in the room actually have the disease. This is why a rule can look excellent in the paper that derived it and disappointing in a new setting without any error by its authors. Prevalence changes with season, with the case mix of the clinic, and with the way patients are referred. A clinician who knows the local frequency of the disease is therefore better placed than one who knows only the score, and anyone who cannot estimate that frequency should treat the score as weak support and confirm the line in use against the current guideline and the local protocol.

Every cut-off is a choice about which mistake is worse. Moving the line in one direction catches more of the patients who have the disease and labels more of those who do not, while moving it the other way misses more true cases and raises fewer false alarms. The authors of a rule make that choice according to the danger of the illness and the harm of the action that follows, so a rule about a clot in the lung is built to miss as few clots as possible and accepts many false alarms, while a rule about a sore throat may accept missing some cases that would have settled anyway. A listener who knows which way a rule leans can use it intelligently, and the exact line in any published rule must be taken from the current source and the local protocol, because the same rule is often reproduced with a different threshold in a later edition.

This chapter will not recite the arithmetic of any score, and the restraint is deliberate. The items, the weights, and the line above which a result is called positive have changed between editions of the same rule, between countries, and between the specialties that use it. A listener who memorizes a number from a spoken chapter will carry it into a clinic where a different number is in force and will have no way to notice the error. What can be carried is the structure, which is the findings the rule considers, the direction in which each one pushes, and the decision the result is meant to support. A clinician who holds the structure can complete the arithmetic from the current source at the bedside, or can reason without it when no source can be reached, and where no source is available the honest course is to reason from first principles, record the uncertainty, and confirm the figures later against the local protocol.

The Wells score formalizes the reasoning about the probability of a clot in a deep vein or in the lung, and it is a good example of a rule built to guide a test rather than to make a diagnosis. Its items are the findings and risk factors that a thoughtful clinician would weigh anyway, among them a recent operation or a long period without moving, a cancer, a pregnancy or the weeks after birth, the use of hormones, a previous clot, a swollen tender calf, and a fast pulse. The reason the probability is estimated before anything is ordered is that the meaning of the next test depends on it. In a patient whose probability is low, a normal blood test for the product of clot breakdown makes the diagnosis very unlikely, and imaging can often be avoided, while in a patient whose probability is high the same blood test changes almost nothing and the decisive test is an ultrasound of the leg veins or a CT of the arteries of the lung. The cut-off that separates these groups is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the version in the original paper and the versions in use today do not draw the same line.

The Wells score was derived and refined in adults who came to emergency departments with a suspected clot, most of them able to walk, speak, and give a history. It performs less well in the groups that were thinly represented in those studies. In pregnancy the normal swelling and edema of the legs and the altered clotting of that state blur the findings the score counts, and the risk of a clot is genuinely higher, so the score is not the instrument to lean on. In the very old, in patients with cancer, and in those who have already had a clot, the baseline risk is high enough that a reassuring number means less than the arithmetic suggests, and in a patient already in hospital and unable to move the score was not designed for that situation at all. The greatest danger is the sentence in which a low number persuades a clinician that a breathless and frightened patient cannot have a clot. The findings that override any score are a sudden dyspnea, chest pain that worsens with a breath, a cough that brings blood, a fainting, or a swollen tender calf, and any of those should lead to a second look and a check against the current guideline and the local protocol.

The Curb sixty-five score formalizes the assessment of severity in a patient with pneumonia acquired in the community, and it answers a different question from the one that asked whether pneumonia is present. Its items are the signs that experience teaches a clinician to fear, among them confusion, a raised level of a waste product the kidney normally clears, a breathing rate that has climbed, a pressure that has fallen, and an age that reduces reserve. The result decides where the patient should be cared for and how closely watched, so it informs a decision about setting and urgency rather than diagnosis. The danger the score cannot see is the patient who looks comfortable and is quietly short of oxygen, whose breathing rate rises only when the patient walks, or whose confusion is mistaken for age. The finding that should override a comfortable score is a breathing rate that has increased between two observations, a new confusion, or a fall in oxygen that appears when the patient moves. The figures that separate the risk groups must be taken from the current guideline and the local protocol, because they have been revised and are applied differently in different services.

The Curb sixty-five score grew out of audits of adults admitted to hospital with community-acquired pneumonia in the United Kingdom, and that origin limits where it travels well. It was not designed for a young patient whose only abnormal finding is a low pressure, for a nursing home resident whose baseline state is already altered, or for a patient whose immune defenses are suppressed and who may carry a severe infection with few signs. It also depends on a measurement of kidney function that a clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer cannot obtain, so there the rule can be started but not finished. The correct response is not to assume the missing item is normal, because that assumption converts an unknown into a falsely reassuring score. The clinician should complete what can be completed, name the missing part openly, and let the decision rest on what was measured together with the appearance of the patient. The threshold used locally must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the cut-offs and the actions attached to them differ between editions.

The Cha two D S two Vasc score formalizes the estimation of stroke risk in a patient with atrial fibrillation, and it exists to support one of the most consequential decisions in medicine, which is whether to treat such a patient with a medicine that reduces clotting. Its items are the conditions that raise the risk of a stroke, among them a previous stroke or a brief disturbance of brain function from a clot, an age above a certain line, a weakened pump, high pressure, diabetes, disease of the vessels, and being female. The reasoning it organizes is that risk is not the same for every patient with an irregular rhythm, and that the decision should follow the estimated risk rather than the rhythm alone. It does not settle the matter by itself, because the same decision must weigh the risk of bleeding, the patient's own values, and the ability to take a medicine reliably. The line above which treatment is recommended is an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because guidelines in different countries have set it at different points and have changed it over time.

The Cha two D S two Vasc score was derived in people with atrial fibrillation that was not caused by disease of the mitral valve, most of them older adults in Europe and North America. It performs less reliably in patients with a mechanical or badly damaged valve, in those with moderate or severe narrowing of the mitral valve, and in people whose irregular rhythm comes and goes rather than persisting. Its power to predict is weaker in the very old, in people with severe kidney impairment, and in populations whose baseline risk of stroke differs from the one in which it was built. A score that predicts a population's risk cannot promise a particular patient's future, and a clinician who treats a low number as a guarantee is making a category error. The danger of the score replacing judgment is greatest when it is used to withhold treatment from a patient who has strong other reasons to receive it, or to give treatment to a patient whose bleeding risk has not been considered. Anyone relying on it should read the current version from its source and check the local protocol, because the items and their relative weight have been revised.

The Ottawa rules formalize a narrower and very practical decision, which is whether a patient who has injured an ankle, a foot, or a knee needs a radiograph. Their purpose is to reduce unnecessary imaging by identifying patients in whom the probability of a fracture is very low, and that purpose is different from finding every fracture. The reasoning behind them is that a small number of examination findings, taken together, can separate the patients who need an image from those who can be treated with support and advice, and using them well means accepting that they are designed to miss almost nothing while sending some patients for an image that turns out to be normal. The danger is not the fracture the rule occasionally allows through but the serious injury it was never built to detect, such as a dislocation that has already returned to place, an injury to a nerve or a vessel, or a wound that has opened the joint. The findings that demand attention regardless of the rule are a foot that is cold, pale, or pulseless, a limb that is numb or cannot be moved, a wound over the joint, and a deformity that returns as swelling settles. The interval after injury within which the rule was designed to be applied must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

The Ottawa rules were derived in alert adults and older children who came to emergency departments soon after an injury, and their limits follow from that population. They were not built for a patient who is intoxicated, who cannot cooperate with an examination, who has a distracting injury elsewhere that masks the pain, or who arrives long after the event when swelling has changed the findings. In the very young child who cannot describe the pain, the examination is less reliable and the rules should not be used as though the child were an adult. In the multiply injured patient the question is not whether one joint needs an image but how the whole patient should be assessed. The greatest danger is the clinician who reads a negative rule as evidence that nothing serious has happened, when the rule was only ever about a fracture. The listener should confirm the version in use against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the items and the excluded groups have been restated in later publications.

The Centor criteria formalize the estimation of the probability that a sore throat is caused by the streptococcal organism, and they guide the decision about testing and treatment rather than make a diagnosis. The findings they gather are the ones a careful clinician would look for anyway, among them a fever, tender and enlarged lymph nodes in the neck, a white coating on the tonsils, and the absence of a cough. The reasoning is that these features together raise the probability enough to justify a test or a treatment decision, while their absence makes the infection less likely and supports another explanation. The danger that no score addresses is the sore throat that is not a sore throat, and every clinician should look for it before counting points. A deep infection of the neck, an infected collection beside the tonsil, or an infection of the flap that guards the airway can all present first as a sore throat, and the findings that reveal them are drooling, a muffled voice, difficulty swallowing, a stiff neck, or a patient who sits forward and refuses to lie down. The cut-off at which testing or treatment is recommended must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the line and the recommended action vary between countries.

The Centor criteria were derived in adults who came to clinics and emergency departments with a sore throat, and they perform least well where that population is left behind. Children have a different pattern of illness and a different risk of complications, so an adult score applied to a child may mislead in both directions. In communities where rheumatic fever still occurs, the consequences of a missed streptococcal infection are far greater than where it has become rare, and the threshold for treatment is correspondingly different. In a patient whose immune defenses are suppressed, the ordinary signs may be blunted and the score falsely reassuring. The most common error is not arithmetic but examination, since a clinician who counts the criteria without looking into the throat, feeling the neck, and checking that the airway is safe has used the rule to avoid the patient. The figures attached to each criterion must be read from the current source and the local protocol, because the criteria have been reproduced with different weights and different advice.

In a well-resourced hospital, scores are used mainly to organize urgency while more information is on its way. An early warning score built from the observations at the bedside tells the ward how often to look again and when to call for help, and its purpose is to make deterioration visible before it becomes irreversible. The quick sequential organ failure assessment formalizes the recognition of a patient whose infection is threatening the organs, using findings that any nurse can obtain, and the Glasgow coma scale records consciousness in a way that can be compared across shifts. None of these instruments diagnoses anything and none of them sees the patient, so a score that improves while the patient looks worse is a signal to trust the patient. The hospital's advantage is that the same measurements can be repeated often and placed beside laboratory results and images, which turns a single score into a trend. The danger is the ritual in which the form is completed, the number is filed, and the thinking stops, and every threshold used to trigger a call for help must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

In a clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, the same reasoning is available even when the same arithmetic is not. The breathing rate, the pulse, the temperature, the pressure, the level of consciousness, the ability to walk, the color of the skin, and the look of the throat can all be assessed with those three instruments and a watch, and those are the items most scores depend on. What cannot be obtained is a laboratory value, an image, or a measurement that needs a machine, and the clinician must judge how much that missing piece matters to the decision at hand. A score with a laboratory component cannot be completed here, and the honest course is to say so rather than treat the missing result as normal. The reasoning then becomes a referral decision, which is whether the patient can be watched here, whether treatment should begin on the clinical picture, and whether the journey to a hospital must start now. In this setting the appearance of the patient and the trend of the observations carry more weight than any instrument, and the figures printed in any rule must be compared with the current guideline and the local protocol, since a rule read in one clinic may have been revised before it is used in the next.

Scores built on adults require particular caution in children, in pregnant women, and in older adults, because normal values and the speed of deterioration differ. A child compensates for a falling circulation by raising the heart rate and keeping the pressure up, and then fails suddenly, so a score that waits for the pressure to fall will wait too long. The findings that should override any reassuring number in a child are a refusal to feed, a floppy body, a rash that does not fade when pressed, a breathing rate that is rising, and a parent who says the child is not right. In pregnancy the normal ranges of pulse, pressure, and breathing are altered and the risk of a clot is genuinely increased, so rules that ignore the pregnant state should not be leaned on. In the frail older patient a score may be normal while the patient has lost the ability to stand, to eat, or to think clearly, and those losses are the finding. Every age-specific threshold must be taken from the current guideline and the local protocol, since the lines that define abnormality in a child are drawn by age and differ between sources.

A score that is not written down has done only half its work, and a score written down without its reasoning can do harm. The note should record the findings that produced the number, not only the number, so that the next clinician can see what was examined and whether the findings have changed. When the score and the clinician's impression disagree, the disagreement should be documented rather than resolved silently in favor of the number. The deepest danger of a scoring culture is that it replaces the act of looking at the patient, so that a number computed at the door becomes an anchor that pulls every later observation toward its first verdict. A low number can license premature closure, so that the examination is finished before it has begun and the patient's own account is never heard, while a high number can begin treatment before the diagnosis has been questioned. The safeguard is to recalculate the score whenever the patient changes and to treat a patient who looks worse than the number as worse. Any figure copied into a record should carry the reminder that it must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol.

A constructed illustration may make the discipline concrete, and it is offered as a teaching device rather than as a portrait of any real person. Imagine a middle-aged patient who arrives in a clinic with a swollen calf and a low score for the probability of a clot, whose score is recorded honestly, and who is sent onward because the clinician notices the swelling and a fast pulse despite the reassuring number. Imagine instead a child with a fever and a rash that does not fade when a clear glass is pressed on the skin, whose score for the severity of the illness is low, and whose clinician sets the score aside and arranges urgent transfer. Now imagine an older patient with an irregular pulse and a low score for the risk of stroke, in whom the decision about treatment is made after the bleeding risk and the patient's own wishes have been discussed rather than from the number alone. None of these patients is real, and none of these stories should be applied to a real person without a full assessment. What the three share is the move that matters, in which the clinician used the score to organize the questions and then let the patient decide the answer.

To summarize, a score is a formalization of clinical reasoning and never a substitute for the examination of the patient. The Wells score organizes the probability of a clot, the Curb sixty-five score organizes the severity of pneumonia, the Cha two D S two Vasc score organizes the risk of stroke in atrial fibrillation, the Ottawa rules organize the decision about imaging after an injury, and the Centor criteria organize the probability of streptococcal pharyngitis, and each was built in a particular population and leans in a particular direction. The lethal possibilities are named where they belong and outrank every number, among them the clot in the lung, the pneumonia that is deteriorating, the deep infection of the neck, the meningitis in a child, and the stroke that the risk score was meant to anticipate. The arithmetic is deliberately not spoken here, and every score, cut-off, reference range, pressure, temperature, rate, and duration mentioned in this chapter, together with every figure attached to any instrument, must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such numbers differ between countries, editions, and laboratories. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of the service in which the patient is seen. A patient who looks worse than the score is a patient who is worse, and the clinician who keeps asking that question is using scores as they were meant to be used.
