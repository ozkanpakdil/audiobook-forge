# Chapter 33 — Altered Consciousness, Confusion and Seizures

A patient who is not thinking clearly presents the most time-critical problem in medicine, because the brain tolerates the loss of oxygen, glucose, and blood flow only briefly. A disturbance of consciousness is therefore an emergency, and the differential that explains it must be run in minutes rather than days. The reasoning turns on two questions asked at once: has the brain itself been injured, or is it reacting to something outside it, such as a poison, an infection, or a failing organ. The states range from the patient who cannot be roused, through the patient who is awake but inattentive, to the patient who has had a seizure and now looks well. This chapter is educational material, it is not a protocol, and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the guideline of your own service.

The lethal possibilities must be named at once, each with the finding that reveals it. Hypoglycemia is revealed by a bedside glucose below the accepted threshold, and it is the cause that most often reverses completely once corrected. Hypoxia is revealed by the work of breathing, by blue lips and tongue, or by a chest that barely moves air. A stroke is revealed by a sudden focal deficit, such as weakness of one side, a gaze turned to one side, or a drifting arm. Meningitis and encephalitis are revealed by fever with a stiff neck, a dislike of light, a rash that does not fade under pressure, or a change in behavior with a seizure. Status epilepticus is revealed by a seizure that will not stop, or by attacks from which the patient does not wake, while raised pressure inside the head, head injury, poisoning, sepsis, and metabolic derangement complete the list.

The first question is not the diagnosis but whether the patient can be woken and whether the airway is safe, because that answer decides everything that follows. A patient who responds to voice and protects the airway can be examined in an orderly way. One who does not respond, whose breathing is noisy, or whose chest barely moves needs attention before any diagnostic reasoning begins. Cardiopulmonary resuscitation, or CPR, starts at once in the patient with no pulse, and the search resumes when a circulation is restored. In a clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer this moment decides whether the patient can be watched, carried, or must travel now.

Consciousness has two components, and separating them makes the assessment precise. Arousal is the level of wakefulness, the degree to which the patient is alert and responsive. Content is what the mind does with that wakefulness, including attention, orientation, memory, language, and the hold on a train of thought. A patient can be fully awake with profoundly disordered content, which is delirium and psychosis, or drowsy with content that stays intact once roused. The record should carry both the ease of rousing and the quality of what the patient says, because a note that says only that the patient was drowsy has lost half the information.

The Glasgow coma scale is the standard way of recording the level of consciousness, and it separates eye opening, verbal response, and motor response. The three components should be written down separately, because they carry different information and the motor response is the most informative. A single figure means far less than the trend, so the score belongs in the record with the time it was taken. The figure at which a service becomes alarmed is not universal, so confirm it against the current guideline and the local protocol, where the threshold and the version of the scale may be stated differently. Where a formal scale is impractical, a short ladder of alert, responsive to voice, responsive to pain, and unresponsive detects a change when it is recorded honestly.

Delirium is the most common disturbance of consciousness in hospital practice, and it is a syndrome rather than a disease, so its presence demands a search for the cause. It develops over hours to days, fluctuates through the day, and its cardinal feature is inattention, so that the patient loses the thread of a question. Orientation is usually disturbed, often to time before place and to place before person, which is why a patient who knows his own name may still be severely delirious. Some patients are restless and hallucinating, which is the hyperactive form everyone notices, while others are quiet and slow, which is the hypoactive form mistaken for depression, fatigue, or good behavior, and the interval over which delirium develops is an illustration, so confirm the definition against the current guideline and the local protocol.

Dementia is a different problem, a chronic and progressive decline in cognition that begins slowly and leaves arousal intact until late. The patient is usually alert and may be fully oriented early, and the family can often date the change in memory or word finding to months. The dangerous error is to attribute a new confusion to a known dementia, because delirium is far more likely to explain a change that arrived over a short interval. Delirium is superimposed on dementia more often than it occurs in a normal brain, so the change from baseline rather than the baseline itself is the message.

Psychosis is the third state to separate, defined by a disturbance of content while consciousness, attention, and orientation remain largely intact. Such a patient may hear voices, hold a fixed false belief, or behave strangely, but can usually say where he is and can attend to a conversation. The distinction matters because a psychotic patient can be examined and can agree to simple steps, while a delirious patient cannot reliably do either. It matters also because a first episode of psychosis in an adult may be the surface of an organic cause, including encephalitis, a drug effect, a metabolic disturbance, or a lesion in the brain, and a clinician who labels such a patient psychiatric without examining the temperature, the pupils, the neck, and the glucose has made a diagnosis by assumption.

The reversible causes are sought systematically, and a framework such as Vindicate forces the clinician to walk through the systems rather than stop at the first plausible answer. The search covers infection of the brain, its coverings, and elsewhere; metabolic and endocrine derangement, including glucose, sodium, calcium, and disorders of the thyroid and adrenal glands; drugs and poisons, including prescribed medicines, alcohol, and traditional remedies; structural disease, including stroke, bleeding, tumor, and raised pressure inside the head; and trauma the patient cannot report. Seizures, failure of the liver or kidney, deficiency of thiamine and vitamin B twelve, and withdrawal from alcohol or sedatives belong in the same sweep. The framework does not rank the causes and cannot say which one is present, because it only guarantees that none has been forgotten by accident, and the ranking comes from the story, the examination, and the few available tests.

The history in a patient who cannot give it must be taken from someone else, and the family, the friend, and the ambulance crew are primary sources rather than interruptions. The useful questions are what the patient was like at baseline, when the change began, whether it came suddenly or crept in, and whether it has fluctuated. A witness to a collapse can describe what the patient did before falling, what the limbs did, how long it lasted, and what the patient was like afterwards, and that account is often worth more than any test. The medicine list and the pill bottles in the bag are part of the examination, because a new, doubled, or stopped medicine explains a great deal of confusion, and where no history is available the clinician must record that fact rather than assume it was normal.

The examination follows a fixed order so that the urgent parts are never postponed by the interesting parts. The clinician confirms the airway, counts the breathing, feels the pulse, measures the blood pressure, takes the temperature, and checks the oxygen saturation if a device is available. The glucose is measured at the bedside, because it is the one reversible cause that changes management at once. The pupils are examined with a torch, and their size, equality, and response to light are recorded, since a difference between the two sides suggests pressure inside the head while pinpoint pupils suggest an opioid effect. The neck is tested for stiffness, the skin is searched for a rash, a wound, a bite, a needle mark, or jaundice, and a patient who cannot give a history should be undressed and examined completely on the assumption that injury is present.

Hypoglycemia must be excluded first and by measurement rather than impression, because it can produce every picture in this chapter, from confusion to deep coma and seizures. It is most likely in the patient who takes insulin or another medicine that lowers glucose, in the patient who has not eaten after activity or illness, and in the patient with advanced liver or kidney disease or severe infection. The finding that reveals it is a bedside glucose below the accepted threshold, and that threshold should be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because it differs between services and patient groups. Where no meter is available the decision becomes clinical, and the balance of risk favors treating a patient who might be hypoglycemic rather than waiting for a test that may never arrive.

Hypoxia and a failure to clear carbon dioxide are the next causes, and they are found with the eyes and the hands before any machine is needed. A hypoxic patient may be restless, confused, or drowsy, with blue lips, tongue, and nail beds, although the change is easier to see in some skin tones than others. A patient retaining carbon dioxide is often drowsy, warm, and flushed, with a headache and a bounding pulse, and may be a person with known lung disease. A falling breathing rate, a chest that moves very little, or a noisy airway demands action before diagnosis, and a normal oxygen reading does not exclude the retention of carbon dioxide.

A stroke is a lethal and time-dependent cause of altered consciousness, and the finding that reveals it is a sudden focal deficit rather than a symmetrical drowsiness. The patient may have weakness of one side, a drifting arm, slurred or absent speech, a gaze turned to one side, or a neglect of one half of the world. A stroke in the back of the brain may present without weakness, with unsteadiness, double vision, difficulty swallowing, or a drowsiness easily attributed to tiredness. The time at which the patient was last known to be well is among the most important facts, because it decides which treatments are possible, and that interval differs between services and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, while a low glucose must always be excluded because hypoglycemia imitates stroke perfectly.

Infection of the coverings of the brain and infection of the brain itself are the next dangers, and both can kill within hours. The patient with meningitis has fever, severe headache, a stiff neck, and a dislike of bright light, and may vomit, while a rash that does not fade when a clear glass is pressed against it is a finding of great importance. The patient with encephalitis is more likely to present with a change in behavior, a seizure, or a fever with confusion, and the neck may be supple. The diagnosis is revealed by examination of the fluid that surrounds the brain, after the safety questions about pressure inside the head have been answered, and where the picture is convincing and that examination cannot be done at once, treating on the clinical suspicion is usually right. Everyone who examines such a patient must use personal protective equipment, or PPE, according to the local protocol, and must remember that in the older patient, in the patient with infection by the human immunodeficiency virus, known as HIV, and in the patient whose immune defenses are suppressed, the neck may be supple and the fever absent.

Raised pressure inside the head is a mechanism rather than a diagnosis, produced by bleeding, swelling, a tumor, an obstruction to the flow of fluid, or infection. The patient becomes progressively drowsy, complains of a headache that is worse on lying flat or on waking, vomits without nausea, and may have a rising blood pressure with a slowing pulse. The pupils may become unequal and then fixed, and the level of consciousness may fall in steps rather than smoothly. The finding at the bedside is a falling level of consciousness with a new pupil change, and the level of pressure or pulse that should cause alarm is not the same in every service, so confirm any such threshold against the current guideline and the local protocol. A lumbar puncture in such a patient can be fatal, which is why the patient with these signs needs senior help and a place where that help exists, without waiting for tests that can be done on arrival.

A head injury is easily missed in the patient who cannot say what happened, in the patient who smells of alcohol, and in the older patient who takes a medicine that prevents clotting. The bleeding may be slow, so the patient can be awake and talking at the first assessment and deteriorate over the following hours, and this interval of apparent recovery is the trap. The finding that reveals the expanding collection is a falling level of consciousness, a new weakness on one side, a worsening headache, vomiting, or a pupil that dilates. A patient whose injury was significant and whose examination is not perfectly normal should be sent where imaging and observation are possible, and a patient sent home must be given a clear account of what should bring him back, given to a person who will stay with him.

A seizure is a symptom rather than a diagnosis, and the question that organizes the assessment is not whether the event was a seizure but what caused it. A first seizure in an adult is a structural or metabolic question until it has been answered, and the causes include stroke, bleeding, tumor, infection, a scar from an old injury, a metabolic derangement, a poison, and withdrawal from alcohol or sedatives. A seizure in a patient with a known diagnosis of epilepsy is still a question about why it happened now, whether a missed medicine, an infection, a lack of sleep, or a new structural problem lies behind it. The clinician should resist the comfort of the familiar label, because the distinction between a provoked seizure and one arising from a permanent tendency changes the decision about long-term management, which belongs to a specialist and to the current guideline.

The witness account is the most valuable evidence in a suspected seizure, and it should be obtained before it fades from memory. The clinician asks what the patient was doing before the event, whether there was a warning, whether the eyes turned or the head deviated, and what the limbs did on each side. The color of the face, frothing at the mouth, a bite to the side of the tongue, and loss of control of the bladder are all useful details. A collapse with brief jerking and a rapid recovery is more typical of a faint, while a slow recovery with confusion and headache is more typical of a seizure. A patient who remains weak on one side may simply have weakness that follows a seizure, but a deficit that persists beyond the expected recovery points to a structural cause, and that expected interval is an illustration, so confirm it against the current guideline and the local protocol.

Status epilepticus is a medical emergency in which a seizure does not stop by itself, or in which seizures recur without the patient recovering consciousness in between. It is lethal through the strain it puts on breathing, the heart, and the brain, and it is one of the few situations in which diagnosis and treatment must begin at the same moment. The finding that reveals it is simply that the event is continuing, or that the patient is not waking between events, and it requires no laboratory. The duration that separates a prolonged seizure from status epilepticus is stated differently in different guidelines and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because acting on a remembered figure can waste the time that matters. Treatment is right when the event is continuing or recurring, when the airway or the breathing is compromised, and when the glucose is low, while it is wrong when the events have stopped and the priority is to find the cause.

Poisoning and the effects of drugs are among the most reversible causes of altered consciousness, and the clues are often on the body rather than in the blood. The patient with an opioid effect has pinpoint pupils, a slow breathing rate, and a deepening drowsiness, while the patient with a sedative effect is drowsy but rousable, and the patient with alcohol intoxication has the smell on the breath and a coarse tremor. Carbon monoxide poisoning produces headache, confusion, and a cherry-red color in some patients, but that color is not reliable, and the diagnosis is suspected from the setting, such as a fire, a faulty heater, or a shared illness in a household. Withdrawal from alcohol or sedatives produces tremor, sweating, a rising pulse, hallucinations, and seizures, so the medicine list and the empty bottles are the best test available and should be read rather than remembered.

Sepsis is a cause of confusion that must be named, because it is common and because the confusion may be its first and only sign, particularly in the older patient and the patient whose immune defenses are suppressed. The finding that reveals it is not the fever, which may be absent, but the combination of a rapid pulse, a climbing breathing rate, a falling blood pressure, cool or mottled skin, and a shrinking passage of urine. A systolic pressure below 90 millimeters of mercury is often quoted as the point at which shock must be assumed, but that figure must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the pressure that matters depends on the patient's usual value and on age and pregnancy. A patient with HIV infection, or a patient taking a medicine that suppresses the immune response, may show none of the classical signs, so the threshold for suspicion must be lower rather than higher, and the examination must look at the chest, the abdomen, the urine, the skin, the joints, and every line and tube in the body.

Metabolic derangement is the group of causes the examination cannot see, and it is found by measuring what can be measured and by reasoning from the pattern of the illness. A low or high sodium, a high calcium, a rising urea and creatinine, a failing liver, and a disturbance of the thyroid or adrenal gland all produce drowsiness, confusion, or coma. The patient with liver failure has a flapping tremor of the outstretched hands, a jaundiced skin, and a musty breath, while the patient with kidney failure may have a dry mouth and deep sighing breathing, and the patient with a high calcium may be drowsy and passing large volumes of urine. Deficiency of thiamine and of vitamin B twelve can produce confusion and unsteadiness, and is common in patients who are malnourished, who drink heavily, or who have had surgery on the gut, so where no laboratory exists the clinician must treat what is plausible and transfer the patient.

In a well-resourced hospital the patient passes through a sequence designed to miss nothing, though it must still be driven by a person who is thinking. The glucose is measured immediately, the oxygen and carbon dioxide in the blood are checked, and the electrolytes, kidney and liver function, calcium, blood count, and markers of infection are sent. Computed tomography of the head is performed early in any patient with a focal deficit, a head injury, a suspected rise in pressure, or an unexplained coma, and magnetic resonance imaging answers questions the earlier scan cannot. Electroencephalography is used when the question is whether the brain is having seizures that cannot be seen from the outside, and cultures and a screen for poisons each answer a question the clinician should be able to state in advance. The intensive care unit, or ICU, is where a patient who cannot protect the airway or who is having continuous seizures is cared for, and the decision to move there rests on the clinical picture rather than on a completed diagnosis.

In a clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, the same reasoning is compressed into fewer steps and sharper decisions. The first question remains whether the patient can be woken and whether the airway is safe, and that answer decides the journey rather than the diagnosis. The clinician counts the breathing, feels the pulse, takes the temperature, and examines the pupils with the torch, because these require nothing but attention. Where the patient cannot be woken, where the breathing is inadequate, where fever with a stiff neck is present, where a focal deficit has appeared, or where a seizure is continuing, the journey starts now and treatment begins on the way. Where the patient is awake, oriented, and improving and a clear reversible cause has been corrected, observation with a defined plan may be right, and the record and the safety plan are the only things that travel with the patient.

Because the level of consciousness is a moving quantity, repeated observation is worth more than any single assessment, and the interval between observations is itself a clinical decision. A patient unchanged at the next review is a different patient from one who has drifted from alert to responsive to voice over the same period. The clinician should record the time of each assessment and what changed since the last, and where the setting cannot provide reliable observation, that inability is itself a reason to transfer. Several habits of thought cause harm here more often than ignorance does, and the first is anchoring on the most visible explanation, such as alcohol on the breath, then failing to look for the head injury, the low glucose, or the infection beside it. Others are treating a normal scan as a completed diagnosis, assuming that a psychiatric label explains the confusion of the present, and failing to ask a witness, and each is corrected by naming the leading explanation, stating what would prove it wrong, and going to look.

A constructed illustration may make the reasoning concrete, and it is offered as a teaching device rather than as a portrait of any real person. Imagine an older man brought to a clinic by his daughter because he has been confused since morning, and imagine that the clinician records that he is awake but inattentive, with a temperature of 38 degrees Celsius and a breathing rate of 28 breaths per minute. That temperature is an illustration of the threshold that defines fever and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, and the same is true of the breathing rate. The clinician finds a red, warm area over the hip and a chest that is dull at one base, and decides that the confusion is delirium with two possible sources rather than a dementia that was never there before. The patient is transferred with a note recording the level of consciousness, the temperature, the breathing rate, the glucose, and the findings on the skin and chest, and no part of this story describes a real patient or should be applied to one.

To summarize, altered consciousness and seizure are emergencies in which the first questions are whether the patient can be woken and whether the airway is safe. The lethal causes are named at the outset with the finding that reveals each, including hypoglycemia, hypoxia, stroke, meningitis, encephalitis, status epilepticus, raised pressure inside the head, head injury, poisoning, sepsis, and metabolic derangement. Delirium, dementia, and psychosis are separated by the speed of onset, the fluctuation, the attention, and the preservation of orientation, and a new confusion in a patient with a known dementia is treated as delirium until its cause is found. A seizure is examined for what caused it and not only for what it looked like, and the witness account and the trajectory over repeated observations carry more weight than any single test. Every score, cut-off, reference range, pressure, temperature, rate, and duration in this chapter is an illustration of reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between countries, editions, and laboratories. This is educational material and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service, and when the patient is deteriorating the reasoning pauses and the patient is helped first.
