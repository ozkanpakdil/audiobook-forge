# Appendix B — Differential Diagnosis Tables by Symptom

This appendix gathers the differential diagnosis of the common presentations into spoken form, so that a clinician who is stuck can run through the possibilities in the order the patient's story deserves. The order applies one principle twice: first by likelihood, so the ordinary explanation is tested early, and then by danger, so the killing diagnosis is named before it is comfortable to do so. Each presentation opens with its can't-miss entry, the diagnosis that must be set aside before any other is settled, and the finding that reveals it is named in the same breath. What follows is a sequence of possibilities rather than an exhaustive list, each attached to the finding that moves it up or down, because a diagnosis without a discriminating finding is only a name. This appendix is educational material and does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of the service in which the patient is seen.

Fever most often arrives without a focus, and the first question is whether the patient is stable enough to be investigated at leisure. The can't-miss entries are meningitis, infection of the bloodstream, malaria in a patient who has traveled, and infection on a heart valve, and each announces itself through a finding rather than through the height of the temperature. A temperature such as 38 degrees Celsius is often taught as the line above which fever is recorded, but that figure must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the accepted threshold depends on the site of measurement, the age of the patient, and the edition in use. A stiff neck, a rash that does not fade under pressure, a new murmur, and a recent journey move those dangerous entries upward. The bedside can tell an alert, hydrated patient from one who is drowsy, mottled, or breathing quickly, while the test that names the organism and the referral that provides it belong to a hospital.

Chest pain is a presentation in which the danger is not proportional to the severity, and the can't-miss entries are an acute myocardial infarction, a dissection of the aorta, a clot in the lung, and a tear of the esophagus. Discomfort that is new, comes at rest, or has changed its pattern moves infarction upward, and a normal examination does nothing to remove it from the list. A tearing pain that travels between the chest and the back, with a difference between the pulses or pressures in the two arms, moves dissection upward. Sudden breathlessness with a swollen calf moves a clot in the lung upward, and vomiting after a violent retch moves a tear of the esophagus upward. At the bedside the clinician can compare the arms, feel the pulses, listen to the heart and lungs, and press the chest wall; the tracing of the heart, the enzyme the laboratory measures, and the imaging of the aorta belong to a hospital.

Dyspnea and cough ask whether the problem lies in the lungs, the heart, the airway, or the blood, and the can't-miss entries are a clot in the lung, a severe infection, a collapsed lung, an airway that is closing, and pulmonary edema. A sudden onset in a patient with recent immobility, surgery, cancer, or a previous clot moves a clot upward. High fever with crackles and dullness moves infection upward, sudden breathlessness with reduced breath sounds and a shifted windpipe moves collapse upward, and stridor with a change in the voice, or swelling of the face and tongue, moves a closing airway to the top at once. The patient who cannot lie flat and coughs frothy, blood-stained sputum moves pulmonary edema upward, and a raised pressure in the neck veins with swollen ankles supports it.

Headache and dizziness divide into the sudden and the gradual, and the sudden onset carries the danger. The can't-miss entries are a subarachnoid hemorrhage, meningitis, a stroke or bleeding into the brain, raised pressure inside the skull from a tumor, and the headache of carbon monoxide poisoning or very high blood pressure. A headache that reaches full intensity almost at once, often described as a blow to the back of the head, moves a subarachnoid hemorrhage to the front, and it must be treated as that diagnosis until a test excludes it, because the first examination is often normal. Fever with a stiff neck moves meningitis upward, a headache with weakness or disturbed speech or vision moves a vascular event upward, and a headache worse on waking, with coughing, or lying flat moves raised pressure upward. Dizziness that truly spins suggests the inner ear, while dizziness that is faintness suggests the circulation, and asking what the patient means separates the two. The bedside can examine the eyes, vision, power, and balance; imaging of the brain and examination of the fluid around it belong to a hospital.

Abdominal pain is where the can't-miss entries change with age, sex, and the possibility of pregnancy, and where dangerous diagnoses hide behind a soft, unimpressive abdomen. The can't-miss entries are a ruptured ectopic pregnancy, a leaking or ruptured aortic aneurysm, a perforated hollow organ, a strangulated hernia, whose blood supply has been cut off, a twisted ovary or testis, and mesenteric ischemia, a loss of blood supply to the bowel. In any woman of childbearing age pregnancy is settled first, because a positive test moves a ruptured ectopic pregnancy to the top at once. Pain out of proportion to the examination, in an older patient with an irregular pulse or known aneurysm, moves a vascular catastrophe upward, while a tender hernia that cannot be reduced, pain that settled before returning worse, and a rigid abdomen move perforation and strangulation upward. The bedside can inspect, palpate, percuss, and listen, and can examine the hernial openings and the testes; ultrasound for free fluid, the pregnancy test, and imaging of the bowel and vessels require a hospital, and a deteriorating patient must travel rather than wait.

Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and constipation are usually self-limiting, and they are dangerous in the few patients who are dehydrated, bleeding, obstructed, or poisoned. The can't-miss entries are dehydration with failing kidneys, obstruction of the bowel, bleeding from the gut, an infection that has spread to the bloodstream, and a metabolic cause such as diabetic ketoacidosis, the accumulation of acid when insulin is lacking. Vomiting that is bile-stained or feculent, abdominal distension, and the absence of stool and gas move obstruction upward, and a high-pitched or absent bowel sound supports it. Blood in the vomit or stool moves bleeding upward, while a dry mouth, weak pulse, and shrinking urine move dehydration upward, and deep, rapid breathing with a smell of acetone moves ketoacidosis upward. A patient who cannot keep fluids down, has lost weight quickly, or has severe pain is not one for watchful waiting.

Bleeding from the gut and jaundice are signs rather than diseases. The can't-miss entries for bleeding are a burst varicose vein in the esophagus, a bleeding ulcer, a tear where the esophagus meets the stomach, and a cancer of the gut; for jaundice they are obstruction of the bile duct, infection of the bile ducts, a failing liver, a cancer of the pancreas, and the destruction of red cells, called hemolysis. Black, tarry stool and vomit like coffee grounds move bleeding from the upper gut upward, fresh red blood from the back passage moves bleeding from the lower gut upward, and a falling pressure with a rising pulse means active bleeding whatever the color of the stool. Jaundice with fever, right upper abdominal pain, and a tender liver is an emergency from infected bile ducts, while painless, progressive jaundice moves a cancer upward. The bedside can look at the color of eyes and skin, feel the liver and spleen, and examine the back passage; the blood count, liver and clotting tests, endoscopy, and imaging belong to a hospital. A hemoglobin such as 70 grams per liter is the kind of figure a laboratory may report after bleeding, and the range against which it is judged belongs to that laboratory and the population it serves, so read it with the local report and the current guideline rather than from memory.

Palpitations and collapse ask two questions at once, whether the heart is the cause and whether the circulation has failed. The can't-miss entries are a rhythm that has made the circulation fail, a clot in the lung, bleeding from the gut, a ruptured aneurysm, an ectopic pregnancy, and a seizure mistaken for a faint. Collapse during effort, with chest pain, or with a family history of sudden death moves a dangerous rhythm upward, and the electrocardiogram, or ECG, answers that question where it exists. A pulse that is very slow, such as fewer than 40 beats per minute, very fast, or irregular without pattern must be recorded and acted upon, but the rate at which a particular patient becomes unstable depends on the heart and the patient, so that figure must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol rather than applied as a rule. The bedside can measure pulse and pressure lying and standing, listen to the heart, and ask what the patient remembers; the ECG and monitoring of the rhythm belong to a hospital.

Back pain is common and mostly mechanical, and the task is to find the few patients in whom it is not. The can't-miss entries are compression of the spinal cord, infection of the spine, a cancer that has spread to the spine, a fracture, and a leaking aortic aneurysm or kidney stone presenting as back pain. Compression of the cord declares itself through weakness of the legs, numbness around the back passage and inner thighs, and difficulty passing urine or holding stool, and it is an emergency that requires referral before the diagnosis is complete. Fever, a history of injecting drugs, a recent spinal procedure, or a suppressed immune defense moves infection upward, while constant pain, worse at night and unrelieved by rest, moves a cancer upward, especially with a known tumor or unexplained weight loss. The bedside can inspect the spine, test power and sensation in the legs, examine the back passage, and check the groin pulses; imaging of the spine and inflammatory markers in the blood require a hospital.

Unintentional weight loss and fatigue are often diagnosed from the history, because the patient's own account of what has changed carries most of the information. The can't-miss entries are a cancer, tuberculosis, infection with the human immunodeficiency virus, or HIV, diabetes, an overactive thyroid gland, depression, and a chronic infection or inflammation. A weight loss of about 5 per cent of the body weight over 6 months is one way its significance is described, but the figure must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the threshold depends on the patient's starting weight and the reference consulted. Night sweats, an enlarged lymph node, a cough that will not settle, and an evening fever move tuberculosis and cancer upward, while thirst, large volumes of urine, and blurred vision move diabetes upward. A fast pulse, heat intolerance, tremor, and a swelling in the neck move an overactive thyroid upward.

Swelling and lumps divide into the generalized and the local, and a local lump raises the question of cancer. The can't-miss entries are a cancer of the breast or another organ presenting as a lump, an infected collection of pus, a clot in a deep vein, and the swelling of heart, kidney, or liver failure. A lump that is hard, fixed, irregular, and painless demands an answer, and any lump in the breast, neck, armpit, or groin should be examined with the whole region and the opposite side for comparison. Swelling of both legs with breathlessness and a raised pressure in the neck veins moves heart failure upward, while swelling beginning in the face and spreading downward suggests a kidney cause, and swelling with jaundice and a swollen abdomen suggests the liver. A single warm, tender calf moves a clot upward, and a hot, red, painful area with fever moves infection upward.

Urinary symptoms and blood in the urine are separated first by whether the patient is passing urine easily and whether the blood is visible. The can't-miss entries are a cancer of the kidney, bladder, or prostate, an infection of the kidney, an obstruction such as a stone, and a serious injury to the urinary tract after trauma. Visible blood, especially in an older patient and especially when painless, moves a cancer upward and must be investigated rather than attributed to infection, because infection and cancer can exist together. Fever, flank pain, and tenderness over the kidney move infection upward, while colicky pain traveling from the flank to the groin moves a stone upward. A poor stream, difficulty starting, and a feeling of incomplete emptying move an obstruction of the outlet upward, and a patient who cannot pass urine at all with a tender, distended lower abdomen needs relief now. The bedside can inspect the abdomen, percuss the bladder, examine the back, and dip the urine where a dipstick exists; microscopy of the urine and imaging of the urinary tract belong to a hospital.

A change in consciousness gives the clinician the least time and needs the widest list, because the causes come from every system at once. The can't-miss entries are a low sugar in the blood, a low oxygen, an infection of the brain or its covering, a stroke or bleeding into the brain, a poisoning or withdrawal from alcohol, a head injury, and a seizure that is continuing or recurring. The bedside can measure the sugar where a meter exists and can assess the airway, breathing, and circulation, and those three are addressed before the cause is named. Fever with a stiff neck moves infection of the covering of the brain upward, while sudden weakness on one side, disturbed speech, or unequal pupils move a vascular event upward. A seizure that has not stopped, or that returns without the patient waking, is an emergency requiring treatment and referral at once. The bedside can look for a rash, a bitten tongue, a head wound, and the smell of the breath; salts and sugar in the blood, imaging of the brain, and examination of the fluid around it belong to a hospital, and a patient who cannot protect the airway needs the intensive care unit, or ICU.

A rash is read by its distribution, shape, color, and whether it fades when pressed, and those four questions separate most dangerous entries from ordinary ones. The can't-miss entries are a rash that does not fade under pressure, which may mean bleeding into the skin from an infection in the bloodstream, a drug reaction that is peeling the skin, a severe reaction with fever and involvement of the mouth and eyes, and the rash of a serious illness such as measles in a vulnerable patient. A purple rash that does not fade when a clear glass is pressed against it must be acted upon before it is explained, and skin peeling or coming away in sheets requires referral at once. A rash beginning after a new medicine, with fever and a rising pulse, moves a drug reaction upward, while a rash with fever, a runny nose, and a cough in an unvaccinated child moves measles upward. The bedside can examine the whole skin in good light, including palms, soles, scalp, and mouth, and can ask what the patient has taken, eaten, or touched; a skin sample, a swab, and blood tests belong to a hospital. A severe skin reaction may need the protective measures of a burns unit rather than an ordinary ward.

Bleeding, bruising, and anemia are grouped because they may share one cause, a failure of clotting, a shortage of red cells, or a loss of blood that has not been seen. The can't-miss entries are a cancer of the gut or the blood, a failure of the bone marrow, the tissue inside bone where blood cells are made, a shortage of platelets, the small cells that help the blood to clot, a clotting disorder, and serious bleeding from an unreported injury. A patient with leukemia, a cancer of the blood-forming cells, may present with bruising, infections, and fatigue before any blood test is taken. Bruising without cause, bleeding gums or nose, blood in the stool, and tiny red spots move a disorder of the blood or clotting upward, while a diet poor in iron, heavy menstrual loss, and chronic loss from the gut move an iron shortage upward.

Used well, this appendix is a discipline rather than a catalogue, and its value depends on the order in which the clinician speaks the possibilities. The habit is to commit to a leading diagnosis and a can't-miss diagnosis before any test is ordered, then to ask what finding would change the ranking, because a test that cannot change it was not worth sending. A framework organizes memory and can also close the mind, so the entries are a prompt and never a fence. Where no test exists, the same sequence still works, and the decision that replaces the diagnosis is a plan: watch and review, begin treatment on the clinical picture, or travel now. The population in which a rule was derived matters, and a pattern learned in one setting can mislead in another, so local experience and local protocol are part of the reasoning. The record should show which entries were considered, which were set aside, and on what finding, because the next clinician will read the reasoning and not the conclusion alone.

To summarize, this appendix offers the differential diagnosis of the common presentations as a sequence of possibilities rather than a list, each opened by the can't-miss entry and each attached to the finding that moves it up or down. The order is likelihood first and danger always, the bedside questions are separated from the tests and the referrals, and a deteriorating patient is treated and moved before the diagnosis is complete. Every figure here, whether a temperature, a rate, a pressure, a proportion, or a concentration, is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such numbers differ between countries, between editions, and between the laboratory and the ward. No table organizes memory into a diagnosis, and no sequence of possibilities examines the patient, so the clinician who holds the list lightly and looks at the person in front of them will use it best. This appendix is educational material and does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service. The entries are meant to be returned to at the bedside and rehearsed until they come without effort, and the patient, not the framework, is always the final authority.
