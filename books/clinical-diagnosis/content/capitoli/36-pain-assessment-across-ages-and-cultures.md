# Chapter 36 — Pain Assessment Across Ages and Cultures

Pain is the symptom that brings more people to a clinician than any other, and it is the one the clinician can never see directly. It is a private experience generated in a nervous system, reported in a language, and shaped by a lifetime of previous pain, so that two patients with the same injury may describe different sensations and behave in different ways. Pain must nevertheless be measured, and measured with the same care as a pulse or a temperature, because measurement is what turns a complaint into a finding that can be compared, recorded, and acted on. The reasoning in this chapter is organized around four axes that correct one another: the patient's own account, the behavior an observer can see, the effect of the pain on what the patient can do, and the response to whatever has already been tried. This chapter is educational material; it is not medical advice, it is not a protocol, and it is not a substitute for training, supervision, or the judgment of the clinician who is with the patient.

The dangerous possibilities are named before the ordinary assessment begins, because each announces itself through a finding that can be sought in the first minutes. The pain that is being treated rather than explained is the hazard running through this chapter, and the analgesia that conceals a surgical abdomen is its clearest form. A patient whose abdomen becomes rigid and board-like, who guards involuntarily and flinches when the examiner's hand withdraws, or whose pain is far greater than the tenderness suggests, may have a perforated organ or a strangulated bowel, and nothing given for relief should be allowed to postpone the decision about surgery. Pain that is sudden and tearing and travels to the back may signal a tear in the aorta, while severe lower abdominal pain in a woman of childbearing age with bleeding and a positive pregnancy test must be treated as a pregnancy outside the uterus until it is proved otherwise.

Pain resists measurement because there is no independent reference standard for it. A temperature can be repeated with a thermometer and a pulse can be counted against a watch, but nothing outside the patient can confirm that the pain is what the patient says it is. The patient's report is therefore not one piece of evidence among several; it is the measurement itself, and a clinician who treats it as an impression to be corrected has already begun to reason badly. That report is also not a raw signal, because it is filtered through expectation, fear, previous experience, cultural meaning, the words available in the patient's language, and what the patient believes will happen if the pain is admitted. A patient who has been disbelieved before may understate the pain, and a patient who hopes for treatment may overstate it, and both are telling the truth about their situation.

The first axis is the account, and it is taken in the patient's own words rather than in the clinician's categories. For an acute pain the Socrates framework keeps the account complete: the site and whether it moves, the onset and what the patient was doing, the character of the sensation, whether it radiates and where, the associated symptoms, the timing, and the factors that make it better or worse. The Oldcarts framework extends the same discipline to the whole history and is useful when the complaint is a pattern of illness rather than a single pain. Both frameworks are nets with holes, and the clinician should know what each one leaves out, because the Socrates questions were designed for pain in the chest of an articulate adult and can miss the pain of a small child, of a person with dementia, or of a patient who describes the sensation through a metaphor the clinician does not recognize.

The character of the pain is a clue to its mechanism, and the mechanism tells the clinician what to examine and what to fear. Visceral pain is dull, deep, poorly localized, and often accompanied by nausea and sweating, because the organs are supplied by nerves that do not report position precisely. Somatic pain is sharp and well localized, and it worsens when the affected part is moved, which is why a patient with inflammation of the peritoneum lies still while a patient with colic of the bowel or the kidney cannot. Neuropathic pain is burning, shooting, electric, or numb, and it follows the territory of a nerve rather than the shape of an organ, so the examination must test sensation, power, and reflexes along that territory.

Referred pain is felt far from its source because the nerves of the organ and the nerves of the distant site enter the spinal cord at the same level. Irritation of the diaphragm by blood, pus, or a pregnancy outside the uterus can produce pain at the tip of the shoulder, so a patient with shoulder pain and a soft abdomen may still have a catastrophe inside. Pain from the heart is often felt in the jaw, the throat, or the inner arm rather than in the chest, and pain from the pancreas or the aorta is often felt in the back. Disease of the hip is frequently reported as pain in the knee, and disease of the spine as pain around the trunk, so the clinician examines the joint above and below the place the patient points to. The place the patient points to is a starting point, not the answer.

The second axis is the effect of the pain on what the patient can actually do, and it is the axis that travels best between languages and cultures. A patient who cannot bear weight, cannot sleep, cannot eat, cannot dress, or cannot hold a child has conveyed something that no number can substitute for. The clinician therefore asks what the pain has stopped, what it has changed, and how the day is now arranged around it, and those answers can be compared between one visit and the next. A pain that confines a previously active person to a chair carries a different weight from the same pain in someone whose life is already limited, and the change from the patient's own baseline is often more informative than the absolute severity. This is also the measurement that a family member or a nurse can report when the patient cannot.

The third axis is the response to whatever has already been tried, which is a natural experiment the patient has usually performed. The clinician asks what was taken, whether it was prescribed or bought, how much was taken, whether it helped at all, how long the relief lasted, and when the pain returned. None of that is a dose to be repeated or adjusted, because this book gives no doses, and the account is evidence about the character of the pain and about the direction the diagnosis should take. The finding that would make stronger treatment the right answer is severe pain with a diagnosis already established and a plan in motion; the finding that would make it the wrong answer is a patient who is deteriorating, in whom relief could hide the change that should call the surgeon.

Formal scales are the fourth kind of measurement, and they are useful only when their limits are understood. The verbal descriptor scale asks the patient to choose a word such as none, mild, moderate, or severe, while the numeric rating scale asks for a number between 0 and 10, and the arithmetic of that range is an illustration of the reasoning that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the wording and the anchors differ between services and editions. A number is reliable within one patient over time and unreliable between patients, because the same number may mean a mild inconvenience to one person and an unbearable state to another. The faces scales ask a child to point to the face that matches the feeling, which turns the question into a comparison rather than an act of arithmetic, and they are used only in children old enough to understand the sequence, an age that varies with the child and must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol.

When the patient cannot report, the clinician measures behavior instead, and in patients who are confused or who have dementia that is a structured task rather than a general impression. A behavioral scale records what can be seen: the face, the posture and restlessness of the body, the tensing of muscles, the sounds the patient makes, the response to comfort, and the pattern of breathing. Such observations are weaker than a report and should never replace one when a report is possible, but they are far better than the assumption that a patient who does not complain is free of pain. The vital signs belong in the same supporting role and no more, because a pulse of 110 beats per minute, a raised pressure, sweating, pallor, and wide pupils may accompany severe pain, yet each reading must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the heart rate that alarms one service is ordinary in another and the response is blunted by age, by training, by diabetes, and by medicines that slow the heart.

Pain is also missed because it is never reported, and the reasons are usually practical rather than mysterious. A patient may believe that suffering should be borne, may fear that admission will lead to an operation, may be unable to pay, may have been dismissed at an earlier visit, or may not have the words in the language of the consultation. The clinician carries assumptions about who suffers more and who complains more, and those assumptions are a common source of unequal treatment. Culture shapes how pain is expressed, what it means, who is permitted to speak about it, and what is expected of a sick person, but it does not change the capacity to feel, and treating a group as though its members feel less is a mistake that harms patients. The correct posture is to ask, to accept the first account, and to let the patient's own description set the standard.

Language is where the assessment most often fails, and the failure is quiet. When the clinician and the patient do not share a fluent language, the interview should be conducted with a trained interpreter rather than with a child or a relative, because a child may be frightened or ashamed and a relative may soften the message to protect the patient or the family. Many languages have no single word for the sensations the clinician is asking about, and a pain described as heavy, hot, pulling, or like a tight band may be pointing to exactly the mechanism the clinician needs. The clinician should use the patient's own word back in the next question and should ask about the worst pain the patient has ever experienced, because that comparison is more informative than any absolute number. When no trained interpreter is available, the clinician slows down, uses simple sentences, avoids metaphor, and accepts that some of the account will be lost rather than pretending it was heard.

Older adults require a different calibration, and the first correction is the belief that pain is a normal part of growing old. The older patient may understate the pain, may attribute it to arthritis or to age, and may present instead with a change in function, a fall, or a new confusion. The dangerous processes also present differently, because a heart attack may produce only breathlessness, sweating, or a feeling of indigestion, and an inflamed or perforated abdomen may be surprisingly soft rather than rigid. The finding that should prompt a full search is therefore not the severity the patient reports but the change from what that patient could do last week.

In infants and young children the assessment is almost entirely behavioral, and the parent is the most useful instrument. The clinician asks how the child feeds, sleeps, plays, and is consoled, and compares today with what the parent describes as normal, because a change in feeding or in temperament may be the first sign of a serious cause. An infant in pain may draw up the legs, screw up the face, and cry in a high pitch, but a child who is quiet, floppy, and unwilling to move may be more ill than one who screams. The dangerous findings at this age are a limb that is not used, a joint that is swollen or hot, an abdomen that is tender and distended with bilious vomiting, a fever with irritability that cannot be consoled, and a headache with a change in consciousness. The tools designed for children, including the faces scales, depend on the age and the development of the child, and the age at which each may be used must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol rather than assumed.

Adolescents present a special problem because their pain is often dismissed as emotional before it has been examined, and because they may not disclose what they fear. Recurrent headache, abdominal pain, and limb pain are common at this age and are usually benign, but the same complaints can be the face of a tumor, an infection, or an inflammatory disease, and the clinician who attributes them to stress without examining the patient will miss that second group. Function is the most useful measure here, because an adolescent who has stopped attending school or has withdrawn from sport is telling the clinician something a number on a scale will not. A normal examination in a distressed adolescent is a reason to look again with a plan, not a reason to conclude that the pain is not real.

Pain in pregnancy is an emergency until it is explained, because the list of causes contains several that threaten two lives. A woman of childbearing age with lower abdominal pain, bleeding, or fainting may have a pregnancy implanted outside the uterus, and the finding that reveals it is a positive pregnancy test with an empty uterus on ultrasound, sometimes with free fluid in the abdomen and pain at the shoulder tip. The clinician must assess the fetus as well, because the well-being of the baby is part of the mother's assessment, and the decision to refer is made on the mother's signs and the fetal signs together. No decision about pain relief should delay that referral.

Acute pain and chronic pain are different problems, and the difference changes the question the clinician is asking. Acute pain is a warning of tissue injury and it has a purpose, so the search is directed at the injury and its cause, while chronic pain has lost that warning function and has become a condition shaped by the nervous system, by sleep, by mood, and by the patient's circumstances. A duration of 3 months is often used to separate the two, though that boundary is an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because different services draw it at different points. The clinician should beware the label of the frequent attender, since familiarity with a patient's chronic pain is exactly what makes a new and dangerous pain easy to overlook.

The safest habit in chronic pain is to treat a change in character as a new pain. A pain that has moved, changed quality, begun to wake the patient at night, or appeared in a new place deserves the same search as a pain that has never been assessed before. Certain companions narrow that search at once: fever suggests infection, weight loss and night sweats suggest a tumor or tuberculosis, a new weakness or numbness suggests compression of a nerve or the spinal cord, bleeding suggests an ulcer or a tumor, and fainting with pain suggests hemorrhage or a sudden fall in the output of the heart. Compression of the spinal cord is one of the few pain emergencies in which hours matter, and the findings that reveal it are progressive weakness, a band of numbness around the trunk, difficulty passing urine, and a loss of sensation in the saddle area. Any of those findings in a patient with back pain turns a clinic visit into an immediate referral.

Some of the most lethal causes produce pain out of proportion to what the examination shows, and the clinician must learn to distrust the calm abdomen as much as the rigid one. When the blood supply to the bowel is interrupted, the pain is severe and continuous and often far greater than the tenderness, and the patient may be older or may have a rhythm disturbance of the heart that has thrown a clot; the finding that reveals it is an abdomen that becomes tender only late, alongside a rising lactate and a patient who is more unwell than the examination suggests. A limb that is tense, swollen, and painful on passive stretching of the muscles may be losing its blood supply or developing a compartment syndrome, and a rapidly spreading, exquisitely tender area of skin with a dusky center may be a necrotizing infection of the soft tissue. Each of these is a reason to call for help and to move the patient now rather than to wait for the next review.

Acute pain in the scrotum must be resolved in minutes rather than days, because a testis that twists on its cord can be saved if it is untwisted in time. A young man or adolescent with sudden severe pain in the testis, a high-riding and tender testis, and no urinary symptoms should be treated as having a twisted testis until a surgeon says otherwise, because an infection of the genital tract is a much less likely explanation at this age and is the error that costs the organ. The examination is performed gently and with a chaperone, and the finding that supports torsion is a testis that is tender, high, and horizontal, sometimes with loss of the normal reflex on the same side. The interval within which the testis can be saved is often quoted as 6 hours, but that interval is an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because it depends on how completely the blood supply is interrupted. A patient who is embarrassed, or a teenager who will not mention the pain, must be asked directly, because the question is the only way the diagnosis will be made.

The decision about treating pain sits inside the diagnostic process and can either support it or conceal it. Relief must not be withheld from a suffering patient merely to preserve the purity of an examination, because pain itself causes harm, and in most cases the examination and the conversation remain interpretable after relief has been given. The finding that would make treatment the right answer is a patient whose diagnosis is established or whose plan is already moving and whose suffering is significant; the finding that would make it the wrong answer is an unstable abdomen, or a patient with signs of bleeding or shock in whom relief could remove the last visible evidence of deterioration. This book gives no doses and no regimens, and the choice of medicine and its amount belongs to the clinician who knows the patient and to the local protocol.

The record of pain is part of the diagnosis, and it should be written so that the next clinician can see what changed. The note should carry the patient's own words, the scale used and the number chosen, the functional effect at the time of the visit, what the patient had already taken, and what the clinician found on examination. Soap notes give a familiar shape to this record, but the shape matters less than the content, and a note that contains a number and nothing else cannot be interpreted by anyone who was not present. Reassessment is the act most often omitted and the one that converts a single measurement into a trajectory, and the interval between the first and the second assessment should be chosen for the condition and confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because a patient with acute pain needs to be seen again far sooner than one whose pain is stable and chronic.

In a well-resourced hospital the assessment is extended by instruments, but the reasoning that directs them is unchanged. The electrocardiogram, or ECG, is the first test in pain of the chest, the upper abdomen, the jaw, or the arm, because a heart attack can imitate many other complaints, while blood tests can reveal anemia, infection, inflammation, or damage to the heart muscle and ultrasound at the bedside can show free fluid, an enlarged organ, or a pregnancy outside the uterus. Computed tomography, or CT, and magnetic resonance imaging, or MRI, answer questions that the hands cannot reach, and their role is to confirm or exclude a hypothesis the clinician has already formed rather than to generate one at random. None of these instruments removes the need for repeated examination, because a scan taken early in an illness can be normal while the process that will declare itself is still developing.

Where there is no laboratory, no imaging, and no colleague, the same assessment is conducted with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, and it is not a lesser form of reasoning. The torch makes the skin, the mouth, the throat, the ears, and the eyes visible, the thermometer turns a suspicion of infection into a measurement, and a watch allows the pulse and the breathing to be counted rather than guessed. The examination is repeated at intervals, and the position of comfort, the ability to walk, the tenderness on gentle pressing, and the change over hours become the substitute for the scanner. The decision that follows is usually one of three: to watch with a clear plan and a time to return, to treat on the clinical picture while continuing the search, or to begin the journey to a hospital now.

The decision to refer is itself a diagnostic act, and it should be based on findings rather than on the clinician's discomfort alone. Pain with a rigid abdomen, pain with signs of shock, pain with bleeding, pain in pregnancy, pain in the scrotum, pain after trauma, pain with a new weakness or numbness, and pain in a patient whose immune defenses are suppressed or whose blood is thinned by medicine all belong in a place where surgery and imaging exist. The patient and the family should be told exactly which signs must bring them back and when, in concrete terms rather than a general instruction to return if things get worse. The record should show what was excluded, what remains uncertain, and what the plan is if the pain does not follow the expected course.

Two constructed illustrations may make the method concrete, and neither describes a real person. Imagine an older woman brought to a clinic because she has stopped walking; she says the hip merely aches, and the clinician, following the axis of function rather than the axis of severity, examines her and finds that the leg is shortened and rotated, with a fracture the mild report had hidden. Now imagine a teenager who arrives with abdominal pain and is about to be sent home with a diagnosis of a stomach upset, until the clinician asks directly about the scrotum and finds a tender, high-riding testis that needs a surgeon at once. Both stories are teaching devices built to show a single habit, and neither should be applied to a real patient without a full assessment. The shared habit is the refusal to let a comfortable explanation close the search before the dangerous one has been considered.

The patient who cannot speak at all requires the most disciplined assessment, because none of the four axes can be taken for granted. A patient who is unconscious, sedated, paralyzed, or unable to produce language after a stroke may still be in severe pain, and the clinician must then rely on behavior, on the observations of those who know the patient, and on the nature of the illness itself. In such patients the threshold both for treating pain and for investigating its cause is lowered rather than raised, because the usual warning of a complaint has been removed. At the end of life the assessment widens to include what has been called total pain, in which physical discomfort is bound together with fear, grief, loneliness, and the meaning the patient gives to the illness, so that treating only the body may leave the suffering untouched.

To summarize, pain is a subjective experience that must still be measured with the discipline given to a pulse, through the patient's account, the behavior that can be observed, the effect on what the patient can do, and the response to what has already been tried. The dangerous causes are named at the outset and not deferred: the analgesia that conceals a surgical abdomen, the tearing pain of a damaged aorta, the pain of a pregnancy outside the uterus, the pain out of proportion to the examination, and the twisted testis that must reach a surgeon. The tools for those who cannot speak, the faces scales for children and the behavioral scales for the confused and those with dementia, are aids to judgment and never replacements for a report when a report is possible. Culture shapes how pain is expressed and what it means, but never how much a person can feel, and the clinician's own assumptions about who suffers more are among the most common sources of error. Every score, cut-off, pressure, rate, interval, and duration in this chapter is an illustration of the reasoning and must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such numbers differ between countries, editions, and laboratories. This is educational material; it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service. A patient in severe pain who is deteriorating is helped first, and the explanation is completed afterwards.
