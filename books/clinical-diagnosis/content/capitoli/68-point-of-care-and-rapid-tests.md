# Chapter 68 — Point-of-Care and Rapid Tests

A point-of-care test is a test performed where the patient is, by the clinician who is already caring for that patient, and answered quickly enough that it can change what happens in the next few minutes. The idea is old, since the urine dipstick and the blood glucose strip have been at the bedside for decades, but the range of tests that now fit in a hand has grown until a clinic without a laboratory can measure things that once required a hospital. The reasoning that organizes this chapter is that a rapid test is a question asked at the bedside rather than an answer delivered from a machine, and it must be judged by three things: what it actually measures, how often it is wrong, and what the clinician will do differently once the result is known. A result that cannot alter a decision is not a test but an expense, and a result that is believed beyond its limits is more dangerous than no result at all. The clinician who works with these strips needs to hold two ideas at once, that the test is an instrument of great power in the right hands and that it is a proxy for the disease rather than the disease itself. This chapter is educational material, and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of the service in which the patient is seen.

Certain lethal possibilities must be named before the ordinary reasoning begins, because each is revealed by a finding that can be sought in the first minutes and each has a rapid test that can mislead. A patient who is confused, sweating, trembling, or unable to be roused may be hypoglycemic, and the finding that reveals it is a glucose measurement taken at once together with the response when the glucose is corrected, since the meter can read deceptively well in a patient whose brain is starving. A patient with fever who has been in a region where malaria is transmitted may have malaria, and the finding that reveals it is the parasite on a stained blood film or its product on a rapid strip, while a single negative strip proves far less than it appears to. A young woman with lower abdominal pain, a missed period, and vaginal bleeding may have a pregnancy that has implanted outside the uterus, and the finding that betrays it is a positive pregnancy test together with tenderness and signs of bleeding inside the abdomen, because this can kill within hours. A patient with chest discomfort may be having an acute myocardial infarction, and the finding that reveals it is a rise in the markers of heart muscle injury over time together with the trace on the electrocardiogram, or ECG, so that one early normal result excludes nothing. These are the presentations in which a strip read too confidently can cost the patient everything.

A rapid test works by fixing a reagent to a strip, a card, or a small cartridge and letting it react with something in a drop of blood, urine, or fluid. What it detects is almost never the disease itself but a proxy: an antigen shed by an organism, an antibody the patient has made against one, an enzyme that reports the presence of a sugar, or a protein released when cells are injured. That proxy travels imperfectly with the disease, appearing only after an interval, persisting after recovery, or appearing for reasons that have nothing to do with the illness in question. The conditions of storage matter as much as the chemistry, because heat, sunlight, moisture, and an expired date can destroy the reagent quietly, and a strip kept in a hot vehicle may fail without any warning on its face. The operator matters too, since too little sample, too much sample, a delayed reading, or a result read after the permitted interval all change the answer. The clinician should therefore ask of every device the same questions that would be asked of a laboratory: how was it kept, who performed it, and what does the maker say about its limits.

The value of a rapid test depends less on the strip than on the patient in front of the clinician, and this is the reasoning that separates a useful result from a misleading one. Every test has a probability of being positive when the disease is present and a probability of being positive when it is absent, and the meaning of a given result shifts with how likely the disease was before the test was performed. A positive result in a patient whose story already makes the disease very likely confirms a diagnosis, while the same positive result in a patient whose story makes it very unlikely is more often a false positive and should be checked rather than obeyed. A negative result in a patient whose story makes the disease very unlikely is reassuring, while the same negative result in a patient whose story makes it very likely should be treated as inconclusive and repeated or confirmed. This is why a clinician should state the probability before reaching for the strip, since that statement is what allows the result to be interpreted instead of merely recorded. The words sensitivity and specificity describe the test, but the question at the bedside is always what this result means for this patient, and the answer depends on both.

The glucose meter is the most widely used rapid test in the world and the one whose result is most often taken at face value. It measures the glucose in a drop of capillary blood by an enzymatic reaction that produces a small current or a color change, and it reports a value that is close to, but not identical with, the glucose measured in the laboratory on plasma. A reading of about 70 milligrams per deciliter is often taught as the line below which hypoglycemia should be assumed, but that figure is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the accepted boundary shifts with the patient, the sample, and the edition of the reference being used. The meter can mislead in both directions, since poor circulation, cold hands, dehydration, swelling, anemia, and a sugar that is changing quickly all distort the reading, and a strip that has been mishandled can report a value that has nothing to do with the patient. The lethal mistake is the patient who is hypoglycemic and whose meter reads a comfortable number, so that the clinician looks elsewhere while the brain is starved. The finding that should override the meter is the clinical picture of sweating, tremor, confusion, abnormal behavior, or a seizure in a patient at risk, and in that situation the sensible act is to treat and to recheck rather than to trust a single strip.

The same meter serves the opposite purpose, since a very high reading in a patient who is drowsy, breathing deeply, passing large volumes of urine, and vomiting may mean a metabolic emergency rather than simple poor control. A reading above about 250 milligrams per deciliter is a common reason to look further, and that cut-off, like every other number in this chapter, is a teaching illustration to be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the value at which a clinician should act depends on the patient, the meter, and the protocol in force. A strip that detects ketones in the urine or the blood adds a second piece of information, because it separates a patient who is merely high from one who is producing acid, and the two demand different decisions. The clinic without a laboratory can follow such a patient with repeated glucose readings, a record of urine output, and repeated examination of breathing, consciousness, and hydration, which together say more than any single number. The clinician should also remember that a reading taken from a fingertip in a cold, shut-down patient may underestimate a truly dangerous level, so the clinical picture keeps its authority. What the test changes is the timing of the decision, not the need for judgment.

The malaria rapid test detects an antigen released by the parasite, and it has changed the care of fever in places where microscopy is not available. Its great strength is speed, and its great danger is that a single negative result is treated as exclusion. In the early hours of an infection the number of parasites may be below the amount the strip can detect, so the first test is often negative in a patient who truly has the disease, and a strip that remains positive for weeks after successful treatment can mislead in the other direction. Some parasite populations do not produce the antigen the strip looks for, which produces a negative result in a genuine infection, and this is one reason the choice of test is tied to the region where it is used. The finding that must outweigh a negative strip is a patient who has been in a region where malaria is transmitted, who has a fever and looks unwell, and in whom no other cause has been found; such a patient needs the test repeated, a blood film where one can be made, and treatment on the clinical case if the diagnosis is likely and the patient is deteriorating. Any interval quoted for repeating the test is an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the timing that matters depends on the assay, the species, and the setting.

The test for the human immunodeficiency virus, known as HIV, detects antibodies that the body has made against the virus, and in many modern formats it also detects a part of the virus itself. Its difficulty is the interval between infection and the appearance of enough antibody to be detected, during which a person can be infected and infectious and still test negative. A negative result in that window therefore cannot be read as proof that the person is free of the virus, and a repeat after an interval is part of the reasoning rather than an admission of failure; confirm that interval against the current guidance where you work, because the length of the window depends on the generation of the assay and the protocol in use. A positive result also needs care, because cross-reacting antibodies, recent vaccination, pregnancy, and certain other conditions can produce a false positive on a screening strip, which is why a positive is confirmed by a second, different test before it is treated as final. The clinic that relies on the strip must protect confidentiality, offer the conversation that follows the result, and be honest with the patient about what the test can and cannot say. The lethal error is the missed diagnosis, since a person who is told wrongly that the result is negative may go untreated, and the second lethal error is the wrong positive, which carries a burden of its own.

The pregnancy test measures human chorionic gonadotropin, a hormone produced by the developing placenta, in urine or in blood. A positive result is strong evidence of pregnancy, and its most important use is not obstetric but urgent, because a young woman with abdominal pain, a missed period, and vaginal bleeding may have a pregnancy implanted outside the uterus, and that diagnosis can kill her within hours if it is missed. The finding that reveals it is a positive test together with tenderness in the lower abdomen, a soft and swollen belly, shoulder tip pain, dizziness, or signs of shock, and the finding that would make the clinician relax wrongly is a negative urine test in a patient whose story fits. A negative result can occur when the pregnancy is very early, when the urine is dilute, or when the level of the hormone is so high that the test behaves strangely, which happens in some abnormal pregnancies. A positive result without pregnancy can follow fertility treatment, some tumors, blood in the urine, or a pregnancy recently lost, so the test is read with the history rather than instead of it. Where a laboratory exists the blood level is measured and followed, and where it does not, the strip plus the examination decides whether the patient must travel now.

The urine dipstick is a row of small reagent pads on a plastic strip, and each pad asks a different question: protein, glucose, blood, ketones, bilirubin, the products of infection, and the acidity of the urine. Its virtue is that it costs almost nothing and takes very little time, and its vice is that it answers so many questions at once that a clinician may order more tests in response to the strip than the patient needed in the first place. A trace of blood is common after exercise, during menstruation, after sexual activity, and in a urinary infection, and following it with imaging and repeated samples may produce a cascade of findings that harm the patient without helping the diagnosis. A pad for nitrite and a pad for white cells are often used together to support a urinary infection, but neither a negative nor a positive result settles the question, because the test can be negative in a genuine infection and positive from contamination. Vitamin C in the urine can suppress several of the pads and make a real abnormality invisible, while a concentrated, heavily colored urine can produce readings that mean nothing. The finding that makes the whole strip uninterpretable is a contaminated or stale sample, and the remedy is a clean, fresh specimen rather than more testing.

Protein on the strip is the pad most often over-read, and its reasoning deserves a paragraph of its own. A positive result may reflect a fever, heavy exercise, a concentrated sample, or a urinary infection, and it may also reflect the harmless leakage that occurs in some people when they stand. The finding that makes protein meaningful is its persistence on repeated, properly collected specimens, together with a pressure that is raised, swelling of the legs, or a change in the urine volume, and where a laboratory exists the amount is measured over a day or compared with a substance in the blood. A dipstick that is positive in a patient with no other evidence of kidney disease should prompt a repeat rather than a diagnosis, because a single reading cannot separate the transient from the persistent. Ketones and bilirubin each add a specific piece of information, one about the metabolism of fat and the other about the liver, and each is interpretable only alongside the clinical picture. Every reference range attached to these pads is set by the maker and the laboratory, so confirm it against the current guideline and the local protocol before it changes a decision.

The cardiac troponin test measures a protein released from heart muscle when it is injured, and in a rapid format it can be performed beside the patient. It is the test that most invites a fatal oversimplification, because a single normal result early in the illness is taken as proof that the heart is not infarcting. The protein appears in the blood only after an interval from the onset of injury, and it rises over hours, so a test performed too early can be normal in a patient who is having an acute myocardial infarction at that very moment. The rise must be demonstrated by repeating the test over time, and that interval is an illustration of the reasoning that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the timing depends on the assay in use and the way the protocol defines a significant change. A raised value does not prove an infarction either, because the same protein leaks in kidney failure, in a clot in the lung, in severe infection, in a fast rhythm, and in a failing heart. The finding that keeps the clinician safe is a story of new or changing chest discomfort together with the ECG and the trend of the marker, and where no ECG and no laboratory exist, the decisive question is not whether the patient is having an infarction but whether the patient can reach a place that can answer the question now. A normal early test in a patient whose story is alarming changes nothing about the need to travel and to be observed.

A blood gas and electrolyte analyzer in a small format reports the acidity of the blood, the pressures of oxygen and carbon dioxide, the bicarbonate, the salts, the lactate, and sometimes the hemoglobin, all from a small sample. It is a powerful instrument in a resuscitation room and a difficult one in a clinic, because the sample itself is easily spoiled, since air in the syringe, a delay before the reading, too little anticoagulant, or a sample drawn from a vein instead of an artery each produce a result that describes the error rather than the patient. The result is also a snapshot of one moment, and a patient who is deteriorating may need the measurement repeated rather than argued with. A lactate of about 2 millimoles per liter is often used to support the recognition of shock, but that figure is an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the boundary depends on the method, the site of sampling, and the population in which it was studied. Lactate rises in many conditions besides shock, including intense muscular activity, a seizure, some medicines, and liver disease, so the number supports a clinical impression rather than replacing it. Where a laboratory exists, the blood gas is confirmed and repeated there; where it does not, the clinician who can measure lactate has gained a signal about perfusion that the bedside examination alone might miss.

Rapid tests for infection now cover streptococcal throat infection, influenza, dengue, typhoid, tuberculosis, syphilis, hepatitis, and several markers of inflammation, and each carries the same lesson about what a proxy can and cannot say. A rapid streptococcal test is highly specific when positive but can be negative in genuine infection, so a negative result in a child with a classic picture does not by itself close the question. A test for inflammation such as C-reactive protein or procalcitonin reports the response of the body rather than the presence of an organism, and it may be raised in injury, in inflammation, and in many conditions that are not infectious, so it cannot separate bacterial from viral disease on its own. A urine test for a product of the tuberculosis organism helps in patients whose immune defenses are suppressed, but a negative result in that group excludes very little, and the diagnosis still rests on samples sent for culture or on molecular testing where it exists. A rapid test for dengue or typhoid may be negative early and positive later, and the timing of the sample matters as much as the choice of the test. The clinician who treats a number rather than a patient will miss the disease that the number was never able to see.

Tests of clotting in a rapid format allow a clinician to measure how long blood takes to clot and, in some devices, to estimate the international normalized ratio used to follow patients on blood-thinning treatment. These are the tests that matter most in trauma, in childbirth, and in any patient who is bleeding, because a failure of clotting turns a controllable hemorrhage into a fatal one. A bedside clotting time of about 20 minutes is sometimes quoted as a sign of trouble, and that figure, like all the others here, is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the acceptable range depends on the method and the clinical situation. The measurement is operator-dependent, since the tube must be kept warm, observed continuously, and read at the right moment, and a result produced carelessly is worse than none. The test also says nothing about the strength of the clot or about the platelets, so a normal time does not exclude a bleeding disorder and an abnormal one does not name its cause. The decision that follows is whether the patient needs blood products, a medicine, or transfer, and that decision belongs to the clinician who can see the patient, the bleeding, and the whole picture rather than one number.

In a hospital where a laboratory exists, the rapid test has a different job, which is to shorten the interval between the question and a usable answer while the laboratory performs the definitive test. The bedside glucose is used to guide immediate care and the laboratory measurement confirms it, the rapid strip for an infection screens a patient while the culture grows, and the blood gas in the resuscitation room is followed by formal analysis. In this setting the rapid test must be subject to the same quality control as any other, with the device checked, the reagents stored properly, the results recorded, and the operator trained and reassessed. The clinician should also name the limits out loud in the notes, because a rapid result may be repeated in the laboratory and the two may disagree. A disagreement between a strip and a laboratory is never resolved by preferring the more convenient answer, and it should prompt a repeat and a review of the sample. What the hospital adds is confirmation and context, and what it must not add is the habit of treating the quick result as final simply because it arrived first.

In a clinic with no laboratory, the same tests are not an inferior copy of hospital practice but the laboratory itself, and the clinic must treat them with the seriousness that role deserves. The strips must be stored within the temperature the maker states, protected from light and damp, and checked against the expiry date before use, because a failed reagent produces a confident wrong answer. The device must be cleaned and checked as instructed, and the readings recorded in a register with the date, the time, the operator, and the action taken, so that the pattern over many patients becomes visible. Whenever a rapid test is stocked, someone must be responsible for the supply, the training, and the waste, since a test that cannot be repeated or replaced is a trap rather than a tool. The clinician must also know what the clinic cannot do and say so to the patient, rather than allowing the presence of a few strips to suggest a completeness that does not exist. The tests extend the reach of the examination, and they do not replace the history, the hands, the thermometer, or the torch, all of which remain the instruments that never expire.

A constructed illustration may make the discipline concrete, and it is a teaching device rather than a portrait of any real person. Imagine a patient brought in drowsy and sweating after a missed meal, whose glucose strip reads a number that seems acceptable, and whose clinician decides to treat for hypoglycemia anyway because the picture fits and the meter can be wrong; the patient wakes, and the diagnosis is confirmed by the response. Now imagine a traveler with a fever and a negative malaria strip, whose clinician repeats the test and looks for another cause while treating on the clinical case, because a single negative result was never able to exclude the disease. Neither patient is real, and neither story may be applied to a real person without a full assessment. What both share is the same move, which is to ask what finding would make the rapid result wrong and to act on the answer rather than on the strip alone. The clinician who names the dangerous diagnosis before reaching for the test, who states the probability first, and who records what the result changed will make fewer of the mistakes this chapter has described, because the reasoning was done in the right order.

To summarize, the tests that fit in a hand are questions asked at the bedside, and they are judged by what they measure, how often they are wrong, and what they change. The glucose meter can read deceptively in a patient who is hypoglycemic, the malaria strip can be negative at the first visit in a patient who truly has the disease, the pregnancy test can be negative in a patient with a pregnancy outside the uterus, the troponin can be normal early in an infarction, and the urine dipstick can generate a cascade of further testing from a single meaningless trace. Every strip is read with a probability formed before it was performed, a positive result in a low-probability patient is confirmed rather than believed, and a negative result in a high-probability patient is repeated or set aside on clinical grounds. In the clinic the tests are the laboratory, and they demand storage, training, records, and the honesty to state what remains unknown; in the hospital they shorten the interval to a definitive answer and never replace it. Every figure in this chapter, whether a glucose value, a lactate level, a clotting time, a reference range, or a cut-off, is an illustration of reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such numbers differ between countries, between editions, and between laboratories. This chapter is educational material and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service. A patient who looks unwell is helped first, and the test is used to sharpen the reasoning rather than to postpone it.
