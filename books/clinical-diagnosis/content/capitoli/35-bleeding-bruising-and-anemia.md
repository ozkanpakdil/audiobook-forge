# Chapter 35 — Bleeding, Bruising and Anemia

Bleeding that will not stop, bruises that appear without a cause, and the tiredness of a falling hemoglobin lead a clinician to the same small set of questions, because each describes a failure in the blood, in the vessel, or in the marrow. The clinician asks whether the problem lies in the platelets, in the clotting factors that turn blood from liquid to gel, or in the vessels themselves, and whether an anemia comes from blood that is lost, made too slowly, or destroyed too fast. This chapter is educational material, and it is not medical advice, it is not a protocol, and it is not a substitute for training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the rules of your own service. The reasoning that organizes the subject is mechanism, because a pattern read correctly points toward the right part of the system long before any laboratory speaks, while a single number alone points nowhere.

Five dangerous possibilities must be named before the ordinary reasoning begins, and each is revealed by a finding that can be sought within minutes. The slow bleed from the gastrointestinal tract may produce no visible blood and announce itself only as a falling hemoglobin in a tired patient whose stool has turned black and tarry. Marrow failure presents as bruising and tiny red spots with pallor, fever, and a mouth that will not heal, and is revealed by that combination rather than by any single mark. A hemolytic crisis destroys red cells suddenly, so the patient turns yellow in the eyes, passes dark urine, and feels pain in the back or the abdomen while the hemoglobin falls fast. Advanced liver disease has stopped making clotting proteins and has opened vessels under high pressure that can bleed heavily into the gut. Bleeding into a closed space, above all into the skull, may follow a trivial injury and is revealed by a headache, vomiting, drowsiness, or a change in behavior rather than by any external mark.

The pattern of bleeding separates these mechanisms more reliably than the volume of blood lost. A platelet problem bleeds from surfaces, so the patient reports bleeding gums, nosebleeds that are hard to stop, and small flat red spots that do not fade when pressed, with small scattered bruises. Bleeding begins at once after a cut, stops with pressure, and then restarts, because the first plug that seals the vessel is the part the platelets were meant to build. A count below 20 thousand per microliter is often quoted as the level at which spontaneous bleeding becomes likely, but that figure is an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since laboratories draw the line differently. A normal count does not exclude a platelet that works badly, as happens with some medicines, in kidney failure, and in certain inherited conditions.

A clotting factor problem bleeds into tissues rather than from surfaces, and its signature is the deep bleed. The patient describes a joint that swells and becomes hot and painful without injury, or a muscle that grows tight and hard after a knock that seemed minor, and bleeding after surgery or a dental extraction may begin hours later. Large bruises that spread under the skin, bleed into the gut wall, or track along a limb are more typical of a factor problem than of a platelet one. A family history of bleeding after circumcision, after a first tooth was pulled, or of a woman who has always had heavy periods points toward an inherited disorder of clotting. The clotting times are the tests that address this mechanism, and any cut-off used to interpret them must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol before it is trusted.

The third mechanism is the vessel itself, and here the tests are often normal, which is exactly why the clinician must think of it. A vessel made fragile by age, by a deficiency of vitamin C, by a connective tissue disease, or by a medicine that weakens the wall produces bruises that appear with little or no trauma, often on the forearms and the backs of the hands of an older person. Inflammatory disease of the vessels produces raised, tender, red or purple spots that may itch or burn and tend to appear in crops on the lower legs. The distinction matters because these conditions are treated by addressing the underlying process rather than replacing something the blood lacks, so the finding that would make a platelet or factor treatment wrong is a normal count, normal clotting times, and a rash that is raised to the touch.

Bruising deserves its own questions because a bruise is a mark in time as well as in space. The clinician asks where the bruise is, what the patient was doing when it appeared, whether the story matches the site, and how the color has changed since it was noticed. Bruises over the shins and knees of a mobile toddler and over the bony points of an older person are ordinary, while bruises on the ears, the neck, the trunk, the buttocks, the genitals, and the soft parts of the arms and thighs are not. Marks carrying the shape of a hand, a belt, or a cord, marks in a child who cannot yet move independently, and marks clustered at different stages of healing require the clinician to consider deliberate injury and to follow the local process for protecting children. The finding that raises this concern is never a single bruise but a pattern, an explanation that does not fit, or a delay in seeking care.

The medicines a patient takes are part of the examination, and in bleeding they are often the whole answer. The clinician asks about every drug by name and purpose, including those bought without a prescription and those taken as herbs or traditional preparations, because many common substances change the platelets, the clotting, or the vessel wall. Drugs that thin the blood deliberately, drugs that reduce inflammation, some antibiotics, some drugs used for seizures, and heavy alcohol all belong on this list. The decision that follows concerns whether the medicine explains the bleeding well enough to leave the diagnosis there, or whether the picture holds features the medicine cannot explain, such as bruising in a patient who takes nothing at all.

The family history is asked in a way that turns a complaint into a pattern. The clinician does not ask only whether relatives bleed, because many people do not know, but asks specifically about bleeding after operations, childbirth, dental work, and injuries that would not normally bleed. Heavy periods across several generations, a brother or an uncle who needed treatment after a small cut, and a family in which boys died young in a pattern never explained are clues of the first importance. A family from a region where an inherited blood disorder is common, and an anemia in childhood that was never investigated, belong in the same conversation, and the answers may need a second visit.

The examination of a patient who bleeds is performed from the outside inward, with the skin read as carefully as any laboratory report. The clinician looks at the whole skin, including the scalp, the back, the buttocks, and the inside of the mouth, and notes the size, shape, color, and distribution of every mark. The gums, the nose, the conjunctivae, and the fingertips are inspected for bleeding spots that may be the only sign of a low platelet count. The joints are felt for warmth and swelling, the muscles for tenderness and tightness, and the abdomen for an enlarged spleen or liver. The eyes are examined for a hemorrhage in the retina, and throughout the clinician asks whether the pattern is one of surfaces, of deep tissues, or of fragile vessels.

Bleeding into the head is the lethal possibility that must be considered whenever a patient who bruises easily develops a headache, vomits, becomes drowsy, or behaves differently. It is especially likely after a fall in an older person, in a patient who takes a medicine that thins the blood, and in a patient whose platelets are low or whose clotting is impaired by liver disease. The finding that reveals it may be nothing more than a headache that will not settle, a change in the level of consciousness, a new weakness, or a pupil larger on one side, and in an older patient the injury may have been forgotten. The clinician should not wait for these signs to become obvious, because the window in which bleeding around the brain can be treated is short, and the decision to seek imaging is made on suspicion rather than certainty. In a hospital the answer comes from computed tomography, or CT, of the head, and in a clinic without it the answer is an immediate referral.

Anemia is not a diagnosis and not a number, it is a state that must be explained, and the explanation is always one of three mechanisms. Red cells are lost, made too slowly, or destroyed before their time, and each mechanism has its own history, its own examination, and its own reasoning. A single hemoglobin value tells the clinician how far the blood has fallen but nothing about why, and the same value may mean a slow bleed that has gone on for months, a marrow that failed within weeks, or a destruction that began yesterday. This is why the history and the examination come before the count, and why the count is then read as confirmation or as surprise.

Blood loss is the most common mechanism, and its pattern tells the clinician whether it is acute or chronic. Acute loss pours out quickly and the patient may be pale, sweaty, breathless, and faint, with a rapid pulse and a falling pressure, while the hemoglobin may still read normal in the first hours because the blood has not yet been diluted. That lag is a trap, and the findings that save the patient are the pulse, the pressure, and the appearance rather than the first count. Chronic loss leaks slowly and the patient adapts, so the hemoglobin falls gradually and the patient reports tiredness, breathlessness on effort, a sore tongue, and a craving to chew ice or earth. The slow bleed from the gastrointestinal tract may come from a stomach ulcer, from inflammation of the gut, from a polyp, or from a tumor, and the finding that reveals it may be a black stool or simply an anemia that will not correct. Any hemoglobin figure used to define anemia, such as 7 grams per deciliter, is an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the reference range depends on age, sex, pregnancy, and the laboratory that issued it.

The history of blood loss is taken in a systematic way, because the patient rarely volunteers the whole story. The clinician asks about the color and frequency of the stool, about blood on the paper or in the bowl, about painkillers that irritate the gut, and about any change in bowel habit that has lasted for weeks. In a woman the menstrual history is taken in detail, including the number of days, the number of pads or cloths used, the presence of clots, and bleeding between periods, because heavy periods are a common and easily missed cause of iron deficiency. A duration such as 3 weeks is often used to separate a recent change from a longstanding one, and that interval must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol because the boundary is drawn differently in different settings. The best question is often the simplest, which is whether the patient has noticed anything that seemed like blood.

Underproduction is the mechanism the clinician thinks of when there is no bleeding to find and the patient does not look as though red cells are being destroyed. Iron, folate, and cobalamin are the raw materials of red cells, and a lack of any of them produces cells that are smaller or larger than normal and a marrow that cannot keep up. The tongue is the first instrument here, because it may be smooth, sore, shiny, or stripped of its papillae, and these changes may precede the fall in the count. The nails carry the same message, since a severe deficiency may hollow them into a spoon shape, crack them, or leave them ridged and brittle. Chronic disease, infection, inflammation, kidney failure, and cancer suppress the marrow in a different way, and their anemia is usually mild and does not respond to iron. A marrow that fails completely produces the picture named earlier, with bruising, tiny red spots, fever, and infection alongside the anemia.

Destruction of red cells produces the most dramatic and the most easily missed picture. The patient may become yellow in the eyes, pass urine that is dark like tea, feel pain in the back, the abdomen, or the limbs, and become breathless and tired within a day or two. The spleen may enlarge and become tender, and the patient may describe earlier episodes called jaundice in childhood or after an infection, a food, or a medicine. A crisis can be triggered by an infection, by a medicine, by a transfusion of incompatible blood, by a defect inherited in the red cell itself, or by an immune attack, and the findings that reveal it are a falling hemoglobin with a rising count of young red cells and a rise in the substance that makes the skin yellow. The danger is that the fall can be very fast, so the clinician must consider it before the patient is profoundly unwell, and must not dismiss yellow eyes as a liver problem alone. The decision to give blood turns on how fast the hemoglobin is falling and how the patient looks rather than on one number, and the finding that would make that decision unnecessary is a stable patient with a mild fall and a clear, treatable trigger.

Marrow failure and cancer of the blood deserve their own paragraph because they present as bruising and are easily attributed to clumsiness. The patient, often a child or a young adult, reports bruises that appeared without a cause, tiny red spots on the legs, bleeding gums, a fever that will not settle, and a tiredness that has grown over a few weeks. The examination may show pallor, enlarged lymph nodes, an enlarged spleen or liver, tenderness over the breastbone, and a gum that is swollen or bleeding, and any of these in a patient with unexplained bruising changes the pace of the encounter. A complete blood count and a peripheral blood smear point toward the marrow, and where the diagnosis is suspected the patient must be referred urgently for the specialist test that samples the marrow itself. The finding that would make a benign explanation safe is a clear injury that matches the bruise, a normal count, and a patient who is otherwise entirely well, and even then the clinician arranges a review.

Advanced liver disease combines bleeding and anemia at the same time, and it is common enough to be named in every clinic. The liver makes the proteins that clot the blood, and when it fails those proteins are scarce, so a patient with cirrhosis bruises easily, bleeds from the gums and the nose, and may bleed heavily from enlarged veins in the esophagus that are opened by high pressure in the portal circulation. The same patient may be anemic from that bleeding, from an enlarged spleen that traps red cells, from poor nutrition, and from the direct effect of alcohol on the marrow. The examination looks for yellow eyes, ascites, an enlarged spleen, spider angiomas on the skin, redness of the palms, and a flapping tremor of the hands, and each of these findings changes the interpretation of a bruise. The finding that makes the bleeding a liver problem rather than a platelet one is the whole picture of chronic liver disease with a prolonged clotting time and a low platelet count. Any threshold used to grade the severity of liver disease must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because those tools were derived in particular populations.

Pregnancy is a state in which anemia is expected and in which it is therefore missed. The volume of plasma rises more than the mass of red cells, so the concentration of hemoglobin falls as a matter of course, and a value that would alarm a clinician in a man may be ordinary in a woman who is expecting. The growing fetus and the placenta consume iron and folate, and vomiting early in pregnancy may reduce what the mother can take in, so a true deficiency can hide inside a normal-looking dilution. The danger is a bleed that is not noticed, from the gut or from the genital tract, and the clinician must ask directly about bleeding in pregnancy rather than wait for the patient to mention it. The examination includes the conjunctivae, the tongue, the nails, and the size of the spleen, and any figure quoted for hemoglobin in pregnancy must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol used by the maternity service.

Anemia in childhood has its own causes and its own dangers, and the same reasoning is performed on a smaller patient. A child grows quickly and needs iron in proportion, so a diet of milk alone after the first year, a diet poor in meat and vegetables, and a period of rapid growth all produce a deficiency that shows as pallor, tiredness, poor appetite, and a child who is slower than usual. Intestinal worms take blood or nutrients from the gut, and malaria destroys red cells, so a child in a region where these are common must have them considered before the anemia is attributed to diet. An inherited disorder of the red cell, such as sickle cell disease, may present as episodes of pain, yellow eyes, an enlarged spleen, and a sudden severe anemia during an infection. Bruising in a child who is not yet walking, or in a pattern that does not match the story, must raise the question of deliberate injury alongside the question of a bleeding disorder, and exposure to lead and chronic infection must also be weighed.

Pallor is the sign every clinician learns first and trusts too much, so it deserves an account of its own limits. The color of a person's skin is the product of the blood, the pigment in the skin, and the light in the room, so pallor is easier to see in one patient than another and is more convincing when the clinician knows the patient's usual appearance. The conjunctivae of the lower lid are examined by drawing the lid down and looking at the inside surface in good light, and the palms, the creases of the hand, the nail beds, and the inside of the mouth are examined in the same way. The finding is most useful when it is marked, when it is new, and when it comes with a rapid pulse or breathlessness on effort, because a mild pallor is a poor test on its own. In a clinic with nothing but a torch and a thermometer, the conjunctivae, the tongue, the nails, the pulse, and the history of bleeding and diet are the whole assessment, and they are enough to decide whether the patient must travel for a count.

The tongue and the nails are examined because they show a deficiency before the count does, and because they are available in every clinic. A tongue that is smooth, shiny, sore, and without its normal rough surface suggests a lack of iron, of folate, or of cobalamin, and it may be accompanied by cracks at the corners of the mouth and by a burning sensation. The nails may become thin and brittle, develop ridges, split, or curve upward at the edges into a spoon shape, and a patient who reports that the nails break easily is describing the same deficiency in the language of daily life. Fingernails can also show small dark lines under them, which may be tiny bleeds from a problem with clots or the heart valves, and pale bands across the nail bed, which may follow an illness or a poisoning. These findings are suggestive rather than decisive, so the clinician uses them to build a hypothesis and then confirms it with the count and the smear where those are available.

The spleen is examined in every patient with anemia or bleeding because its size changes the list of causes. It sits under the left ribs and is normally not felt, so a spleen that can be touched below the rib margin is enlarged. The examiner stands on the patient's right side, places the left hand behind the lower left ribs, and asks the patient to breathe in while the right hand is brought up under the rib margin, feeling for the tip of the organ as it descends. An enlarged spleen is distinguished from an enlarged kidney or a mass by its notch, by its movement with breathing, and by the fact that the examiner cannot get above it, but the confident answer usually requires an ultrasound in a hospital. Enlargement suggests a destructive process, an infection, a disease of the blood, or a raised pressure in the portal circulation, and tenderness over it may signal an infection or an impending rupture, so that patient should not be sent home to wait.

The history for anemia is taken with the same discipline as the examination, and it is built from a short list of questions asked in every patient. The clinician asks about diet, including meat, green vegetables, and how often each is eaten, about the craving to chew ice, earth, or starch, which is a curious clue to iron deficiency, and about the amount of alcohol taken. The medicinal history is taken again here, because painkillers, drugs that reduce stomach acid, drugs used after a transplant, some antibiotics, and some drugs used for seizures all change the marrow or the gut. The clinician asks about chronic illness of the kidney, the joints, or the bowel, about previous surgery on the stomach or the bowel, about pregnancy and childbirth, and about any family history of anemia, yellow eyes, or an enlarged spleen. The travel history matters because parasites, malaria, and tuberculosis all change the picture, and the occupation matters because exposure to lead produces an anemia that will not correct with iron.

In a well-resourced hospital the reasoning is unchanged and the evidence is wider. The complete blood count gives the hemoglobin, the number and size of the red cells, the white cells, and the platelets, and the peripheral blood smear lets a trained eye see the shape of the cells and often the diagnosis itself. The count of young red cells answers whether the marrow is responding, the iron and vitamin studies name the missing raw material, and tests of the clotting times describe the coagulation system. Where bleeding from the gut is suspected, an endoscopy looks directly at the esophagus, the stomach, and the first part of the bowel, and a colonoscopy examines the rest, chosen by the site the history has suggested. Imaging by ultrasound, computed tomography, or magnetic resonance imaging finds an enlarged spleen, a mass, or blood inside a closed space, and a sample of the marrow may be needed when the count suggests a failure of production. Every reference range in this paragraph is a property of the laboratory that issued it, so the clinician must confirm the values against the current guideline and the local protocol before acting on them.

In a clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, the same reasoning is performed with fewer instruments. The torch makes the conjunctivae, the tongue, the gums, the nails, and the skin visible, and the clinician counts the pulse and the breathing rather than estimating them, records the temperature, and feels the abdomen for the spleen and the liver. The diet, the menstrual history, the stool color, the medicines, and the family history carry the diagnosis in most cases, and the clinician who takes them carefully can often name the likely mechanism without any test. The decision that follows is a direction rather than a diagnosis, which is whether the patient needs a blood count urgently, whether a course of iron is justified while the cause is sought, and what the patient should watch for in the meantime. Treatment with iron is the right decision when the picture clearly fits a deficiency and there is no suggestion of a bleed that needs investigation, and it is the wrong decision when the patient has an unexplained anemia with a positive stool test, because replacing the iron can conceal a cancer while it corrects the count.

To summarize, bleeding, bruising, and anemia are read by mechanism and pattern rather than by quantity, and the pattern separates a platelet problem from a clotting problem from a fragile vessel. The slow bleed from the gut, the marrow that fails and presents as bruising, the hemolytic crisis, the bleeding of advanced liver disease, and the anemias of pregnancy and of childhood are named early because each is revealed by a finding that can be sought at the bedside. The conjunctivae, the tongue, the nails, and the spleen carry the assessment as far in a clinic as the count and the smear carry it in a hospital, and the two worlds ask the same questions. Every score, cut-off, reference range, pressure, temperature, rate, and duration in this chapter is an illustration of reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between countries, editions, and laboratories. This is educational material and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service. A patient who is bleeding now, or who has a headache and is drowsy after a trivial injury, is helped first and explained afterward.
