# Chapter 16 — Musculoskeletal Examination

The musculoskeletal examination asks a question no machine answers: what does this joint do now that it did not do before, and what does that change cost the patient. A joint is examined as part of a body that walks and works, so findings are read against function from the first minute. Before any maneuver the examiner must hold the dangerous possibility in mind, because the most feared catastrophe here is an infected joint, and septic arthritis destroys cartilage quickly. The finding that reveals it is not subtle: one joint that is hot, swollen, and held rigid, hurting on the smallest passive movement, often with fever and a patient who looks unwell. That picture is examined first, and everything else is ordered around the speed it demands. This is educational material only, and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the local protocol.

The history comes before the hands, because it decides what the hands should seek. The examiner asks which joints are involved, how the problem began, what makes it better and worse, and what the patient can no longer do. The pattern outweighs any single answer: a joint that flared suddenly differs from many joints that drifted into pain over a long period. Morning stiffness that lingers after waking and eases with use points toward inflammation, while pain that worsens through the day and with activity points toward mechanical wear. A prosthetic joint, a suppressed immune system, diabetes, or injected drug use raises the chance that a hot joint is infected, and a normal temperature does not remove that chance. The examiner also asks who the patient is, because a swollen knee means something different in a young runner and in an older person who has just finished treatment that weakens the immune response.

Inspection is done with both sides visible, because the patient's other limb is the best available control. The examiner looks at posture and how the limb is held, at swelling, redness, wasting, alignment, deformity, scars, and the overlying skin. Swelling of a whole joint suggests fluid or thickened lining, whereas swelling clearly outside the joint, over a bursa or tendon sheath, points elsewhere. Wasting above and below a painful joint shows the problem is not new, because wasting takes time to appear. Redness and a shiny tense skin are serious and demand that infection be excluded rather than assumed away. The examiner also notices what the patient does with the limb while talking, since a leg that is never straightened is itself a finding.

Palpation follows gently, because the aim is information and not a test of endurance. The back of the hand is used for warmth, since it senses temperature better than the fingertips, and both sides are compared in the same room and at the same moment. The examiner feels the joint line, a boggy fullness that suggests thickened lining, a fluid thrill that suggests effusion, and tenderness over bone, tendon, or ligament rather than over the joint. Sharp tenderness at one point of bone suggests fracture or infection in the bone, while tenderness in a band along the joint line suggests disease of the joint. A joint that is hot compared with its partner is the single finding that should stop the examination and redirect it toward infection. The comparison is repeated at the end, because warmth that has increased during the encounter strengthens the same conclusion.

A measured temperature adds to the hand but does not replace it, and a normal temperature does not exclude deep infection. A temperature of 38 degrees Celsius is often taught as the line between normal and fever, yet it varies with the site of measurement, the age of the patient, and the routine of the ward, so confirm it against the current guideline and the local protocol. What matters more than the exact figure is the direction of travel: a temperature rising since the last check, with a joint that has become more painful, is a message that cannot be ignored. In a frail, elderly, or immunosuppressed patient the temperature may be normal while the joint is destroyed, so the examiner trusts the joint and the general state as much as the thermometer. A patient who is sweating, confused, or breathing quickly beside a hot joint is seriously ill and must be treated as such while the joint is assessed.

Movement is tested actively first, because this shows what the joint can do under the patient's own control and reveals pain and weakness together. Passive movement follows while the patient relaxes, and the comparison between the two is the heart of the assessment. If active movement is limited by pain but passive movement goes further, the problem is more likely in muscle, tendon, or the pain itself, whereas a joint that stops at the same point both ways has a mechanical restriction. A joint that hurts at the very start of passive movement should not be moved again until infection has been considered. The examiner notes not only how far the joint goes but what stops it: pain, spasm, a hard block, or a soft springy resistance each tells a different story. Restriction that is painless and longstanding is usually wear, while restriction that is new and painful is a reason to keep looking.

There is no single normal range, because the range of a joint varies with age, sex, build, occupation, and the population in which it was measured. The knee, for example, is commonly described as bending to about 135 degrees, but that figure is an average drawn from one group of people and should be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, since published values differ between editions and populations. The wiser approach is to compare the affected side with the unaffected side in the same patient, which supplies an individual reference no table can give. The range should be recorded as the patient reaches it, not as the examiner forces it, because a range obtained by pressure measures the examiner as much as the joint. A small painless loss may matter little, while a small painful loss that is new may be the earliest sign of serious disease.

Effusion is excess fluid within the joint, detected by feel and movement rather than by looking alone. A small effusion may show only as fullness beside the joint line or as a bulge that shifts when the examiner presses one side and watches the other. A larger effusion in the knee can be felt as a tap when the examiner presses the kneecap against the bone beneath, and the sensation is unmistakable once it has been felt a few times. Crepitus is the grating felt or heard as the joint moves, and its character matters: fine crepitus in a worn joint is common and often harmless, while coarse crepitus with a sudden loss of movement may follow a fracture or a loose fragment. Neither effusion nor crepitus names a disease by itself, and both must be read with warmth, tenderness, and the general state of the patient. An effusion that appeared overnight with heat is very different from one that has been present for a long time without pain.

Special tests are the part of this examination most often taught as ritual and most often misunderstood. Each test is a question asked in a particular way, and its value lies in the question rather than the movement. A test that stretches a ligament asks whether that ligament is torn, a test that compresses a meniscus asks whether it is caught, and a test that narrows a passage asks whether a tendon is pinched. The examiner should know what each test is meant to exclude, perform it the same way each time, and record the result as positive, negative, or uncertain rather than as proof. A positive test raises the probability of its target and a negative test lowers it, but neither settles the matter, because the same test behaves differently in different populations and in different hands.

Several tests are worth describing so the listener can picture them. To test the ligaments of the knee, the examiner holds the thigh with one hand and draws the lower leg forward or pushes it back with the other, asking whether the tibia slides more than it should; the test asks about the cruciate ligaments and is unreliable in a guarding or swollen knee. To test the menisci, the examiner bends the knee, rotates the lower leg, and straightens it while watching for a click or a catch, which asks whether a torn fragment is trapped. To test the shoulder for impingement, the examiner raises the arm through an arc and looks for pain in the middle of the movement, which asks whether a tendon is compressed under the bony arch. Each test refines the history, and none is decisive; an examiner who treats a click as a diagnosis has stopped reasoning too early.

The spine is examined as a column that carries load and protects the cord, and the examination is organized by those two jobs. The examiner inspects from behind and from the side, looking at the curve of the back, the level of the shoulders and hips, the muscle bulk beside the spine, and any patch of hair or dimple over the lower back. Movement is tested in bending forward and backward, in bending to each side, and in rotation, and the examiner watches how the whole spine moves rather than only how far. Tenderness is sought along the spinous processes and in the muscles beside them, because tenderness over bone may mean infection or fracture, whereas tenderness in muscle usually means strain. The examiner also notices how the patient stands and sits, since a spine that hurts only when loaded behaves differently from one that hurts at rest.

The spine carries the most time-critical danger here after the infected joint, and it must be named where the examination is described. Compression of the spinal cord or of the nerves at its lower end can be caused by infection, tumor, or a disc, and the findings that reveal it are numbness around the saddle area, difficulty starting or stopping the flow of urine, weakness or numbness in both legs, and loss of bowel control. Any of these turns back pain from a nuisance into an emergency, and the patient must be referred without waiting for imaging, because the interval in which function can be saved is short. Infection in the spine may declare itself with fever, pain that wakes the patient at night, tenderness over a single vertebra, or pain worse on movement and unrelieved by rest. Malignancy is more likely in an older patient with a history of cancer, constant nocturnal pain, weight loss, and bone tenderness. Weight loss with night pain in an older patient is a combination that must never be followed by reassurance alone.

The sacroiliac joints sit at the base of the spine and are examined by provocation rather than direct feel, because they are deep. The examiner presses down and outward on the pelvis, or squeezes its two sides together, and asks whether pain is reproduced over the joint rather than elsewhere. The pattern that points to inflammation in these joints is pain in a younger person that begins gradually, is worst in the early morning and at rest, improves with exercise, and often wakes the patient at night. That differs from the mechanical back pain of someone who hurt the back lifting and is now better lying still. The examiner should also look for inflammation elsewhere, in the eye, the skin, the gut, and the tendons, because these joints are rarely inflamed alone.

Three patterns are held side by side, because the examination is a search for which one the patient shows. Mechanical pain is related to use, worse with activity and better with rest, localized, and unaccompanied by swelling, warmth, or systemic illness. Inflammatory pain is worse at rest and in the morning, improves with movement, often affects more than one joint, and is accompanied by lingering stiffness and sometimes by swelling of the lining. Infective pain is severe, begins quickly, is accompanied by heat, swelling, and a patient who looks unwell, and is made unbearable by any movement. These patterns overlap in real patients, and the examiner who finds features of more than one must follow the most dangerous until it is excluded. A person can have longstanding wear in a joint and then develop an infection in it, and the second problem is the one that must be recognized.

Septic arthritis deserves a closer look because delay in recognizing it causes the most harm. The joint fills with pus, cartilage and the bone beneath are damaged quickly, and the patient may become systemically unwell, so the examination aims to find it before that damage is done. The joint is usually single, most often the knee, hip, shoulder, or wrist, and it is held still because every movement hurts. The skin over it is hot and often red, the joint feels full, and the patient resists even the gentlest passive movement. Fever may be present or absent, and the white cell count may be high or normal, so neither the temperature nor a single blood test can rule the condition out. Risk is higher with a prosthetic joint, a recent joint injection or operation, a skin infection, diabetes, a suppressed immune system, or injected drug use, and a child with fever and a limp must be treated as infected until that is excluded. In a hot painful joint the decision that matters is whether to aspirate the joint fluid, and it is made on the examination rather than on a waiting result.

Gout and other crystal diseases produce a joint that is hot, red, swollen, and extremely painful, so they imitate infection closely and are among the commonest reasons a septic joint is missed. A first attack of gout often strikes the joint at the base of the big toe, reaches its worst quickly, and may bring fever and a raised inflammatory marker, which makes the two nearly indistinguishable at the bedside. The examiner should look for tophi, for previous similar attacks that settled, and for the same problem in other joints, since these favor crystals over infection. None of these features is decisive, and the only reliable way to separate the two is to examine the joint fluid, a laboratory step that may not be available where the patient sits. When the fluid cannot be examined and the joint is hot, the safe course is to treat the situation as infection until it is proved otherwise. A past history of gout must not close the question, because a person who has had gout can still develop an infected joint.

Inflammatory arthritis is recognized by its distribution and the character of the swelling rather than by any single sign. The examiner looks for the same joints on both sides being affected, for the small joints of the hands and feet, and for sparing of the joints nearest the nails, which are usually the first to wear with age. The swelling of inflammation is soft and boggy and feels like thickened lining, whereas the swelling of wear is hard and bony. Movement is often full but painful, and any crepitus is fine rather than coarse. Tendon sheaths and bursae can also be inflamed, and tenderness that follows a tendon rather than a joint line points away from the joint. The examiner should look for inflammation in other systems too, because arthritis is often the visible part of a disease that also affects the skin, eyes, lungs, or kidneys.

Wear of a joint produces a picture that is more mechanical and more local. The joint may be enlarged by bony outgrowths, the examiner may feel hard swelling at the margins, and movement is reduced with crepitus that is often coarse. Pain is worse with use and better with rest, morning stiffness is brief, and the joint is not hot or red unless something else is happening. The hands may show bony nodes at the joints nearest the nails and at the middle joints, and the knees may be bowed or the hips may lose rotation early. The presence of wear does not exclude inflammation or infection, and the examiner must ask whether the current pain has the same character as the usual pain. A joint that has grumbled for a long time and has suddenly become hot is a new problem in an old joint and should be treated as one.

Fracture is the third common possibility and the one most easily missed when the patient is calm. It shows itself through deformity, tenderness sharply localized over one point of bone, inability to bear weight or use the limb, and pain increased by any movement at the site. In an older patient with thin bones, a fracture of the hip may present with no deformity at all, and the only findings may be an inability to walk and pain when the leg is gently rotated or the heel is tapped. In a child, a fracture may be incomplete with little swelling, and the child may use the limb until the examiner tests it. The examiner should compare both sides and examine the joints above and below the injury, because a second injury may be less obvious. Where a fracture is suspected and the limb is deformed or the skin is broken, the limb should be supported and the patient referred rather than examined further for completeness.

Two soft tissue emergencies belong here because they are mistaken for ordinary muscle pain and are lethal if missed. Necrotizing infection of the soft tissues begins as pain far greater than the appearance suggests, with swelling, discoloration, and eventually skin that is numb or hard, together with a patient who is rapidly becoming unwell. Compartment syndrome follows an injury and produces pain out of proportion to it, worse when the muscle is stretched, with a tense compartment and a limb whose pulses may still be present. Both progress while the patient looks stable, and the finding that reveals them is pain the patient cannot tolerate and that does not match the story. The examiner who finds pain out of proportion should escalate the patient immediately rather than simply record it, and let the team that can open the tissue decide. Waiting for a picture or a result in either condition is the error that costs the limb or the life.

Not all musculoskeletal pain comes from a joint, and the examiner who separates the joint from the structures around it finds the diagnosis faster. Pain from a tendon appears when the tendon is stretched or its muscle contracts against resistance, and it is localized to a point along the tendon rather than the joint line. Pain from a bursa lies over the bursa between bone and skin or bone and tendon, and is worse when that space is compressed. Pain from muscle appears with contraction and stretching, is often diffuse, and comes with weakness, while pain from bone is deep, constant, and tender over a point and should suggest infection or tumor rather than strain. The shoulder shows the principle well, because pain appearing in the middle of an arc of movement suggests a pinched tendon, weakness of rotation with little pain suggests a tear, and pain spreading down the arm with neck position suggests the neck. The examiner who treats the shoulder while missing the neck wastes everyone's time.

The hip and the knee are the joints most often examined, and each has its own logic. Pain from the hip is often felt in the groin or front of the thigh rather than over the hip, and the earliest loss is usually rotation rather than bending. The examiner rotates the leg gently while the patient lies relaxed and compares both sides, because a hip that has lost rotation is a hip with a problem. The knee lies close to the surface and can be felt and seen in detail, which is why it is the joint most often aspirated and the one where effusion and meniscal injury are best assessed. The examiner should remember that pain felt in the knee may come from the hip and the reverse, and should examine the joint above and below before naming a source. Both joints should also be examined standing, because a joint that is stable unloaded may not be stable under weight. The examination ends with function, because that is what the patient came to restore, and the examiner watches the patient rise from a chair, walk a few steps, turn, and if possible climb a step, noticing favouring, speed, and effort. Any time measured for that task is a local convention rather than a universal standard, so confirm the reference used in your own service against the current guideline and the local protocol.

In a well-resourced hospital the examination is extended by imaging and the laboratory, and the examiner must decide what each adds. A radiograph shows the structure of bone and the alignment of a joint and can reveal a fracture, an erosion, or a loss of space, but it cannot show early inflammation or infection, so a normal radiograph does not remove the dangerous possibility. Ultrasound can show fluid in a joint or around a tendon and can guide the needle that removes it, which makes it valuable when infection is suspected. A magnetic resonance imaging scan, or MRI, shows the soft tissues, the marrow of bone, and early inflammation, and it is the test of choice when infection, tumor, or a tear is suspected in a structure that cannot be seen directly. None of these replaces the examination, because the question that decides what to image is asked with the hands and answered with the history. The most decisive step for a hot joint remains the removal and examination of the joint fluid, which the examiner should pursue without waiting for the picture. A normal result must never overturn a convincing clinical picture, and a worrying picture must never be cancelled by a reassuring image.

In a clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, the same reasoning is done with fewer inputs and no less discipline. The examiner compares both sides in the same light, measures the temperature, inspects the skin with the torch, and uses the hands to feel for warmth, swelling, and tenderness. What cannot be done is to look inside the joint or examine the fluid, so the decision becomes one of referral rather than diagnosis, and the question is which patient must travel today. A joint that is hot, hurts on any movement, and belongs to a patient who looks unwell must be referred at once, because the treatment that saves it cannot wait for a radiograph that has not yet been done. A painful joint that is not hot, in a patient who is otherwise well and can use the limb, may be managed with advice and review, provided the patient is told what to watch for and where to return. The interval before the radiograph and the uric acid result is the hardest part, and the examiner should record which joints, which side, how warm, how much movement, and what the patient can and cannot do, so that a later comparison is possible. The decision to treat a first attack of crystal arthritis is reasonable when the picture is typical, the patient otherwise well, and the joint less severe than infection would make it, whereas treating the situation as infection is safer when the patient is systemically unwell or the joint is exquisitely tender. That choice is a decision about treatment and not a recommendation of a drug, it must follow the protocol of the setting, and the patient should be told what would make the clinician change the plan.

To summarize, the musculoskeletal examination moves from the history to inspection, to palpation, to active then passive movement, and reads each finding against function and against the dangerous possibilities. The infected joint is the emergency that must be named and excluded first, and the hot joint that hurts on the smallest movement is the finding that should change the pace of the encounter. Mechanical, inflammatory, and infective patterns guide the reasoning, but they overlap, and the most dangerous explanation is followed until it is ruled out. Every number in this chapter, including the temperatures and the ranges of movement, is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because these values differ between countries, editions, and laboratories. This is educational material, not medical advice, and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the local protocol. If a patient is deteriorating, or a joint is hot and the patient is unwell, the examination stops and the patient is treated and referred, because no diagnostic completeness is worth the delay of a person who is becoming more ill.
