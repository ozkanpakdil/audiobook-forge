# Appendix D — Red-Flag Summary Cards

A red flag is not a diagnosis; it is one finding that changes the order of what happens next. Ordinary reasoning weighs probabilities, but a red flag demands action before the explanation is complete. Some conditions kill within minutes to hours, and the interval between finding and action is the only part of the outcome still changeable. This appendix gathers those findings by presentation, so a worried clinician can find the reason. It is educational material and never a substitute for training, supervision, clinical judgment, or local protocol. Return to these entries between shifts rather than hearing them once.

Each entry has four parts, and naming them makes the entries easier to remember. The first is the finding, in the words a clinician would use at the bedside. The second is what it threatens, the harm that follows when a dangerous sign is treated as merely interesting. The third is the action required, which may be treatment at once, a call for senior help, a journey to hospital, or close observation with a written plan. The fourth is the diagnosis it protects against, the possibility that must be set aside before the clinician relaxes. Red flags are stronger in company, because lethal conditions rarely announce themselves with one sign.

A red flag is evidence of danger, but its absence is not evidence of safety. A normal examination does not exclude a heart attack, an ordinary calf does not exclude a clot in the lung, and a warm hand does not exclude infection in the blood. These signs are easy to see when present, so the clinician must know when they are missing, in the older patient, in the patient whose immune defenses are suppressed, and in pregnancy. In each of them the same disease produces fewer signs and a faster decline, so the threshold for help rises as the number of findings falls. The clinician who waits for the complete picture has usually waited too long.

Vital signs are the first red flag because they can be measured anywhere with a watch, a thermometer, and a cuff. A systolic pressure below 90 millimeters of mercury is the classic line at which shock is assumed, and it must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the pressure that matters depends on age, pregnancy, and the patient's usual value. A breathing rate above 30 breaths per minute is often recorded carelessly, and the point at which it should alarm differs between services and editions, so it belongs to the local chart and the current guideline rather than to memory. A pulse above 130 beats per minute, a temperature below 36 degrees Celsius, and an oxygen saturation below 92 per cent each need checking against the current guideline and the local protocol, since age, fitness, altitude, and the instrument all move that figure. The direction of travel across readings matters more than any single number.

The airway supplies red flags measured in seconds rather than numbers. A patient who cannot speak in full sentences, who is gasping, who drools and cannot swallow, or whose chest is silent is losing the airway or the drive to breathe. Swelling of the tongue, lips, or throat after a sting, a new medicine, or a food is the same emergency arriving faster. The threat is death from lack of oxygen within minutes, and the action is to call for help, keep the patient upright, be ready to begin cardiopulmonary resuscitation, or CPR, and never leave the patient alone. The diagnoses protected against are a severe allergic reaction, an infection of the epiglottis, which is the flap guarding the windpipe, air trapped around a lung under pressure, and an inhaled object. At the roadside the red flag means help is called now.

Chest pain produces several red flags that must be separated before a comfortable explanation is accepted. A crushing central discomfort with sweating, nausea, and pain running into the jaw or arm may be an acute myocardial infarction, and a normal examination does not exclude it. A sudden tearing pain between the chest and the back, especially with different pulses or pressures in the two arms, may be a dissection of the aorta, the great vessel leaving the heart. A difference of 20 millimeters of mercury between the arms is a further warning that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because technique and accepted figure vary. Sudden breathlessness with pain on breathing in and a swollen calf may be a clot that has traveled to the lung, and the leg may look ordinary. The action is help now, an electrocardiogram, or ECG, where one exists, and a journey to a place where the heart can be assessed.

The abdomen supplies red flags that are felt rather than read. A sudden severe pain with a rigid, board-like wall, pain that worsens when the examiner's hand is withdrawn, and a patient who lies still because movement hurts may have a perforated organ or peritonitis, an infection of the lining of the abdominal cavity. Pain far worse than the examination suggests, in a patient who seems disproportionately ill, points to a loss of blood supply to the bowel, which kills quickly. A pulsating mass in the middle of the abdomen with severe back pain, a falling pressure, and a faint may be a leaking aortic aneurysm, and that patient must not be examined repeatedly or moved about. A woman of childbearing age with lower abdominal pain, a missed period, and a faint may have a pregnancy growing outside the uterus, and that entry is treated before any scan. At the roadside the rigid abdomen, the faint, and the pulsating mass each mean transport now.

Headache and the nervous system give red flags that are short in duration and long in consequence. A headache that reaches its worst within 1 minute, often the worst of the patient's life, with vomiting, neck stiffness, or a brief loss of consciousness may be a bleed from an artery at the base of the brain, called a subarachnoid hemorrhage. That interval is a teaching illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since sources draw the boundary differently. The action is urgent imaging and specialist assessment while the patient is kept still, and a more likely alternative diagnosis does not remove the urgency. A new weakness on one side, a fallen face, and difficulty finding words may be a stroke, and that patient is moved to imaging at once because time decides what can be done. A drooping eyelid with headache and a widened pupil may be pressure on the nerve that moves the eye, and it is an emergency rather than an eye problem.

Fever with headache and a stiff neck is the classic entry for meningitis, the infection of the membranes covering the brain and spinal cord. The finding that matters most is a rash that does not fade when a clear glass is pressed against it, because a rash that stays visible under pressure may mean bleeding into the skin from an infection of the bloodstream, called meningococcal disease. The other findings are dislike of bright light, vomiting, an altered level of consciousness, cold hands with fever, and in an infant a bulging soft spot. The threat is death within hours, and the action is treatment and senior help at once, before the lumbar puncture and before the diagnosis is settled.

Fever becomes a red flag when it is joined by evidence that the organs are failing. Confusion or drowsiness, a climbing breathing rate, a falling pressure, cool and mottled skin, a shrinking passage of urine, and a patient who looks gravely ill mean the response to infection is more dangerous than the infection itself. A patient whose white cell count has fallen after cancer treatment, a patient whose immune defenses are suppressed by medicines, and a patient with advanced infection by the human immunodeficiency virus, known as HIV, may carry severe infection with almost no fever. A count of infection-fighting cells below 500 per microliter is often quoted as the point of special danger, and it must be read against the current guideline and the local protocol, since laboratories report it in different units. A traveler from a region where malaria is transmitted, a person with a device in the body, and a person who injects drugs belong in the same entry. The action is to find the source and treat on the clinical picture without waiting for every result.

Back pain is usually benign and occasionally catastrophic, and the red flags point to the spinal cord. Pain that is severe at night, pain that runs in a band around the trunk, progressive weakness of the legs, numbness around the buttocks, and difficulty starting or controlling the flow of urine are the findings of cord compression, which is pressure on the spinal cord from a growth, an infection, or a broken vertebra. The bundle of nerves continuing below the cord can be compressed in the same way, and the earliest sign is often numbness in the saddle area with trouble passing urine. The threat is permanent paralysis and permanent loss of bladder control, so the action is urgent imaging and immediate referral to a center that can relieve the pressure. The diagnosis protected against is a cancer that has spread to the spine, which may be its first evidence. An older patient, or a patient who injects drugs, with back pain and fever may have an infection in a vertebra, and that entry is treated before imaging is arranged.

The limbs produce red flags that are seen rather than reported. A limb painful out of proportion to its appearance, tense, pale, and painful when the fingers or toes are stretched may be losing its blood supply inside a closed compartment of muscle, a condition called compartment syndrome. A limb that is cold, pale, pulseless, and numb below a sudden line has lost its artery, and the threat is loss of the limb within hours. A joint that is hot, swollen, and exquisitely painful with fever may be infected, and it must not be treated with rest and review alone. A leg that swells on one side after an operation, a long journey, or immobility may carry a clot, and the danger is the part that breaks away to the lung.

The skin and soft tissues give red flags that progress while the clinician watches. Pain out of proportion to a wound, dusky or gray skin, blisters filled with dark fluid, a crackling sensation under the fingers, and a patient rapidly becoming unwell suggest a necrotizing infection, which kills tissue beneath the surface and spreads between the muscles. A rash that does not fade under pressure and spreads within hours, especially with fever and a falling pressure, may be bleeding into the skin from an overwhelming infection, the same finding described in the entry on fever and the neck. A rash that loosens and sheds the skin after a new medicine suggests a severe drug reaction, and so does a rash with blisters around the mouth, eyes, or genitals. The action is senior help and surgical assessment now, because the surface often looks far better than the tissue beneath. The diagnosis protected against is an infection reaching the deep tissues and the bloodstream faster than any dressing can follow.

Pregnancy changes ordinary findings and adds red flags of its own. A woman with a missed period, lower abdominal pain, vaginal bleeding, a faint, and pain at the tip of the shoulder may have a pregnancy implanted outside the uterus, called an ectopic pregnancy, and that possibility is treated as an emergency before any scan. A pregnant patient with severe headache, flashing lights or other visual change, upper abdominal pain, and swelling of the face or hands may have a dangerous rise in the blood pressure of pregnancy. A pressure above 140 over 90 millimeters of mercury is often quoted as the line at which concern begins, and it must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the accepted line differs between settings. Reduced movements of the baby, bleeding in the later months, fever in pregnancy, and a seizure in a pregnant patient each require immediate assessment. The action at the roadside and in hospital is the same, which is to treat the patient as seriously ill and move her to a place where the pregnancy and the pressure can be assessed.

Infants and children hide danger better than adults, and their red flags are behavioral as much as physical. A child who will not wake properly, will not feed, has a high-pitched or weak cry, is floppy or stiff, or cannot be consoled needs urgent assessment, whatever the temperature reads. Grunting, head bobbing, drawing in of the lower chest wall, and a breathing rate above the expected range for the age are signs of a child working hard to breathe, and that range is set by age and by the chart in use, so it must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. An infant younger than 3 months with a temperature of 38 degrees Celsius or more, a bulging soft spot, or a rash that does not fade under pressure is treated as seriously ill at once, although the age and the temperature that define that concern are set differently by different guidelines and must be checked against the local protocol. Green vomiting, blood in the stool, and sudden pallor with screaming need referral without delay, and the parent's own impression that the child is not right deserves the same weight as the findings.

The eye and the mind also give quiet red flags that are permanent if missed. A sudden painless loss of vision in one eye may be a blocked vessel serving the retina, the light sensitive layer at the back of the eye, and a red, painful eye with halos around lights may be an abrupt rise in pressure inside the eye, both needing same-day assessment. Confusion developing over hours, a changed personality, and drowsiness that cannot be roused point to a physical cause until one has been excluded. A patient who expresses a wish to die with a plan, or who wants to leave against advice while confused or short of oxygen, carries real danger and must not be left alone.

A number reported by a laboratory is a statement about a method and a population, not a law about the person in front of the clinician. A hemoglobin of 7 grams per deciliter, a platelet count of 50,000 per microliter, a sodium of 120 millimoles per liter, and a potassium of 6 millimoles per liter are the kinds of figures laboratories report, and each means something different in a child, in pregnancy, and in a patient whose usual value is abnormal. The ranges behind those lines belong to the laboratory that produced them and to the population it serves, so the report in front of the clinician governs, and the current guideline and the local protocol must be consulted before the figure is treated as a threshold. A single value is a question rather than an answer, and the trend across several samples often carries more information. The same caution applies to the physical findings here, because pressure, temperature, rate, and duration are measured with instruments that differ between countries and editions.

The same entry is used differently at the roadside and in a teaching hospital, and this appendix is built to be heard in both. At the roadside the red flag usually decides a journey rather than a diagnosis, so the airway is kept open, bleeding is pressed upon, and the patient is moved warm and still to the nearest place that can treat. In hospital the same red flag decides urgent actions, so blood is drawn, an ECG is performed, imaging is arranged, a senior clinician is called, and the patient may be cared for in the intensive care unit, or ICU. The finding does not change between the two settings, and neither does the principle that it is acted upon before it is explained. A clinician working alone should name the red flag in the notes and the handover, so the next person inherits the concern rather than a settled diagnosis. Protective clothing and other personal protective equipment, or PPE, belong to the roadside entry, because a bleeding or coughing patient may be a hazard to the person helping.

Every red flag deserves to be written down with the time it was found and the action it produced. A note recording the finding, the concern it raised, the person informed, and the plan for review lets the next clinician continue the same thought, while a note that records only the diagnosis hides the reasoning. The patient and the family should be told, in plain words, which findings must bring them back sooner, among them a headache that becomes severe without warning, a rash that does not fade under pressure, a fever that will not settle, and a new weakness. A constructed illustration may make this concrete, and it is a teaching device rather than a portrait of any real person. Imagine a person who develops the worst headache of his life at a market, vomits once, and feels briefly faint; the finding is the sudden severe headache, the threat is a bleed around the brain, and the action is to keep him still and move him to imaging rather than send him home. Neither that person nor any story here is real, and none should be applied to a real patient without a full assessment.

To summarize, a red flag is a finding that changes the order of what happens next, recognized by the pattern of the presentation rather than by the completeness of the diagnosis. The entries here gather the dangerous findings of the history, the examination, and the vital signs by presentation, and each names the finding, what it threatens, the action it requires, and the diagnosis it protects against, among them the subarachnoid hemorrhage, the meningococcal rash, the ectopic pregnancy, the compression of the spinal cord, and the leaking aortic aneurysm. A red flag is acted upon before it is explained, in a clinic and at the roadside as much as in a teaching hospital, because the interval between the finding and the action is the part of the outcome that can still be changed. Every figure here, whether a pressure, a temperature, a rate, or a reference range, is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such numbers differ between countries, editions, and laboratories. This appendix is educational material and does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service. The absent red flag is not safety, the normal examination is not exclusion, and a red flag is acted upon before it is explained, so the patient who worries the clinician deserves another look and a clear plan.
