# Chapter 56 — Psychiatry

Psychiatry is the part of medicine in which the organ under examination is the mind, and the clinician who treats it as separate from the body makes two opposite mistakes. The first is to accept a psychiatric label as the whole explanation, so that a patient who is anxious or depressed is never examined for the physical disease that produced the mood. The second is to treat a psychiatric symptom as a failure of character, so that the patient is blamed rather than assessed. The reasoning here is the reasoning of the rest of this book: a problem is described, a differential diagnosis is assembled, the dangerous causes are placed first, and the findings raise or lower each possibility. Psychiatry differs in that the history is the principal instrument and the patient's account of the inner world is the chief evidence. This chapter is educational material, it is not a protocol, and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of the service in which the patient is seen.

Several lethal possibilities must be named before any comfortable psychiatric explanation is accepted, because each announces itself with a finding that can be sought in the first minutes. The patient who is confused, inattentive, and fluctuating through the day may have delirium, which is a disorder of the body expressing itself through the brain, and the finding is attention that cannot be held with an alertness that changes from hour to hour. The patient who is drowsy, sweating, feverish, rigid, and tremulous after a medicine that alters dopamine or serotonin may have a drug-induced emergency, and the finding is rigidity with fever in a mind that has suddenly changed. The patient whose speech has stopped, whose body holds a posture, and who will not eat or drink may have catatonia, which is an emergency rather than an attitude, and the finding is the stillness with resistance to being moved. The patient who is agitated, hallucinating, and trembling after heavy alcohol use may be in withdrawal, which kills through seizures, an abnormal rhythm of the heart, or infection, and the finding is the tremor with a racing pulse and a rising temperature. The patient who is despondent and has spoken of death is at risk of suicide, and the finding that raises urgency is a plan, a means, and the intention to use them.

The mental state examination is the psychiatric equivalent of the physical examination, and it is structured so that nothing is left to impression. The clinician observes appearance and behavior first, because dress, grooming, posture, eye contact, and movement are visible before a word is spoken. Speech is judged for its rate, volume, and whether it can be interrupted, since pressure of speech and the leap from one idea to the next carry information the content alone does not. Thought is examined for its form, whether it moves in a straight line or jumps, and for its content, which includes preoccupations, delusions, and ideas of harm, while perception is examined for hallucinations, which are perceptions without an object. Attention is tested by reciting the months in reverse or counting backward in sevens, and recent memory by naming 3 objects and asking for them after a delay. Where a formal cognitive score is used, its contents and the figure at which it raises concern vary between versions and populations, so the result must be read against the current guideline and the local protocol rather than against memory.

Delirium is the presentation most often mistaken for a psychiatric disorder, and the mistake is dangerous because delirium is almost always a symptom of something physical. It develops over a short interval, and the interval that defines it is stated differently in different editions, so any figure offered for it is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the boundary between delirium and a chronic decline is drawn differently in different countries and services. The two most useful features are inattention and fluctuation, so the patient cannot hold a task and the alertness rises and falls across the day, often worse at night. Orientation may be disturbed, but a patient can be fully oriented and still be delirious, and the patient may hallucinate, most often visually, and may be frightened by what is seen. Whenever delirium is present, the clinician stops looking for a psychiatric explanation and begins a physical search.

The physical search for the cause of delirium follows a fixed list, because the causes that kill are common and treatable. Infection is the most frequent, and in an older patient the infection may be in the chest, the urine, the skin, or the bloodstream, with a temperature barely raised or absent. A shortage of oxygen from lung disease or from a clot in the lung, a low sodium, a failing liver or kidney, and a disturbance of calcium all alter the mind before they alter anything the clinician can see. A blood glucose below 3 millimoles per liter can produce confusion and sweating, and that figure is a reference range each laboratory sets for itself, so the value that matters must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. Medicines are a leading cause, including sedatives, drugs for pain, drugs for the bladder, steroids, and anything recently started, stopped, or increased. Alcohol and sedative withdrawal, a head injury, a seizure, an infection of the brain or its coverings, and a stroke or a bleed inside the skull belong in the same list. The finding that most often reveals the cause is the combination of what changed, a temperature, a blood glucose where it can be measured, and every medicine the patient takes.

Depression is not sadness, and the difference matters because the decision to treat depends on the syndrome rather than the mood. The reasoning rests on features that must be present together for a sustained interval: a low mood, a loss of interest, a change in sleep and appetite, a change in weight, fatigue, poor concentration, guilt, and thoughts of death. How long those features must last is set differently in different manuals, so the interval is only an illustration; verify it in the guideline your service follows and in the local protocol, because the editions in circulation do not draw the line in the same place. Depression commonly presents as a physical complaint, with headache, back pain, insomnia, or a fatigue the patient believes is bodily, and a clinician who looks only for sadness will miss it. A loss of 5 per cent of body weight over a few weeks is often quoted as a sign of physical illness, but the figure and the interval must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the threshold differs between countries, editions, and laboratories. The severity matters more than the label, because a patient with psychotic features, with a refusal to eat, or with a settled intention to die is in a different category from mild symptoms.

A long list of physical illnesses imitates depression, and the search for them is part of the assessment rather than a separate errand. An underactive thyroid produces low mood, fatigue, weight gain, constipation, cold intolerance, and slowed thinking, and the finding is a slow pulse, dry skin, and a swelling at the front of the neck. Anemia produces fatigue, breathlessness, and pallor, and the finding is the pale conjunctiva and the rapid pulse. A cancer that has not declared itself, an infection such as tuberculosis or infection with the human immunodeficiency virus, known as HIV, a deficiency of folate or of thiamine, an underactive adrenal gland, and a high calcium can all present this way. Medicines are a frequent cause, including steroids and some drugs for blood pressure, and the history of what was started before the mood changed is the key. The finding that makes a physical cause less likely is a clear psychosocial story with a normal examination and normal tests where those exist, and even then the two can coexist.

Suicide risk is assessed directly, in plain words, and the fear that asking will plant the idea is not supported by evidence; the patient who has been waiting to be asked is often relieved. The clinician asks whether the patient has thought about death, whether the thought has become a wish to die, whether there is a plan, whether the means are available, and whether the patient intends to act. Past attempts, a family history of suicide, a recent loss, a serious physical illness, chronic pain, isolation, and heavy drinking all raise the level, and a patient who is agitated, sleepless, and hopeless is at greater risk than one who is withdrawn and silent. The clinician must ask about access to means, including medicines kept at home, firearms, and other dangerous items, because reducing access is one of the few measures that reliably lowers risk. No score replaces the conversation, and a low figure on any checklist must never be used to send a patient home against the clinician's own unease.

Mania is a sustained elevation or irritability of mood with an increase in activity and energy, and it is dangerous because the patient may not see it as illness. The patient sleeps very little without feeling tired, speaks rapidly, moves from one idea to the next, feels unusually important, spends money or takes risks, and may become grandiose or irritable when opposed. How long the picture must last before it is called mania is set differently in different manuals, so the interval is only an illustration; verify it in the guideline your service follows and in the local protocol, since the editions and countries that use them do not agree. A first episode of mania in an older patient deserves a physical search, since steroids, thyroid disease, stimulant drugs, and delirium can all produce the picture. The risk is harm through reckless behavior, harm to others, and exhaustion, and it is highest when the mood is mixed with despair, because that combination carries suicide risk. The finding that changes the plan is a patient who cannot be kept safe by those around them, because that patient needs a place where observation is possible.

Psychosis is the presence of delusions, hallucinations, or a disorganization of thought so marked that the patient cannot be understood, and the first task is to decide whether it is primary or secondary to something else. A hallucination in clear consciousness suggests a primary psychotic illness, while a visual hallucination in a patient whose alertness fluctuates points toward delirium and a physical cause. A first episode in a young person may begin a lifelong illness, but the same picture can follow cannabis, stimulant drugs, or a steroid, and the finding that would change the diagnosis is a clear link in time to a substance. Red flags that demand a physical search are a fever, a headache, a stiff neck, a seizure, focal weakness, an abnormal level of alertness, and a first presentation at an unusual age. A patient with HIV infection or another cause of immune suppression can develop an infection or a tumor of the brain that presents as psychosis. The finding that most often reveals a secondary cause is a focal neurological sign or an abnormal level of consciousness, and either one moves the patient to a medical pathway at once.

Anxiety is a normal response that becomes a disorder when it is disproportionate, persistent, and disabling, and it is a positive diagnosis rather than a diagnosis of exclusion. The patient describes worry that cannot be controlled, restlessness, a racing heart, breathlessness, sweating, tremor, chest tightness, dizziness, and a fear of losing control or of dying. The mimic list includes a heart attack, an abnormal rhythm of the heart, a clot in the lung, a low blood glucose, an overactive thyroid, an adrenal tumor, asthma, and withdrawal from alcohol or sedatives. Where an electrocardiogram, or ECG, is available it answers one of those questions and not all of them, because a normal tracing does not exclude a clot in the lung. The examination looks for a rapid or irregular pulse, a raised pressure, a tremor, a swelling in the neck, and signs in the chest, and the clinician who finds none has lowered the probability of physical disease without excluding it. Alcohol and sedatives are a common hidden cause, because the patient may be in withdrawal between doses, and the finding is a tremor and a pulse faster than the story would suggest.

Substance use is a medical condition with a physical examination, and the withdrawal states are emergencies that must be recognized before the patient deteriorates. Alcohol withdrawal begins after the last drink, and the interval over which it appears is stated in different sources, so any figure given for it is a teaching illustration that must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, because services organize their observation periods differently. The early features are tremor, sweating, a racing pulse, anxiety, and insomnia, and they may progress to seizures and then to a confused, hallucinating, feverish state that can kill. A patient with a rapid pulse above 120 beats per minute, a temperature of 38 degrees Celsius or more, sweating, and confusion after stopping alcohol is severely ill, and both of those numbers are illustrations to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the values that should cause alarm depend on the patient, the country, and the edition of the reference in use. Opioid withdrawal is extremely uncomfortable but rarely lethal, while opioid intoxication kills through a slow breathing rate and a reduced level of consciousness, and the finding is pinpoint pupils with shallow breathing.

An assessment never becomes grounds for dismissing a physical complaint, and this is the single most important rule in psychiatric practice. A patient with a known mental illness can develop a heart attack, a pneumonia, a broken bone, or a clot in the lung, and the symptoms will be attributed to the mind unless the clinician actively looks for the physical cause. The error is more common in patients who cannot give a clear history, including patients with a communication difficulty, patients who are sedated, and patients who are in pain but do not complain. A new confusion or a change in behavior in such a patient is a physical alarm, not a relapse, until proven otherwise. The clinician who writes down what was considered, what was excluded, and what remains uncertain has protected both the patient and the reasoning.

In a clinic with time and privacy, the psychiatric assessment is done slowly, and the setting itself is part of the treatment. The clinician sits at the same level as the patient, allows silence, and lets the patient set the order of the story before directing it. Privacy allows the questions that are hard to ask elsewhere, about mood, about sex, about violence at home, about drinking, and about suicide, and the clinician should explain why each is asked so that it does not feel like an interrogation. The physical examination is still performed, because the body is where many psychiatric presentations begin, and the clinician looks at the thyroid, the skin, the tongue, the weight, the pulse, the pressure, and the color of the conjunctiva. A thermometer and a torch extend the examination, and a watch turns the pulse and the breathing into measurements rather than impressions. Where the patient cannot speak freely, a family member may be seen separately with permission, and that second account often changes the picture.

In a general hospital the same assessment is compressed and performed at the bedside of a patient who may not want to be seen. The referral question must be read carefully, because the team that asks for help is often asking whether the patient is safe, whether the behavior is a medical symptom, or whether the patient can decide about treatment, and the answer to each is different. The consultant gathers collateral history from the nursing staff and the family, since the patient may be too ill, too drowsy, or too suspicious to give it, and the nurses often hold the most useful observations. Restlessness, aggression, and refusal in a hospital bed are frequently caused by pain, a full bladder, constipation, a shortage of oxygen, sepsis, withdrawal, or a medicine, and each is sought before any psychiatric label is applied. The clinician examines the body as thoroughly as the ward allows, because an uncooperative patient may still have a wound, an ulcer, a swollen joint, or a distended abdomen.

Capacity is a decision-specific judgment and not a global label, so the question is always whether this patient can make this decision at this time. The clinician asks whether the patient can understand the information, retain it long enough to weigh it, use it to reach a choice, and communicate that choice. A patient who disagrees with the team does not lack capacity, and a patient with a psychiatric diagnosis does not automatically lack it either. Capacity can be lost for one decision and preserved for another, and it can change as the illness changes, so the assessment is repeated when the situation changes. A patient who lacks capacity must still be involved as far as possible, and the decision must be made in the patient's best interest with the people who know the patient involved. When there is doubt, the clinician should seek help rather than settle the question alone, because the consequences of a wrong judgment fall on the patient.

The decision to treat a psychiatric illness is a diagnostic decision as much as any other, and it is made on the severity, the risk, and the cause rather than the name alone. Treatment is the right answer when a severe depression has psychotic features, when catatonia threatens nutrition and hydration, when delirium has a treatable physical cause, when a withdrawal state is progressing toward seizures, and when the risk of suicide cannot be managed where the patient is. Treatment is the wrong answer when a physical cause has been missed, when the presentation is a response to a life event that is resolving with support, and when the medicine that would be chosen could harm a patient whose illness is not of that kind. The finding that supports the choice is the response over time and the absence of the harm that was feared, and the finding that would reverse it is a new physical sign or a deterioration in the mental state. Naming the decision and the evidence for it is more useful than naming a dose, because this book teaches reasoning and not prescribing.

A constructed illustration may make the reasoning concrete, and it is offered as a teaching device rather than a portrait of any real person. Imagine a man in his sixties brought to a clinic because his family says he has become quiet and has stopped eating, and in the first minutes the clinician notices that he is slow, cold, constipated, and that his pulse is slow; the mood looks like depression and the examination suggests the thyroid. Now imagine a young woman brought to an emergency department because she has not slept and believes the neighbors are watching her, and the clinician finds a fever, a stiff neck, and a fluctuating alertness that move her care in a different direction within minutes. Neither person is real, and neither story should be applied to a real patient without a full assessment.

A patient who is sent home after a psychiatric assessment must leave with a plan that is clear and written down, because the risk of suicide is highest in the period after discharge. The plan names the warning signs the patient and the family should watch for, the person to call, and the place to go at any hour. The clinician should reduce access to the means of self-harm in the home, involve the family where the patient agrees, and make sure the next appointment is real rather than promised. A follow-up interval is a figure like any other, so the time between visits must be set against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the appropriate interval depends on the severity of the illness and the support available. The patient should be told that returning sooner is not a failure, and that a new physical symptom deserves the same attention as a new mental one.

Psychiatry across the lifespan changes the weighting of causes rather than the method, and some periods of life carry a much higher risk of severe illness. In an older patient, delirium, depression, dementia, and the effects of medicines overlap, and the same presentation may contain all four, so the physical search and the cognitive assessment are done together. The weeks after childbirth are the most important window of risk, because a woman who becomes sleepless, confused, suspicious, or preoccupied with the baby may have postpartum psychosis, an emergency for the mother and the infant. The finding that should change the plan is a mother who is not sleeping at all, whose speech is disorganized, or who believes the baby is damaged or that she must harm herself, and that woman needs help and observation now rather than an appointment later. Other windows include the period after a bereavement, after a serious illness or an operation, and after a discharge from hospital, and a previous severe episode or violence at home lowers the threshold for help.

The differential diagnosis in psychiatry is assembled as anywhere else, by running through the great classes of disease before settling on a favored explanation. The Vindicate framework asks the clinician to consider in turn the vascular, the infectious, the neoplastic, the degenerative, the iatrogenic, the congenital, the autoimmune, the traumatic, and the endocrine or metabolic causes of the picture. In a patient with a change in mood or behavior, the vascular cause may be a stroke or a bleed inside the skull, the infectious cause may be an infection of the brain or the body, and the metabolic cause may be a disturbance of the thyroid, the glucose, or the sodium. The iatrogenic group deserves particular attention, because a medicine recently started, increased, or stopped is one of the most common causes of a new psychiatric symptom. Each possibility is then raised or lowered by the history, the examination, and the tests that exist, and the ranking is decided by both likelihood and danger.

The interview is the principal instrument of psychiatric assessment, and how it is conducted decides how much the patient will reveal. The clinician begins with open questions, allows the patient to finish, and uses silence rather than filling it, because the most important sentence often follows a pause. Distress is expressed differently in different cultures and families, and a symptom that seems disproportionate in one setting may be an ordinary language of suffering in another, so the clinician asks what it means to this patient. Where the patient and the clinician do not share a language, an interpreter who is not a family member is preferable, because a relative may soften, omit, or add to what is said. The clinician should be honest about the limits of confidentiality, especially when the risk of harm to the patient or another person is present.

The physical examination of a patient with a psychiatric presentation is not a formality, and several findings are visible only if the clinician looks for them. Scars on the forearms, the thighs, or the abdomen may be the record of earlier self-harm, and puncture marks in the veins of the arms, the legs, or the neck suggest injecting drug use. The weight, the hydration, the state of the teeth, and the color of the conjunctiva tell the clinician how long the illness has lasted and how much the body has suffered. A tremor, a rapid pulse, sweating, and a swollen neck point toward thyroid disease or withdrawal, while a slow pulse, dry skin, and slowed movement point toward an underactive thyroid. The skin is examined for a rash, a bruise, an ulcer, and a wound, and the abdomen for a full bladder and a loaded bowel, since both can produce agitation in a patient who cannot explain the discomfort.

To summarize, psychiatric presentations are assessed with the same method as every other problem in this book: the history first, the dangerous causes named early, the body examined as carefully as the mind, and the findings used to rank the differential diagnosis. Delirium is treated as a physical illness until proved otherwise; suicide risk is asked about directly and without euphemism, and no checklist takes the place of the conversation; depression, anxiety, mania, and psychosis are each examined for the physical illnesses that imitate them, including thyroid disease, anemia, infection, a deficiency of a vitamin, medicines, and the withdrawal states. The mental state examination supplies the structure, and it is carried into a clinic with time and privacy and into a hospital bedside where the patient does not wish to be seen. Assessment never becomes a reason to dismiss a physical complaint, and a new confusion or a new behavior in a patient with a psychiatric diagnosis is a medical alarm until it is explained. Every figure in this chapter, whether a score, a cut-off, a temperature, a rate, a reference range, or an interval, is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such numbers differ between countries, editions, and laboratories. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service.
