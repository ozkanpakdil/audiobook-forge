# Chapter 69 — Choosing and Interpreting Tests in Context

Every test in medicine is a question asked of the body, and the whole discipline of investigation lies in knowing what question is being asked before the sample is taken or the image is made. A result has no meaning by itself; it has meaning only against the probability that the patient already carried and against the decision that the clinician was about to make. This chapter is about that discipline, which governs laboratory tests, tracings, images, and the strip that changes color in a clinic with no laboratory at all. The organizing question is short and severe: what would I do differently if the result comes back positive, and what would I do differently if it comes back negative. If the answer to both is nothing, the test is not neutral; it is a harm with a cost, a risk, and a consequence. This material is educational and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of the service in which the patient is seen.

Before anything else, the lethal possibilities that a reassuring test can hide must be named, because the most common fatal error in this chapter is not a wrong result but a right result misunderstood. A patient with a clot in the lung may have a normal chest radiograph and normal oxygen measurement, and the finding that reveals the danger is the story of sudden breathlessness with a swollen calf or a recent operation, together with a pulse and a breathing rate that stay high while the patient insists nothing is wrong. A patient with a dissection of the aorta may have a normal tracing of the heart's electricity, and the finding that betrays it is a tearing pain that moves between the chest and the back with a difference in pressure or pulse between the two arms. A patient with a severe infection of the bloodstream may have a white cell count inside the reference range, and the finding that reveals it is a change in behavior, a falling pressure, or a passage of urine that has shrunk. A patient with meningitis may be sent home because a single normal test was reassuring, and the finding that should have stopped that decision is a neck that resists flexion or a rash that does not fade when pressed. A patient in the earliest hours of a heart attack may have an unremarkable tracing, and the finding that reveals it is a discomfort that is new or that has changed its pattern in a patient who looks unwell. In every one of these, the test was not wrong; the mistake was to let a single result close a question that the story had opened. The clinician who keeps the lethal possibility in view while reading a normal result is doing the work that no test can do.

The first habit is to write the question down before the order is sent, in words that can be read later by the person who receives the result. A useful question names the finding, the patient, and the decision it will change, such as whether this breathless patient has a clot that would make treatment necessary tonight. A question that cannot be put in that form is usually a wish for reassurance rather than a diagnostic step, and reassurance is the weakest reason to test. The clinician should also decide in advance what a negative result would mean, because a test whose negative cannot be acted on is as useless as one whose positive cannot. When both outcomes lead to the same decision, the test has already been answered by the reasoning, and ordering it adds only the chance of confusion. The order that survives this scrutiny is short, targeted, and tied to a moment in the patient's care.

Every result is read against the probability that existed before it, and the same finding means different things in different patients. A positive result in a patient whose chance of disease was very low is more often a false positive than a true one, while a negative result in a patient whose chance was high rarely removes the diagnosis. This is why the history and the examination still carry the weight, and why a test cannot repair a clinician who has not formed a view. The clinician who estimates the probability honestly, in words such as unlikely, possible, or probable, has already decided how much a result can move it. The estimation is not arithmetic; it is the accumulated sense of how this patient's story compares with the stories of similar patients seen before. Writing that estimate down before the result arrives is the simplest defense against being led by the number.

A test is described by two behaviors that are worth understanding even when the arithmetic is never performed at the bedside. The first is how often it is positive in people who truly have the disease, and the second is how often it is negative in people who truly do not. A test that is positive in 9 of 10 people with the disease sounds impressive, but that proportion is measured in a particular group of patients, and such proportions vary with the population tested and with the laboratory, so confirm them against the current guideline and the local protocol rather than carrying a figure from a book. When a test is studied in patients who are already in hospital, it usually performs better than it will in a clinic where the disease is milder and earlier. The clinician who remembers that the study population is not the waiting room will be slower to trust a result that seems too clean.

Reference ranges deserve their own caution, because they are the numbers a listener is most tempted to memorize. A range is built from measurements taken in a group of people defined as healthy, and the boundary is drawn so that a proportion of that group falls outside it by design. A hemoglobin range such as 12 to 16 grams per deciliter is therefore a statement about a reference population rather than a rule about the person in front of the clinician, and it must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because each laboratory sets its own limits on its own machines and in its own population. The patient's own usual value matters more than the printed range, which is why a result from last year is worth more than a general figure. A value just outside the range in a patient who looks and feels well is a question, not a diagnosis, and the reasoning should ask what else would be expected if the abnormality were real.

An unexpected result has a momentum of its own, and the cascade it begins is one of the most recognizable patterns in modern medicine. A minor abnormality on a scan leads to another image, which leads to a sample of tissue, which occasionally leads to a complication from the procedure itself, and the patient arrives at harm by a path that no one chose. The cascade usually starts with a test that was ordered without a question, because a result that no decision required has no natural stopping place. Breaking the cascade takes a deliberate act, which is to return to the patient and ask what the first result meant for this person, and whether pursuing it would change anything that matters. A clinician who says aloud that a small finding will be watched rather than chased is practicing medicine, not neglect. The record should carry the reasoning, so that the next clinician understands why the chain was stopped.

A false positive that is never corrected becomes a diagnosis, and that label is often harder to remove than it was to apply. The patient may be treated for a disease that was never present, the treatment may carry its own harms, and the label follows the patient into every future consultation. The error is compounded by anchoring, because once the diagnosis is written, later findings are read in its light and contradictory evidence is discounted. The remedy is a confirmatory test with a different basis, ordered before the label is fixed, and a willingness to state plainly when the first result proved to be wrong. Where two tests disagree, the clinician must decide which question each one answered rather than averaging them. A diagnosis made on a single imperfect result is a hypothesis, and it should be written as one.

Not every test is ordered for the patient, and honesty about that is part of the discipline. A test may be ordered to satisfy a family who cannot accept reassurance, a senior who expects a complete panel, an employer or an insurer who wants documentation, or the clinician's own fear of blame if something is missed. Defensive testing feels safe and is often the opposite, because every test carries a chance of a false result and a cascade that follows it. The clinician who recognizes whose anxiety the order serves can address that anxiety directly, with explanation, with a clear plan for review, and with the honesty to say that the test would not change the plan. That conversation is harder than writing the order and usually does more for the patient. A test ordered to protect the clinician rather than to help the patient should be named as such in the reasoning, even if it is ultimately sent.

Incidental findings are the price of looking, and the clinician should decide in advance how they will be handled. An image made for one question will sometimes answer another that was never asked, and the finding may be harmless, uncertain, or genuinely important. The response should be proportionate, which means asking whether the finding explains the patient's problem, whether it needs treatment, and whether knowing about it will help or only worry. Some findings are best watched over time rather than pursued, and that decision belongs to a clinician who knows the patient and can explain the choice. The patient must be told what was found and what will be done, because silence about an incidental finding is a breach of trust that no protocol can repair.

Ordering a test creates a duty to its result, and that duty is where systems fail most often. The result must reach the clinician who ordered it, be read against the question that was asked, be communicated to the patient in words the patient can use, and be acted on or deliberately set aside with a reason. A result that arrives after the clinician has gone off duty belongs to the service rather than to the individual, which is why handover must carry pending tests by name. The patient who was told that someone would call with the result must actually be called, and a result that is never followed up is a diagnosis lost in the space between people. A clinician who orders a test and does not own its result has done the easy half of the work.

Timing changes what a test means, and a value without its time is an incomplete result. A measurement taken at the wrong moment can be falsely reassuring, and the same measurement repeated later can be unmistakable, which is why serial results often carry more than any single one. A sample drawn after treatment has begun may no longer show what it would have shown before, so the order of drawing and treating is itself a clinical decision. The interval at which a test should be repeated is a figure that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because different services and different editions set different rhythms for the same measurement. The trend, read alongside the patient's condition, is usually more informative than the isolated number. A clinician who compares the patient today with the patient yesterday is using the laboratory well.

In a hospital the pressure runs toward more testing rather than less, because the machines are close, the results return quickly, and ordering feels like thoroughness. The daily panel drawn before the ward round, the repeated image ordered because the previous one was inconveniently timed, and the test added to the list because a colleague mentioned it are all familiar, and none of them is a question. The patient in the intensive care unit, known as the ICU, may have blood taken several times a day, and each sample has a cost in blood, in sleep, and in the chance of a spurious result that prompts another test. Availability is not an argument for use, and the clinician with the most tests at hand has the greatest need for restraint. In this setting the skill is to choose the one test that answers the question and to leave the others unasked. The patient who is unstable is treated on the clinical findings first, and the tests follow rather than lead.

Used well, the hospital turns uncertainty into evidence quickly, and the reasoning does not change because the tools improve. The clinician reviews the results already in the record before ordering anything new, because the answer is often there and the repeat adds nothing but delay. Each test is chosen for the decision it serves, and the clinician knows before the result arrives what would be done with either answer. The patient's own values, recorded over time, are read as a story rather than as isolated lines, and the clinician notices the direction in which they are moving. When a test cannot help, the clinician says so and offers the plan instead, whether that is observation, treatment, or discharge with clear instructions. A hospital that tests less and thinks more is not a poorer hospital; it is a safer one.

The cost of a test is not only money, though money is real and belongs in the reasoning. Every sample consumes the time of a person who could be doing something else, the reagent or the electricity that runs the machine, and the attention of the clinician who must interpret it. In a service where the laboratory is shared, a test ordered for no decision delays the test that someone else urgently needs. The harm is invisible because it falls on a patient the clinician never sees, and that invisibility is exactly why it must be named. A clinician who treats the laboratory as a free and infinite resource is spending someone else's scarcity. The same reasoning that justifies a test for the patient in front of the clinician should consider the patient who is waiting behind.

In a clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, scarcity performs the discipline that a hospital must impose on itself. A test must be justified twice, once by the question it answers and once by the journey or the money it costs the patient, and the clinician feels both. The scarcity does not lower the standard of reasoning; it raises it, because the history and the examination are the tests that are always available. The torch makes the throat, the ear, the eye, and the skin visible, the thermometer turns an impression into a repeatable figure, and the hands find the enlarged organ, the tender area, and the swelling that changes the plan. A breathing rate above 20 breaths per minute is worth recording rather than treating as a diagnosis, and that boundary must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because what counts as fast shifts with age, pregnancy, and fitness. Where a single rapid test exists, it is used for the one decision it can settle rather than as a general screen, and its limits are stated to the patient. The reasoning is the same reasoning, conducted with fewer instruments and the same duty.

Where the test cannot be done, the referral is the test, and it carries the same obligations. The decision to send the patient onward is a clinical judgment about probability and danger, and the clinician should state what is suspected and what finding would confirm it. The result of that referral must return, because a diagnosis made elsewhere is still the business of the clinician who sent the patient. If the patient does not go, or the appointment is missed, that is a result of the test, and it must be followed rather than forgotten. The record should show why the journey was thought necessary, so that the next clinician can see the reasoning rather than a bare request. A referral without a question is as unhelpful as a laboratory order without one.

The two worlds differ in their instruments and not in their logic, and the clinician should be able to move between them without changing the reasoning. The well-resourced clinician must ask the same question before ordering and must resist the pull of the available machine. The clinician in the clinic must own the result of the one test that was possible and must carry the referral to its conclusion. Both must remember that a test is a question and that the patient, not the result, is the subject of the work. When the same discipline is applied in both places, the patient receives the same quality of thought even when receiving very different technologies. The transferable part of medicine is the reasoning, and that is what this chapter is trying to protect.

A constructed illustration may make the contrast concrete, and it is offered as a teaching device rather than as a portrait of any real person. Imagine a patient in a large hospital who mentions a mild, intermittent discomfort in the abdomen, and whose scan is ordered to quiet the clinician's unease rather than to answer a question, and whose scan reveals a small, unrelated change that begins a chain of further images and a sample of tissue. Now imagine a patient in a clinic with a fever and a cough, in whom the clinician asks what the single available test would change, uses it for that one purpose, examines the chest carefully, and sends the patient home with a clear plan for review. Neither patient is real, and neither story should be applied to a living person without a full assessment. The difference between the two is not the machine; it is the question asked before the machine was touched.

The test must be explained to the patient before it is done and interpreted for the patient after, in language that carries the uncertainty honestly. The patient should know what the test is for, what it can and cannot show, and what will happen if the result is unclear. A false positive is far easier to accept when the possibility was mentioned in advance, and far more damaging when it appears as a surprise. The clinician should avoid the language of certainty, because very few tests deliver it, and should say plainly when a result does not settle the question. The conversation after a normal result is as important as the conversation after an abnormal one, since a normal result that is over-read becomes false reassurance. Consent for a test includes understanding its limits as well as its purpose.

A test whose result is pending creates a period of risk, and the plan for that period belongs in the record and in the patient's understanding. The patient should know which symptoms would mean returning before the result arrives, and how the result will be communicated if it is delayed or if the patient cannot be reached. A follow-up appointment arranged before the patient leaves is stronger than a promise to call, because it survives a lost telephone number and a busy week. The clinician should decide in advance what will be done if the patient does not return, rather than assuming that silence means recovery. Safety-netting is a diagnostic skill, and it is often the step that prevents a missed lethal diagnosis from becoming a death.

The record should show the question, the reasoning, and the plan, not only the result. A note that lists the tests ordered without saying why leaves the next clinician unable to interpret them and unable to stop. Writing the question beside the order is a small habit with a large effect, because it forces the clinician to have a question. The record should also show what was considered and set aside, and what the patient was told, so that the reasoning can be reviewed rather than reconstructed. A result that contradicts the working diagnosis must be recorded as a contradiction, not quietly omitted. The written record is the memory of the reasoning, and it is what the next shift inherits.

A test measures a surrogate rather than the patient, and the distinction matters when the two disagree. A number can be abnormal while the patient feels well, and a number can be normal while the patient is seriously ill, and both situations are common. The clinician should ask what the measurement represents in this person, and whether the usual interpretation applies to someone of this age, this pregnancy, this weight, or this illness. The patient's account of how they feel is data of a different kind, and it is not overruled by a result that does not fit. When the two conflict, the safest course is usually to repeat the observation and to watch the patient rather than to choose one and discard the other.

The most dangerous order in medicine is the one written to rule something out, because exclusion is a stronger claim than a test can usually support. A negative result lowers the probability in proportion to how often the test misses the disease, and it never reduces it to zero. The clinician who treats a negative result as proof of absence has mistaken a useful tool for a guarantee. This is especially true when the test was performed early, when it was performed on the wrong sample, or when the patient had already been treated. A rule-out order should therefore be paired with a statement of how much the negative changes the plan, and with a plan for what happens if the story continues. When the probability was high before the test, a negative result is a reason to reconsider the test rather than to relax.

The clinician's own mind is an instrument that needs checking, because a test can be ordered to confirm a belief rather than to test it. The habit that protects against this is to ask, before the result arrives, what finding would make the clinician abandon the working diagnosis. If no result could change the diagnosis, the diagnosis has stopped being a hypothesis and has become a commitment. A clinician should also notice when a test is ordered to answer the patient's theory rather than the clinical question, and should say so kindly. Seeking the result that would disprove the current view is not doubt for its own sake; it is the method that keeps the diagnosis honest. A diagnosis that has never been challenged is the one most likely to be wrong.

Over-testing has its own pathology, and it is a clinical error with consequences rather than a matter of etiquette. It produces anxiety in patients who were well, treatment for conditions that would never have caused harm, procedures that carry risk, and a diagnostic label that alters how a person is seen for the rest of their life. It consumes the attention of clinicians, so that the patient with a real problem waits behind a queue of investigations that no decision required. It also teaches patients that every symptom deserves a scan, which is a lesson that is difficult to unlearn. The correction is not a rule about numbers of tests but a habit of asking what the test will change. A clinician who can answer that question for every order is already practicing restraint.

Some of the most important decisions in medicine are made by not testing, and that decision deserves the same care as the decision to test. A patient with a clear, self-limiting illness and no danger signs may need an explanation and a plan rather than a sample. A patient whose symptoms are fully explained by a condition already known may need treatment for that condition rather than a search for another. The clinician who withholds a test should say why, so that the patient understands that the omission was a judgment rather than an oversight. Withholding is legitimate when the probability is low, when the result would not change the plan, and when the harm of the test outweighs its benefit. The clinician who can explain that reasoning has done more than the one who simply orders.

To summarize, every test is a question, and the discipline of investigation lies in asking it before the sample is taken, in deciding what either answer would change, and in owning the result until it has been acted on or deliberately set aside. The lethal possibilities that a reassuring result can hide are named at the bedside, among them the clot in the lung with a normal radiograph, the dissection with a normal tracing, and the severe infection with a normal white cell count. Over-testing is treated as the clinical error it is, with its cascades, its false positives that harden into diagnoses, its incidental findings, and its orders written for someone other than the patient. The hospital with its unlimited machines and the clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer share one question and one duty to the result, and the reasoning transfers between them unchanged. Every score, cut-off, reference range, pressure, temperature, rate, or interval named in this chapter is an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between countries, editions, and laboratories. This is educational material and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service.
