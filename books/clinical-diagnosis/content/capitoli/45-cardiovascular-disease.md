# Chapter 45 — Cardiovascular Disease

Cardiovascular disease is a single phrase for two different kinds of failure, and the clinician who keeps them apart reasons better. One is a failure of the pump, in which the heart cannot move blood forward without demanding more pressure or more filling than the body can provide. The other is a failure of the pipes, in which the vessels that carry blood are narrowed, blocked, stretched, or torn. The two overlap, since a blocked pipe starves the pump and a failing pump congests the vessels, but the questions they ask are distinct. This chapter follows the diseases as a patient brings them: a pressure found by chance, a chest discomfort on effort, a breathlessness that grows, a heartbeat that has lost its rhythm, a murmur heard through a stethoscope, and a swollen leg or a sudden shortness of breath. The material is educational and does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of the service in which the patient is seen.

Several lethal possibilities should be named before any comfortable explanation is entertained, because each announces itself with a finding that can be sought in the first minutes. The patient with crushing central chest pain, cold sweat, nausea, and pain running into the jaw or the arm may be having an acute myocardial infarction, and a normal examination does not exclude it, so the electrocardiogram, or ECG, and the history together carry the decision. The patient with a sudden tearing pain that moves between the chest and the back, together with a difference in the pulses or the pressures between the two arms, may have a dissection of the aorta, which is a diagnosis made on suspicion rather than on any single sign. The patient who becomes suddenly breathless may have a clot in the lung, and the finding that supports it is a swollen, tender calf on one side, although the leg may look entirely normal. The patient whose heart races or stops and who faints, sweats, or cannot be roused may have a rhythm that has made the circulation fail, and that patient is treated before the rhythm is named. The patient with a fever and a new murmur may have an infection on a valve, and a murmur that was not there before turns a vague illness into a specific search. These are the presentations in which the next few minutes matter more than the next few tests, and they are kept in view throughout.

Understanding what the patient feels begins with the cycle of the heart, which fills while it relaxes and empties while it contracts. When the pump weakens, the pressure inside the chambers rises and is transmitted backward to the lungs and to the veins, and it is that backward pressure, rather than the weakness itself, that produces most of the symptoms. Breathlessness on effort appears first, because the lungs are the first place to feel congestion when the patient moves. Breathlessness on lying flat, which the patient describes as needing extra pillows, and breathlessness that wakes the patient at night are later and more specific signs of the same mechanism. Swelling of the ankles follows, because the pressure in the veins of the leg rises and fluid leaves the vessels, and fatigue is the quietest symptom and the easiest to attribute to age or to a lack of fitness. The clinician who understands this backward march of pressure knows what to ask, and knows in what order the findings tend to appear.

The examination of the heart follows a fixed order so that nothing is skipped and each finding is understood before the next is sought. The pulse is felt for its rate, its rhythm, its volume, and the character of its rise, and a pulse that is slow to rise or that collapses quickly says as much as any murmur. The pressure is taken in both arms when a dissection is possible and with the patient standing when fainting is part of the story. The jugular venous pressure is the window on the right side of the heart, and it is judged by watching the column of blood in the neck as the patient lies at an angle. The heart is examined by looking for the position of the apex beat and by listening through the stethoscope over the valve areas with the patient turned, sitting, and holding the breath as each position requires, and the lungs, the liver, and the legs are examined in the same sitting. Each step is a question whose answer changes the next, so the order is part of the reasoning rather than a ritual.

High blood pressure is the most common cardiovascular disease and the one most often discovered without the patient feeling anything at all. It damages the arteries quietly for years and declares itself through what it has already done: a stroke, a heart attack, a weakened pump, a damaged kidney, or a change in the vessels at the back of the eye. A cut-off such as 140 over 90 millimeters of mercury is often quoted as the line above which the diagnosis is made, but that figure is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the accepted line differs between countries, between editions, and between the ways a measurement is taken. The measurement itself is a skill, so the cuff must be the right size for the arm, the patient must be seated and rested, and the arm must be supported at the level of the heart. A single high reading is not a diagnosis, and the clinician who repeats the measurement, and who arranges readings at home or across a day where that is possible, will separate the patient whose pressure is always high from the patient whose pressure rises only in the consulting room. The examination then looks for damage, among which are an enlarged heart, an abnormal sound in the arteries of the neck, changes in the vessels of the retina, protein in the urine, and a raised substance in the blood that speaks of the kidney. The patient with a very high reading together with chest pain, breathlessness, a severe headache, visual disturbance, or a change in consciousness may be in a hypertensive emergency and needs treatment and senior help at once.

The decision to treat high pressure is a decision about risk rather than about a single number. The finding that makes treatment the right answer is damage already present, another risk factor such as diabetes, or a pressure that stays high when measured properly and repeatedly. The finding that makes treatment the wrong answer for now is a pressure raised only in the clinic, because treating that patient may do harm without benefit. In a clinic with no laboratory, the reasoning still works, because the eye, the heart, and the urine give real evidence of damage, and the one limit that remains is the fine judgment about kidney function, which should be stated to the patient rather than hidden.

Ischemic heart disease appears in two forms that must be separated at the bedside, the stable and the acute. Stable angina is a predictable discomfort in the chest that comes with effort and settles with rest, and its predictability is its most reassuring feature. The patient typically feels it behind the breastbone, sometimes as a pressure or a heaviness rather than a pain, and it may travel to the left arm, the jaw, the throat, or the upper abdomen. The clinician asks how far the patient can walk before it begins, what makes it come and go, and whether the pattern has changed in recent weeks, using the Socrates framework to keep the questions in order. A typical episode lasts a few minutes and eases within a short rest, and that window is offered only as a teaching illustration, because the duration that should raise concern is stated differently in different sources and must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol. The differential diagnosis includes reflux from the esophagus, pain from the muscles and ribs of the chest wall, and anxiety, and the clinician who considers all three is less likely to be misled by a story that seems clear.

The acute form of ischemic heart disease is the one that kills, and it is recognized by a change in the pattern rather than by the severity of the pain. A discomfort that is new, that comes at rest, that lasts longer than the patient's usual angina, or that wakes the patient from sleep is acute until proved otherwise, and it belongs to the same family as the classic crushing pain with sweating and nausea. The examination may be entirely normal, and that normality is a trap, because a normal examination never excludes an acute myocardial infarction. Where an ECG exists it is performed early and repeated, since a single tracing taken at the wrong moment may look innocent, and where a laboratory exists the substances released by damaged heart muscle are measured and repeated. In the clinic with no ECG, the decision is not the diagnosis but the journey, and a patient with new or changing chest discomfort who looks unwell, who is sweating, or whose pulse or pressure is abnormal must travel to a place where the tracing can be done. The finding that would make immediate treatment the wrong answer is a confirmed alternative cause that fully explains the picture, and even then the patient must be observed while confidence grows.

Heart failure is a syndrome rather than a disease, and it is the final common path of many causes, so the first task is to identify what sort of failure it is and what produced it. The failing pump has a weak muscle that dilates and cannot empty, and this patient usually has an apex beat displaced outward and downward, a third heart sound, and congestion of the lungs and the veins. The stiff heart has a thick muscle that cannot relax, and it is often seen in older patients with a long history of high pressure, in people with diabetes, and in those whose rhythm has become irregular; the heart is not weak in this pattern, but it is unable to fill. The two differ in what helps and in what harms, so the distinction is not academic. The findings that separate them are the size and position of the apex beat, the sounds heard at the apex and the base, the appearance of the ECG, and the estimate of how much blood leaves the heart with each beat, which in a well-equipped hospital comes from an ultrasound of the heart. The patient whose symptoms come with a normal-sized heart and preserved emptying may have a lung problem, an anemia, a thyroid disorder, or a failure of the heart to fill, and each of those changes the plan. Confirming which pattern is present matters even more than naming the syndrome, because the pattern decides whether the treatment given is likely to help or to harm.

Acute pulmonary edema is the lethal face of heart failure, and it announces itself with a patient who cannot lie flat, who is fighting to breathe, and who may cough up frothy, blood-stained sputum. That patient needs treatment and senior help immediately, and the finding that would make a different diagnosis more likely, such as a widespread infection of the lungs, changes only the treatment and not the urgency. Between emergencies, the examination seeks the signs of congestion one by one: a raised jugular venous pressure, judged by the height of the column in the neck relative to the angle of the sternum, with a figure such as 3 centimeters above that angle offered as a teaching illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol because the reference point and the technique vary between services. Crackles at the lung bases, dullness where fluid has collected around a lung, an enlarged and tender liver, swelling of the ankles that pits when pressed, and a weight that has climbed by several kilograms over a few days all point the same way. The weight is worth a word of its own, because a gain of 2 kilograms over 3 days is often described as a sign of retained fluid, and both the figure and the interval are illustrations to be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the meaningful change depends on the patient's usual weight and on the scale in use. A blood test for a natriuretic peptide can support the diagnosis, but its reference range is set by the laboratory that performs it and must be confirmed locally rather than taken from a textbook. The clinician who weighs the patient at every visit and records the trend has a measure that costs almost nothing and reflects the whole circulation.

An arrhythmia is a disorder of the rate, the rhythm, or the order in which the heart's chambers contract, and its importance lies less in its name than in what it does to the circulation. The patient may describe a racing heart, an irregular flutter, a pause, dizziness, breathlessness, or a faint, and the examination may find a pulse that is too fast, too slow, or irregular in a way the hand can feel. An irregularly irregular pulse, in which the beats come without any pattern, suggests atrial fibrillation, and the finding that often accompanies it is a difference between the rate felt at the wrist and the rate heard at the heart, because not every beat moves enough blood to reach the arm. Atrial fibrillation matters for two reasons that are easy to state and easy to forget: it allows clots to form in the heart and travel to the brain, and it takes away the contribution of the atria to filling, which can tip a borderline heart into failure. A rate above 150 beats per minute or a rate below 40 beats per minute may be poorly tolerated, but the figure at which a particular patient becomes unstable depends on the heart itself, so any such number must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol and never applied as a rule. The patient whose rhythm is fast or slow and who has a falling pressure, a change in consciousness, chest pain, or breathlessness is unstable, and the rhythm is treated before it has been fully identified. The patient who is pulseless and unresponsive needs cardiopulmonary resuscitation, or CPR, at once, and the tracing that names the rhythm comes after the hands have started.

Valvular disease is heard before it is understood, and the murmur is the beginning of the reasoning rather than the end of it. A narrowed aortic valve produces a harsh murmur in systole that travels to the neck, and the pulse is slow to rise and small in volume; the patient may have chest pain on effort, breathlessness, or a faint on exertion, and the faint on effort is a warning that cannot be ignored. A leaking mitral valve produces a murmur that runs through systole and travels toward the armpit, and its consequences are an enlarging heart, an irregular rhythm, and a slow decline in the ability to exercise. A narrowed mitral valve produces a low rumble in diastole that is heard best with the bell of the stethoscope and with the patient lying on the left side, and because it raises the pressure in the lungs it may produce breathlessness, coughing of blood, and a raised pressure on the right side of the heart. A leaking aortic valve produces an early diastolic murmur and a pulse that is large and collapsing, and the patient may notice the head nodding with each beat. A murmur is not automatically a disease, and the clinician should judge it by its timing in the cycle, its site, where it travels, what it does when the patient stands or holds the breath, and what else the examination shows. A murmur described by its loudness on a scale of 6 is an illustration of a convention that differs between examiners and between editions, so any such grade must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol and never carried in a note without the description that produced it.

The consequences of a valve lesion are what decide when it must be addressed, and those consequences include a heart that enlarges, a rhythm that becomes irregular, a pressure in the lungs that rises, and symptoms that limit the patient's life. A damaged or replaced valve is also a place where infection can settle, and a fever with a new or changed murmur in such a patient is an emergency of its own; the finding that makes treatment the right answer is a positive culture from blood drawn before any medicine is given, and the finding that makes it the wrong answer is a clear alternative source of the fever that fully explains the illness. Rheumatic heart disease remains a common cause of valve damage in many parts of the world, and it begins with a throat infection in childhood and returns years later as a murmur, an arrhythmia, or breathlessness. A previous sore throat that was not treated is worth asking about in any young patient with a murmur, because the question costs nothing and the answer changes the whole story. The patient with a new murmur and breathlessness or fever should be referred for an ultrasound of the heart where one can be reached, and where it cannot, the decision is whether to begin treatment and travel.

Venous thromboembolism is the clot that forms in a deep vein and the clot that breaks away to the lung, and it must be considered in any unexplained breathlessness or swelling of a leg. The reason is that the second half of the disease kills, sometimes within minutes, and the examination of a patient who dies a few hours later was often described as normal beforehand. The findings of a clot in the leg are swelling of one calf, warmth, tenderness, and a difference in circumference between the two sides, but the leg may look and feel entirely ordinary, and the absence of those signs proves nothing. The findings of a clot in the lung are a sudden breathlessness, a rapid pulse, pain in the chest that worsens with a breath, a cough that may bring blood, and sometimes a faint, and the patient may have no chest signs at all. The risk factors belong in the history: a recent operation, a long period of immobility whether in bed or on a journey, a cancer, a pregnancy or the weeks after birth, the use of hormones, a previous clot, a close relative with one, and an inherited tendency of the blood to clot. The clinician who asks those questions before examining the leg is doing the reasoning in the right order, and a patient with a sudden breathlessness and a normal examination is a patient to be taken seriously, because that combination is the one that most often delays the diagnosis.

Where tests exist, they are chosen to answer a question that the clinical assessment has already framed. A blood test for the product of clot breakdown, known as D-dimer, is useful when the probability is low, because a normal result in that setting makes the diagnosis very unlikely, while a raised result is common in many other conditions and settles nothing on its own. The Wells score formalizes the reasoning about the probability of a clot in the leg or the lung, and any cut-off attached to it is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the version in the original paper and the versions in use today do not set the same line. An ultrasound of the leg veins and a computed tomography, or CT, of the lung arteries are the decisive images where they are available, and each carries its own limits, including the clot that is too small or too peripheral to see. In a clinic with no laboratory and no imaging, the reasoning has to carry the whole weight, and it is still strong: a patient with a plausible story and a high probability is treated while traveling, because the harm of a missed clot is greater than the harm of treatment given on a good clinical case. The finding that would make that decision the wrong answer is a likely bleeding tendency or an alternative explanation that accounts for every symptom, and the clinician who writes down the probability, the reasoning behind it, and the plan for review makes the uncertainty visible rather than hiding it.

In a hospital, the cardiovascular examination is followed by a set of tests that are chosen to answer specific questions. The ECG answers the question of rhythm and of the pattern of injury or strain, and it is repeated because a single tracing is a snapshot of a moment that has passed. An ultrasound of the heart answers the question of how the chambers are sized, how the valves move, and how much blood leaves with each beat, and it is the most informative single test in most cases. A stress test answers whether the demand of exercise provokes the evidence of a narrowed artery, and angiography answers directly which vessels are narrowed and how badly. An ultrasound of the leg veins and a CT of the lung arteries answer the questions of venous thromboembolism, while a CT or a magnetic resonance imaging, or MRI, of the aorta answers the questions raised by a tearing pain. Blood tests for the substances released by heart muscle, for the salts, for the kidney, and for the blood count answer questions about damage and about cause, and each should be ordered because its result would change something. The clinician who knows what each test would mean before it is performed is using the hospital well, and the clinician who treats a test as an answer rather than as a question will be misled by a normal result in a patient who is clearly unwell.

The clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer is not a lesser place to reason, and much of cardiovascular diagnosis is made there. The history carries more weight than ever, because there is no test to correct it: how far the patient can walk, how many pillows are needed to sleep, what has changed in the last week, and what the patient can no longer do. The pulse and the pressure can be counted and recorded properly, and a resting rate above 100 beats per minute or a breathing rate above 20 breaths per minute is a finding to record and to follow rather than a diagnosis; the line at which such a rate becomes abnormal shifts with age, fitness, and medication, so confirm it against the current guideline and the local protocol. The jugular venous pressure can be seen in good light, the heart can be listened to in the positions that bring each murmur out, and the legs can be pressed for swelling. The torch makes the vessels of the eye and the color of the skin visible, the thermometer turns the impression of fever into something repeatable, and a watch makes the rate a measurement rather than a guess. A walk along the corridor, with the patient observed while walking and asked what the patient feels, is one of the most useful tests in medicine and requires no equipment at all. The decision that follows is usually not a diagnosis but a plan, which is to watch and review, to begin treatment on the clinical findings, or to send the patient onward now, and a normal examination in a patient whose story is alarming is a reason to look again.

To summarize, cardiovascular disease is approached as a failure of the pump and a failure of the pipes, and the reasoning follows the patient's presentation rather than a list of diagnoses. High pressure is sought because it damages quietly; chest discomfort is separated into the predictable and the acute; breathlessness and swelling are read as the backward march of pressure from a heart that cannot keep up; an irregular or extreme rate is judged by what it does to the circulation rather than by its name; a murmur is interpreted by its timing, its site, and its consequences; and venous thromboembolism is considered in every unexplained breathlessness or swollen leg because it can kill within minutes. The lethal possibilities are named where they appear, together with the finding that reveals them, and the patient who is unstable is treated before the diagnosis is complete. Every figure in this chapter, whether a pressure, a cut-off, a rate, a temperature, a duration, or a reference range, is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such numbers differ between countries, between editions, and between laboratories. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service. A patient whose story worries the clinician deserves another look, another set of observations, and a clear plan, and the reasoning in this chapter is the tool for that, not a substitute for being with the patient.
