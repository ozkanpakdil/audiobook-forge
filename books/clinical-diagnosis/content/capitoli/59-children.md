# Chapter 59 — Children

The child is not a small adult, and the reasoning that works in the grown patient fails in him for reasons that are easy to state. Growth and development change the normal itself, so the same pulse, the same breathing rate, the same pressure, and the same behavior mean different things at different ages, and the clinician who carries an adult reference into the pediatric room will misjudge the child in front of him. The second difference is that the child rarely gives the history, so the account comes from a caregiver who is frightened, and the task is to hear what the caregiver knows while watching what the child shows. The third difference is that a small number of illnesses account for most of the deaths in childhood, and each of them announces itself through signs that can be sought in the first minutes. This chapter organizes the assessment first by the developmental expectations for each age, because the normal is what makes the abnormal visible, and then by the complaints that bring the child: fever, difficulty breathing, diarrhea with dehydration, and failure to grow. The integrated management of childhood illness is used throughout as a systematic approach to those complaints, a way of asking the same questions in the same order so that nothing is left out when the room is busy and the queue is long. This chapter is educational material; it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service.

Before any comfortable explanation is entertained, the lethal possibilities should be named, each with the finding that betrays it. A child who is breathing fast, drawing in the lower ribs, grunting, or unable to feed may have a severe pneumonia, and the finding that confirms the severity is the visible effort of breathing together with a fall in the oxygen level measured by a device, where such a device exists. A child with fever, a bulging soft spot on the top of the head, a stiff neck, a dislike of light, a seizure, or a level of alertness that is not right may have meningitis, and the sign that must never be missed is a rash that does not fade when pressed. A child with diarrhea who has sunken eyes, a skin fold that returns slowly, and a drowsiness may be severely dehydrated, and the finding that reveals the depth of the loss is the combination of those signs rather than any single one of them. A child who is wasted, whose feet hold a pitting swelling, or whose hair and skin have changed may have severe malnutrition, and the danger is that such a child can die from an infection that produces almost no fever and from a fall in the sugar of the blood. The child who has become quiet when he should be crying, who lies still, who does not resist examination, or whom the caregiver cannot wake is the child who worries the clinician most, and that quietness is a sign in its own right rather than a gap in the examination.

The developmental expectations are the reference range of childhood, and they belong in the assessment as surely as the chest or the abdomen. A newborn fixes briefly on a face and startles at a sudden noise. By about 6 to 8 weeks he smiles in response to a face, and by 4 to 6 months he holds his head steady and reaches for objects, and those ages are offered as an illustration of the sequence rather than as a rule, because the local schedule and the current guideline state the boundaries differently and must be consulted. He sits without support near the middle of the first year, pulls himself to stand and walks in the second year, and speaks single words before joining two of them, and each step is a question about the brain rather than a competition about timing, so the exact month printed in any chart should be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol. What matters more than the date of any single step is the direction of travel, because a child who is gaining skills is usually on a different path from a child who has lost them. Loss of a milestone already gained, a stiffness or a floppiness that has newly appeared, a hand preference that arrives too early, or a failure to use one side of the body are findings that demand an explanation, and the possibilities they raise include an infection of the nervous system, a bleed or a clot inside the head, a tumor, a long-standing shortage of oxygen or of thyroid hormone, and the effects of a heavy metal or of an injury that was not accidental.

The history in a child is taken from the caregiver, and it is taken with the child present, because the child's behavior during the conversation is itself evidence. The clinician asks who the child lives with, who prepares the food, and who noticed the illness first, and then asks what the child eats and drinks in a day, how many wet cloths or diapers there have been, how feeding compares with a week ago, and whether there has been vomiting, diarrhea, cough, or a rash. The birth history belongs in the same conversation, including the weight at birth, the events of the delivery, the age at which the child first fed, and whether any illness, medicine, or injury touched the mother during the pregnancy. The immunization record should be looked at rather than asked about, because a card answers in a way that memory cannot, and a missing dose changes the list of diseases that must be considered. The family history asks about a similar illness in a brother or sister, a death of a young child in the family, a long illness in a parent, a known shortage of blood, and any inherited disease the family has been told about. The social history asks where the household gets its water, how it disposes of waste, whether animals share the house, whether the sleeping place is shared and how it is covered, and whether the family can reach a clinic and by what means. Each of those answers does not produce a diagnosis on its own, but together they decide which of the dangerous possibilities deserves the next question.

The examination begins with the child observed at a distance, before any instrument is used and often before the child is undressed. The clinician notes whether the child is alert and looking around, whether he is playful or listless, whether he lies still or moves restlessly, how he responds to the caregiver, and whether the cry is strong, weak, high-pitched, or absent. The color of the lips and the tongue, the work of the muscles between the ribs and above the breastbone, the position the child chooses, and the way he holds his head are all read at this stage, and the reading costs nothing. The quiet child who does not cry when a stranger approaches, or whose cry has become a whimper, is a child to be taken seriously, and the same is true of the child who feels floppy when lifted or who arches stiffly backward. A child who can be consoled and who reaches for a toy is usually reassuring, while a child who cannot be consoled, or who alternates between irritability and drowsiness, is a child whose brain or circulation may be in trouble. The hands and feet are touched for warmth, the skin is pressed for a rash that fails to fade, and the whole surface is inspected for marks, because a pattern of injury that does not match the story told belongs in the differential diagnosis from the beginning.

Fever in a child must be taken more seriously the younger the child is, because the ability to localize an infection and to mount the usual signs is least in the first months of life. A temperature of 38 degrees Celsius is often used as the line above which fever is said to be present, but that figure is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the accepted line depends on the site of measurement and on the age of the child. The measurement itself is a skill, since the same thermometer used in the armpit, in the ear, on the skin, or in the mouth gives figures that are not interchangeable, so the record should name the route as well as the number. The fever is only the beginning of the question, and the rest of the assessment looks for a source and for the child behind the fever, because a child who is drinking, playing, and passing urine is more reassuring than any single reading would suggest. A rash that does not fade when a clear glass is pressed against it, a stiff neck, a headache the child can describe only by holding his head, a complaint of pain in the ear, a refusal to move an arm or a leg, and a passage of urine that has become painful or frequent each narrow the search in a different direction. In a region where malaria is transmitted, the fever of any child must be tested for the parasite at once, and where no test exists the residence and travel history carries the decision, because a child who appears well in the morning can deteriorate within hours. A fever that lasts for several days without settling, or that comes with a loss of weight, a cough that lingers, or a swelling of the neck, needs a longer look rather than another medicine, and the interval that counts as prolonged is stated differently in different services and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

A child with a cough is assessed by counting the breaths and by looking at what breathing costs him, and both are done before the stethoscope is taken out. The breathing must be counted while the child is calm and the count taken again whenever the first attempt is interrupted, because crying, feeding, and fever all raise the rate, and the observer should count more than once when the first count is borderline. The drawing in of the lower ribs on inspiration, called chest indrawing, is the sign that separates a child who needs close attention from one who may be watched at home, and a child who grunts with each breath, whose nostrils flare, or whose head nods with the effort is working too hard. A breathing rate of 50 breaths per minute is often quoted as the line above which a young child deserves treatment for pneumonia, but that figure is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the threshold changes with age and the published guides do not draw it in the same place. Where a device for measuring the oxygen level in the blood exists it should be used, and the finding that decides the need for oxygen is a saturation below the local threshold, which the protocol of the service sets rather than this book. The examiner then listens for the sounds that distinguish the patterns, since a widespread wheeze with a prolonged outward breath suggests a narrowing of the small airways, fine crackles in one place suggest fluid or pus in the lung, and a harsh sound on inspiration with difficulty swallowing suggests an obstruction above the vocal cords that must be handled as an emergency. The child who cannot drink, who vomits everything, who is drowsy, or who has a blue tinge to the lips has a severe illness whatever the breath count was, and treatment and the journey to a hospital should begin together.

Dehydration is graded by signs that any clinician can observe with the hands and the eyes, and the ladder runs from no dehydration through some to severe. The clinician asks whether the child is thirsty and drinks eagerly, whether the eyes are sunken, whether a fold of skin on the abdomen returns slowly, whether the mouth is dry, whether tears appear when the child cries, and how much urine has been passed. Severe dehydration is present when the child is lethargic or unconscious, when the eyes are very sunken, when the skin fold returns very slowly, and when the child cannot drink or drinks poorly, and that combination is the one that demands fluid into a vein rather than by mouth. The decision about how much fluid and by which route is a decision about the findings and not a number to be taken from a page, and the same is true of fluid given by mouth, because the amount changes with weight, with the degree of loss, and with the plan in force where the child is treated. The finding that would make fluid into a vein the wrong answer is a child who is alert, drinking well, and passing urine, in whom the mouth route is safer and simpler, while the finding that would make it the right answer is a child who cannot swallow or who deteriorates while being rehydrated by mouth. A child given fluid too quickly can develop swelling of the brain or of the lungs, so the speed must be set by the protocol in use and watched against the child's breathing, alertness, and urine rather than given from memory. The caregiver should be shown how to prepare the home solution and told what should bring the child back, and that instruction is part of the treatment rather than an extra.

Malnutrition is diagnosed by measurement and by looking, and the two must be done together, because a child with swelling of both feet may weigh more than his true tissue would suggest. Weight is plotted against the growth chart for the child's age, the length or height is measured and plotted in the same way, and the circumference of the middle of the upper arm is taken with a tape, since a measurement of 115 millimeters is often used as a line that identifies severe wasting, though that number is an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the tape, the chart, and the line differ between services. Both feet are pressed for a pitting edema that is symmetrical, and the hair is inspected for thinning and loss of color, the skin for peeling and dark patches, and the eyes for the dry, dull change that speaks of a shortage of vitamin A. The dangerous complications of severe malnutrition are infections that produce no fever, a fall in the sugar of the blood, a fall in body temperature, and a shortage of the salts in the blood, and each of these can kill a child who looks calm. The decision to treat begins with warmth, feeding, and a search for infection, and the amount and the speed of feeding are set by the protocol of the service that knows the child, because a starved child fed too fast can develop a dangerous shift of salts and fluid. The finding that would make a simple feeding plan the wrong answer is a child who is not gaining at the review, who has diarrhea, or whose edema is increasing, and that child needs a review of the whole plan rather than more of the same.

Infection of the membranes around the brain is the diagnosis that must be considered in every child with fever and a change in behavior, and the classic signs are often absent in the youngest. The soft spot on the top of the head may be bulging in an infant, the neck may resist flexion, the child may cry when handled or dislike the light, and a seizure or a drowsiness may be the only sign. The rash that does not fade with pressure is the finding that must be sought over the whole body, because it may mark an infection of the bloodstream that progresses within hours. Where a lumbar puncture can be done it answers the question directly, but it is deferred when the child is unstable, when there are signs of raised pressure inside the head, or when the skin over the site is infected, and treatment then begins on the clinical picture while the test waits. Where a computed tomography, or CT, scanner exists it is used to answer the question of raised pressure before the needle is placed, and where it does not, the decision is made on the examination and on the judgment of the senior clinician who has been called. The level of consciousness is recorded with the Glasgow coma scale, which formalizes the response to voice, to pain, and to command, and the exact contents of that scale and the figure at which it should cause alarm must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the version in use varies between services and between editions. A child who has a seizure for the first time, whose seizure lasts a long time, or who does not return to his usual self afterward needs treatment and senior help at once.

Vomiting and diarrhea are the complaints that most often bring a child to a clinic, and most of the time they settle by themselves, but a small number of patterns demand immediate action. Green, bile-stained vomiting in a young infant is a warning of an obstruction of the bowel and not of a simple feeding problem, and it must be treated as an emergency until it is explained. A child who draws his legs up and screams in waves, who passes a stool mixed with blood and mucus, or in whom a sausage-shaped mass can be felt in the abdomen may have an intussusception, in which one part of the bowel has slid into the next, and that diagnosis is made on suspicion and confirmed by an ultrasound where one is available. Pain that begins around the navel and settles in the right lower abdomen, with tenderness there, a refusal to walk, and a rising pulse, suggests appendicitis, and the examination is repeated rather than trusted once, because the signs often take hours to declare themselves. Distension of the abdomen, absent bowel sounds, and a tender lump in the groin or at the umbilicus each ask a different question, and the whole abdomen is examined gently, with the child's face watched rather than the examiner's hands. A rectal examination is performed only when it will change the decision, and the genitalia are inspected in every child with abdominal pain, because a torsion of the testis is a surgical emergency that can present as abdominal pain alone.

Some children are brought with nothing that a first look can explain, and the value of the assessment is largely in deciding who may go home and who may not. A child who looks well, drinks, plays, and has no danger sign may still carry an infection of the urine, the ear, the throat, or the lung, so the ear is examined with the torch, the throat is looked at, the chest is listened to, and a sample of urine is obtained before a course of treatment is considered. A sample of urine collected in a bag is useful when it is normal, because a clean result makes an infection of the urine unlikely, while a contaminated one settles nothing, and a sample taken directly from the bladder or after careful cleansing answers the question better. The child who is failing to grow, who tires during feeding, who sweats on the head while feeding, or who breathes fast at rest may have a heart lesion rather than an infection, and the cluster of findings is a murmur together with an enlarged liver, a fast pulse, and a difference between the pulses felt in the arm and in the leg. The child with a limp, a warm swollen joint, or a refusal to bear weight on a leg must be examined for an infection in the joint or the bone, because that is a surgical emergency, and a tumor of the bone belongs in the same list. The child who has been well and then has a long fever with a falling weight, a swelling of the neck, or a dark patch on the skin needs a test for tuberculosis and for a cancer of the blood, because both imitate ordinary illness and both are easier to treat when they are found early.

In a pediatric ward the assessment is the same, but it is written in a chart, repeated at set hours, and supported by tests that answer specific questions. The nurse counts the breaths and the pulse and records the trend, the laboratory measures the blood count, the salts, and the sugar, the laboratory grows the organism from the blood, the urine, or the fluid around the brain, and a chest radiograph shows the lung beneath the place where the sound was dull. An ultrasound answers the question of a collection of pus or of an obstruction, and a CT or a magnetic resonance imaging, or MRI, answers the question of the brain when the signs point there. In a village with a mid-level provider and a single register, none of those tools may exist, and the reasoning must carry more weight rather than less. The register is not a lesser instrument, since it holds the weight of every child seen, the immunization given, the treatment started, and the date of the next visit, and a line of weights falling across the page makes malnutrition visible before a single measurement looks alarming. The provider counts the breaths with a watch, looks in the ear and the throat with the torch, presses the skin for a fold and the feet for edema, and then decides among the plans available, which are to treat and review, to refer now, or to send home with clear instructions about what should bring the child back. The decision to refer is a diagnosis in its own right when the tools are absent, and the provider who names the danger sign, writes it in the register, and arranges the journey has done the most important part of the work.

A constructed illustration may make these habits concrete, and it is offered as a teaching device rather than as a portrait of any real child. Imagine a child of a few months brought to a village clinic with a cough, a fever, and a refusal to feed, whose breathing is counted twice at 60 breaths per minute, whose lower ribs are drawn in with every breath, and in whom the provider sees the effort and arranges the journey to a hospital at once, even though the clinic has no oxygen and no radiograph. That count and the indrawing are the findings that make the referral right, and the figure itself, like every other figure in this chapter, must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the line that separates fast from too fast is drawn differently in different guides. Now imagine a child in a pediatric ward whose weight has fallen on the chart over 3 weeks, a trend whose meaning depends on the chart in use and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, whose feet hold a pitting swelling, and whose temperature is low rather than high, and in whom the search for infection finds nothing until the whole body is examined again. Neither child is real, and neither story should be applied to a real patient without a full assessment, yet the two share the move that matters, which is that the clinician looked for the danger sign first, and the finding rather than the diagnosis decided the next step.

To summarize, the child is assessed against the developmental expectations of his age, because the normal is what makes the abnormal visible, and then against the small number of illnesses that kill. The dangerous possibilities are named where the child is seen and not deferred, and they are severe pneumonia with fast breathing and chest indrawing, meningitis with a change in behavior and the rash that does not fade, severe dehydration with sunken eyes and a slow skin fold, severe malnutrition with edema and an infection that shows no fever, and the child who has gone quiet when he should be crying. The integrated management of childhood illness gives the order of the questions, and the same order holds in a pediatric ward with a laboratory and in a village with a mid-level provider and a single register, where the register itself becomes a diagnostic tool as the line of weights falls. Every figure in this chapter, whether a temperature, a breathing rate, a measurement, a score, an age, or a duration, is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such numbers differ between countries, between editions, and between the charts and the laboratories in use. This chapter is educational material and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service. A child who is quiet, who cannot feed, or whose caregiver is frightened deserves another look, and the finding that changes the plan is usually the one that was sought in the first minute.
