# Chapter 2 — Cognitive Biases and Diagnostic Error

Diagnostic error is not the same thing as ignorance, and the difference matters more than any list of mistakes. Ignorance is the absence of knowledge, and it is cured slowly by study. Error is the failure of a process that worked well enough to feel correct, which is why it survives study and returns in a new shape. A clinician who knows the sign, looks for it, finds it and still reaches the wrong conclusion has failed in reasoning, and will repeat that failure until the reasoning itself is examined. This book is written for that second failure, because it is the one that follows the experienced clinician home.

The beginner and the expert do not fail in the same way, and the difference is not simply one of degree. The beginner fails through absence, because the wrong question is not asked, the relevant history is not taken, and the finding is not sought. The expert fails through the very patterns that made the expert, because a mind that has seen many patients recognizes a shape quickly and then arranges the evidence around it. Both are errors, but only the expert's arrives with a feeling of certainty attached.

Anchoring is the name for the moment when a first impression becomes the frame inside which every later fact is placed. The anchor is often reasonable, because the common disease is common and the first version of the story usually points somewhere sensible. The trouble is what happens next, because once the anchor is set the questions that follow are no longer neutral. The clinician asks about the features that fit the anchor and skips the features that would break it, and the examination drifts toward the parts of the body the anchor has already chosen. By the end of the encounter the record looks like careful work and reads as a single argument for the first idea.

An early label given by someone else is even more powerful, because it arrives with authority and without the reasoning that produced it. A referral that opens with the word pneumonia tells the receiving clinician where to listen, what to look for and which alternative to leave unexamined. The patient also learns the label and begins to describe the illness in its language, so the original guess returns to the clinician as independent confirmation. A constructed illustration makes the shape of it clear: a man is sent to hospital with a note saying gastritis, and for the next several shifts nobody asks about the chest pain that comes with effort, because the note has settled the question.

Premature closure is the moment the search stops because a plausible answer has arrived. Plausible is the operative word, since the answer need not be probable, only satisfying. A diagnosis that explains the main symptom and fits the patient's age and habits can feel complete long before the dangerous alternative has been considered. The remedy is not more knowledge but a deliberate refusal to close while a lethal possibility remains unexcluded, which is an act of discipline rather than of memory.

Every presentation carries at least one diagnosis that must be excluded before the comfortable one is accepted, and the experienced clinician names it early rather than late. In a patient with chest discomfort the possibility that must be excluded is a problem in the heart or the great vessels, and the finding that would reveal it is sought in the first minutes rather than after the ward round. In a patient with headache the possibilities that must be excluded are bleeding and infection inside the head, and the questions about sudden onset, fever and neck stiffness belong at the start. It means that the comfortable diagnosis is accepted only after the uncomfortable one has been looked for and not found.

Availability is the tendency to judge how likely a diagnosis is by how easily an example comes to mind. Memory is not a fair sample of disease, and it does not try to be, because it keeps what was vivid, recent, frightening or personally costly. A clinician who has just cared for a patient with a rare clot in the lung will see that clot in the next several breathless patients, and a clinician who has just been humiliated by a missed infection will over-investigate the next patient with a fever. The last dramatic patient therefore distorts the next ordinary one.

A constructed illustration of this is easy to picture. A clinic spends a difficult week with several patients who turn out to have a serious tropical infection, and by the following week every patient with a fever is being assessed for it. Meanwhile the ordinary bacterial infection that is far more common in that place and season is treated late, because it has become uninteresting to the person doing the thinking. Availability is not corrected by resolving to be fairer, because the clinician cannot feel the cases that are missing. It is corrected by looking at what the local data actually show, and by asking what an ordinary week in this clinic usually contains.

Confirmation bias is the tendency to seek and to notice the evidence that supports the leading hypothesis while passing lightly over the evidence that threatens it. It operates in the questions and in the observation at the same time. The clinician asks about weight loss because the leading hypothesis predicts weight loss, and forgets to ask about the night sweats that would point elsewhere. On examination, the finding that fits is written down and the finding that does not is described as equivocal, or simply not recorded. This is why the same patient can be examined by different competent clinicians and yield sets of findings that are each internally consistent.

The remedy for confirmation bias is to look deliberately for the finding that would make the leading hypothesis wrong. This is a different habit from looking for more support, and it feels uncomfortable because it works against the momentum of the encounter. If the leading hypothesis is an infection of the urinary tract, the clinician should ask what would be found if the truth were something else entirely, and then check that. If the leading hypothesis is a benign headache, the clinician should ask which single feature, if present, would change the plan, and then hunt for that feature specifically. This is not doubt for its own sake; it is the cheapest form of safety available at the bedside.

Search satisficing is the failure to keep looking once the first abnormality has been found. A patient falls, a fracture is seen on the image, and the search stops there, though a fall in an older person often has a second explanation and the fracture may be the consequence rather than the cause. A patient has one abnormal laboratory value that explains the presentation, and the other abnormal values on the same report are never examined. After finding the first abnormality, the clinician should look once more through the whole of the available information before deciding that the search is complete.

Base-rate neglect is the tendency to weigh the impressive features of a case more heavily than the ordinary frequency of the disease in the population in front of the clinician. The rare diagnosis is more interesting, more memorable and more flattering to the person who makes it, and none of those qualities has anything to do with probability. A student who has just learned about an uncommon disease will see it everywhere, and a clinician working where a common infection is rampant may still reach first for the exotic explanation. The common disease in an unusual dress is far more frequent than the rare disease in its classical dress. The first question about any presentation is not what could cause it, but what usually causes it here, in this season, in this patient.

A constructed illustration of base-rate neglect is worth carrying in mind. Consider a pair of patients who arrive with the same ordinary complaint of fever and cough. One is diagnosed with a rare tropical infection because the clinician has just read about it, and the other is diagnosed with the common respiratory infection that is going around the ward. If the rare disease occurs in a very small number of people each year in that district and the common one in a very large number, then the unimpressive diagnosis is the more likely one in almost every patient who walks through the door. This does not excuse missing the rare disease, but it means the rare diagnosis must be sought for a reason found in the patient rather than in the clinician's reading.

Framing is the effect of the words in which the problem arrives. The same set of facts, described as a patient who is anxious and breathing quickly, leads the next clinician toward one set of questions and away from another; described as a patient with sudden shortness of breath and a fast pulse, it leads elsewhere. The referral letter chooses the frame, sometimes deliberately and usually not, because the person writing it has already performed an act of diagnosis and can only report what that act produced. Handover does the same thing in speech, and a tired clinician at the end of a shift transmits a summary rather than a set of raw findings. The practical response is to ask for the observations behind the label, and to look at the patient before reading the note.

Framing is not corrected by ignoring the referral, because the referral contains real information and the person who wrote it has usually seen more of the patient than the receiver will. It is corrected by separating what was observed from what was concluded. When a colleague says that a patient looks septic, the useful question is which findings produced that impression, and when a note says that the chest is clear, the useful question is who examined it and how. The frame is then kept as one opinion among several rather than as the shape of the whole case.

Overconfidence is the mismatch between the certainty a clinician feels and the accuracy actually achieved. Certainty is a feeling and not a measurement, and it is generated by fluency, because a diagnosis that comes to mind easily feels true while one that has to be assembled feels doubtful. Clinicians are usually better at ranking possibilities than at estimating probabilities, and they are often worse at estimating their own error rate than at estimating anything else. The dangerous zone is not the case that feels difficult, since a difficult case summons care, but the case that feels easy and is not. Calibration, the habit of comparing confidence with outcomes, is learned only by looking back at what actually happened.

The absence of feedback is one of the deepest problems in clinical reasoning, because a wrong conclusion that is never corrected is stored as a right one. A clinician who discharges a patient with a benign diagnosis and never learns that the patient returned with something serious will continue to feel that the original judgment was sound. This is why units that track their own errors and review their own misses improve, while units that do not, do not, even when the individuals within them are equally able. Every clinician should therefore know how a patient who was sent home actually fared, and should make it easy for that information to arrive. A system that hides the outcome is manufacturing overconfidence one patient at a time.

Hindsight bias is the tendency, after an outcome is known, to believe that the outcome was visible all along and that any competent person would have seen it. The record is read backwards, and findings that were ambiguous at the time become obvious warnings, while the alternatives that were reasonable then are quietly forgotten. The clinician who missed the diagnosis is judged by a standard that nobody could have met at the bedside, with the answer in hand and unlimited time. The defense is to review decisions in the order the information actually arrived, and to ask what a reasonable clinician could have known at each point.

Not every diagnostic failure is a cognitive error, and the distinction decides what should be fixed. A result that was never returned, a message that was never delivered, a handover interrupted before the important sentence, a chart that cannot be read and a patient moved between wards without the notes are failures of the system, and the clinician may have reasoned perfectly inside a process that lost the information. The clinician who reasons well inside a system that leaks information will still produce a wrong diagnosis, and no amount of personal vigilance will repair the leak. Judging the two together is how units end up punishing individuals for the failures of their tools.

Blaming the individual alone fixes nothing, and often makes the next error more likely. If the response to a missed result is a reprimand, then the next clinician will be slower to report a near miss, and the unit will lose the information it needs. Punishment drives error underground, where it cannot be studied, and it teaches everyone to protect themselves rather than the patient. A just response separates the person from the process, holding people accountable for concealment and for reckless disregard while treating ordinary human fallibility as a property of the system to be designed around. The question after an error is not who is at fault but what made this outcome possible and what would make it less likely next time.

The first debiasing move available at the bedside is to generate alternatives deliberately, before the leading hypothesis has hardened. This is not the same as listing every disease that could cause the symptom, which is an exercise in memory rather than in reasoning. It means producing more than one plausible alternative with different consequences, so that the plan must accommodate more than one world. The list should be made before the tests are ordered, because a test ordered after the decision is usually a test chosen to protect the decision. A list of possibilities built in this way is short, ranked and useful, and it can be revised without embarrassment.

The question that does most of the work is the plain one: what else could this be? It is worth asking aloud, and worth asking early, because it changes the clinician's posture from defending an answer to testing one. The next question is which of the alternatives would be most dangerous if it were true, and whether anything has yet been done to exclude it. In a patient with abdominal pain, for example, the dangerous alternatives include a perforated bowel, a vascular emergency and a pregnancy outside the uterus, and each of these has a question or a finding that speaks to it. The purpose is not to investigate all of them, but to know which ones have been considered and to have a reason for setting each one aside.

The previous question becomes operational through a pair of habits. The first is the search for the finding that would disprove the leading hypothesis, which requires the clinician to name in advance what would change the plan. The second is the diagnostic time-out, a deliberate pause before the decision is committed, in which the case is restated from the beginning as though it had just arrived. A time-out costs little and is best performed aloud, because speech exposes gaps that silent review smooths over. Done properly, it asks a short set of questions: what is the leading diagnosis, what is the greatest danger, and what would make me change my mind.

Cognitive forcing strategies are methods that make the mind slow down at the moment it would prefer to leap. One is to state the diagnosis together with the evidence that supports it and the evidence that argues against it, so that the reasoning is exposed rather than implied. Another is to consider the opposite of the first impression, asking what the patient would look like if the leading diagnosis were wrong, and then checking whether the patient looks like that. Another is to write the list of possibilities down before seeing the result of any test, so that the result is interpreted rather than obeyed. Each of these costs only the willingness to be uncomfortable for a moment, and the discomfort is the point, because a reasoning process that never feels resisted is usually not being tested.

A checklist has a place in diagnosis when a case is hard, and its value lies in what it forces the clinician to consider rather than in what it concludes. A useful diagnostic checklist is short, and it asks about the things most often omitted under pressure, including the vital signs, the full set of observations, the medicines, the allergies, the pregnancy status where relevant, the possibility of infection and the possibility of a vascular event. It should also instruct the clinician to ask what is missing from the story, and to confirm that the results which were ordered have actually been seen. Used in the right place, however, it catches the omissions that expert reasoning is most likely to make.

Asking a colleague is the most underused debiasing strategy, and often the most effective. The value is not that the colleague knows more, though sometimes that too, but that the colleague does not share the anchor. A fresh listener hears the story without the first impression, and asks the obvious question that has become invisible to the clinician who has been inside the case for hours. The request should be specific, not a general plea for help but a short statement of the leading diagnosis, the reason for doubt and the question that needs answering. No clinician should be embarrassed to ask, and units should make such conversations easy rather than heroic.

Individual habits are strongest when the system supports them, and weakest when they depend on the character of one tired person at the end of a long shift. A unit that expects a second opinion in defined situations, that schedules time for difficult cases and that treats a request for help as ordinary will get more second opinions than a unit that leaves the matter to individual courage. The same is true of the diagnostic time-out and of handover, because if they are not part of the routine they happen only when someone remembers, and someone remembers least often when the shift is worst. The design of the working day is therefore part of the reasoning, and it can be changed.

When an error has occurred, the honest disclosure of it to the patient and to the team is part of the clinical work rather than an administrative aftermath. Patients generally want to know what happened, what it means for them and what will be done now, and they are entitled to that. The conversation is difficult because it requires the clinician to say plainly that a mistake was made, without hiding behind the passive voice or behind an explanation that blames the process in order to avoid the person. Disclosure protects the patient first and the clinician second, and it is the only version of the story that can be acted upon.

An apology is not an admission of incompetence, and its absence is usually felt by the patient as a second injury. Saying that the clinician is sorry this happened, and that the patient was not treated as well as they should have been, costs nothing and repairs a great deal. It must be followed by an account of what will change, because an apology that leads to no change is heard as an attempt to close the subject. A unit that handles these matters well is one in which errors surface early, and early errors are smaller errors.

A book about reasoning has to teach the failure modes alongside the skills, because the skills are learned quickly and the failure modes are not. Every clinician is taught how to take a history, and almost none is taught how the history will be distorted by the first idea. The rules of evidence, the meaning of a sign and the interpretation of a test can all be acquired from a text and improved by practice. The biases cannot, because they are part of the equipment that does the learning, and they operate whether or not the clinician believes in them. The only reliable defense is to know their names, to recognize their arrival in the middle of an encounter, and to have a habit ready that interrupts them.

Reasoning is a craft, and every craft is taught with its failure modes attached. Medicine has been slower than others to teach its own, partly because the stakes make the errors painful to discuss and partly because they feel like personal defects rather than like properties of a mind. Naming them changes that, and turns a private shame into a shared object that a team can work on. The clinician who can say that the case was anchored, or that the search was satisfied too early, is describing a process rather than confessing a character. That vocabulary is the beginning of correction, and it is also the beginning of teaching.

The habits described here are small, and they are meant to be small, because a strategy that requires an hour will not be used in a clinic that has only a few minutes for each patient. Each of them fits into the encounter as it already happens, as a question asked aloud, a pause before the decision, a sentence written before the test is ordered, or a colleague consulted at the door. What matters is that the habit exists before the difficult case arrives, because the difficult case is exactly the one in which the clinician will not think of it. Debiasing is a practice in both senses of the word, since it is done repeatedly and it is done professionally.

Numbers enter the reasoning at every point, and each of them is local. A temperature of 38 degrees Celsius is called a fever in one protocol and investigated from a different value in the next, so that figure must be checked against the current edition of the guideline and against the local protocol before it decides anything. A pulse of 110 beats per minute is comfortable in a young athlete and alarming in a patient who takes a medicine that slows the heart, so the threshold has to be confirmed in the local protocol and in the current guideline that governs the ward. A systolic pressure of 90 millimeters of mercury is a decision point in some settings and an ordinary finding in others, and it too must be verified against the current guideline and the local protocol, which differ between countries, editions and hospitals.

Other measurements behave in the same way, and each belongs to the place where it was made. A fluid loss of 2 liters means one thing in a patient who is already dry and another in a patient whose kidneys are failing, and the value that prompts action varies with the guideline and the local protocol in force. A history of 3 days is a short illness in one clinic and a long one in another, depending on the local pattern of disease, so it must be read against the current local guidance rather than against a memory of a textbook. A frequency of 1 in 4 patients may be quoted for a complication in a specialist center and be much lower or higher in a district hospital, so that figure requires confirmation from the local data and the current protocol before it shapes a decision.

Reference ranges are the clearest example of a number that cannot be carried between places. A laboratory range for a blood count or a liver enzyme is derived from its own population, measured on its own machines and reported in its own units, so a value that is normal in one hospital can be abnormal in another. The clinician should therefore read the range printed beside the result rather than the range remembered from training, and should confirm it against the current reporting standard in local use. The same caution applies to any score, because a score is a formalized piece of reasoning and its cut-off is a decision about a particular population, so the score must be checked against the current guideline and against the population in which it was derived. The listener should treat every score as a question to the local source, not as an answer.

To summarize, the clinician's task is not to avoid having a mind that takes shortcuts, because no such mind exists, but to build habits that catch the shortcuts before they reach the patient. Anchor the case deliberately and then test the anchor, keep the search open until the dangerous possibilities have been excluded, generate alternatives before the tests are ordered, ask what would disprove the leading idea, pause for a time-out when the case is hard, and ask a colleague without embarrassment. Treat every number, cut-off, pressure, temperature, rate and reference range as a local fact that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol before it is acted upon, because these values differ between countries, editions, laboratories and wards. Separate the cognitive error from the system failure, because blaming the individual alone repairs neither, and disclose what went wrong to the patient and to the team. None of this replaces training, supervision or the judgment of the clinician who is with the patient, and none of it is a licence to delay care in an emergency. The aim is a reasoning process that is honest about its own weaknesses, and a clinician who is therefore safer than confidence alone would make him or her.
