# Chapter 17 — Head, Eyes, Ears, Nose, Throat and Mouth

The head, the eyes, the ears, the nose, the throat, and the mouth form the one region of the body that a clinician can inspect almost completely with nothing more than light and a willingness to look closely, and that accessibility is the reasoning that organizes this chapter. Because these structures are open to direct vision, much of the diagnostic information is available at once, before any instrument is unfolded and before any test is ordered. The same openness is also a trap, because a visible abnormality is convincing and a clinician may stop at the first thing seen rather than ask what lies behind it. This chapter therefore treats the region in two ways at once, as a set of surfaces to be described precisely and as a set of doorways into spaces that cannot be seen, the orbit, the middle ear, the sinuses, the deep neck, and the intracranial cavity. What follows is educational material only, and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the local protocol that governs your own setting. Every finding described here must be interpreted in the whole patient, and no examination of this region is complete until the clinician has decided whether the patient is safe to send home.

The value of inspecting this region directly is that the eye perceives pattern, color, symmetry, and movement at a glance, and these are exactly the qualities that separate one disease from another at the bedside. Redness, swelling, discharge, ulceration, and a change in the surface of a membrane are all visible without magnification, and their distribution often matters more than their mere presence. A clinician who describes what is seen in plain words, rather than reaching immediately for a diagnosis, keeps the observation available for reinterpretation when new information arrives. In a well-resourced hospital the examination continues into an ophthalmoscope and an otoscope, and from there into imaging and laboratory work. In a clinic with a torch, a spoon, and a thermometer, the same reasoning is performed with less magnification, and the clinician must be more deliberate about what can and cannot be concluded. The principle is identical in both rooms: look, describe, compare the two sides, and then ask what the appearance would mean if the dangerous explanation were true.

The eye is where several dangerous diagnoses hide behind a common complaint, and the clinician should name them early. A painful red eye with a fixed, mid-dilated pupil, a hazy cornea, and a globe that feels stony hard on gentle palpation through the closed lid suggests acute glaucoma, in which the pressure inside the eye rises sharply and sight can be lost quickly if the problem is not recognized. A foreign body, especially one that struck at speed, may sit on the cornea or lie hidden under the upper lid, and the finding that reveals it is a careful eversion of that lid in good light. A corneal ulcer is a defect of the surface, often with surrounding haze, and it is dangerous because it can perforate or scar the eye. A chemical injury is an emergency of a different order, and the question that changes everything is what substance entered the eye and when. In each of these situations the clinician's task is to recognize, protect the eye, and arrange urgent review by someone with the right instruments, because the eye can be lost faster than almost any other organ in this region.

A red eye is among the most common presentations in any clinic, and most of the time it is self-limiting, which is exactly why the dangerous minority is missed. The features that point away from simple surface inflammation are severe pain rather than mild irritation, a marked reduction in vision, a pupil that is unequal or sluggish, a cornea that looks cloudy rather than clear, and tenderness when the globe is touched through the lid. Discharge is also informative, because a watery discharge with itching suggests allergy, a purulent discharge suggests a bacterial surface infection, and a discharge with a tender node in front of the ear suggests a viral cause. Photophobia, which is pain in bright light, warns that the inflammation involves deeper structures rather than the surface alone. A clinician who examines both eyes, compares them, and tests the vision of each eye separately will rarely be caught by the dangerous red eye. When the history includes a foreign body, a chemical, a contact lens, or recent eye surgery, the threshold for urgent referral falls at once.

The pupil is examined in a dim room, with the patient looking at a distant object so that the near response does not confuse the picture. The clinician notes the size, the shape, and the briskness of the reaction to light and to accommodation in each eye, and then compares one side with the other. A pupil that is large and fixed suggests that the structures controlling it are being compressed or that the eye itself is under pressure, while a pupil that is small and unreactive may point to a problem deeper in the brain or to the effect of a substance. The swinging torch test, in which light is moved from one eye to the other, is valuable because it detects a weakness of one optic nerve that examining each eye on its own can miss. An eyelid that droops, a pupil that is small on the same side, and a failure of sweating on that side of the face together suggest a lesion along a specific nerve pathway, and that combination deserves urgent neurological attention. A pupil that is changing is often more important than a pupil that is abnormal, so the finding must be recorded in the words used to find it.

Visual acuity is tested one eye at a time, with the other eye covered and with the patient's own glasses in place, because acuity measured with both eyes open is a different measurement. In a fully equipped clinic the patient reads a standard chart at the correct distance, and the result is recorded for each eye. In a clinic with a torch and no chart, the clinician asks the patient to read the smallest available print, to count fingers held at a short distance, and finally to say whether light is perceived and from which direction it comes. A patient who can perceive light but cannot see a hand moving in front of the face is in a very different position from one whose acuity is merely reduced. Visual fields are tested by confrontation, with the clinician sitting opposite and bringing a moving finger from the periphery towards the center while the patient keeps looking straight ahead. Comparing the two sides, and comparing the upper with the lower half of the field, turns a crude test into a useful one, because a defect that respects a boundary tells the clinician where in the visual pathway the problem lies.

Sudden loss of vision in one eye must never be managed by observation alone. A painless loss that comes on abruptly, with a pale retina and a cherry-red spot at the center of the macula, suggests that the blood supply to the retina has been interrupted, and the underlying cause may lie in the heart or in the arteries of the neck. The same painless loss in an older patient, especially with a tender and thickened scalp artery, jaw pain on chewing, or a headache of a new kind, raises the possibility of inflammation of the cranial arteries, which threatens the other eye and requires urgent assessment. Loss accompanied by floaters, flashes, or a curtain drawn across the field suggests bleeding into the vitreous or a detachment of the retina, and both need prompt specialist review. Loss with pain, redness, and a cloudy cornea points back towards the eye itself. Because these causes carry different consequences and different urgencies, the clinician should record the speed of onset, whether the loss was complete or partial, and whether any recovery has occurred.

The ear is examined from the outside in, and the reasoning follows the same path. The clinician looks at the pinna and the skin behind it, presses gently over the mastoid bone, and moves the pinna to see whether movement causes pain, because tenderness in these places separates infection of the outer canal from infection of the middle ear. The canal is then inspected with an otoscope where one is available, and with a torch and a simple speculum where it is not, noting discharge, swelling, debris, and whether the drum can be seen at all. A drum that is red, bulging, and immobile suggests a middle ear infection, while a drum that is dull, retracted, and scarred suggests old or chronic disease. A temperature of 38 degrees Celsius may be called a fever in one service and not in another, so confirm the threshold against the current guideline and the local protocol before resting any decision on it.

Hearing is tested first by voice, because that is how the patient lives, and then by tuning fork, because that is how the clinician localizes the problem. The clinician stands behind the patient, masks the non-testing ear, and speaks words at a distance, comparing the two ears and comparing the result with what the patient reports in daily life. A tuning fork struck and held against the bone behind the ear, and then held in the air beside the canal, tells the clinician whether the loss comes from a problem conducting sound to the inner ear or from a problem within the inner ear and its nerve. In a conductive loss the sound is heard better through bone than through air, and it localizes to the affected ear when the fork is placed on the forehead. In a sensorineural loss the sound is heard better through air, and it localizes to the better ear. The exact intervals and frequencies used in such testing are conventional rather than universal, so confirm them against the current guideline and the local protocol, because practice varies between services and editions. Sudden hearing loss in one ear, especially with dizziness or ringing, is a reason for urgent referral rather than reassurance.

A discharging ear becomes dangerous when the infection leaves the middle ear and spreads into the bone or the intracranial cavity. Persistent discharge with pain, fever, and swelling or redness behind the ear, and a pinna that is pushed forward, suggests that the infection has reached the mastoid bone and needs urgent assessment. Discharge lasting 2 weeks or more despite treatment deserves specialist review, though the interval that counts as persistent varies between services, so check it against the current guideline and the local protocol. A headache, neck stiffness, drowsiness, or a seizure in a patient with longstanding ear disease suggests that the infection has spread inwards, and the finding that reveals it may be entirely outside the ear. Facial weakness on the same side as a discharging ear indicates that the inflammation is involving the nerve that runs through the temporal bone, and it converts an ordinary ear complaint into an emergency. A cholesteatoma, which is a growing collection of skin debris behind the drum, can erode bone silently and present only when a complication appears.

The nose and the sinuses are examined by looking, by palpating, and by asking about the character of the discharge and the timing of the symptoms. The clinician inspects the outside of the nose, presses gently over the frontal and maxillary sinuses, and uses a torch to look inside, noting the color of the mucosa, polyps, a deviated septum, or a mass. A clear discharge with sneezing and itching suggests allergy, a thick purulent discharge with facial pain and pressure suggests sinus infection, and blood-stained discharge from one nostril in an adult deserves direct inspection of the nasal cavity. Nosebleeds are common and usually arise from a small vessel at the front of the septum, where pressure and local measures settle them, but a bleed that pours into the throat, that continues despite first measures, or that occurs in an older patient with hardened arteries or a disorder of clotting is a different problem and may require packing by a specialist. Sinus pain alone, without any supporting finding, is a poor guide, because the sinuses are small and their pain overlaps with that of the teeth, the eyes, and the temples.

The complications of sinus infection are rare but severe, and they are the reason a clinician should not treat every sinus complaint as trivial. Swelling around the eye, a red eye, pain on moving the eye, double vision, or a pupil that does not react suggests that infection has spread from the sinuses into the orbit, and this requires urgent imaging and specialist care. Severe headache, fever, drowsiness, or a seizure in the same setting suggests spread into the intracranial cavity, and the finding may be a change in consciousness rather than anything visible in the nose. A patient whose immune defenses are weakened, or whose diabetes is poorly controlled, is at greater risk of these complications, and the threshold for referral should be lower. The clinician should also remember that not all facial pain is sinus pain, since disease of the teeth, the jaw joint, and the nerves of the face all produce pain in the same region. When the diagnosis is uncertain and the patient is not improving, the safer course is review rather than a repetition of the same assumption.

The throat is the part of this region where the most rapidly lethal diagnoses occur, and they must be named at the bedside rather than after a delay. A patient who is drooling, sitting forward, speaking with a muffled voice, and making a high-pitched noise on breathing may have an infection of the epiglottis that can close the airway without warning, and the clinician must not attempt to examine the throat forcibly in such a patient. A retropharyngeal abscess may present with fever, neck stiffness, difficulty swallowing, and a bulging swelling behind the back of the throat, and it can obstruct the airway or spread into the chest. A peritonsillar abscess, by contrast, usually pushes the tonsil and the soft palate to one side and causes a hot-potato voice, and although it is less immediately lethal it still needs drainage and specialist assessment. In each of these cases the priority is the airway and the arrangement of safe transfer, not the completion of a routine examination. The finding that matters most is how the patient is breathing while sitting still, because a patient who is calm when undisturbed can still be at the edge of obstruction.

Sore throat is far more often a viral illness than a bacterial one, and the reasoning that separates them is a study in the difference between likelihood and danger. The features that raise the probability of a bacterial cause include fever, tender and enlarged nodes in the neck, pus on the tonsils, and the absence of a cough, and clinical rules have been built to combine these into a score. Any such score must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol before it is used, because the weight of each feature and the recommended action differ between countries, between editions, and between the clinic and the hospital. The clinical question is not whether bacteria are present but whether treating them would change the outcome, and that is why the decision to use an antibiotic rests on the balance of the findings rather than on the appearance of the throat alone. The features that make antibiotic treatment clearly the wrong answer include a dry cough, a hoarse voice, and a runny nose without fever, because these point to a viral cause. The features that make it clearly the right answer in a high-risk patient include a severe illness with difficulty swallowing and a suspected deep collection of pus, which requires drainage rather than a prescription.

The mouth is examined last only by convention, and it is often where the most useful information is found. The clinician needs good light, a tongue depressor or the handle of a clean spoon, and a moment of the patient's cooperation, and then inspects the lips, the corners of the mouth, the inner surface of the cheeks, the gums, the teeth, the floor of the mouth under the tongue, the tongue itself, the palate, and the back of the throat. The examination is incomplete unless the tongue is lifted and the floor of the mouth is seen, because that is where a cancer often begins, and unless both sides are compared, because asymmetry is frequently the first sign. The clinician should note the color of the mucosa, the moisture, and the presence of ulcers, white patches, bleeding, swelling, or an odour, and should record where each finding sits and how large it appears. A mouth that is dry, a tongue that is smooth and red, and cracked corners of the mouth together suggest a nutritional or systemic problem rather than a local one. The mouth is examined with gloves and with the patient's consent, and the clinician explains what is being looked for so that the patient can help.

An ulcer in the mouth is common and usually harmless, but the features that separate the harmless from the dangerous are worth remembering. A minor ulcer is small, shallow, round, and painful, heals on its own, and recurs in a recognizable pattern as part of a recurrent aphthous illness. An ulcer that is large, irregular, indurated, painless, or persistent, or one that sits at the side or the floor of the mouth in an older patient who smokes or drinks heavily, must be regarded as a possible cancer until proven otherwise, and it requires urgent specialist review. A single ulcer that does not heal after an obvious local cause has been removed, or that bleeds on touch, is the finding that should end watchful waiting. Ulcers that come with fever, weight loss, or lesions elsewhere on the body suggest a systemic cause, and ulcers on the genitalia or the eyes point to a condition that affects more than the mouth. Any ulcer in a patient whose immune defenses are suppressed deserves a lower threshold for investigation, and any ulcer surrounded by a white or red patch that cannot be wiped away deserves a biopsy. The clinician should record the duration, the size, and the exact site, because a described ulcer can be followed while a vague one cannot.

White patches in the mouth are a common finding, and the reasoning lies in whether the patch can be wiped away. Thrush, which is an overgrowth of a yeast, produces white patches that can be scraped off and leave a red, sometimes bleeding surface beneath, and it is common in infants, in people who use inhaled medication, in those who wear dentures, and in anyone whose immune defenses are weakened. A white patch that cannot be wiped away is a different problem, because it may be a pre-malignant change and needs specialist assessment, particularly when it sits on the side of the tongue or the floor of the mouth. Oral thrush in an adult who is otherwise well should prompt the clinician to think about an underlying condition, including infection with the human immunodeficiency virus, or HIV, as well as poorly controlled diabetes and diseases that suppress the immune system. The mouth may also show the first signs of a systemic illness, with pallor of the mucosa suggesting anemia, a smooth red tongue suggesting a vitamin deficiency, and bleeding gums suggesting a disorder of the blood or of the platelets. A patient with unexplained oral thrush, weight loss, and enlarged lymph nodes needs a broader assessment rather than a repeated local treatment.

The teeth and the gums are a source of infection that is easily overlooked and occasionally lethal. Dental decay can extend into the pulp of a tooth and then into the bone of the jaw, producing pain, swelling, fever, and difficulty opening the mouth, and the infection can then track downwards into the spaces of the neck. A patient with a swollen face or jaw, a stiff neck, a muffled voice, difficulty swallowing, and a fever may have an infection that has reached the deep spaces of the neck, and this is a surgical emergency because it can obstruct the airway or spread to the chest. The finding that should raise immediate concern is swelling that is firm and spreading under the jaw or down the neck, especially with drooling or difficulty breathing, and such a patient should not be sent home with a prescription. The clinician should inspect the teeth for cavities, the gums for bleeding and recession, and the floor of the mouth for swelling, and should feel the neck for tenderness and crackling under the skin.

The tongue and the mucosa are read as a mirror of the body, and each change carries a limited set of explanations. A tongue that is smooth, red, and sore suggests loss of the normal papillae, which may follow a nutritional deficiency or a chronic illness, while a tongue covered with a thick white coat may reflect poor oral hygiene or a dry mouth. A tongue that deviates to one side when it is protruded, or that shows wasting and fine irregular movements, points to a problem in the nervous system rather than in the mouth, and it should be examined alongside speech, swallowing, and movement of the palate. A tongue that looks blue in a patient who is short of breath may reflect a low level of oxygen in the blood, and that is a sign to look at the whole patient rather than at the tongue alone. Pale mucosa, a smooth shiny tongue, and cracks at the corners of the mouth are seen together in some forms of anemia, and the diagnosis is confirmed by a blood test rather than by appearance alone. The clinician should examine the mouth in good light and ask about pain, taste, and swallowing, because these symptoms often reveal a problem that the eye alone would miss.

The practice of this examination changes with the equipment available, but the reasoning does not. In a hospital, an ophthalmoscope allows the clinician to see the back of the eye, an otoscope gives a magnified view of the drum and the canal, a nasal speculum opens the nostrils, and imaging answers questions about the orbit, the sinuses, and the deep neck. In a clinic with a torch and a spoon, the clinician substitutes shadow, position, and comparison for magnification, and must be honest about the limits of what can be seen. A torch held to the side of the eye can reveal a cloudy cornea, an irregular pupil, and a globe that is red or swollen, and a spoon held with its convex surface towards the eye can focus light onto the cornea in a crude but useful way. The back of the throat can be inspected with a spoon handle and a torch, though a patient who is drooling or breathing noisily should not be forced to open the mouth. Where the instruments are lacking, the decision that matters is whether the patient can be managed locally at all, and that decision rests on the pattern of findings rather than on the name of a disease.

The safety net is built from the findings that change the urgency of the problem, and the clinician should say them out loud to the patient before the patient leaves. Difficulty breathing, drooling, a muffled or changed voice, difficulty swallowing, a stiff neck, confusion, or a fever in a patient with a throat or neck complaint are reasons to return immediately rather than to wait. Sudden loss of vision, severe eye pain, a red eye with reduced vision, or the appearance of a curtain or flashes in the vision are reasons for urgent review rather than a routine appointment. Persistent hoarseness, a lump in the neck, an ulcer that does not heal, a white patch that cannot be wiped away, or blood in the saliva or the nose are reasons for referral even when the patient feels well. The clinician should record what was found, what was considered, and what the patient was told to watch for, so that the next clinician can follow the reasoning rather than repeat it. A patient who understands what would change the plan is a patient who can act before a small problem becomes a large one.

To summarize, the head, the eyes, the ears, the nose, the throat, and the mouth are open to direct inspection, and that is exactly why the clinician must look carefully, compare the two sides, and then ask what dangerous process could lie behind a common appearance. The eye hides acute glaucoma, a foreign body, a corneal ulcer, and sudden loss of vision; the ear hides mastoid disease and intracranial spread; the nose and sinuses hide infection that can reach the orbit and the brain; the throat hides epiglottitis and deep abscesses that threaten the airway; and the mouth may reveal a dental infection tracking into the neck or the first sign of a systemic disease. Every number mentioned in this chapter, whether a temperature, a rate, a score, a cut-off, or a reference range, is an illustration of reasoning only and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between countries, between editions, and between laboratories. This chapter is educational material; it is not medical advice, it is not a protocol, and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the local rules of your own service. The clinician who examines this region with a hypothesis in mind, who names the dangerous possibility early, and who arranges review when the answer is uncertain will use both a hospital and a simple clinic well.
