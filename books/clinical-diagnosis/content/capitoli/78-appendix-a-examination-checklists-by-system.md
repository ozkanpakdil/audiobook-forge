# Appendix A — Examination Checklists by System

This appendix gathers the examinations of the body into sequences that can be run from memory, one system at a time, in the order the hands and eyes actually move. A checklist is not a form to be completed and not a substitute for thought, which is why it is written as a sequence of observations rather than as a set of headings that cannot be spoken at the bedside. The order carries the reasoning, so the clinician who inspects before touching, and who listens before palpating in the abdomen, is following the logic of the body rather than a ritual. This appendix is meant to be returned to rather than heard from beginning to end, and it is written for the student, the house officer on the ward round, and the clinician in a clinic with a stethoscope and a torch. It is educational material, it is not medical advice, it is not a protocol, and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the local protocol of the service in which the patient is seen.

Several habits run through every checklist that follows and are worth stating once rather than repeating with each system. The first is comparison, because almost every sign is judged against the other side of the body or against the patient's earlier state, and a symmetrical finding is described differently from one that is not. The second is the patient's account, since the examination is a conversation as much as an inspection, and a sign that appears while the patient speaks of it means more than one found in silence. The third is the record, because a finding not written down cannot be compared at the next visit, and the words used should describe what was seen rather than the diagnosis it suggests. A clinician who runs these sequences in the same order every time will eventually run them without thinking, which is the purpose of a checklist, to free the mind by making the hands reliable. No sequence here is a screening program, and each is performed only with consent, privacy, and a chaperone where the examination requires one.

The examination of any patient begins before a hand is laid on the skin, with a general inspection that no checklist may omit in any setting. The clinician looks at alertness, posture, and movement, at whether the patient lies flat comfortably or is propped up and fighting for each breath, and at the color of the face, the lips, and the visible skin. The hands are examined next, because they show clubbing, pallor, tremor, coolness, and staining of the fingertips, and the eyes for jaundice and pallor. Hydration and nutrition are judged from the skin, the tongue, and the shape of the body. The vital signs belong here as the first quantitative observations, so the pulse and the breathing are counted over a stated interval, the temperature is taken by a stated route, and the pressure is measured with a cuff that fits the arm, while weight and height give the body mass index, spelled out in full because the short form has no place at the bedside. A temperature of 38 degrees Celsius is the figure many textbooks print as the line above which fever is recorded, and that figure is an illustration of the reasoning that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the accepted threshold shifts with the route of measurement, the age of the patient, and the edition in use. A general inspection takes less time than the telling of it, and it is the part of the examination least often recorded and most often decisive.

The cardiovascular examination begins with the hands, examined for temperature, capillary refill, and the changes that speak of infection on a valve, and it then moves to the pulse. The pulse is felt at the wrist for its rate, rhythm, volume, and the character of its rise, and then at the other wrist and down the leg, because a pulse absent or delayed in one place changes the whole assessment. The pressure is measured in both arms when a dissection is possible, and with the patient standing when fainting is part of the story. The neck is inspected for the height and character of the jugular venous pulse, judged with the patient lying at an angle and light falling across the neck. The chest is inspected for the apex beat and for surgical scars, and the heart is then listened to over the valve areas with the patient turned, sitting, and holding the breath as each position requires. A systolic pressure of 90 millimeters of mercury is often quoted as the point at which shock must be assumed, and that figure is an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the pressure that matters depends on the patient's usual value, age, and pregnancy. The legs are pressed for swelling, the abdomen is examined for an enlarged or tender liver, and the eyes for the retinal vessels. The examination is not finished until the patient has been watched standing or walking where that is safe, since pressure and pulse may be ordinary lying and abnormal on rising.

The respiratory examination begins with the hands, where clubbing, a fine tremor, and a bluish tinge of the nail beds are sought, and with the chest, inspected for its shape, symmetry, and movement. Breathing rate, depth, and effort are observed before the chest is touched, and the use of the neck muscles and the drawing in between the ribs belong to inspection rather than to the ears. The trachea is felt for its position, because a shift tells which side is losing volume and which is under pressure. The chest is then felt for expansion and for the vibration of the voice, and percussed from top to base, with the note compared side against side. Listening follows, over the front and the back, and the clinician deliberately listens to the same spot on both sides before moving, so that a difference is heard rather than remembered. Breath sounds are described for quality, added sounds for timing and character, and voice sounds for the change that indicates solid lung beneath. A silent chest, a stridor heard without a stethoscope, and a trachea pushed to one side may never be omitted in any setting, because each signals a threat to the airway or the circulation.

The abdominal examination is the one in which the order of the hands changes what is found, so the sequence is strict. The patient lies flat with the knees bent and the arms at the sides, the clinician stands on the right, and the hands are warmed before they touch the skin. The abdomen is inspected for shape, movement with breathing, scars, visible veins, and the bulges that appear when the patient coughs. Listening comes before touching, since the sound of the bowel is altered by the pressure of a hand. Palpation begins lightly and away from the place the patient says hurts, then becomes deeper, with the liver, spleen, kidneys, and bladder sought by name and the aorta felt for its width and its pulse. Guarding, rigidity, and the pain that returns when a hand is withdrawn quickly are sought last, and tenderness out of proportion to the pressure applied is taken seriously rather than explained away. The examination is not complete until the hernial openings, the back, the perineum, and the external genitalia have been inspected, and until a rectal or pelvic examination has been considered and either performed or deliberately omitted with a reason.

The neurological examination is long when done in full and short when done well, and it begins with the level of consciousness. The response to voice, to a command, and to a painful stimulus is recorded using the Glasgow coma scale, whose exact contents and whose alarming figure must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the version in common use differs between services and editions. The pupils are examined for size, symmetry, and reaction to light, and eye movements are checked in all directions. Power is tested group by group in the arms and legs, tone is felt at the wrists and knees, and the reflexes are struck and recorded, with the plantar response sought by stroking the sole. Coordination is judged by finger to nose and heel to shin movements, sensation is tested for light touch, pinprick, vibration, and position, and speech is heard for fluency and clarity. The neck is flexed and the meningeal signs sought in any patient with headache, fever, or a change in consciousness, and the back is examined for a midline defect or a tender spot. The examination ends with the patient standing and walking where it is safe, and sudden weakness of one side, a pupil that is fixed and wide, or a neck that will not bend may never be postponed.

The musculoskeletal examination asks first how the patient moves rather than where it hurts. Gait is watched from the front, the back, and the side, posture and the shape of the spine are inspected, and the patient is asked to perform the movements of daily life that the complaint has made difficult. Each symptomatic joint is inspected for swelling, redness, warmth, and deformity, felt for tenderness and fluid, and moved through its range by the patient and then by the examiner, because a movement the examiner can perform but the patient cannot differs from one blocked in both. Strength is tested against resistance, the joint above and the joint below are examined, and the other side is examined for comparison. The spine is inspected and its movements tested in the neck and lower back, the sacroiliac joints are stressed, and the muscles around the joint are examined for wasting and tenderness. A hot, swollen joint the patient will not move is treated as an infection until proved otherwise, and a limb that is cold, pale, and pulseless after an injury is an emergency that stops the checklist.

The examination of the head and neck gathers the senses and the structures that admit air, food, and speech, and it is performed with a light and a torch everywhere. The head is inspected for shape, scars, symmetry, and the movement of the face, and the scalp is parted with the fingers so that the skin beneath the hair is seen rather than assumed. The ears are examined outside and, where an instrument exists, inside, the nose is inspected for deformity, discharge, and bleeding, and the sinuses are pressed for tenderness. The mouth is opened with a light, and the lips, gums, teeth, tongue, palate, and back of the throat are inspected in turn, because a dental abscess or hidden ulcer can explain a fever nothing else explains. The neck is inspected for swelling and for the position of the trachea, and felt for lymph nodes in each region, for the thyroid gland, and for the movement of the larynx with swallowing. A patient with drooling, a muffled voice, a stiff neck, or difficulty swallowing is examined for a threatened airway and referred at once, and a fixed, hard lymph node in the neck of an adult is investigated rather than observed. The short form ear, nose, and throat, or ENT, is used here only for convenience, and the examination itself is always the same set of observations.

The skin is examined from the scalp to the soles, in good light, with the surface uncovered in stages so that the patient is not exposed more than necessary. The clinician notes color, moisture, temperature, and texture, then looks deliberately at the places usually hidden, which include the scalp, behind the ears, the armpits, the groin, the perineum, the buttocks, and between the toes. The nails and the mucous membranes of the mouth and eyes are part of the same examination, since pallor and small bleeding spots are often seen first in the mouth. Any rash is described by its distribution, shape, border, color, and whether it fades when a clear glass is pressed against it, and a rash that does not fade is treated as an emergency until a cause is found. The hair and scalp are inspected for loss, scaling, and infestation, and the lymph nodes draining the area of any skin lesion are felt in the neck, the armpits, and the groins. A skin examination in a patient with fever is not complete until the whole surface has been seen, because the finding that changes the diagnosis is often a single lesion the patient cannot see.

The examination of the genitourinary system and of the breast requires the most care with consent, privacy, and the presence of a chaperone, and it is performed only when the history or general examination gives it a reason. The breasts are inspected with the patient sitting and then lying with the arms raised, and each is examined in a fixed sequence that covers all of its tissue, with the armpit and the area above the collarbone felt in the same sitting. The clinician notes any lump by its size, shape, firmness, and movement within the tissue, and looks for a change in the skin, a retraction of the nipple, or a discharge. In the male patient the external genitalia are inspected and the testicles felt for size, tenderness, and a lump, and a sudden severe pain in a testis with a high-riding organ is treated as a twisted testis until proved otherwise. The groin is examined for hernia and enlarged lymph nodes, and the prostate is felt through the rectal wall where that examination is indicated. In the female patient a speculum and pelvic examination are performed only by a clinician trained and consented to do them, and the external genitalia, the urethral opening, and the perineum are inspected in every patient whose complaint could arise there. A pregnant patient with bleeding or pain is not examined in a way that delays her journey to obstetric care, and the finding that changes the plan is often the pressure, the pulse, and the abdominal examination rather than the pelvic one.

The mental state examination begins as soon as the clinician meets the patient and continues through every other system. Appearance, dress, posture, and cooperation are noticed, behavior and movement are observed, and speech is heard for its rate, volume, coherence, and direction. Mood is asked about directly and judged from what is said and shown, and the content of thought is explored for worry, for fixed false beliefs, and for the intention to harm the self or another. Perception is tested by asking whether the patient hears or sees anything that others do not, and insight is judged by what the patient believes about the illness. Cognition is examined by testing attention, orientation, memory, and the ability to follow a simple command, and the difference between a clouded attention that fluctuates and a memory that has failed over months is one of the most useful distinctions in medicine. The history is completed with an account from a relative or caregiver, because a patient with failing memory may describe a life that no longer matches the one the family sees.

Some parts of these sequences may be omitted when the patient is dying in front of the clinician, and some may never be. In an emergency the fine special tests of the joints, the formal testing of cognition, and the examination of the breast and the pelvis are postponed until the immediate threat has been handled. What may never be omitted is the general inspection, the assessment of the airway and the breathing, the pulse and the pressure, the level of consciousness, the skin for a rash that does not fade, the neck for stiffness, and the response of the pupils, because each decides whether the patient is treated now or observed. The examination of the abdomen in a patient with pain may never be abandoned in favor of a machine, since the hand that finds rigidity is often ahead of any image. In the clinic with a stethoscope and a torch, the same sequences run in the same order with fewer instruments, so the torch replaces the lamp, the watch replaces the monitor, and careful comparison of one side with the other replaces the imaging that is not available. What changes is the depth of the testing and the certainty of the diagnosis, not the reasoning, and the clinician who records what could not be examined has told the truth about the limits of the assessment. A checklist followed rigidly in a patient who is deteriorating is itself a danger, and the rule that governs all of them is that the patient sets the order.

Constructed illustrations may show how the sequences behave when used rather than recited, and both are teaching devices rather than portraits of any real person. Imagine first a patient brought in unresponsive: the general inspection notes breathing and color, the pulse and pressure are taken, the pupils are examined, the neck is flexed, and the skin is checked for a rash, while the joints and the formal mental state wait. Now imagine a patient in a clinic with a swollen ankle and no fever: the gait is watched, both ankles are compared, the pulses of the foot are felt, the skin is inspected, and the joints above are examined, because a swollen ankle is sometimes the first sign of a problem that began elsewhere. What both share is that no part of the sequence was chosen at random, and that the finding which changed the plan came from an observation a hurried clinician might have skipped. A checklist used this way is a memory aid and not a replacement for the patient in front of the clinician, and the clinician who senses that something is wrong should return to the beginning rather than push to the end.

To summarize, this appendix sets out the examination of each system as a sequence of observations that can be run from memory at the bedside, beginning with the general inspection and moving through the cardiovascular, respiratory, abdominal, neurological, musculoskeletal, head and neck, skin, genitourinary and breast, and mental state examinations. The order carries the reasoning, which is what is looked at before what is touched and what is listened to before what is pressed. The items that may be postponed in an emergency and those that may never be omitted are both named, so that each is chosen deliberately. Every figure here, whether a temperature or a pressure, is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between countries, editions, and laboratories. This appendix is educational material and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service, and no checklist, however carefully followed, substitutes for the patient in front of the clinician.
