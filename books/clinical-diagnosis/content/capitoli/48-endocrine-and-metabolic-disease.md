# Chapter 48 — Endocrine and Metabolic Disease

The endocrine and metabolic diseases arrive dressed as ordinary complaints. The patient who is tired, thirsty, passing urine at night, losing or gaining weight, feeling the cold, or feeling too warm rarely says the word hormone, and a clinician who waits for an endocrine vocabulary will wait a long time. The reasoning that organizes this chapter is one idea: a hormone is a messenger, so a disorder of a gland is a disorder of a signal, and the findings are the predictable consequences of too much or too little of one messenger acting on organs built to respond to it. Once the clinician knows what the messenger normally does, excess and deficiency can be reasoned out rather than memorized, and the examination becomes a set of confirmations instead of a search. That is why the history and the hands still carry most of the diagnosis, and why a laboratory that measures hormones within hours confirms a hypothesis rather than replacing it. This chapter is educational material; it is not medical advice, it is not a protocol, and it is not a substitute for training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service.

Two of the lethal presentations are metabolic, and either can be present before any disease of a gland has been named. Hypoglycemia, a glucose too low for the brain to work with, produces sweating, trembling, hunger, a racing heart, confusion, peculiar behavior, and finally a decline in consciousness, and the finding that reveals it is a low glucose measured during the episode or a recovery that follows its correction. The patient who takes insulin or another medicine that lowers glucose is the one in whom it must be suspected first, and a drowsy patient without an obvious cause is the one in whom it must not be missed. Diabetic ketoacidosis announces itself with deep sighing breathing, a breath that smells of acetone, vomiting, abdominal pain, thirst, heavy urination, and a drowsiness that can be mistaken for a surgical abdomen. The finding that identifies it is a raised glucose with ketones in the blood or the urine and a shift in the body's acid balance. Neither emergency can be settled by the shape of the illness alone, which is why both are named before the ordinary reasoning begins.

The other fatal presentations belong to the thyroid, the adrenal gland, and calcium, and each is revealed by a pattern rather than by a single number. Thyroid storm is severe overactivity of the thyroid gland, usually in a patient with known disease who is then stressed by infection, surgery, or childbirth, and it produces fever, a pulse far faster than the temperature would explain, agitation, sweating, and sometimes heart failure or a decline in consciousness. The patient with warm moist skin, a fine tremor, and a staring look is the one in whom it must be considered. Myxedema coma is the opposite extreme in long-standing untreated thyroid failure, and it produces a low temperature, a slow pulse, shallow breathing, dry skin, a hoarse voice, and a stupor that deepens. Adrenal crisis is the failure of the adrenal gland to make the hormone that maintains pressure and salt balance, and it presents as shock that the obvious cause does not explain, with a fall in pressure on standing, abdominal pain, vomiting, and sometimes darkened skin. Hypercalcemia produces confusion, constipation, abdominal pain, thirst, heavy urination, weakness, and stones in the kidney, and the finding that reveals it is a raised calcium on the laboratory result, sometimes with a shortened interval on the electrocardiogram, or ECG.

None of that list needs to be memorized once the underlying loop is understood, because each gland corrects itself through a signal that travels back to the brain. The hypothalamus senses what the body needs and instructs the pituitary, which instructs the thyroid, the adrenal cortex, and the gonads, and each target gland sends a signal back that quietens the instruction when enough hormone is present. This is why a hormone result must be read as a pair rather than as a single value: a low target hormone with a high instructing hormone means the gland itself has failed, while a low target hormone with a low instructing hormone means the problem lies above it. A clinician who holds this loop in mind can predict that a failing thyroid will slow the pulse and cool the body, while an overactive one will speed the pulse and warm it, and can then look for exactly that.

Diabetes is the endocrine disease that most often walks in through the ordinary door, and it appears in two main forms. In type 1 diabetes the cells that make insulin are destroyed, usually by an immune attack, so the hormone that moves glucose into the tissues disappears and glucose accumulates in the blood while the cells starve; this form tends to appear in the young and to declare itself quickly. In type 2 diabetes the hormone is still made but the tissues answer it poorly, so the gland works harder until it cannot keep the glucose down; this form appears later, is often silent, and is frequently found beside raised pressure and a large waist. The classic symptoms of both are thirst, passing large volumes of urine, particularly at night, weight loss, blurred vision, tiredness, and repeated infections of the skin or the genital tract. The weight loss of type 1 diabetes is the loss of muscle and fat because the body cannot use the glucose it has, which is why a young person who loses weight while eating well is a diagnostic signal rather than a good sign. Nothing here is a matter of discipline; it is a failure of a signal, and the clinician who hears it early can prevent the emergencies that follow.

The diagnosis rests on measuring glucose rather than on the story alone, and the figures used to draw the line are illustrations that must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, because thresholds differ between countries, editions, and laboratories. A fasting glucose at or above 7 millimoles per liter is commonly used as one such line, and the listener should treat that figure as reasoning made visible rather than as a rule that can be applied without the local standard. The glycated hemoglobin, which reflects the average glucose over the preceding weeks, is used in many services, and its own threshold must be confirmed in the same way, since assays and populations differ. What matters diagnostically is that a single raised value is not a diagnosis; it is a reason to repeat the measurement, to ask whether the patient was fasting, and to ask whether illness or a steroid was raising the glucose. In a clinic without a laboratory, the same reasoning is carried by the history of thirst and heavy urination, by the smell of acetone on the breath, by a wound that will not heal, and by the weight measured at successive visits.

Diabetic ketoacidosis occurs when insulin is so deficient that the body breaks down fat for fuel and makes acids called ketones faster than it can clear them, and it is most common in type 1 diabetes, though it can appear in type 2 diabetes under severe stress. The patient is thirsty, passes large volumes of urine, vomits, complains of abdominal pain, breathes deeply and rapidly, and may have a fruity smell on the breath, while the skin is dry and consciousness can fall. The findings that identify it are a raised glucose, ketones in the blood or the urine, and a shift in the acid balance, and the clinician must look for the infection, the missed medicine, the heart attack, or the pregnancy that provoked it. The hyperosmolar state belongs to type 2 diabetes, where enough insulin remains to prevent ketones but not enough to hold glucose down; the glucose climbs very high over days, the patient passes enormous volumes of urine and becomes profoundly dry, and drowsiness deepens. Because ketones are absent, that picture can be mistaken for a stroke or an infection.

Hypoglycemia is the mirror emergency and the more immediately dangerous of the two, because the brain has no store of glucose and begins to fail within minutes. The early signs come from the body's own alarm, with sweating, trembling, a racing heart, pallor, hunger, and a feeling of dread, and they may be absent in a patient who has had diabetes for many years or who takes a medicine that blocks them. The later signs are the signs of a brain without fuel: confusion, strange behavior that may be mistaken for drunkenness, difficulty speaking, blurred vision, a seizure, and a decline in consciousness. The finding that confirms it is a low glucose measured during the episode, and the finding that proves it afterwards is a recovery that follows the correction of glucose, which is one of the few diagnostic acts in medicine that is also the treatment. The clinician should ask who is at risk: the patient who has taken too much glucose lowering medicine, eaten too little, exercised unusually, or drunk alcohol, and the patient whose kidneys or liver are failing. The finding that would make the correction of glucose the wrong answer is another cause of the same picture, such as a stroke or a seizure disorder, but when the glucose is low the correction is never wrong to try.

The complications of diabetes are where ordinary complaints return, and each is the predictable consequence of glucose damaging small and large vessels and the nerves. Damage to the vessels of the eye causes bleeding and swelling in the retina, which is why the back of the eye is examined in every patient with diabetes, and why failing vision in such a patient is not automatically a matter of new glasses. Damage to the vessels of the kidney causes protein to leak into the urine and the filtering power to fall, and it declares itself first through a urine test rather than through symptoms. Damage to the nerves causes numbness in a glove and stocking pattern, burning pain at night, and the loss of the warning sensation that protects the foot, so a patient can walk on a blister until it becomes an ulcer and then a deep infection. Damage to the large vessels causes heart attacks, strokes, and poor circulation in the legs, and the patient with diabetes may not feel the chest pain that would bring another patient to the door. The clinician who examines the feet, the eyes, and the urine at every opportunity is doing the highest yield examination in this chapter, and it costs almost nothing.

An overactive thyroid gland floods the body with a messenger that sets the metabolic rate higher, and every sign can be predicted from that single effect. The patient loses weight while eating well, feels hot, sweats, has a racing and sometimes irregular pulse, feels anxious and restless, has a fine tremor of the outstretched hands, and passes frequent stools. The skin is warm and moist, the hair thins, the eyes may bulge or stare, the neck may show an enlarged gland, and a woman may notice that her periods have become light or have stopped. In an older patient the same excess may appear only as an irregular pulse, breathlessness, or weight loss, and the diagnosis is easily missed. Thyroid storm is this state pushed to an extreme by stress, and it is why a patient with a racing pulse, a fever, and agitation must have the thyroid considered even when an infection seems to explain everything. Where a laboratory exists, the finding that reveals the excess is a raised thyroid hormone level with a suppressed instructing hormone, and where it does not, the combination of warm moist skin, a tremor, a goiter, and weight loss has to carry the diagnosis.

An underactive thyroid gland produces the mirror image, and again every sign follows from a metabolic rate that has fallen. The patient gains weight, feels cold when others are comfortable, moves and speaks slowly, sleeps more, has constipation, dry skin, coarse hair, and a low mood that can be mistaken for depression. The pulse is slow, the tendon reflexes relax slowly when they are tapped, the voice may become hoarse, the face puffy, and the ankles may swell without heart or kidney disease. In an older patient the picture can be mistaken for dementia, and in a pregnant patient it matters for the developing child. Myxedema coma is the extreme form, arriving when long-standing thyroid failure is stressed by cold, infection, or a sedative medicine, and the temperature falls, the pulse slows, breathing becomes shallow, and consciousness declines. The finding that reveals the underlying state is a low thyroid hormone level with a raised instructing hormone, but in the clinic the diagnosis is made from the appearance, the slow pulse, the cool skin, and the history, and the medicine, when it is given, is judged by the response.

The adrenal cortex makes two kinds of hormone that matter here: cortisol, which defends the body against stress, and aldosterone, which holds salt and water and keeps the pressure up. When the gland itself fails, both are lost, and the patient complains of tiredness, weakness, weight loss, dizziness on standing, a craving for salt, nausea, and abdominal pain, while the skin may darken in the creases, the scars, and the gums. The finding that suggests it is a fall in pressure when the patient stands, and the laboratory may show a low sodium with a raised potassium, a pattern few other conditions produce. Adrenal crisis is the decompensation, presenting as shock, dehydration, vomiting, abdominal pain, and confusion, often after an infection or a surgical procedure in a patient whose glands cannot answer the demand. The finding that reveals it is a low cortisol measured at the moment of stress together with the clinical picture, and the response to the correction of the missing hormone is itself strong evidence. This is the diagnosis behind a patient who does not improve with the treatment that should have worked, and it is why the history of steroid medicines is asked in every collapse.

Too much cortisol produces a different and equally predictable set of signs, whether the excess comes from a tumor of the pituitary, a tumor of the adrenal gland, or, most commonly, a steroid medicine taken for another disease. The patient gains weight around the trunk while the arms and legs stay thin, the face becomes round and red, the skin thins and bruises easily, purple stretch marks appear on the abdomen, the thigh muscles weaken, and the pressure rises. The finding that reveals the cause is the level of cortisol and its behavior over the day, but the first step is always to ask what medicines the patient takes, because a steroid is the commonest explanation and the one the clinician can see. It is also the reason a steroid must never be stopped suddenly after a long course, since the suppressed adrenal glands may not wake up in time and the withdrawal can produce a crisis. When the problem lies above the adrenal glands, the signs of cortisol excess are joined by headache, visual disturbance, and a loss of other hormones, and the finding that points upward is a visual field missing its outer half.

Calcium is the metabolic subject that connects the bones, the kidneys, the gut, and the nerves, and its disorders show how a single ion can produce a whole patient. When calcium is too high, the patient may have stones in the kidney, bone pain, abdominal pain with constipation, confusion, drowsiness, thirst, and heavy urination, and the causes to consider are an overactive parathyroid gland and a cancer that releases a substance mimicking it. The finding that reveals it is a raised calcium on the laboratory result, and the electrocardiogram, or ECG, may show a shortened interval, but a single raised value must be repeated and read with the level of the protein that carries calcium in the blood. When calcium is too low, the nerves become excitable, and the patient has tingling around the mouth and in the fingers, cramps, muscle twitching, and sometimes a seizure or a spasm of the hand when the arm is compressed; the finding at the bedside is a twitch of the face when the nerve in front of the ear is tapped. Both extremes must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because reference ranges are set by the laboratory and the level at which treatment begins depends on the symptoms and the speed of the change.

Obesity belongs in this chapter as a diagnostic subject and not as a moral one, and the first task is to measure it rather than to judge it. The body mass index is a weight in kilograms divided by the square of the height in meters, a rough tool that says nothing about where the fat lies, while a waist circumference adds the information that matters most, because fat around the abdominal organs is the fat that is metabolically active. The figures often quoted for these measures are illustrations of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the boundaries differ with ancestry, age, and the purpose for which the measure is used. A body mass index of 30 kilograms per square meter and a waist above 94 centimeters in a man or 80 centimeters in a woman are commonly used as such boundaries, and they are reasoning made visible rather than universal rules. The diagnostic value of the examination is to separate the common picture, in which weight gain comes from food and inactivity, from the uncommon pictures with another cause: an underactive thyroid, an excess of cortisol, a tumor of the pituitary, a medicine such as a steroid, and a disorder of the ovary.

The history in endocrine disease is asked in the same disciplined way as anywhere else, and the Oldcarts framework keeps the account complete while the mind runs ahead. The clinician asks about change in weight, appetite, thirst, urine volume, bowel habit, heat and cold tolerance, sweating, mood, energy, sleep, sexual function, and menstrual history, and about every medicine the patient takes, including every steroid, every supplement, and every product bought without a prescription. The family history matters more here than in most chapters, because many endocrine diseases cluster in families, and the past history matters for surgery, radiation to the neck, head injury, childbirth, and autoimmune disease. The pregnancy history and the medicines taken in pregnancy belong in the same conversation, since thyroid disease and diabetes both change the care of a mother and a child.

The examination begins before the patient is undressed, with the weight, the height, the waist, the pulse, the pressure, and the temperature, and with the general impression of the face, the skin, and the way the patient moves. The clinician looks for a fine tremor of the outstretched hands, warm or cool skin, dry or moist palms, thinned hair, a puffy face, a hoarse voice, and a slow or quick way of talking and moving. The neck is inspected and palpated while the patient swallows, because the thyroid gland rises with the swallow and a nodule or a goiter is often found only this way, and the eyes are examined for a stare, a bulge, and a lid that lags behind the movement of the eyeball. The eyes are also examined for the pale inner lining that suggests anemia, and the mouth for darkening of the gums, which belongs to adrenal failure. The abdomen is examined for the distribution of fat and for purple stretch marks, the legs for swelling, and the feet for numbness, ulcers, and the pulses that should be felt. The pressure is measured with the patient lying and then standing, and the proximal muscles are tested by asking the patient to rise from a chair, because weakness there is a sign the clinician can see while the patient moves.

In a hospital the laboratory turns a hypothesis into a result within hours, and the reasoning that directs it is the same reasoning used at the bedside. A glucose measurement at the bedside answers the first question in any patient who is confused, drowsy, or unwell, and it is the single most useful test in these emergencies because it is immediate and because its correction treats what it finds. The thyroid hormones with the instructing hormone answer the thyroid question, cortisol measured at the moment of stress answers the adrenal question, and calcium, sodium, potassium, and the acid balance answer the metabolic questions, and each must be read against the reference range of the laboratory that produced it. Imaging by ultrasound, computed tomography, or CT, and by magnetic resonance imaging, or MRI, finds tumors of the pituitary and the adrenal glands, while a sample of a thyroid nodule examined under the microscope names the cells. The clinician should say what he expects each test to show before it is sent, so that a result at the edge of the reference range is recognized as an uncertainty rather than absorbed as an answer.

In a clinic whose equipment is a stethoscope, a torch, a thermometer, and perhaps a urine test, the endocrine diagnosis rests on the appearance, the history, and the response to a single correction, and it is a serious form of reasoning rather than a lesser one. The clinician weighs the patient at every visit and records the change, counts the pulse, measures the pressure lying and standing, and examines the skin, the hair, the face, the eyes, the neck, and the feet in a fixed order so that nothing is skipped. A urine test that shows glucose and ketones changes the plan within minutes, and an inexpensive measurement of glucose in a drop of blood, where it exists, does the same. The response to a correction is the clinic's laboratory: a drowsy patient who wakes when glucose is given has hypoglycemia, a patient whose pressure returns when the missing hormone is replaced has adrenal failure, and a patient whose confusion clears when calcium is corrected has hypercalcemia. That logic has limits, because a response can be produced by more than one cause, so the clinician should record what was given, what changed, and what did not. The decision that matters most here is not which diagnosis to name but whether the patient can be watched in this clinic or must begin a journey to a hospital now.

The treatment decision follows the same rule as everywhere in this book: name the finding that would make treatment right and the finding that would make it wrong, and leave the dose, the route, and the duration to the protocol that governs the place where the patient lies. Treatment is the right answer when an emergency threatens the brain, the circulation, or the acid balance, when a deficiency is producing symptoms that limit life, or when an excess is damaging an organ that cannot wait. It is the wrong answer when the abnormality is mild and found by chance, when it is likely to be the effect of another illness or medicine, when it is measured at a time of acute stress, and when the patient is stable enough to allow the diagnosis to be completed first. The record of an endocrine problem should show the reasoning and not only the numbers, and a patient sent home must be told which changes should bring him back: vomiting, fever, a drowsy or confused state, a wound that will not heal, a pulse that feels too fast or too slow, or a weight that continues to fall.

A constructed illustration may make these habits concrete, and it is offered as a teaching device rather than as a portrait of any real person. Imagine an older man brought in drowsy after several days of thirst, heavy urination, and a chest infection, whose bedside glucose is markedly raised, whose skin is dry, and whose breathing is deep; the clinician who measures the glucose first finds the thread that explains the confusion and then looks for the infection that provoked it. Now imagine a patient who collapses after an illness and whose pressure does not respond to the treatment that should have worked, in whom a fall in pressure on standing and darkening of the gums are the clues that lead to the adrenal gland. None of these patients is real, and none of the stories should be applied to a real person without a full assessment, but each shows a different road into the same reasoning.

To summarize, the endocrine and metabolic diseases are disorders of messengers, and the signs of each are the predictable consequences of too much or too little of a signal acting on organs built to answer it. The lethal presentations are named at the bedside and each carries its revealing finding: hypoglycemia, a low glucose with a confused or drowsy patient; diabetic ketoacidosis, deep sighing breathing with ketones and an acid shift; thyroid storm, fever with a pulse out of proportion to it; myxedema coma, hypothermia with a slow pulse and declining consciousness; adrenal crisis, shock that the obvious cause does not explain; and hypercalcemia, a new confusion with constipation, thirst, and a raised calcium. The reasoning is the same where a laboratory measures hormones within hours and where a clinic has only a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, because in both places the history and the examination form the hypothesis and the test confirms it. Every score, threshold, reference range, pressure, temperature, rate, and duration named in this chapter is an illustration of reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since such figures differ between countries, editions, and laboratories. This chapter is educational material and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service. A patient who is drowsy, thirsty, or collapsing without an explanation deserves a glucose measurement and a second look before any other answer is accepted.
