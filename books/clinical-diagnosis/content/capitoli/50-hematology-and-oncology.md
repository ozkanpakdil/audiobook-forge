# Chapter 50 — Hematology and Oncology

Hematology and oncology meet at the bedside wherever a patient arrives with a tiredness that will not lift, a lump that was not there before, a fever with no source, or a bruise that appeared without a fall. The reasoning that organizes both subjects begins with what the patient noticed rather than with what the laboratory will later report. The blood is a tissue that can be sampled directly, which is its great advantage, and it is also a tissue that fails quietly, which is its great danger, because a marrow that produces too few cells gives no pain until the shortage becomes severe. A missed cancer is among the most serious diagnostic failures of ordinary practice, and it is usually missed not because the signs were absent but because they were never assembled. The clinician's task is to decide which product of the marrow is missing, why it is missing, and whether the cause must be answered today or can be investigated calmly over the following weeks. This chapter is educational material; it is not medical advice, it is not a protocol, and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service.

Several dangers must be named before the ordinary reasoning begins, each with the finding that would reveal it. The patient whose marrow is suppressed and who develops a fever may have an infection in the bloodstream that the ordinary defenses cannot contain, and the finding is a raised temperature in someone known to have too few infection-fighting white cells. The patient with an extreme number of abnormal white cells may have sludging of the blood in the small vessels of the lung and the brain, revealed by breathlessness, confusion, a headache, or a change in vision. A patient with a known or suspected cancer who develops a band of pain around the chest or the abdomen, weakness in the legs, and difficulty passing urine may have compression of the spinal cord, and that combination demands immediate help rather than an appointment next week. The patient whose face and neck swell, whose veins stand out on the chest wall, and who becomes breathless when lying flat may have obstruction of the large vein that returns blood to the heart. Each of these is a reason to act at once and to confirm afterwards, and none of them should wait for a scheduled review.

The marrow produces three things, and the reasoning from a low count follows the product that is missing. Red cells carry oxygen, and their shortage produces pallor, tiredness, breathlessness on effort, a racing pulse, and sometimes a rushing sound in the ears. White cells defend against infection, and their shortage produces fever, mouth ulcers, a sore throat, and infections that behave unusually or last longer than expected. Platelets seal small breaks in the vessels, and their shortage produces bleeding into the skin, bleeding from the gums and the nose, and bleeding that will not stop after a small cut. A count that shows one line low asks one question, a count that shows two asks another, and a count that shows all three low asks whether the whole marrow is being suppressed, replaced, or destroyed. This division turns a set of numbers into questions that the history and the examination can begin to answer.

Anemia is a finding and never a diagnosis, and the clinician who stops at the word has stopped before the work begins. The history asks how long the tiredness has been present, whether it came on slowly or suddenly, whether there has been bleeding from any site, whether the diet contains enough iron, and whether a long-standing illness, an infection, a pregnancy, or a medicine could explain it. The examination looks for pallor in the conjunctiva and the palms, for a smooth and sore tongue, for brittle nails, for an enlarged spleen, and for any sign of blood loss from the bowel or the womb. The pace matters more than the depth, because an anemia that develops over months allows the body to adapt, while one that develops in hours may present as collapse. A heart that races and a pressure that falls in an anemic patient are signs that the compensation is failing rather than that the anemia is mild.

The most important single step in anemia is the discovery of iron deficiency in an adult, because in a man or in a woman past the change of life it usually means that blood is being lost from somewhere, and the loss may be from a cancer of the bowel. The finding that reveals it is a low amount of iron in the blood together with a low store of iron, sometimes with small and pale red cells on the film, in a patient who has noticed nothing else. The stool may look normal and the bleeding may be invisible, so a normal appearance of the stool is not reassurance. The clinician must ask about a change in bowel habit, a sense of incomplete emptying, pain on passing stool, and any family history of cancer of the bowel. Iron deficiency in a young woman with heavy periods has an obvious explanation, but even then a second cause should be sought rather than assumed away. Marrow failure is recognized by the failure of more than one product at once. A patient may present with an infection that will not settle, bleeding from the gums, and a tiredness that has been growing for weeks, and that combination should raise the possibility that the marrow itself is the problem. The causes range from a shortage of a vitamin, through a medicine or a poison that suppresses the marrow, to a cancer of the blood that fills the marrow with abnormal cells, and to a marrow that has simply stopped working. The finding that begins to separate them is the film, where the appearance of the cells and the presence of immature forms change the direction of the search. The dangerous presentation is the patient with too few infection-fighting cells who develops a fever, because infection can advance within hours while the usual signs of inflammation are blunted or absent. In that patient, treatment is the right answer before the cause is confirmed, and the finding that would make treatment wrong is a clear alternative explanation for the fever, such as a reaction to a medicine.

Bleeding and bruising are read by their pattern, and the pattern points to the part of the clotting system that has failed. Small round spots that do not fade when pressed, called petechiae, together with bleeding from the gums, the nose, or the bowel, suggest that the platelets are too few or are not working, and they appear in a patient whose clotting proteins may be entirely normal. Large bruises in unusual places, bleeding into a joint, and bleeding that restarts hours after a cut has stopped suggest a problem with the clotting proteins, whether inherited or acquired. The history should ask about a tendency present since childhood, about medicines that thin the blood, about liver disease, and about a recent infection or pregnancy. The dangerous event is bleeding into the brain, and the finding that should raise it is a sudden severe headache with drowsiness, vomiting, or weakness in a patient known to have a bleeding tendency.

An enlarged lymph node is a common finding and a rare cancer, and the reasoning lies in the features of the node together with the story around it. A node that is small, tender, mobile, and accompanied by a sore throat or a skin infection is usually reactive, and it settles as the infection settles. A node that is hard, painless, fixed to the tissues beneath it, and growing over weeks deserves a different answer, especially when it sits above the collarbone, because that site drains the chest and the abdomen. The examination should describe the size, consistency, mobility, and exact site of every enlarged node, and it should look in the armpit, the groin, and the bend of the elbow as well as the neck. It should also look for an enlarged spleen, an enlarged liver, a rash, a wound, and a source of infection in the mouth, the throat, or the skin. The finding that would make a cancer the wrong answer is a clear local infection that fully explains the node together with a node that shrinks as that infection resolves, though the shrinkage must then be confirmed rather than assumed.

Any mass that a patient can feel, or that a clinician finds, must be explained rather than watched indefinitely, and the explanation follows the region in which it lies. A lump in the breast that is hard, irregular, fixed, or accompanied by a change in the skin or the nipple requires assessment, and what matters is not the texture alone but the whole pattern including the armpit. A lump in the testicle that is painless but firm, or a testicle that has become heavy, must never be treated as an infection without review, because delay changes the outcome. A lump in the neck, the mouth, or the throat that persists beyond the ordinary course of a sore throat, especially with hoarseness or difficulty swallowing, requires examination of the whole upper airway. A change in a mole, an ulcer that will not heal, and a new lump in the abdomen all belong to the same rule, which is that a persistent unexplained mass earns a diagnosis rather than a wait. The size at which a node or a lump becomes concerning is an illustration of reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the accepted figure varies with the site and with the age of the patient.

Some cancers announce themselves through general symptoms that have no localizing sign, and these are the symptoms most easily attributed to stress, age, or a passing illness. Weight loss that was not intended, night sweats that soak the bedding, an itching that has no rash, a fever that comes and goes, and a loss of appetite or an early fullness after eating all belong in a short list that deserves investigation. The clinician should ask about the time course, whether the weight loss was deliberate, what the patient has stopped eating, and whether there is pain anywhere that has not yet been mentioned. The examination then looks for an enlarged liver or spleen, for enlarged nodes, for a mass, for pallor, and for a change in the skin or the nails. Fatigue is the most common of these symptoms and the least specific, and it acquires meaning only from its duration and from what accompanies it. The finding that reassures is a benign explanation that accounts for all the symptoms and that responds as expected, while a normal result in a patient whose story remains unexplained is a reason to continue rather than to stop.

Leukemia is better understood at the bedside by the speed at which it presents than by its classification, because the classification belongs to the specialist and the presentation belongs to the clinician who meets the patient first. The acute form arrives over days or a few weeks with the signs of marrow failure, so the patient may have an infection, bleeding, and profound tiredness together, and the film shows immature cells. The chronic form is often found by accident when a blood count is done for another reason, and the patient may have an enlarged spleen, a mild tiredness, or no symptoms at all, so the finding is the count rather than the complaint. The dangerous presentation of the acute form is the sludging of abnormal cells in the small vessels, revealed by breathlessness, confusion, a headache, and blurred vision in a patient whose count is extremely high. Whenever that combination is present, the patient needs urgent specialist help, and the clinician should not wait for a confirmatory test before asking for it. The exact figure that defines a dangerously high count is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the threshold that triggers action differs between services and editions.

Lymphoma presents in much the same way whether it is given one name or another, and the reasoning follows the node and the general symptoms rather than the microscopic label. The patient may notice a painless lump in the neck, the armpit, or the groin, or may come with weight loss, night sweats, and itching, and sometimes with a fever that comes and goes. The examination looks for nodes in every accessible region, for an enlarged spleen and liver, and for a mass in the abdomen, and it records whether the nodes lie on both sides of the diaphragm, because that distribution guides the referral. The general symptoms matter because they change how quickly the patient should be seen, and the clinician should ask about them directly rather than wait for the patient to volunteer them. The dangerous complications are compression of the spinal cord, obstruction of the large vein returning blood to the heart, and blockage of the airway by a mass in the chest, and each has a finding that can be sought in the first minutes. A patient who is breathless when lying flat, whose face is swollen, or whose voice has changed must be treated as an emergency rather than as an outpatient.

The blood film is the most valuable single test in this chapter, and it is worth understanding what it can and cannot say. It can show the size and color of the red cells, which points toward iron deficiency or a shortage of a vitamin, and it can show immature white cells, which points toward leukemia. It can show whether the platelets are few, large, or clumped, and it can reveal parasites and abnormal shapes that change the whole direction of the assessment. The clinician who requests a film should say what question it is meant to answer, because a film reported without a question is often a film reported without an answer. When the film is abnormal or the picture does not fit, the next step is a sample of the marrow, and that decision belongs to the specialist who will interpret it. A normal film does not exclude serious disease, and it should not be used to dismiss a patient whose story continues to worry the clinician.

The warning signs of cancer are best remembered by walking through the systems rather than by memorizing a list, and the clinician who asks about them routinely will find the cases otherwise missed. In the chest, a cough that has changed or lasted for weeks, blood in the sputum, a hoarse voice, and breathlessness that is new all require a chest radiograph and an assessment of the upper airway. In the gut, difficulty swallowing, persistent indigestion, vomiting of blood, black stool, blood on the stool, and a lasting change in bowel habit are the signs that deserve a look inside. In the urinary tract, blood in the urine that is visible or found on testing, a poor stream, and a new need to pass urine at night require examination and imaging of the urinary tract. In a woman, bleeding after the change of life, bleeding between periods, and a discharge that is bloodstained or foul all require pelvic assessment. Each of these findings is ordinary in itself and becomes important through its persistence and through the age of the patient.

Other systems carry their own signs, and the same rule of persistence applies to every one of them. A lump in the breast or the armpit, a change in the nipple, and a discharge from the nipple in a woman who is not feeding require assessment, and in a man a lump in the breast is uncommon and should be treated with the same care. A change in the size, shape, color, or edge of a mole, an ulcer that will not heal, and a sore that bleeds require inspection of the whole skin rather than of the lesion alone. A new headache different from the patient's usual headache, a change in personality, a weakness on one side, and a seizure in an adult all require imaging of the brain. A lump in the testicle, a change in the voice, and a new and persistent pain in the back that is worse at night belong in the same set, and each should lead to a decision rather than to an explanation that the patient accepts politely. The clinician who names these signs aloud while examining the patient is far less likely to miss them.

Many patients who come with a fear of cancer do not have cancer, and the reasoning that helps them is the same reasoning that helps everyone else, conducted with an explicit acknowledgment of the fear. The clinician should ask what the patient has noticed, what the patient believes it means, and whether someone close has been ill, because the fear itself changes how the story is told and how the examination is received. The finding that reassures is a specific benign explanation that fits all the symptoms, together with a plan that says what will be done, what will be watched, and what should bring the patient back. A normal test on its own is a weak reassurance when the symptom remains, and a clinician who offers only a normal result may leave the fear untouched. Where a genuine risk factor is present, such as age, smoking, a family history, or a previous cancer, the reassurance must come from an appropriate assessment rather than from a kind manner alone. The patient dismissed with an explanation that does not fit the symptoms is the patient who returns later, and sometimes with a disease that has advanced in the interval.

In a hospital the blood count, the film, the clotting tests, and the chemistry of the blood are available together, and the reasoning decides the order in which they are used. The count answers how many of each cell are present, the film answers what they look like, and the clotting tests answer whether the proteins that form a clot are working, so each answers a different question. A sample of the marrow and a biopsy of a node or a mass give the tissue diagnosis, and they are requested when the less invasive tests have pointed to the marrow or to a particular organ. Imaging by computed tomography, or CT, and by magnetic resonance imaging, or MRI, shows the extent of a mass, the involvement of nodes, and the state of the organs, and it answers a specific question rather than surveying the whole body. The substances called tumor markers are easily misused, because they can be raised in benign conditions and normal in cancer, and they are best used to follow a disease that is already known rather than to find one that is not. A test is worth ordering when its result would change what is done next, and the clinician who cannot say what either result would mean should not send it.

Where the clinic has a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, and perhaps the ability to send a blood film, the same reasoning is compressed into fewer steps. The history is taken with more care rather than less, because it is the one instrument that never runs out, and the examination is repeated rather than performed only once. The torch makes the mouth, the throat, the eyes, the skin, and the perineum visible, and it allows the conjunctiva and the palms to be inspected for the pallor of anemia. The decision that follows is usually not a diagnosis but a destination, and the clinician must decide whether to treat, to review, or to refer, and must say which finding would change that decision. A blood film, where it can be obtained, is worth more than a general count, because it can show cells that the count alone would hide. A referral made early with a clear question is a better gift to the patient than a diagnosis made late with a confidence the evidence did not support.

The decision about treatment is part of diagnosis even in a chapter about recognition, and it is best expressed as the finding that would make treatment right and the finding that would make it wrong. Treatment is the right answer when a patient with too few infection-fighting cells develops a fever, when abnormal cells are sludging in the small vessels, when the spinal cord is being compressed, and when bleeding continues from a site that cannot be controlled locally. In each of these situations the patient is at immediate risk, and waiting for confirmation would cost more than acting. Treatment is the wrong answer when a clear benign cause explains the findings, when the abnormality is a laboratory artifact that a repeat sample corrects, and when the diagnosis is not yet secure enough to justify a treatment that carries its own harms. The decision to begin or to withhold belongs to the clinician who knows the patient and the local protocol, and it is never made from a single number. A constructed illustration may fix the reasoning, and it is offered as a teaching device rather than as a portrait of any real person. Imagine a man of about 60 who has had back pain for some weeks and now cannot lift his feet properly, who has a band of numbness around the lower chest, and who has had difficulty starting his urine; the combination means compression of the spinal cord until it is proved otherwise, and it is an emergency. Imagine a woman who has lost weight without trying, who sweats at night, and who has a hard node above her left collarbone that does not hurt; that node drains the abdomen, and it earns an urgent assessment rather than a course of antibiotics. Imagine a young man with a painless swelling of one testicle that he mentions almost in passing, and the clinician who examines it and refers immediately rather than treating it as an infection. None of these people is real, and none of these stories should be applied to a real patient without a full assessment. Every age and interval used here is an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. What the three stories share is that the decisive finding was available to the hand and the eye before any test was ordered.

Blood diseases and cancers also present through the consequences of a weakened defense against infection, and that route deserves its own place in the reasoning. A patient with a cancer of the blood, a patient receiving treatment that suppresses the marrow, and a patient with advanced infection by the human immunodeficiency virus, known as HIV, may all develop infections that behave unusually or that are caused by organisms which rarely trouble a healthy person. The finding may be a fever with no localizing sign, a mouth ulcer that will not heal, a cough that persists, or a weight loss with diarrhea, and the clinician must ask about the treatments the patient is receiving and about the risk factors for HIV. The examination looks at the mouth, the skin, the nodes, the chest, and the perineum, because these are the places where such infections declare themselves. Tuberculosis belongs in this list, and it can present with a prolonged fever and weight loss in a patient whose immune defenses are suppressed, even when the chest radiograph looks unremarkable. The finding that would make an infection the wrong answer is a clear non-infectious cause, but the threshold for treating a suppressed patient on the clinical picture is lower rather than higher.

To summarize, the blood diseases and the cancers are approached through the reasons a patient presents, which are anemia, infection and bleeding from a failing marrow, a node or a mass that must be explained, and the general symptoms of malignancy that have no localizing sign. The dangers are named early and each carries its revealing finding: a fever in a patient whose marrow is suppressed, sludging of abnormal cells in the small vessels, compression of the spinal cord, and obstruction of the large vein returning blood to the heart. Leukemia and lymphoma are read by their speed of onset and by the pattern of nodes and general symptoms rather than by their classification, and iron deficiency in an adult is treated as a possible cancer of the bowel until that possibility is set aside. The warning signs of cancer are sought by walking through the systems, and the patient who fears cancer is answered with a specific benign explanation and a plan rather than with a normal result alone. Every figure in this chapter, whether a count, a size, a reference range, or an interval, is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such numbers differ between countries, between editions, and between laboratories. This is educational material and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service. The same reasoning serves the hospital with a full laboratory and the clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, and in both the decisive step is often the finding that the hand can reach.
