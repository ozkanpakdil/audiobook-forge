# Chapter 10 — General Inspection

General inspection is the oldest instrument in medicine and the only one that needs nothing more than light and attention. It is the deliberate act of looking at a patient before the hands arrive, before the instruments are unpacked, and before any question has been answered with a number. Its authority rests on a plain fact about the body: disturbances announce themselves on the surface long before anyone names them, so a clinician who looks properly is already reasoning while the patient settles onto the couch. This chapter treats inspection as a skill with its own logic rather than as a pause before the real examination begins. By the end, the listener should be able to describe what is seen, to explain what the finding means inside the body, and to decide what must be done next.

The first look happens at the end of the bed, and it costs only a few seconds. From there the clinician takes in the whole patient at once, asking whether this person is comfortable or distressed, still or restless, flushed or pale, thin or swollen, awake or drifting. Nothing is measured and nothing is touched, yet this single glance often separates a patient who can wait from a patient who cannot. The dangerous finding is not exotic. A patient who is pale and clammy, with cool skin and a rapid, thready pulse, may be bleeding internally from a ruptured aortic aneurysm, a gastrointestinal source, or a ruptured ectopic pregnancy, and that patient must be treated as an emergency while the pressure is still being measured. Inspection does not prove the diagnosis; it changes the speed at which the rest of the assessment is allowed to proceed.

Appearance and distress are read through several channels at once, and each channel carries a different kind of information. The face alone shows fear, pain, exhaustion, withdrawal, and the flat expressionless look that can accompany severe illness or a disorder of mood. The position the patient chooses is equally informative, because someone who sits bolt upright and refuses to lie back is protecting an airway or fighting for breath, while someone who lies curled and motionless is guarding an abdomen or a spine. Restlessness that cannot be settled is a warning in its own right, and it often accompanies a shortage of oxygen, an infection, or concealed blood loss. A clinician should say aloud what the posture suggests, because a hypothesis spoken is a hypothesis that can be tested.

The work of breathing is visible before the chest is examined and before any device is attached. A patient who is using the muscles of the neck, who has flared nostrils, who draws in the spaces between the ribs, or who cannot finish a sentence is telling the clinician that breathing has become an effort. In children the drawing in of the lower ribs and the rocking of the whole abdomen are signs of the same struggle, and they are worth more than a number on a screen. The patient who has grown quiet and drowsy after a period of hard breathing may not be improving at all, because the effort may be failing, and the level of consciousness may then be the more urgent finding. That distinction, between fighting and tiring, is one of the most consequential judgments in the whole examination.

Smell and sweat belong to inspection even though they are gathered through the nose and the hand rather than the eye. Sweating that soaks the clothes in a patient who is not obviously hot suggests shock, a low blood sugar, a heart attack, or a severe infection, and it belongs to the picture of the pale, clammy patient described a moment ago. The breath may carry the sweet smell of a metabolic emergency, the heavy sweetness of advanced liver failure, the urinous smell of kidney failure, or the smell of alcohol and smoke. A wound, an ulcer, or a discharging ear has its own message, and the nose often reaches that message before the eye does. None of these smells confirms anything by itself, yet each one narrows the field and directs the next question.

Nutrition and wasting are judged by the eye, then supported by the weight and by a measure such as the body mass index, which is a ratio of weight to height spoken as a single figure. The clinician looks for the loss of the fat pads of the cheeks, hollowing above the collarbones, prominence of the ribs, and loose skin where muscle used to be, and compares both sides of the body for symmetry. Loss of muscle and loss of fat are not the same finding, and neither is the same as the swelling of edema or the bloating of a distended abdomen. Weight that has fallen without intent, especially when appetite is preserved or when the loss has been rapid, raises the possibility of cancer, tuberculosis, infection with the human immunodeficiency virus, diabetes, or a long-standing infection, and it demands a systematic search. A single value of the body mass index is an illustration rather than a verdict, so the figure that defines wasting must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the cut-offs differ between populations and editions.

Hydration is judged from the moisture of the mouth, the appearance of the eyes, the skin, and in the very young the soft spot on the top of the head. A dry tongue, sunken eyes, skin that stays pinched when released, and a history of reduced urine suggest that fluid has been lost, while the same appearance can also follow mouth breathing, fever, or a medicine that dries the mouth. The clinician should not rely on thirst alone, because thirst is unreliable in the old, the very young, and the very ill. In a patient who is both dehydrated and confused, the dehydration may be the cause of the confusion or a consequence of the illness that produced it, and the reasoning must hold both possibilities at once. Skin turgor is also altered by age and by weight loss, which is why the finding is best read next to the rest of the picture rather than on its own.

Pallor is the loss of the normal pink color of the skin and the lining of the eye, and it reflects reduced flow of blood through the surface rather than a direct measurement of blood. The clinician looks at the conjunctivae, the inside of the lip, the palm, and the nail beds, and compares what is seen with the patient's own color over recent months when that can be established. Pallor of sudden onset with sweating and a racing heart points towards acute blood loss, whereas pallor that has crept in slowly suggests anemia from a chronic cause such as a slow gastrointestinal bleed, a dietary deficiency, or disease of the marrow. Pallor can also accompany shock from any cause, and it can be entirely absent in a patient who is profoundly anemic and has adapted to it. Because the appearance varies so much, a blood count remains the test that settles the question, and the reference range it is read against must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, since laboratories differ in how they report it.

Jaundice is the yellow discoloration of the skin, the whites of the eyes, and the mucous membranes caused by a rise in bilirubin. It becomes visible in the eyes before it is obvious in the skin, and it is best seen in daylight rather than under a warm bulb, which can hide it. The reasoning that follows the color divides the problem by where the bilirubin has accumulated, before the liver, within the liver, or after it, and the accompanying signs of pale stools, dark urine, itching, fever, and abdominal pain move the balance between those groups. The dangerous possibility in an older patient with painless jaundice is a cancer of the pancreas or the bile ducts, and the finding that would reveal it is a palpable, non-tender gallbladder or a mass in the upper abdomen. Any threshold for what counts as a raised bilirubin must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the same value is reported in different units in different places.

Cyanosis is the blue or gray tinge that appears in the lips, the tongue, the ears, the fingers, and the nail beds when the blood carries less oxygen than it should. It is divided into the central kind, seen in the tongue and the mucous membranes, and the peripheral kind, seen in cold extremities while the tongue stays pink, and that division carries the reasoning. Central cyanosis suggests a problem in the lungs, the heart, or the blood itself, and it is a finding that must be taken seriously at once. Peripheral cyanosis usually reflects poor circulation from cold, shock, or heart failure, and it improves as the extremity warms. Cyanosis is a late and insensitive sign, so its absence never rules out dangerous hypoxia, and the oxygen saturation that is judged acceptable must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the value changes with age, altitude, and the condition being assessed.

Clubbing is the painless enlargement of the soft tissue at the end of the fingers, with loss of the angle between the nail and the nail fold, and it develops over weeks or months rather than hours. The clinician looks for swelling of the fingertip, sponginess of the nail bed when pressed, and increased curvature of the nail, and asks whether the change is symmetrical across both hands. Its mechanism is not fully settled, but it accompanies chronic shortage of oxygen and certain diseases rather than any single one. Clubbing appears in lung cancer, in chronic lung disease that destroys the air spaces, in tuberculosis, in some diseases of the heart, in inflammatory bowel disease, and in disease of the liver. The dangerous possibility behind new clubbing is a lung cancer, and the finding that would reveal it is a persistent cough, weight loss, or a shadow on a chest radiograph, which must be arranged promptly rather than at leisure.

Edema is the swelling caused by fluid collecting in the tissues, and inspection establishes where it sits and whether it pits when pressed. The clinician notes whether the swelling is confined to a single leg or is present on both, whether it reaches the ankles only or extends to the thighs and the lower back, and whether the skin is tight, shiny, warm, or tender. Swelling of a single leg that is tender and warm suggests a clot in the deep veins, and the danger that follows it is a clot travelling to the lungs, which may present as sudden breathlessness or chest pain. Swelling of both legs that pits and rises as the day goes on suggests heart failure, kidney disease, or liver disease, and the accompanying examination of the jugular venous pressure, the lungs, and the abdomen helps to separate them. The pitting is tested by pressing firmly over the shin for a moment and watching how long the mark takes to refill, and what is seen is recorded in words. Any grading or duration of edema quoted in a local chart is an illustration, and it must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the scales in use are not the same everywhere.

The lymph nodes are inspected before they are felt, because a visible node that the patient has not mentioned is a finding in its own right. The clinician looks for swelling in the neck, above and below the collarbones, in the armpits, and in the groin, and notes whether the overlying skin is red, whether the swelling is single or multiple, and whether it is fixed to the tissue around it. A node that is hard, painless, matted, and fixed suggests cancer, while a node that is tender, warm, and mobile suggests infection, and the way a node behaves over 2 or 3 weeks carries as much information as its size. A node above the left collarbone deserves particular attention, because it can signal disease in the abdomen or the chest rather than in the neck. Any interval used to define a persistent node must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the period that prompts investigation differs between services.

The skin as a whole is the largest organ on display, and it is inspected for color, moisture, temperature, texture, and the pattern of any eruption. The clinician looks for bruises that do not match the story, for tiny bleeding spots that do not fade when pressed, for dry scaling, for ulcers, for scars, and for the marks of injections or self-harm. A rash that does not blanch in a patient with fever is a medical emergency, because it may signal meningococcal disease, and such a patient must be treated before any test confirms the diagnosis. The distribution of a rash matters as much as its appearance, since whether it favours the trunk, the limbs, the skin folds, or the areas exposed to the sun changes the list of possibilities. Inspection of the skin is also the moment to look at the hair and the nails, which reflect nutrition, disorders of hormones, and long-standing disease. Because skin findings change, they are best examined in good light with the body exposed as far as consent and dignity allow.

No single sign in general inspection is decisive, and the value of the exercise lies in the pattern that several signs make together. Pallor with sweating and a racing pulse means something different from pallor with a slow pulse and a swollen abdomen, even though the color of the skin is the same in both. Jaundice with a mass in the upper abdomen, weight loss with a hoarse voice, and clubbing with a long-standing cough each narrow the field towards a particular set of explanations. The clinician should therefore move from the individual finding to a short list of possibilities, and then decide which of them is dangerous enough to be excluded first. The inspection ends with a sentence formed in the mind, or spoken aloud to the team, that states what the appearance suggests and what would disprove it.

In a well-lit hospital bay the sequence can be performed in a fixed order so that nothing is forgotten. The clinician stands at the foot of the bed and looks at the whole patient, then moves to the side and inspects the hands and nails, then the face, the eyes, the mouth, and the neck, then the chest wall and the abdomen, and finally the legs and the feet. Each step takes seconds, so the whole inspection is cheap in time and generous in return. The hospital setting adds tools that sharpen the eye, among them a monitor showing the oxygen saturation and the rhythm, a glucose meter that answers the question of low blood sugar almost at once, and imaging that can confirm a suspicion raised at the bedside. Those tools should be used to test the hypothesis formed by looking, not to replace it, because a clinician who watches only the monitor will miss the patient who is plainly unwell while the numbers remain reassuring.

The hospital also allows the inspection to be extended when the first look raises a specific question. A patient with new clubbing can be sent for a chest radiograph the same morning, a patient with jaundice can have liver tests and an ultrasound arranged before the next meal, and a patient with a rash that does not blanch can be moved to a resuscitation area while the blood cultures are drawn. The value of this speed is not convenience but the closing of the interval in which a dangerous diagnosis can hide. The clinician should also remember that resources create their own traps, because an incidental finding on a scan ordered for another reason can set off a cascade of further tests, and a normal result can relax a vigilance that the patient's appearance did not justify relaxing. The patient at the end of the bed remains the primary instrument in the most equipped room in the building.

In a clinic where the tools are a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, the same inspection is performed with whatever light is available. The clinician waits a moment for the eyes to adjust, then uses the torch at an angle rather than straight on, because side lighting reveals the relief of a rash, the depth of a wound, and the color of the conjunctivae far better than a beam shone directly into the face. Daylight through a doorway is better than any bulb for judging jaundice and cyanosis, so the patient is positioned to face the window whenever that is possible. A cold room changes the findings, because extremities turn blue and pulses become hard to feel, so the hands are warmed before judgment is passed. The patient wrapped in blankets must be uncovered enough to see the legs, the feet, and the skin the blankets have been hiding, with consent given and warmth maintained as far as it can be.

The limited clinic makes some judgments more important rather than less. A patient who is pale, sweating, and confused in a room without a laboratory may be bleeding internally, and the correct action is not a test but an urgent journey to a place where the bleeding can be found and stopped. A patient with fever and a rash that does not fade under pressure must be moved with the same urgency, and the clinician should not wait for a confirmation that will arrive too late to change anything. A thermometer, a torch, and a watch supply enough to identify these patients, provided the clinician looks at the patient rather than at the equipment. Where the road is long and the return uncertain, the inspection also decides what must be written in the referral note and what must be said to the family before the patient leaves.

A constructed illustration makes the sequence concrete. A middle-aged man is brought to a clinic with 2 days of vague tiredness and no pain; he is pale, his skin is cool and damp, his pulse feels fast and thin under the fingers, and he answers questions slowly while asking for water. The torch shows no rash, the eyes are not yellow, and there is no swelling, but the conjunctivae are strikingly pale and the abdomen is slightly distended and tender on the left. Taken alone, each of these findings is ordinary, while together they suggest blood loss from the gut, and the correct decision is urgent transfer rather than a wait for the next clinic day. This example is a teaching device built to show how inspection orders a differential diagnosis, and it is not a description of any real patient. The listener should notice that the diagnosis was available before the abdomen was examined in detail and before any test was ordered. The exact value of the pulse or the blood count does not matter here, but the direction of the reasoning does, and every figure that is later applied must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

Inspection has limits that must be stated as plainly as its strengths. Color perception differs between clinicians and is affected by lighting, by the tint of the skin, and by the observer's own eyes, so a finding that is equivocal deserves a second look or a second person. The very young, the very old, the pregnant, and the immunosuppressed often look less ill than they are, and the patient with deeply pigmented skin may show jaundice and cyanosis in places that are easy to miss, such as the palate, the conjunctivae, and the nail beds. Obesity can hide wasting, blankets can hide swollen legs, and clothing can hide the rash that matters. The clinician who knows these limits looks harder in the places where the signs are usually found rather than concluding that nothing is there.

What is seen should be written down in words that another clinician could picture, because the inspection cannot be repeated by the next reader in the same light. A note that records only that the patient looked unwell is useless, whereas a note that records pallor of the conjunctivae, cool clammy skin, a pulse that felt thready, and a reluctance to lie flat transfers the reasoning to whoever reads it next. The inspection should also be repeated at intervals, because the appearance of a patient at the start of a shift and the appearance at the end of it may tell a different story, and the direction of change is often the most sensitive evidence of deterioration. The clinician should look again after every intervention and at every handover, however briefly, and should record what changed rather than only what was present.

Age changes what inspection shows and what it means. A newborn baby is inspected for color, tone, feeding, and the alertness of the cry, and a baby who is quiet when handled and difficult to rouse is not simply sleepy. An older adult may show none of the expected signs of serious illness, so a fall, a new confusion, or a loss of appetite becomes the finding that demands a search rather than a discharge. In both groups the inspection is brief, gentle, and repeated, and in both the clinician should be slow to accept a benign explanation for a change in function. The same principle applies to the patient who cannot speak, whose posture and breathing may be the only account available.

The hands repay a longer look than they usually receive. The clinician notes the warmth, the color, and the refill of the nail beds, the shape of the fingers, the presence of tremor or of a fine flap of the outstretched hands, and the stains, scars, or calluses that speak of occupation and habit. Splinter hemorrhages under the nails, small painful nodules on the finger pads, and a dusky mottling of the skin can each point towards infection of the heart or a disturbance of the circulation, and they are easily missed when the hands are examined as a formality. The palms may be sweaty in an overactive thyroid or dry and cool in an underactive one, and a fine tremor of the hands is a useful clue in the same conditions. A capillary refill that takes longer than a few seconds suggests poor perfusion, and the number of seconds that counts as delayed must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since practice and ambient temperature alter it.

General inspection is an examination like any other, so it requires consent, an explanation, and the same respect for privacy that the rest of the encounter demands. The clinician should say what will be looked at and why, offer a chaperone when the inspection will include intimate areas, and keep the patient covered except for the part being examined. On a busy ward the inspection is often done through a curtain with the patient half dressed, and the temptation to skip the feet or the back is strong, yet those are precisely the places where swelling, ulcers, and pressure damage hide. A patient who declines part of the inspection has the right to decline it, and the record should state what was not seen as clearly as what was. The order of the inspection should follow the patient's comfort as far as it can, while ensuring that nothing important is left unexamined.

The reasoning must come before the looking, or the looking becomes a ritual. Before entering the bay the clinician should already hold a short list of questions, among them whether this patient is perfusing adequately, whether this patient is oxygenating, whether this patient is losing fluid or blood, and whether there is a surface sign of a disease that has spread through the whole body. Each part of the inspection is then chosen to answer one of those questions rather than performed because it appears on a list. A finding that was not predicted is still welcome, because it is the least contaminated by expectation, and it should be written down and followed up rather than explained away. The clinician who looks with a question will see more than the clinician who looks at everything.

What transfers between the well-lit bay and the clinic at the end of the road is the sequence of questions, not the equipment. The hospital clinician who understands why the conjunctivae are inspected will still inspect them with a torch, and the clinic clinician who understands why the legs are uncovered will still ask the patient to remove the blankets. The principle is physiological, because the surface of the body reflects the volume of the circulation, the oxygen in the blood, the work of the liver and the kidney, and the activity of the immune system. Once that is understood, a finding is not a name to be memorized but a consequence to be reasoned from. The equipment changes what can be confirmed; it does not change what should be looked for.

To summarize, general inspection is the diagnostic act performed before the patient is touched, and it takes seconds while returning information that shapes every later step. It reads the work of breathing, the posture, the color, the moisture, the smell, the nutrition, the hydration, the lymph nodes, the skin, the nails, and the limbs, and it treats each of those findings as a physiological statement rather than a label. The appearance of a pale, clammy, restless patient is the finding that must never be allowed to wait, because internal bleeding can be fatal within a short interval. Every threshold, score, duration, and reference value mentioned in this chapter is an illustration only and must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, since such numbers vary between countries, editions, and laboratories. This is educational material; it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or local protocol, and no real patient has been described here.
