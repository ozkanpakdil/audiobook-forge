# Chapter 40 — Respiratory Failure and Airway Compromise

Breathing can fail in two different ways, and telling them apart organizes this chapter. In one, the lungs receive air but cannot move its oxygen into the blood. In the other, air never arrives in sufficient quantity, or the pump that drives it is losing strength, so carbon dioxide accumulates while oxygen falls. The first is a failure of oxygenation, the second a failure of ventilation, and they behave differently at the bedside even when the distress looks the same. The clinician who can decide which failure is present knows where to look and how quickly to move. This chapter is educational material; it is not medical advice, not a protocol, and not a substitute for training, supervision, or the judgment of the clinician who is with the patient.

The lethal causes must be named while the subject is described, each with the finding that would reveal it. Epiglottitis, an infection that swells the flap above the voice box, gives a sudden fever, drooling, a muffled voice, and a patient who refuses to lie down. A foreign body gives a sudden onset during eating or play, a cough that will not settle, and breath sounds that differ between the two sides of the chest. Anaphylaxis gives swelling of the lips, tongue, and throat with wheeze and a falling pressure. A hematoma in the neck, after surgery, injury, or a procedure on the large vessels, closes the airway from outside as it expands. An abscess beside the tonsil gives fever, a muffled voice, drooling, and a tender swollen neck. An exhausted patient who is about to stop breathing is the last of these dangers, and the finding is a fading effort, a falling breathing rate, and a drowsiness that can look almost like calm.

Oxygenation fails when blood travels through regions of lung that are perfused but not ventilated, so it returns to the heart without picking up oxygen. Pneumonia fills the air spaces with fluid and cells, pulmonary edema floods them from the capillaries, and acute respiratory distress syndrome makes both lungs stiff and wet after injury or severe infection. A collapsed segment leaves blood flowing past an airway that no longer opens, and a large clot in the pulmonary arteries wastes ventilation on regions that receive no blood. Because carbon dioxide crosses the lung far more easily than oxygen, a patient whose only problem is oxygenation can keep the carbon dioxide normal or low by breathing harder. The price of that effort is work, and when the work cannot be sustained the same patient retains carbon dioxide and becomes a ventilatory failure as well. A normal carbon dioxide in a struggling patient is therefore evidence of effort rather than reassurance.

Ventilation fails when the pump that moves air is weakened or when the airway resists the flow. The pump begins in the brain, travels down the spinal cord, crosses the junction between nerve and muscle, and ends in the diaphragm and the chest wall muscles. A sedative medicine, a stroke, a head injury, a disease of the spinal cord, a disorder of the peripheral nerves, and a disease of the muscles all reduce the air moved with each breath. Obstruction of the airway adds a second load, because the muscles must pull against a narrowed tube. Carbon dioxide then accumulates, producing a warm flushed face, a bounding pulse, a headache, a fine tremor of the hands, and a drowsiness that deepens as the level rises. A patient in this state may breathe quietly and look deceptively peaceful, and that quiet is the danger rather than the comfort.

The work of breathing and the mental state are the two bedside discriminators, and both are visible before any instrument is applied. The clinician looks for neck muscles that stand out with each breath, for skin drawn in between the ribs and above the breastbone, and for nostrils that flare in a child. The patient may sit forward with the arms braced on the knees, using the shoulders as an extra pump, and may purse the lips to hold the airway open during a slow expiration. Sweating, a look of alarm, and an inability to finish a sentence in one breath show that the demand has outgrown the reserve, while quiet drowsiness may mean the drive to breathe is fading. A respiratory rate of 30 breaths per minute is often quoted as a point of alarm, but that figure is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the rate that should worry a clinician depends on age, pregnancy, and the usual state of the patient. The Glasgow coma scale is the usual way of recording consciousness, and its exact contents and the figure that should cause alarm must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the version in use varies between services and editions.

The movement of the chest wall tells the clinician where the resistance lies. A chest that rises fully on both sides but admits little air suggests obstruction above the lungs, because the pump is working and the air is not arriving. A chest that barely moves, with the abdomen drawn inward as the diaphragm descends, suggests weak muscles, and that inward movement against the expected outward movement is called paradoxical breathing. A long wheezing expiration with a barrel-shaped chest suggests obstruction in the smaller airways, while a chest that moves less on one side suggests air or fluid that has separated the lung from the wall. Where the problem lies decides the urgency more than the size of the disturbance does, because a little swelling above the voice box can close a narrow tube. Sorting the patient by the site of the problem is the first diagnostic act, and it is done with the eyes and the ears before any test.

The signs of an airway that is narrowing rather than already closed must be recognized from a distance. Stridor is the harsh musical sound of turbulent air through a narrowed segment, and its timing carries the message. Inspiratory stridor points above the chest, expiratory stridor points to the smaller airways below, and stridor in both phases points to the windpipe or the voice box. The sound is a late sign, because a tube must already be narrowed before flow becomes turbulent, and a tired patient may lose the stridor as the effort fails. The disappearance of a loud stridor is therefore warning rather than improvement, just as a silent chest in severe asthma signals danger. A changed voice is often earlier: hoarseness means swollen, inflamed, or paralyzed cords, and a muffled voice means the space above the cords is filling.

Drooling and an inability to lie flat mean the airway has narrowed enough to change behavior, and they should stop the examination rather than prompt more of it. A patient who cannot swallow his own saliva, or who speaks as if the mouth is full, is protecting an airway that is partly closed. The same meaning belongs to a patient who will not lie back, who sits bolt upright, or who becomes distressed when the head is lowered, because the tongue and swollen tissue fall backward. The clinician should not force such a patient to lie down, and should not pass an instrument into the throat in the hope of seeing the swelling. The character of the cough adds information: a barking cough in a young child belongs to swelling below the cords, a muffled cough to swelling above them, a weak cough to failing muscles or nerves, and a wet cough with fever and crackles to infection of the lung tissue. The neck should be examined for swelling, bruising, or a scar, and felt for crackling under the skin, which means air has escaped into the tissues.

Epiglottitis is the prototype of the narrowed upper airway, and it is diagnosed from the story and the posture rather than from inspection. It begins quickly, with a high fever, a severe sore throat, and a rapid decline, and it is most feared in the young child who has not been immunized, though adults are affected as well. The patient drools, speaks with a muffled voice, sits forward with the chin extended, and resists lying down, and there may be no cough because the swollen structures do not move. Attempting to examine the throat with a spatula can provoke a spasm and close the airway completely, so the instrument is left alone. The patient is kept calm, upright, and accompanied, and the combination of drooling, a muffled voice, and a refusal to lie flat should trigger a call for help at once. What the clinician must not do is observe such a patient in the hope that the picture becomes clearer.

A foreign body in the airway presents as narrowing rather than closure. In a young child it follows a moment of eating, playing, or crying with something in the mouth, and the story may be missing because no adult saw it. In an adult it follows a meal in a patient with difficulty swallowing, a reduced level of consciousness, or a disorder of the nerves and muscles of the throat. The finding that reveals it is a difference between the two sides of the chest, with a wheeze or reduced breath sounds on one side and no fever, while a complete obstruction produces no air movement at all. A foreign body lodged for some time can produce fever, cough, and a shadow on the radiograph, and it is then mistaken for infection. A one-sided wheeze in a patient who has never wheezed before should raise the question of a foreign body before asthma.

Anaphylaxis is the danger most easily missed when the skin is spared. It follows a food, a medicine, a sting, or a contact within a short interval, and it produces swelling of the lips, tongue, and throat with wheeze, a falling pressure, and a sense of impending doom. The skin signs of hives and flushing are common but not universal, and their absence must not exclude the diagnosis when breathing and pressure are changing. The finding that reveals it is the rapid involvement of more than one system, such as swelling of the face with wheeze, or a rash with a falling pressure, in a patient who was well moments before. A patient with a known allergy, a previous reaction, or a self-injecting device at home is at higher risk, and that history belongs in the first questions. The decision to treat is made on the clinical picture, because waiting for confirmation here costs the airway itself.

A hematoma in the neck is a mechanical emergency that is easily overlooked, because the patient may look well at first. It follows surgery on the thyroid or the neck, a procedure on the large vessels, an injury, or a line placed into a large vein, and it is more likely in a patient who takes a medicine that prevents clotting. The swelling expands inside a compartment that cannot stretch, and it pushes the windpipe aside, compresses the veins, and finally closes the airway. The early findings are a tense tender neck, a new hoarseness, difficulty swallowing, and a feeling of pressure, while the late findings are stridor, a displaced windpipe, and a patient who can no longer lie flat. Because the change can occur very quickly, a patient who has just had such a procedure is watched closely and the neck is examined at every review. A quiet period can precede a sudden deterioration, and that pattern is the reason for vigilance rather than a reason to relax it.

An abscess beside the tonsil, or one that has spread into the deep spaces of the neck, begins as a severe sore throat with fever and develops a muffled voice, drooling, difficulty opening the mouth, and offensive breath. The finding that reveals it is a swelling that pushes the soft palate and the uvula to one side, while a patient who cannot open the mouth widely is showing a protective spasm. The exhausted patient is the next danger, and it most often surprises the clinician who was reassured by the first examination. Exhaustion develops when the muscles of breathing have worked against a load too long, and it is recognized by a fall in the breathing rate after a period of rapid breathing, a weaker cough, and drowsiness that replaces agitation. A rising carbon dioxide level produces its own drowsiness, so the patient may appear calm while the blood is becoming more acid, and that calm is the most deceptive sign in this chapter.

A tension pneumothorax is the lethal cause that must be recognized from bedside findings alone, because waiting for an image can cost the patient's life. Air enters the space around the lung and cannot escape, and the pressure rises until it pushes the heart and great veins aside, so the return of blood to the heart falls. The signs are sudden severe breathlessness, absent breath sounds on one side, a chest that is hyperresonant to percussion, distended neck veins, and a falling pressure with a rapid pulse. The windpipe may be shifted away from the affected side, though that is a late sign, and the patient may be restless, sweaty, and confused. The clinician who finds this combination must treat it as a mechanical emergency and call for the help and equipment to relieve the pressure, rather than wait for a radiograph. In a clinic with no imaging, the same combination is the reason to begin the journey at once.

A large clot in the pulmonary arteries is the second lethal cause of sudden breathlessness, and its examination is often deceptively quiet. The patient becomes suddenly short of breath, often with pleuritic pain, a rapid pulse, and a low oxygen, while the chest sounds clear and the radiograph may be normal. The risk factors include recent surgery, a period of immobility, a long journey, active cancer, pregnancy, a previous clot, and a medicine that prevents clotting having been stopped. In the massive form the pressure falls, the neck veins are distended, and the patient may lose consciousness. The finding that most changes the decision is the combination of sudden breathlessness with a clear chest and a risk factor, and the test that answers the question must be chosen according to what is available. Where there is no test, the decision is whether the patient can travel to a place where one exists, and the answer is usually that he must.

Disease within the lung tissue produces a different pattern, and the common causes are told apart by the story, the examination, and the radiograph. Pneumonia gives fever, a productive cough, crackles over one region, and dullness to percussion, and the oxygen falls in proportion to the lung involved. Pulmonary edema from a failing heart gives breathlessness that is worse when lying flat, crackles at the bases, distended neck veins, and swollen ankles. Acute respiratory distress syndrome follows severe infection, injury, an operation, or inflammation of the pancreas, and it makes both lungs stiff and wet so that oxygen falls severely while the chest sounds quiet. Asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease are failures of airflow that can progress to a failure of ventilation when the effort cannot be sustained, and their dangerous signs are a silent chest, a patient who can no longer speak, and a drowsiness that suggests retained carbon dioxide. In a patient with long-standing disease who tends to retain carbon dioxide, oxygen given without attention to the target can worsen the retention, so the target and the monitoring must be set by the current guideline and the local protocol.

The assessment begins with a rapid ordered look that takes only a few moments. The clinician asks a simple question and listens to whether the answer comes in a full sentence, a few words, or not at all, because that observation measures the reserve better than any instrument. He watches the position, the work of breathing, the color of the lips and tongue, the movement of the chest, and the level of awareness, and he listens for stridor, wheeze, or a muffled voice. If the patient is unresponsive and not breathing normally, the response is cardiopulmonary resuscitation, or CPR, with a call for help, and no further diagnosis is attempted at that moment. If the patient is breathing but the effort is exhausting or the airway is narrowing, the response is to keep him upright, stay with him, and summon the people and equipment that can secure the airway. This first look decides whether the rest of the assessment happens here or somewhere else.

The oximeter changed the bedside assessment of oxygenation, and its limits matter as much as its reading. It measures the proportion of hemoglobin carrying oxygen in the small vessels under the probe, and it says nothing about ventilation, so a patient with an acceptable reading may still be retaining carbon dioxide. The reading is unreliable when the circulation to the finger is poor, when the patient is cold or moving, when the probe is badly placed, and when the skin is deeply pigmented, in which case the device may read higher than the truth. It is also misled by carbon monoxide, which binds to hemoglobin and makes the reading look falsely reassuring. A saturation of 90 per cent is widely quoted as a level below which concern is justified, but that figure is an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the boundary depends on the patient's usual state, on altitude, and on the condition being treated. The trend over time and the clinical picture are what the reading is used for, never the single figure alone.

An arterial blood gas separates the two failures directly, because it measures oxygen, carbon dioxide, and acidity at the same moment. A low partial pressure of oxygen with a normal or low partial pressure of carbon dioxide belongs to a failure of oxygenation, while a high carbon dioxide with a low oxygen and an acid blood belongs to a failure of ventilation. A partial pressure of oxygen of 60 millimeters of mercury and a partial pressure of carbon dioxide of 50 millimeters of mercury are figures often quoted as points of concern, but both are illustrations of the reasoning and each must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the acceptable values vary with age, altitude, and the laboratory that reports them. The acidity and the bicarbonate tell the clinician how long the problem has been present, because a kidney with time to compensate will show a higher bicarbonate. A blood gas is a picture of one moment, and it is read beside the work of breathing and the mental state rather than in place of them.

Imaging answers questions that the hands and ears cannot, and each image is chosen to answer a particular question. A chest radiograph shows air around the lung, pneumonia, collapse, fluid in the pleural space, and the size of the heart, and it is the first image in most hospitals. A bedside ultrasound can show fluid around the lung, a failing heart, a clot in the leg, and the movement of the diaphragm while the patient is still being examined. Computed tomography, or CT, gives the most complete picture of lung tissue and vessels, while magnetic resonance imaging, or MRI, answers particular questions the other methods cannot. Other tests add detail: a peak expiratory flow below half of the predicted value is often treated as a sign of severe asthma, though that boundary is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because predicted values depend on age, sex, height, and the reference population used. None of these tests may delay the treatment of a tension pneumothorax or the support of a patient who is tiring.

In a clinic that has a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, the same assessment is performed with different instruments and the same reasoning. The watch takes the place of the oximeter, because the breathing rate counted over a full interval is one of the most reliable signs of a failing respiratory system, and it is repeated to show its direction. The eyes take the place of the blood gas, because the work of breathing, the ability to speak, the position, the color, and the mental state together tell the clinician whether ventilation is adequate. The decision there is not a diagnosis but a direction: a patient with stridor at rest, drooling, a muffled voice, or a refusal to lie flat must not be watched, because the airway may close before help can be arranged. A patient who is exhausted, drowsy, unable to speak, or whose breathing rate is falling after a period of rapid breathing must be moved at once, and a patient with absent breath sounds on one side or a falling pressure may have a tension pneumothorax or a massive clot, so the journey is part of the treatment. A patient who is comfortable, speaking in full sentences, and symmetrical on examination may be watched, provided the plan for review is explicit and the patient can return.

Simple maneuvers can be diagnostic as well as helpful, and the clinician should notice what changes them. Lifting the chin and jaw forward opens the airway when the tongue is the obstruction, and improvement with that movement points to position rather than to swelling in the tissues. The treatment decision in this chapter is not about which medicine to give but about which failure is present and how soon it must be answered. Treatment is the right answer when the airway is narrowing with drooling, stridor, a changed voice, or an inability to lie flat, and also when the patient is tiring or the carbon dioxide is rising with an acid blood. It is the wrong first answer when the patient is alert, speaking comfortably, and moving air well, and when an intervention on the airway would add risk without changing the course.

Children and older adults sit at opposite ends of the same problem, and both need their own warning. A young child increases the breathing rate and effort long before the oxygen falls, so an acceptable reading can coexist with a serious problem, and the fall, when it comes, is sudden. Croup produces a barking cough, a hoarse voice, and an inspiratory stridor worse at night, while epiglottitis produces drooling, a muffled voice, and a child who will not lie down, and a foreign body produces a sudden onset and a one-sided wheeze in a child who was well a moment before. Stridor at rest is a different finding from stridor that appears only with crying, and sedating a struggling child can remove the drive to breathe. In the older adult the signs are quieter: fever may be absent or low, the breathing rate may rise only a little, and the first change may be confusion, a fall, or a refusal to eat. A patient with long-standing lung disease may live with a low oxygen and a high carbon dioxide at baseline, so the useful comparison is with his own previous values and usual activity rather than with a rule from a different population.

A constructed illustration may fix these habits, and it is offered as a teaching device rather than as a portrait of a real person. Imagine a young child who was well in the morning, who now sits forward with a muffled voice, drools into a bowl, and refuses to lie back, with a fever that the thermometer confirms. The clinician who recognizes the posture, keeps the child upright, and calls for help is doing the diagnostic work, while the clinician who reaches for a spatula is taking a risk the story has already warned against. Now imagine an adult a few hours after an operation on the neck, whose dressing is tight, whose voice has become hoarse, and whose neck is swelling and tender, observed closely because the finding is a change rather than a dramatic sign. Neither patient is real, and neither should be used as a pattern for a real person without a full assessment. The record and the conversation are part of the diagnosis: the clinician writes what was found, what was decided, and what should bring the patient back, such as a muffled voice, difficulty swallowing saliva, breathlessness at rest, or a new drowsiness.

To summarize, breathing fails either at the exchange of oxygen or at the movement of air, and the bedside separates the two by the work of breathing, the mental state, the movement of the chest wall, and the place where the problem lies. The narrowing airway is recognized before it closes, through stridor, a changed voice, drooling, a muffled cough, and the refusal to lie flat, and the lethal causes are named at once, among them epiglottitis, a foreign body, anaphylaxis, a hematoma in the neck, an expanding abscess beside the tonsil, a tension pneumothorax, a large clot in the lungs, and the exhausted patient whose effort is fading. The same reasoning is performed in a hospital where an oximeter, a blood gas, and a ventilator are within reach and in a clinic that has a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, where the question is whether help must be called now. Every figure in this chapter, whether a temperature, a rate, a pressure, a partial pressure, a saturation, or a score, is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such values differ between countries, editions, and laboratories. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service. A patient who is tiring, drooling, or unable to lie flat is a patient to help now, and the diagnosis is completed afterwards.
