# Chapter 6 — Taking the History

The history is the most powerful instrument a clinician owns, and it is the only one that is always available, in the teaching hospital and in the clinic at the end of the road alike. Everything that follows in this book, from the first inspection to the last laboratory result, is built on the account a patient gives before anyone touches the body. The reasoning that organizes this chapter is simple. A story is a body of evidence, and like any evidence it can be collected well or badly, in an order that preserves its meaning or in an order that destroys it. Our purpose here is to learn how to let the story arrive, how to guide it without breaking it, and how to hear the one detail that decides the diagnosis. This is educational material, and it is not a substitute for training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the local protocol of your own service.

The encounter begins with the chief complaint, and it should begin with the patient's own words rather than with a category the clinician supplies. A patient who says that his chest feels as though a belt is tightening around it has given information that the phrase chest pain does not contain. A patient who says that her head feels as though it is being pushed apart from inside has described an onset and a quality in one image. When the clinician rewrites that sentence immediately as possible angina or possible migraine, the translation is not wrong, but it arrives too early, and it closes the door on the words that were actually spoken. The chief complaint should therefore be recorded close to the original phrasing, because the phrasing itself is data. The word the patient chooses often points to the diagnosis the clinician has not yet considered.

Some stories contain a lethal cause so plainly that the history alone identifies it, and this is why the history must be taken before the examination rather than after it. A patient who describes a headache that reached its full intensity within seconds, like a blow to the back of the head, is describing the onset of bleeding around the brain, and the finding that reveals it is that abrupt maximal onset together with vomiting, neck stiffness, or a brief loss of consciousness described in the account. A patient who describes pain that began in the back and moved to the front, tearing from the inside, may be describing a tear in the aorta, and the revealing feature is the migration of the pain and its quality rather than its location. A patient with a fever and a rapidly spreading dark rash may be describing an infection of the blood, and the history of its speed is the warning. These are not rare curiosities to be remembered for an examination; they are the reason the first minutes of the history are treated as an emergency of listening. The clinician who waits for the examination to find them may have lost the time in which they could be treated.

The history of the present illness is built by opening the story wide before narrowing it. The first question is deliberately broad, something like what happened, or tell me about it from the beginning, and then the clinician's main task is to be quiet. Open questions produce longer answers, more unexpected detail, and more of the patient's own reasoning about what is wrong. Narrow questions produce short answers, and a patient who is asked only narrow questions will confirm whatever the clinician already suspects. The order matters as much as the content: breadth first, then depth. A clinician who has heard the whole story can narrow with purpose, because he knows which corners of it deserve pressure.

Narrowing is not forbidden, only mistimed. Once the account has run its course, the clinician narrows deliberately, using closed questions to establish the facts the story left unclear: when exactly it began, what the patient was doing at that moment, whether anything similar has happened before, and what the patient did about it. Each closed question should exist because some diagnosis depends on the answer, and not because it appears on a form. The transition from open to closed is announced in the clinician's own mind rather than in the room, and the patient barely notices it. The skill lies in knowing when the account is complete, which is usually when the patient begins to repeat himself, and in resisting the urge to cut the story short because a familiar pattern has already appeared.

Chronology is the spine of the present illness, and it is worth building in the patient's own sequence before it is reorganized. What was the first thing that was not right, what came next, and what has changed since. Onset, duration, and trajectory carry more diagnostic weight than any single feature, because they separate the illness that is still rising from the one that has already peaked and the one that is smouldering. A pain that is worse every hour has a different meaning from the same pain unchanged for a week. The patient's sense of direction, whether things are getting better or worse, is a diagnostic finding in its own right. Ask for that sense explicitly, because patients often assume the clinician can see it.

The Oldcarts framework is a way of organizing the whole illness so that nothing essential is left unasked, and it is best understood as a set of reasons rather than a script. It prompts the clinician to establish when the problem started, where it is felt, how long it has persisted, what it feels like, what makes it worse, what makes it better, how it behaves over time, and how severe it is. Each of those prompts is a hypothesis in disguise. The onset separates the sudden from the gradual, the location narrows the anatomy, the character suggests the tissue, the aggravating and relieving factors test the mechanism, the timing exposes the pattern, and the severity estimates the effect on the patient's life. A framework of this kind was assembled from the way clinicians reason in a particular tradition, so it organizes the common presentations well and can quietly miss the unusual one. The clinician should therefore use it as a net and not as a cage, returning to the patient's own words whenever the framework and the story disagree.

When the complaint is pain, the Socrates framework gives the same discipline a sharper edge. It directs the clinician to the site of the pain, its onset, its character, whether it radiates and where, what accompanies it, how it varies with time, what provokes or relieves it, and how severe it is. The reasoning behind each element is easy to state. Radiation describes the path of a shared nerve supply, so it tells the clinician which structures might be implicated together. Associated features such as sweating, nausea, or breathlessness describe the response of the whole body rather than the site alone. Timing separates the pain that is constant from the pain that comes in waves, and that distinction changes the list of causes before any test is ordered. The framework is a memory aid for the questions a careful clinician would ask anyway, and it earns its place only when the answers are heard rather than ticked.

Frameworks fail in two characteristic ways. The first is recitation, in which the clinician works through the prompts in order and the patient's story is chopped into a questionnaire, so the meaning that held the story together is lost. The second is omission, in which the clinician trusts the framework so completely that a feature the framework does not contain is never explored. Both failures come from the same mistake, which is treating a tool for organizing a story as a substitute for hearing it. It is also worth saying plainly that such frameworks were derived in particular populations and particular settings, and their usefulness elsewhere is an assumption rather than a fact. They are safe only in the hands of a clinician who remains willing to be surprised by the patient in front of him.

Silence is a diagnostic instrument, and most clinicians underuse it. After a patient finishes an answer, the temptation is to fill the pause, and the pause is frequently where the most important sentence is waiting. A patient who hesitates is usually deciding whether the next detail is worth mentioning, and a clinician who speaks first makes that decision for him, in the negative. Silence of a few seconds is not an awkward gap in the interview; it is an invitation, and patients accept it more often than clinicians expect. The clinician who can tolerate silence learns things that no question would have produced. It is one of the few diagnostic skills that costs nothing but composure.

Interruption is the opposite instrument, and it is nearly always a loss. A patient who is cut off in the opening sentence may never return to the thing he was about to say, and he will often read the interruption as a judgment that the detail was unimportant. Interrupting also teaches the patient a style of answering, so that the rest of the interview becomes a series of short replies to the clinician's agenda. This is efficient in appearance and expensive in content. A clinician who notices himself interrupting should stop, apologize briefly, and hand the floor back. The few seconds recovered by the interruption are rarely worth the information that was cancelled.

There is a particular value in the question that is asked twice, because the first answer is often a performance and the second is the truth. Asked whether he drinks alcohol, a patient may answer with the amount he believes is socially acceptable, and asked later, in a different way and with a different reason attached, he may answer more accurately. Asked whether the pain is the worst he has ever felt, a patient may say yes to convey how much it hurts, and asked again after a pause, may distinguish between the worst pain and the most frightening one. The repetition must be deliberate and unaccusing, because a patient who feels caught will close rather than open. Asking the same content in new words is also a check on the clinician's own hearing, which is fallible in a noisy room and a tired mind. A clinician should never be embarrassed to ask again; the second answer frequently changes the diagnosis.

Every question in a good history has a reason behind it, and the reason should be visible to the clinician even when it is never spoken. A question about night sweats is asked because a chronic infection or a malignant disease can announce itself at night, and the clinician who knows that will listen differently to the answer. A question about waking at night with breathlessness is asked because it points to the heart rather than to the lung. A question about a change in shoe size or hat size is asked because a slow hormonal illness enlarges the tissues. When the reason is known, the follow-up question arrives naturally. When the reason is forgotten, the answer is recorded and not used.

The line between an acute illness and a chronic one is drawn by time, and time is one of the first things the history establishes. A complaint present for 3 days invites a different list from the same complaint present for 3 months, even when the words used to describe it are identical. Treat that interval as an illustration of the reasoning rather than a rule, and confirm the figure against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the boundary between acute and chronic is drawn differently in different services. The direction of travel matters more than the label: a symptom that is stable, worsening, or improving changes the urgency of the whole encounter. The clinician should ask not only how long but what has happened during that time. A timeline that is reconstructed carefully will often exclude a diagnosis before any test is ordered.

On a quiet ward, the history can be taken in the way it deserves. The clinician sits at the same level as the patient, keeps the notes closed for the first minutes, and lets the account run without looking at a screen. The room is arranged so that the patient is not straining to be heard and the clinician is not standing over the bed. The full present illness is reconstructed, the frameworks are used quietly in the clinician's own mind, and the summary is read back before the examination begins. The examination is then planned around what the story suggested rather than performed as a fixed tour. In such a setting the clinician has time to be thorough, and the temptation is to be thorough about the wrong things: many questions, little listening.

In a crowded clinic the same work must be done inside a few minutes, and this is a different craft rather than a lesser one. Suppose that 3 minutes are all that is available for the conversation, and treat that figure as an illustration of scarcity, because what a service can permit varies between countries and between clinics, so check the local arrangement rather than a remembered figure. Within that limit the clinician still opens with an open question, because it costs almost nothing and returns the patient's own frame. What changes is the triage of attention: the dangerous possibilities are asked about first, the story is allowed to run only as far as it is productive, and the closed questions are chosen for what would change the decision. The summary read back is shortened to a single sentence. A clinician working at this speed must know in advance which questions he will never omit, and that list is personal and must be practised.

In a clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, the history carries even more of the diagnostic load, because there is no laboratory to fall back on and no imaging to defer to. Here the clinician cannot order his way out of uncertainty, so the questions about duration, trajectory, exposure, and contact become the tests. A fever with a clear story of where it came from, or of who else is ill at home, may be diagnosable without a single measurement. A cough with a history that names the exposure may be named without a radiograph. The absence of equipment does not make the reasoning weaker, it makes it more visible, and the clinician who is honest about uncertainty does better than the one who pretends to a certainty he cannot reach. Where the journey back is long, the history also sets the safety net, because the patient must be told what would bring him back.

In a well-resourced hospital the opposite error is available. Because a scan or a blood test can be ordered within the hour, the history can be shortened without appearing to fail, and the machine is asked to answer a question the clinician never formulated. This is the source of a great deal of unnecessary investigation, and of the occasional diagnosis missed because nobody took the story that would have directed the scan to the right place. A computed tomography scan of the head performed on a patient whose history was never heard may be normal and irrelevant at the same time. The history is what tells the clinician which test is worth doing, in which order, and what a normal result would mean. Riches do not remove the need to think; they only make it easier to postpone.

The order of the encounter is itself a diagnostic decision. The history comes first because the hypothesis must exist before the hands arrive, and an examination performed on a mind with no question in it becomes a search for something to believe. If the clinician examines first and asks afterwards, the findings are free to be interpreted in the light of whatever the examination suggested, and the story is then bent to fit them. A prediction made from the story before the examination is a small act of honesty, and it is what makes the examination evidence rather than decoration. This is why the habit of examining while the history is still being taken, however convenient, damages the reasoning. Listen, form the question, and only then look.

A constructed illustration may make this concrete, and it is a teaching device rather than a record of any real person. A patient arrives with a fever that has lasted several days and no localizing complaint, and the examination is unremarkable. The rushed response is to order a broad set of tests, or to give a treatment and observe. The history, taken properly, asks what the patient does for a living, whether he has been in contact with anyone ill, whether he has traveled, whether an animal has bitten or scratched him, and whether he has had a procedure or an injection recently. One of those answers may name the source and change the whole plan. The examination was not wrong; it was simply silent, and the history was the only instrument that could speak.

Another constructed illustration shows the same principle in a dangerous direction. A patient describes pain in the upper abdomen that came on suddenly and has been constant, with a history of heavy alcohol use and a pulse of 110 beats per minute. The pulse supports the story but does not prove it, and the number that should quicken the clinician's questions is not fixed, so verify the rate your own service treats as abnormal against the current guideline and the local protocol. The examination may be unimpressive in the early hours, while the history of sudden onset, constant severe pain, and a background that makes a complication likely points to a diagnosis that can kill. The clinician who trusts a soft abdomen and a normal temperature in the first hours without hearing the story has been reassured by the wrong evidence. A temperature of 38 degrees Celsius would be a fever in many services, and a normal reading in the same patient would not remove the concern, so check what your own protocol calls a fever rather than resting on a remembered figure.

Closing the history with a summary read back to the patient is a diagnostic test on the clinician's own understanding. The clinician states the story in a few sentences, in the patient's own terms, and asks whether it is right. The patient will correct a date, a sequence, or a severity that the clinician had assumed, and each correction is information that would otherwise have been carried forward as error. The summary also shows the patient that he has been heard, which changes what he is willing to add. It is worth leaving a deliberate pause after the summary, because the correction often arrives a moment after the agreement. A history is not finished until the patient has heard it returned.

The history has limits that should be named as clearly as its power. Memory is reconstructive, and patients under stress compress, reorder, and sometimes misattribute what happened. Fear changes what is reported, and so does the wish not to be a burden or not to seem weak. Some patients do not know the answer, because they were unconscious, very young, or living with a cognitive impairment. Others answer with what they believe the clinician wants to hear. The clinician should therefore treat the history as strong evidence and not as proof, and should look for corroboration where it exists. A history that conflicts with a reliable observation deserves to be investigated rather than dismissed in either direction.

How a symptom is expressed varies with age, language, culture, and experience, and the clinician who mistakes the expression for the disease will misjudge the severity. Pain may be described as pressure, tightness, or heat rather than as pain. Distress may be shown through the family rather than by the patient. Infants and older adults may show illness as irritability, poor feeding, or a change in behavior rather than as a named symptom. The clinician must therefore ask what the symptom prevents the patient from doing, because function is more comparable between people than vocabulary. The same question about the same complaint can mean very different things in different mouths, and the history is only as good as the clinician's awareness of that.

Numbers belong to the history as much as to the examination, and they should be heard as measurements with uncertainty rather than as verdicts. A blood pressure of 90 millimeters of mercury recorded at home may be the most important number the patient brings, and it must be interpreted against his usual value rather than against a single remembered threshold, so confirm the cut-off that matters in the current guideline and the local protocol, because the value that defines a low pressure is revised between editions. A weight that has fallen without effort is a finding, and the interval over which it fell changes its meaning. A temperature taken at home and reported as high is weaker evidence than one the clinician measures, but it is not nothing. The clinician should ask who measured the number, with what instrument, and when. A number without its context is a fact that has lost its meaning.

A history can also mislead, and the clinician should expect it to happen. Patients attribute symptoms to causes they know, so a smoker may call breathlessness age and a patient with a family history may call indigestion the family illness. A dramatic event in the recent past can capture the explanation and hide a quiet one that started earlier. The clinician who notices that the story explains only part of the picture should keep the remainder open rather than force it into the same shape. Re-asking is not a sign of a failed interview; it is part of a competent one. The best protection against a misleading history is a second account, taken later, and a willingness to revise.

Time is a resource that is allocated by systems rather than by clinicians alone, and the pressure of a full waiting room is real. The response to that pressure should be to protect the few minutes that carry the most diagnostic weight, not to abandon the method. A clinician can decide in advance which questions he will always ask, which dangerous possibilities he will always consider, and what he will say to a patient he cannot fully assess today. That decision, made calmly, is worth more than speed improvised under pressure. The clinician who names his uncertainty to the patient and arranges a way back is practising good medicine, not failing at it. Good history taking is not a luxury of well-funded systems; it is the part of medicine that travels best.

None of the frameworks, questions, or figures named in this chapter is a rule that can be applied without thought, and none of them replaces the judgment of the clinician who is with the patient. Every threshold, interval, and measurement mentioned here is an illustration of a way of thinking and must be confirmed in the current guideline and the local protocol of your own service before it influences a real decision. The account a patient gives is the beginning of diagnosis, not its end, and it is always to be weighed with the examination, the available tests, and the passage of time. This material is educational only, and it is not medical advice, not a protocol, and not a substitute for training, supervision, or local practice. Where a patient is deteriorating, the interview pauses and the patient is treated, because no question is worth the delay of someone who is becoming more ill.

To summarize, the history is the highest-yield instrument in medicine, and it is taken by opening the story wide, hearing the patient's own words, and narrowing only when the account is complete. The Socrates framework for pain and the Oldcarts framework for the present illness organize the questions, but they are scaffolds and not questionnaires, and they exclude and assume as much as they reveal. Silence earns more than interruption, the second answer is often truer than the first, and dangerous possibilities such as bleeding around the brain or a tear in the aorta announce themselves in the manner of onset. The same work is done differently on a quiet ward and in a crowded clinic where only a few minutes exist, but the questions and the priority of danger do not change. Every number, interval, and threshold in this chapter is an illustration that must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between countries, editions, and laboratories. This is educational material, it is not a substitute for training, supervision, clinical judgment, or local protocol, and no patient should be managed on the strength of a chapter alone.
