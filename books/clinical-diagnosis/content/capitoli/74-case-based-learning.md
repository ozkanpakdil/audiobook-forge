# Chapter 74 — Case-Based Learning

Case-based learning is the habit of taking a clinical presentation apart so that the reasoning becomes visible instead of staying buried in the conclusion. Its subject is a method rather than a disease, and its organizing idea is the branch point, the place in the story where the reasoning could have gone one way and went another. A book of principles can tell a listener what ought to be done, while a worked case shows where the ought is lost in the noise of a real morning. Four failures carry most of the teaching, and they are the differential that was too narrow, the test that was ordered too early, the sign that was missed, and the diagnosis that was correct but reached by luck. Every case here is a constructed illustration rather than a portrait of any identifiable patient. The material is educational, and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of the service in which the patient is seen.

Before any case is opened, the lethal possibilities that recur across presentations should be named with the finding that reveals each. A patient with chest discomfort may be having an acute myocardial infarction, and the revealing finding is a tracing that shows injury or a rise in the substances released by damaged heart muscle, although a normal examination proves nothing. A patient with fever and a change in behavior may have meningitis, and the revealing finding is resistance when the neck is flexed, sometimes with a rash that does not fade under pressure. A woman of childbearing age with abdominal pain and a missed period may have a pregnancy outside the uterus, and the revealing finding is a positive pregnancy test together with evidence of blood in the abdomen. A patient who becomes suddenly breathless may have a clot in the lung, and the revealing finding may be a swollen calf, although the leg can look entirely normal. These causes are named early because a case is a rehearsal for a real encounter, and the rehearsal should rehearse the danger first.

Three decisions about method are worth stating before the cases begin. Every case below is a constructed illustration, assembled to make a point and not drawn from any identifiable patient, so no detail in it should be matched to a real person. Each case is read twice, once with the instruments of a well-resourced hospital and once with the stethoscope, the torch, and the thermometer of a small clinic, because the instruments change and the reasoning does not. The listener should also build cases of his own, since a clinician who can construct a plausible case learns to see the branch points in the patients he actually meets. In a real encounter the branch point is never labeled, and learning to see it in a case is the first step in seeing it while the patient is still talking.

The first constructed case concerns a man in his sixties who comes to a clinic because of a burning discomfort behind the breastbone that has troubled him on and off for weeks. He says it comes on when he hurries and goes away when he stops, and he has decided that it is his stomach, because it improves when he belches and because a friend told him that heartburn behaves in this way. He takes a tablet for his stomach from time to time and says that it helps a little. He does not mention that he has diabetes, that he smoked for many years, or that his father died suddenly at about his own age, because nobody has asked him a question that would bring those facts out. The first words of the presentation are therefore a story about the stomach, and the branch point is already present in the first minute. A clinician who is listening for the branch point hears the word burning and does not stop there.

The narrow differential is the failure here, and it is easy because the story is coherent. A clinician who accepts the word heartburn has closed the question before examining the patient, and ischemic heart disease is often described as burning rather than crushing, which is why the phrase is dangerous. The revealing findings concern pattern rather than severity, such as discomfort that comes with effort, travels to the jaw or the arm, arrives with sweating, and eases with rest. The dangerous cause is acute coronary ischemia, and it must be named at once, not after the stomach has been treated for weeks. One question separates the two stories, and it is asked before any test, which is what the patient does when the discomfort begins. The patient who stops and waits for it to pass is describing effort angina, and the patient who reaches for an antacid is describing what he believes. The Socrates framework takes that description in a fixed order so that nothing is left out.

In the well-resourced reading, the clinic has an electrocardiogram, or ECG, and a laboratory, and the man is moved to a monitored area rather than sent home. The tracing is performed early and repeated, and the substances released by damaged heart muscle are measured in sequence rather than once, because a single normal result in the first hours settles nothing. A discomfort that has lasted about 20 minutes at rest, or that is new and different from what the patient knows, is treated as acute until it is proved otherwise, and that interval is offered only as an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the timing that matters shifts with the definition in use. The decision to treat before the results return belongs to the clinician who is with the patient, and the finding that would make immediate treatment wrong is a clear alternative cause that explains every part of the story. Even then the patient is observed rather than discharged, because observation is a plan and not a delay.

In the low-resource reading, the man arrives at a clinic that has a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, and no tracing and no laboratory. The history carries more weight because there is nothing to correct it, and the effort relationship is established by asking the patient to walk while he is watched. The examination seeks the signs that change the plan, which are the pulse, the pressure, the sounds of the heart and lungs, the signs of heart failure, and the pulses in the feet. The decision is then not a diagnosis but a journey, and a man with a new or changing exertional discomfort who looks unwell, who is sweating, or whose pulse or pressure is abnormal must travel to a place where a tracing can be done. The finding that would make the journey unnecessary is a confirmed and fully explanatory alternative cause, and a clinician who writes down what was considered and why the patient was sent onward has done the whole of the work that the setting allows.

The second constructed case concerns a young woman who comes to an emergency department with pain in the lower abdomen that began suddenly on the previous evening. She describes it as sharp and one-sided, with some dizziness when she stands, and she has had a small amount of vaginal bleeding that she took for an early period. She is pale, her pulse is quick, and she says she feels as though she might faint. She does not volunteer that her period is late by several weeks, and the triage note records pain and bleeding and nothing else. The clinician who reads only the note begins to think of an infection of the urine or a problem of the bowel, and the dangerous possibility drifts further away with every minute that the wrong question is asked.

The test ordered too early is the failure here, and it is subtle because the test itself was not wrong. The clinician sent her for an ultrasound of the kidneys and bladder before asking whether she could be pregnant, and the scan showed a little free fluid that was called normal. The decisive step, a pregnancy test, came hours later, after her pressure had fallen, and the delay changed everything. The dangerous cause is a pregnancy outside the uterus, which can bleed into the abdomen and kill, and the revealing findings are a positive pregnancy test, an empty uterus on imaging, free fluid, and a falling blood count. The sequence of tests is itself a clinical decision, and the cheapest question that would reframe the assessment should come first. A test is never neutral, because it consumes time, shapes the thinking that follows, and can be believed more than the patient is. The right order is therefore whether she could be pregnant, then whether she is bleeding internally, and only then what else this might be.

In the well-resourced reading, the pregnancy test is performed at the first contact, a line is placed into a vein, the blood count and the blood group are sent, and an ultrasound of the pelvis is requested with the specific question of whether there is a pregnancy inside the uterus. The patient is observed where her pressure can be checked repeatedly, and the finding that would prompt immediate surgical help is a falling pressure, worsening pain, or a growing collection of blood in the abdomen. The finding that would allow a quieter path is a stable patient with a negative pregnancy test and an alternative explanation that accounts for every symptom. The hospital has no special insight into this young woman, only the ability to measure the direction of her illness minute by minute and to act on what it measures. That ability is wasted when the first question has not been asked.

In the low-resource reading, the clinic has no laboratory and no scanner, and the single most valuable instrument is a question asked without embarrassment. A pregnancy test, where one is available, is worth more than any other test in this situation, and where it is not available the reasoning rests on the missed period, the pain, the dizziness, the bleeding, and the signs of internal loss. The clinician looks for a rapid pulse, a pressure that falls when the patient sits up, pallor of the inner eyelids and the palms, tenderness in the lower abdomen, and pain felt when the cervix is moved. The decision that follows is not a diagnosis but an evacuation toward surgical care, begun before the diagnosis is certain, because a woman who may be bleeding into her abdomen has no time to spare. The finding that would change that decision is a clearly stable patient with a benign and fully explanatory cause, and even then the plan is review and a short leash.

The third constructed case concerns an older man brought to a hospital because of severe pain in the lower back that came on suddenly while he was lifting a box. He has felt unwell since, has vomited once, and is restless in a way that the nurse finds difficult to describe. His skin is clammy, his pulse is fast, and the pain is not changed by movement of the spine in the way that a muscular strain would be. He is given a medicine for the pain and is left to wait for a radiograph of the spine. The important finding was available at the bedside to anyone who placed a hand on his abdomen, and the clinician who orders the radiograph first has asked the machine a question that the hand could have answered sooner.

The missed sign is the failure here, and it is a pulsating, expansile mass in the middle of the abdomen, felt slightly left of the midline, with a murmur over it. The dangerous cause is a leaking aneurysm of the abdominal aorta, which can rupture and kill within minutes, and the revealing findings are that mass, pain running into the back, a falling pressure, and a difference between the pulses in the two legs. The diagnosis is made with the hands, and no image is needed to justify calling for senior help and surgical care at once. A clinician who attributes back pain in an older man to muscular strain without palpating the abdomen has not examined the patient, whatever was written down. Any older patient with sudden severe back pain, especially one who smokes or has high pressure, should be considered to have an arterial cause until the abdomen has been felt. The examination is gentle, with the flat of the hand, and the diagnosis is unlikely when the aorta feels soft and no wider than expected and the pain is reproduced by movement.

In the well-resourced reading, the man is moved to a resuscitation area, the pressure is measured in both arms, a line is placed into a vein, and blood is sent for the count and for matching while a bedside ultrasound and a computed tomography, or CT, of the aorta are arranged. A pressure below a figure such as 85 millimeters of mercury is treated as a warning that rupture may be under way, and that figure is only a teaching illustration and must be reconciled with the current guideline and the local protocol, since the pressure that should alarm depends on the patient's own usual value. The patient is kept still, and the surgical team is called before the imaging is complete, because the image confirms what the hands have found. The finding that would change the plan is a mass that is clearly not arterial, and the finding that would make a slower path safe is a stable patient with a normal aorta on imaging. Speed matters here, and it is speed in the right direction only when the right question has been asked.

In the low-resource reading, there is no scanner and no operating theater, and the reasoning compresses into a single decision about whether this patient needs to travel now and how he can travel without dying on the way. The abdomen is examined for the pulsatile mass, the pulses in the groin and the feet are compared, the pressure is taken sitting and lying, and the color and the level of consciousness are recorded. The absence of imaging does not weaken the case for transfer, and a clinician who waits for confirmation that will never arrive loses the patient. The finding that justifies immediate transfer is the pulsatile mass with pain and a fall in pressure, and the finding that would make watchful review reasonable is a soft aorta with a mechanical pain. The hand is the most portable instrument in medicine, and the clinic cannot see the aneurysm and does not need to see it in order to act on it.

The fourth constructed case concerns a young man who arrives at a clinic complaining of sudden breathlessness and pain on the right side of the chest that worsens when he breathes in. He is tall and thin, he smoked a little in the past, and he looks anxious but not unwell. The clinician recognizes the picture, remembers a lecture, and treats him for a clot in the lung before any test is done, and the treatment is given with confidence. He improves, and the clinician records the case as a success. The diagnosis may well have been right, and the reasoning that produced it was almost entirely absent.

The correct diagnosis reached by luck is the failure here, and it is the hardest of the four to see because the outcome was good. The same picture can be produced by a collapsed lung, and a tension pneumothorax is lethal within minutes, with the revealing findings of absent breath sounds and increased resonance on one side, a windpipe pushed away from the affected side, and distended veins in the neck. It can also be produced by a myocardial infarction, by an infection of the lung, by a fracture of a rib, and by anxiety alone, and each of those carries a different decision. A clinician who cannot say what would have made him change his mind has not made a diagnosis, because he has made a guess that happened to land. The remedy is to speak the reasoning aloud before treating, naming the diagnosis he is acting on, the finding that supports it, the alternatives that would demand different action, and the observation that would make him stop. A right answer is not evidence of a right process, and the two must be judged separately when a case is reviewed.

In the well-resourced reading, the tracing and the chest radiograph are performed early, the oxygen level in the blood is measured, and the substances released by heart muscle and the test for the breakdown products of a clot are used to answer specific questions. A clinician who has named his hypothesis already knows which result would change his plan, and he repeats the examination after treatment rather than assuming that improvement proves the diagnosis. The finding that would redirect the whole assessment is a lung that does not move air on one side, and the response to that finding is immediate and physical rather than pharmaceutical. The hospital's power lies in the speed with which it can test a thought, and that speed is wasted when there is no thought to test. The tests are servants of the reasoning, and they become masters when the reasoning is absent.

In the low-resource reading, the same young man is examined with the eyes, the hands, and the ear, and much of the decisive information comes without any instrument. The clinician watches both sides of the chest move, listens over each side while the patient breathes through the mouth, taps the chest to compare the note, and feels the windpipe above the breastbone. The breathing rate is counted over a full minute and compared with a later count, and a rate that climbs, such as one reaching 30 breaths per minute, is an illustration of the reasoning to be checked against the guideline in force and the protocol of the local service, since the line between a comfortable and an alarming rate shifts with age and fitness. The decision is whether to treat on the clinical picture or to send the patient onward, and a patient whose air entry is absent on one side travels at once. Where a trained clinician can place a needle safely and the picture is a tension pneumothorax, the decision to relieve it is made on the examination alone, because waiting for an image is waiting for permission that arrives too late.

The differential that was too narrow deserves a paragraph of its own, because it is the failure that most often follows a correct and confident first impression. Anchoring begins with a plausible explanation and then bends the subsequent history toward it, so that the question about smoking is never asked and the answer about the walk is never heard. The remedy is structural rather than moral, because the clinician writes down at least one alternative that would change the plan and asks at least one question whose answer could break his first idea. The Vindicate framework is a way of forcing the field open, since it walks through the vascular, infectious, neoplastic, degenerative, intoxication, congenital, autoimmune, traumatic, and endocrine possibilities in turn. Using it on a case after the fact shows how many doors were never opened, and using it at the bedside keeps them open. A differential is not a list to be admired but a set of plans, each of which would be different.

The sign that was missed is the third failure mode, and it is usually missed because it was never sought rather than because it was hidden. The remedy is to make the examination deliberately complete at least once, in a fixed order, so that the parts easy to forget are performed whether or not the story points to them. The neck, the abdomen, the groin, the back, the perineum, the pressure areas, and every line and tube placed in the body are examined directly, because these are the places where evidence waits without announcing itself. Palpating the abdomen of every older patient with back pain is not an excess of caution, but the difference between a diagnosis made with the hands and a diagnosis missed with them. The clinician should record the findings that were sought and not only the ones that were found, because a note that says the abdomen was soft and the aorta of normal width tells the next reader that the question was asked. A complete examination performed once, deliberately, is worth more than many partial ones performed in a hurry.

Reviewing one's own cases begins with a written record made at the time. The note should carry the reasoning and not only the findings, which means what the clinician thought the problem was, what else it might have been, what was looked for and not found, and what was expected to happen. Soap notes are one way of arranging the record so that reasoning sits beside the findings and the plan, and a note that states the working diagnosis and the findings that would overturn it becomes a lesson in a few lines. Return to the case after the outcome is known and reconstruct the branch points in order, asking at each one what information was available and what was done with it. The review is best done against fixed questions, because a question not asked in advance is answered by memory in whatever way flatters the reviewer. Was the dangerous possibility named at the bedside, what finding would have revealed it, and was the differential wide enough to include a plan that differed.

Constructing cases for teaching carries obligations, and they are the same obligations that govern any discussion of a patient. The case must be explicitly described as constructed, its details must be altered so that no individual can be recognized, and the clinical content must be plausible enough to be worth rehearsing. A case that depends on a bizarre detail teaches the listener to look for the bizarre, while a case built from ordinary material teaches the pattern that is actually met. The instructor should decide in advance which failure mode the case is meant to demonstrate, and should leave the branch point visible without pointing at it. When the case is discussed, the first question should be about the reasoning and not about the answer, because the answer is the least transferable part of the exercise. A teaching case that ends with a diagnosis and no branch points has taught a fact, and facts are not what this method exists to depend on.

Case review is also where the numbers used in practice should be examined, because a figure carried from a course into a clinic can do quiet harm. A score, a cut-off, a reference range, a pressure, a temperature, a rate, or a duration named in a discussion is an illustration of the reasoning, and the reviewer should ask where it came from and in which population it was derived. Check every such figure against the guideline currently in force and the protocol of the local service and laboratory, because the same measurement is reported differently from one country to the next and the editions do not agree. A number whose origin cannot be stated should not decide anything about a person. In a clinic with no laboratory the review is conducted from memory unless the clinician keeps a simple log, and a log is worth the effort. A few lines for each patient, written the same day, recording the presentation, the reasoning, the decision, and what happened, will after a year hold more teaching than many textbooks. The instrument is unimportant, and the regularity is not.

The limits of case-based learning should be stated as plainly as its uses, so that it is not mistaken for the whole of clinical education. A case is a reconstruction, and a reconstruction is cleaner than the event it describes, because the irrelevant details have been removed and the chronology tidied. The listener learns to reason about described patients, and the skill that remains is the one that cannot easily be described, which is being present with a person who is frightened, uncomfortable, or unable to explain. That skill is learned at the bedside under supervision, and no case can replace it. A clinician who wishes to review his own cases can begin with one habit, which is to write down the reasoning at the time and return to it after the outcome is known. He then asks where the branch points were, which failure mode appeared, and what he would do differently with the same information and no more. Done regularly, this turns every case into a lesson, and it is the only form of case-based learning that a clinician can practice alone.

To summarize, case-based learning is the deliberate reconstruction of an encounter so that the reasoning becomes visible, and its organizing idea is the branch point where the story could have turned. Four failure modes carry most of the teaching: the differential that was too narrow, the test ordered too early, the sign that was missed, and the diagnosis correct but reached by luck. The constructed cases here are illustrations only, never drawn from an identifiable patient, and each is read in a well-resourced hospital and in a clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, where the instruments change and the reasoning does not. The lethal possibilities are named where the presentation is described, with the finding that reveals each, because a rehearsal should rehearse the danger first. Every score, cut-off, range, pressure, temperature, rate, and duration mentioned here is an illustration rather than a rule, and each must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures vary between countries, editions, and laboratories. This chapter is educational material, and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of the service in which the patient is seen.
