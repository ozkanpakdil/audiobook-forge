# Chapter 65 — ECG Interpretation

The electrocardiogram, or ECG, records the small electrical currents that spread through the heart with each beat, and it is the most widely available picture of the heart in the world. It does not show the muscle but the order and speed of the electrical wave, so a tracing can be normal in a failing heart and grossly abnormal in one that is coping. Its value lies in the questions it answers at once: Is the rhythm too fast, too slow, or disordered? Is a coronary artery blocked? Has a salt disturbance changed conduction? Because those answers change what is done next, the tracing must be read by a fixed sequence, so that the second abnormality is not lost behind the first. The sequence is rate, rhythm, axis, intervals, the chambers, the segments, and the comparison with any earlier tracing. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the local rules of your own service.

Six patterns must be named before any comfortable explanation is accepted, because each changes what is done at once. Acute coronary occlusion appears as elevation of the segment joining the sharp ventricular complex to the T wave, sometimes with the T wave turned upside down in the views facing the injured wall. A wide complex tachycardia, a fast rhythm whose ventricular complexes are broad, must be treated as ventricular in origin until the tracing and the patient prove otherwise. Complete heart block shows P waves marching at their own rate while the ventricles beat slowly and independently. A high level of potassium first gives tall, narrow, peaked T waves, then a widening ventricular complex and a fading P wave. A clot in the lungs usually gives nothing more than a fast sinus rhythm with strain on the right side of the heart, which is why it is so easily dismissed. The sixth is an abnormally long interval from the start of the ventricular complex to the end of the T wave, the door through which a chaotic rhythm can enter.

The reading begins with the rate, because everything else is judged against it. The rate is counted from the distance between two ventricular complexes, and counting matters because the eye fails when the rhythm is irregular. A fast rate may be compensation for anemia, fever, pain, or a failing heart, and it may also be the disease itself. A slow rate may be normal in a fit young person and lethal in an older one, so the number alone decides nothing. A rate below 60 beats per minute is often called slow and a rate above 100 beats per minute is often called fast, but those lines are illustrations of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the accepted range shifts with age, fitness, pregnancy, and edition. What matters more is whether the rate is keeping the pressure up.

The rhythm is read next, because the rate means little without knowing where the beats come from. A normal rhythm starts in the sinus node, the natural pacemaker in the upper right atrium, travels through the atria, pauses at the junction, and spreads through the ventricles. On the paper this gives a small P wave before every sharp ventricular complex at a constant distance. If each ventricular complex follows a P wave at the same distance, the rhythm is likely sinus. If the P waves are absent or appear at their own rate, the rhythm is generated elsewhere, and the clinician must ask whether that place lies above or below the ventricles, because that is what makes a rhythm dangerous. An irregularly irregular rhythm, with beats falling without pattern, is the signature of atrial fibrillation, and the hand on the pulse feels it too. A fast regular rhythm may instead be an abnormal circuit in the atria, and the P waves often separate the two.

The axis describes the average direction in which the electrical wave travels through the ventricles, read by noticing which recorded views show an upward deflection and which show a downward one. The wave normally travels downward and toward the patient's left. When it turns away from the left, the axis is deviated to the left, often the footprint of damaged conduction, a thick left ventricle, or an unusual route. When it turns toward the right, the axis is deviated to the right, which may mean a thick right ventricle, a clot in the lungs, a damaged left side, or simply a tall, thin, young person. The axis is rarely a diagnosis, but it is a direction sign that tells the clinician where to look next. A change compared with an earlier tracing means far more than any single measurement, and because the normal range depends on age and build, its boundary should be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

The intervals are the pauses within the beat, and they are where conduction disease announces itself. The interval from the beginning of the P wave to the beginning of the ventricular complex measures how long the impulse takes to travel from atria to ventricles; it is normally shorter than about 0.2 seconds, and a longer one means delay at the junction. That figure is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the upper limit is stated differently in different texts and changes with age. A delay that lengthens with every beat until a ventricular complex is dropped warns that complete block may be coming. The width of the ventricular complex is normally narrower than about 0.12 seconds, and a broader complex means the wave is traveling by a slow route, which is the most useful clue to whether a fast rhythm is dangerous; check that limit against the current guideline and the local protocol, since it moves with age and with the machine. The interval from the ventricular complex to the end of the T wave, when long, allows a second wave to arrive while the ventricles recover, and its limit depends on rate and sex, so confirm it locally.

The chambers are examined next, because a heart that has worked against a load for years leaves its mark. A thickened ventricle produces taller deflections on the side where it lies, telling the clinician that the heart has faced a resistance from high pressure, a narrowed valve, or lung disease that has raised right-sided pressure. An enlarged atrium produces a broader or taller P wave in the views that face it, a clue that the atrium has been stretched by a valve lesion or a long irregular rhythm. These signs do not diagnose the cause; they send the clinician to look for one. They are also easy to overread, since youth, thinness, and athletic training produce tall deflections in a healthy heart. A tracing suggesting a thickened ventricle in a breathless patient is a reason to examine again, not a diagnosis in itself.

The segments are the flat stretches between the waves, and they are where ischemia, injury, and inflammation are written. The segment joining the sharp ventricular complex to the T wave is studied first, because a sudden coronary blockage usually changes it before anything else. Elevation of that segment, especially when limited to the views facing one region and accompanied by the opposite change on the far side, says that a coronary artery is occluded now. Depression of the same segment, or an upside-down T wave, speaks of muscle starved of blood but not yet dying. The T wave also changes with salt disturbance, inflammation of the sac around the heart, stroke, and many innocent variations, so it must be read with the patient present. The dangerous error is to dismiss an elevation as a variant in a patient with chest pain who looks unwell, and the safe habit is to compare with any earlier tracing and repeat the recording while symptoms are present.

The last step is the comparison with a previous tracing, and it most often turns an uncertain reading into a clear one. A deflection that looks abnormal alone may have been there for years, and a genuinely new abnormality may be subtle without anything to compare it with. The clinician should ask for the old tracing before concluding anything and place the two side by side in the same light and at the same calibration. Has the axis changed, has a segment moved, has the complex become wider, has a new rhythm appeared? A small change is more important than a large fixed abnormality that the patient has carried harmlessly for a decade. Where no earlier tracing exists, that absence is a real limitation on the reading and should be recorded as one.

Before any of this, the tracing must be trusted, and the first questions concern the paper and the patient rather than the heart. The name and the time must be checked, because a tracing belonging to another patient is not a rare error. The calibration matters, since the height given to a known voltage and the width given to a known time determine how every measurement is read. In many services that means 10 millimeters for each millivolt and 25 millimeters per second, but these are conventions rather than laws, so confirm them against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the machine, the paper, and the edition of the manual differ between services. Electrode positions matter, because an electrode placed too high or too low produces a pattern that imitates disease and repeats the error on every later tracing. Movement, shivering, a loose electrode, and nearby electrical equipment produce wandering or jagged lines that hide real abnormality. A tracing that cannot be trusted should be repeated rather than interpreted.

Acute coronary occlusion deserves its own reading, because it most often changes what happens at once. The tracing shows elevation of the segment between the ventricular complex and the T wave in the views facing the blocked vessel, and often the opposite depression on the far side. It can be abnormal before the pain is severe and normal while the pain is severe, so neither symptom nor paper is sufficient alone. Inflammation of the sac around the heart most often imitates it, producing widespread rather than regional elevation, often with fever and a rub heard through the stethoscope. An old scar from a previous infarction can hold a segment up for years, which is why comparison matters so much. Where no earlier tracing exists, repeat the recording and look for movement over time. The decision is whether to restore blood flow urgently, and the finding that makes it right is a new regional pattern supported by the story and the examination; a convincing alternative explanation in a patient who looks well makes urgent treatment wrong.

A fast rhythm with wide ventricular complexes is the second emergency, and its reasoning rests on one principle. A wide complex means the wave is traveling slowly through the ventricles, and the most dangerous explanation is that the rhythm arises inside the ventricles, where it can degenerate into a rhythm that pumps no blood. It can also arise above the ventricles and travel down a damaged pathway, and the two may look identical on one sheet. A dissociation between atria and ventricles, with P waves at their own rate bearing no fixed relationship to the complexes, supports a ventricular origin, as does a fusion beat, though it may appear only briefly. Because the distinction can be impossible to make, the safe rule is to treat a wide complex tachycardia as ventricular until proved otherwise, and to treat the patient rather than the paper. The patient who is unconscious, whose pressure has fallen, or who has chest pain is treated at once, and the exact rhythm is named afterwards.

Complete heart block is the third pattern that must be recognized without delay, and it is found by looking at the relationship between atria and ventricles. The P waves continue at a steady rate of their own while the ventricular complexes continue at a much slower rate of their own, and the two never relate to each other. The patient may feel dizzy, may faint, or may have no symptoms, and the danger is that the slow rhythm can stop without warning. The cause may be age-related disease of the conduction tissue, a heart attack damaging the junction, infection, a medicine, or a salt disturbance, so the tracing opens questions rather than closing them. The clinician must decide about continuous monitoring and urgent help, and a slow rate with a faint, a falling pressure, or altered consciousness makes that decision right. A rhythm dropping every second or third beat behaves similarly, and where no monitoring exists the patient must be moved to where there is some, with the tracing traveling alongside.

A high level of potassium in the blood, called hyperkalemia, is the fourth emergency and the one most easily missed, because its first changes are subtle. The earliest finding is a tall, narrow, peaked T wave like a tent, appearing before the patient feels anything. As the level rises, the P wave shrinks and vanishes, the interval from the P wave to the ventricular complex lengthens, and the ventricular complex widens until it merges with the T wave. The rhythm can then slow, become chaotic, or stop, even in a patient who is talking and feels relatively well. Potassium governs how heart muscle returns to its resting state after each beat, and an excess delays that return until conduction fails. Those at risk include patients with failing kidneys, those taking medicines that raise potassium, those with diabetes, and those with severe tissue injury or infection, and the blood test reveals the cause. Where no test exists, a peaked T wave in a patient with a failing kidney or a long illness is an urgent warning. Whether to act on the tracing before the laboratory confirms it depends on how ill the patient is.

The pattern said to accompany a clot in the lungs, called pulmonary embolism, is the fifth emergency, and it is important to understand what it does and does not mean. The commonest finding is simply a fast sinus rhythm, which is not specific, and a normal tracing does not exclude a clot. In a minority, the right side of the heart shows sudden strain, with a tall P wave, axis turned right, and an upside-down T wave in the right-facing views. That classic pattern is rare, and a clinician who waits for it will miss the diagnosis in most patients. The reasoning therefore runs from the patient to the tracing, not the reverse: sudden breathlessness, a fast pulse, chest pain worse on breathing, and a tender or swollen calf raise the probability, and the tracing is used to look for support and alternatives. The finding that would reveal a large clot is sudden right-sided strain in a patient with none of the ordinary lung findings. Where imaging or a laboratory exists the decision is made with them, and where it does not, clinical probability carries the whole weight.

The sixth emergency is the abnormally long interval from the ventricular complex to the end of the T wave, dangerous because it creates the conditions for a chaotic rhythm that can end in death. When the ventricles take too long to recover, a second electrical wave can arrive while they are refractory in some places and ready in others, producing a twisting rhythm that pumps nothing useful. On the paper the interval is visibly stretched, and the T wave may be flat, notched, or followed by a small extra bump. Causes include low potassium, low calcium, low magnesium, certain heart medicines, some antibiotics, some medicines used for mental illness, vomiting and diarrhea, and starvation, and several may coexist. A blood test for the salts and a review of every medicine, including anything bought without a prescription, reveals the cause. Treatment means correcting the cause and withdrawing the offending medicine, and the finding that makes that right is a long interval together with fainting or a chaotic rhythm in the history.

Beyond the emergencies, the tracing answers ordinary questions and opens windows on other systems. Atrial fibrillation produces an irregularly irregular ventricular rate with no consistent P waves, and it matters for the clots it allows to form and for the strain on a heart that has lost atrial filling. A slow rhythm may come from a failing sinus node, a medicine, an underactive thyroid gland, or raised pressure inside the head, and each has a different answer. Low potassium produces a flat T wave with a small extra wave after it, while low calcium stretches the interval from the ventricular complex to the T wave without widening the complex. A medicine that slows conduction, taken in excess or with a failing kidney, produces a characteristic sagging of the segment between the complex and the T wave and a variety of slow rhythms. A large clot in the lungs, a tumor pressing on the heart, fluid around the heart, and a replaced or assisted heart each leave traces of their own. The tracing is therefore read with the medicine list, the salt results, and the story, never alone, because the ECG is a test and not a diagnosis.

In a patient with chest pain, breathlessness, palpitations, or collapse, the tracing answers a specific question rather than being admired. In chest pain the question is whether a coronary artery is occluded, so the tracing is taken early and repeated, especially during symptoms. In breathlessness the question is whether the strain is right-sided, supporting a clot in the lungs, or left-sided, supporting a failing heart. In palpitations or collapse the question is whether the rhythm is fast, slow, or irregular, and whether its complex width makes it dangerous. In an unconscious patient the tracing comes after the hands have begun cardiopulmonary resuscitation, or CPR, because a pulse check and compressions precede any electrode. The clinician who states the question before looking at the paper is far less likely to be captured by an incidental finding.

In a well-resourced hospital the reading is a shared act, and the conditions around it matter as much as the tracing. A previous tracing is found and placed beside the new one, a senior colleague looks at anything uncertain, and the patient is examined while the paper is studied. The machine is checked for calibration and electrode placement, and the recording is repeated while the symptom is present, since a single tracing is a snapshot of a moment already gone. The laboratory provides the salts, the substances released by damaged heart muscle, and the blood count, each read against the question that prompted it. Continuous monitoring is used when the rhythm is unstable, with alarm limits set by the team rather than left at the default. An ultrasound of the heart follows when the tracing raises a question about structure or function. If the patient is unstable, the team treats first and studies the paper afterwards.

The clinician alone with one tracing and no one to ask must reason differently, and the safe default is to treat the patient rather than the paper. Without an earlier tracing there is no way to tell whether a strange deflection is old or new, so the clinician leans on the story, the pulse, the pressure, the breathing, and the general appearance. The features readable by any careful clinician are the rate, the rhythm, the width of the ventricular complex, the presence or absence of P waves, and a clear elevation of the segment between the complex and the T wave. Those five answer most urgent questions without a specialist. The difficult features are subtle T wave changes, the axis, and the distinction between a dangerous pattern and an innocent variant, and these should be described honestly as uncertain. A normal tracing in a patient who looks unwell proves nothing, and sending that patient home on the strength of the paper lets the tracing make the decision. The decision is usually a plan rather than a diagnosis: watch and repeat, treat on the clinical picture, or send the patient onward now.

The clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer but no ECG still has the reasoning that the tracing supports. The pulse is felt for rate, rhythm, and volume, and the hand can detect an irregularity that the paper would confirm. The pulse at the wrist and the beat heard at the heart are counted together, and a difference suggests that some beats are too weak to reach the wrist, which is common in irregular rhythms. The rate is counted at rest and written down with the time, because the trend over visits is worth more than any single figure. The stethoscope gives the heart sounds, a murmur, a rub, and the added sounds of a failing heart, and the lungs are listened to for crackles that would explain the breathlessness. The torch makes the vessels at the back of the eye and the color of the skin visible, and the thermometer turns an impression into something measurable. The decision that follows is whether the patient can be watched, whether to treat on the clinical picture, and whether the journey to a place with a tracing must start now.

The reading of tracings has its own characteristic errors, and what is written down matters as much as the reading. The commonest error is to stop at the first abnormal finding and build a story around it, so that a second and more important abnormality is never seen. Another is to accept a suggestive pattern as diagnostic, which happens most with the strain pattern said to accompany a clot in the lungs and with elevation that mimics an infarction. A third is to read the paper without the patient, so that a fast rate is treated as disease when it is a response to pain, and a slow rate is dismissed when it causes a faint. The record should carry the time, whether the patient had symptoms then, the rate, the rhythm, the width of the complex, and any abnormal interval, together with whether an earlier tracing was available and what changed. A tracing called normal in a patient who is clearly unwell should be accompanied by the observation that the test does not exclude the illness.

A constructed illustration may make the difference between the two settings concrete, and it is offered as a teaching device rather than a portrait of any real person. Imagine a patient brought to a hospital with chest pain, where the tracing is taken within minutes and compared with one from a previous admission; the segment that had been flat for years has now risen in the views facing one region of the heart, and the team moves at once to restore blood flow. Now imagine a patient in a small clinic with a racing heart, where a single tracing shows wide complexes and no clear P waves and the nearest help is hours away; the clinician there does not name the rhythm but treats the patient as unstable and arranges immediate transfer, because the width of the complex is enough to know that the rhythm may be dangerous. Both are good uses of the same test, one changing the diagnosis and the other changing the journey. Neither patient is real, and neither story should be applied to a real person without a full assessment. What they share is that the reader asked a question before looking, trusted the findings that could be trusted, and did not wait for certainty before acting. The reasoning is the same; the resources are not.

A clinician who reads a tracing and sends a patient home must say clearly what should bring the patient back. The patient should be told which symptoms matter: chest pain that is new or changing, breathlessness, a fainting, a racing or very slow heartbeat, and a weakness or faintness that was not there before. A follow-up tracing arranged in advance, particularly for an irregular rhythm or a long interval, is worth more than a promise to return if things worsen. The patient should also be told to report every medicine, including anything bought without a prescription and any herbal remedy, because many change the tracing. Where the reading was uncertain, that uncertainty should be shared in plain words rather than hidden.

To summarize, the ECG is read by a fixed sequence of rate, rhythm, axis, intervals, the chambers, the segments, and the comparison with any previous tracing, and the sequence exists so that the second abnormality is not lost behind the first. The six patterns demanding immediate recognition are acute coronary occlusion, the wide complex tachycardia, complete heart block, a high level of potassium, the strain pattern of a clot in the lungs, and the abnormally long interval from the ventricular complex to the end of the T wave, each named with the finding that reveals it. The tracing is one instrument among several and never replaces the history, the examination, or the judgment made at the bedside. Every figure in this chapter, whether a rate, an interval, a calibration, or a reference range, is an illustration of reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such numbers differ between countries, editions, and laboratories. In a well-resourced hospital the reading is shared and the recording repeated while symptoms persist, while the clinician alone with one tracing and no one to ask treats the patient rather than the paper.
