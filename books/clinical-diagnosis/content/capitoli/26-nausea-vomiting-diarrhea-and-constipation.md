# Chapter 26 — Nausea, Vomiting, Diarrhea and Constipation

Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea and constipation are taught as four separate symptoms and are usually met as one disturbance, because a gut that empties too fast and a gut that empties too slowly are answering the same questions about tone, contents and control. They also travel far beyond the abdomen, since a brain under pressure, a diabetic crisis, an infection in the blood and a medicine taken for the heart can each produce them with no lesion in the bowel at all. This chapter therefore treats the four together, reading each by its mechanism, its timing and its content before any name is attached to it. As with every section of this book, what follows is educational material and cannot replace training, supervision, clinical judgment or the protocol of your own service. Every patient mentioned here is a constructed illustration rather than a record of a real person.

Seven dangers deserve to be named before the ordinary causes are considered, each with the finding that would betray it. Bowel obstruction declares itself through colicky pain that comes in waves, abdominal distension, vomiting, and a failure to pass stool or gas, and a strangulated hernia may be the cause hiding quietly at one of the natural orifices. A perforated viscus produces a rigid, board-like abdomen with pain on any movement, quiet bowel sounds, and often a rising pulse as the peritoneum becomes inflamed. Mesenteric ischemia gives pain that is severe and continuous while the abdomen remains surprisingly soft, and that mismatch between the complaint and the finding is the alarm rather than the reassurance. The older patient and the patient whose immune defenses are suppressed may show all of these dangers through a muted version of the same story.

Severe dehydration from any cause shows itself in a rising pulse, a falling blood pressure, cool clammy skin, reduced urine, sunken eyes and a patient who is drowsy rather than merely thirsty. Invasive bacterial infection is suggested by a fever such as 38 degrees Celsius or higher, blood mixed in the stool, severe cramping, and a patient who looks far more ill than the diarrhea alone would explain, although the figure your own service treats as a fever must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the threshold shifts with age and with the site measured. Diabetic ketoacidosis presents with vomiting, deep sighing breathing, abdominal pain that can mimic a surgical abdomen, and a sweet or fruity odor on the breath. Raised intracranial pressure produces vomiting, often in the early morning, with headache, visual change and a level of consciousness that is not quite right. Each of these is a reason to move quickly rather than to watch, and the finding that reveals it is named here so that the search is deliberate rather than accidental.

Vomiting is a reflex with a final common pathway, and knowing its inputs explains why the same symptom points in different directions. A center in the brainstem receives signals from the gut through the vagus nerve, from the balance apparatus of the inner ear, from a zone that samples the blood, and from higher centers that carry memory, smell and expectation. Gut-driven vomiting is usually preceded by nausea, follows a meal or a pain, and relieves the discomfort for a while. Vestibular vomiting arrives with vertigo and is worse with head movement, while vomiting from a chemical or metabolic cause may come with little warning and little nausea. Vomiting driven by the brain itself often arrives without nausea and may be projectile, which is a different kind of warning.

What is vomited, and when, carries more information than the act itself. Undigested food suggests a stomach that cannot empty or an esophagus that cannot pass its contents, while bile-stained vomit means the obstruction lies below the point where the bile duct enters the gut. Fecal-smelling or fecal-looking vomit is a late and serious sign of a bowel that has been blocked long enough for its contents to stagnate, and it demands a surgical opinion rather than a symptomatic remedy. Coffee-ground material means blood altered by stomach acid, and fresh red blood means bleeding that is active, so the color of the vomit redirects the whole assessment. Vomiting long after eating points to delayed emptying, while vomiting during or immediately after a meal suggests an ulcer, a stricture or a nervous habit. Projectile vomiting in an infant is its own category and belongs to the assessment of the newborn and the young child.

Nausea without vomiting is a symptom in its own right and should not be treated as a milder version of the same thing. It can arise from the gut, from the inner ear, from a medicine, from a metabolic disturbance, from pregnancy and from anxiety, and the company it keeps is what identifies it. Nausea with early morning headache and visual change belongs to the brain, while nausea with vertigo and ringing in the ears belongs to the balance apparatus. Nausea with epigastric pain before meals belongs to the stomach, and nausea with lower abdominal pain and a missed period belongs to the pelvis. The clinician who asks what the nausea comes with, rather than how severe it is, has already narrowed the field, because tolerance varies between patients more than the diseases do.

Diarrhea is read first by duration, because the passage of time changes the list of causes almost completely. An episode of short duration is usually infective, dietary or caused by a medicine, while diarrhea that persists for weeks suggests malabsorption, inflammatory disease, a chronic infection or a tumor. The distinction is one of reasoning rather than a fixed calendar, since the interval that separates the acute from the chronic differs between sources, so confirm the limit your own service uses against the current guideline and the local protocol. The character of the stool is the second question, since watery stool points to a disturbance of absorption or secretion, fatty stool that floats and is hard to flush points to malabsorption of fat, and stool containing mucus or blood points to inflammation or invasion. Frequency and volume matter most for the risk of dehydration, while weight loss and waking at night are the features that make a chronic cause more likely.

Blood in the stool is never dismissed, because it separates illnesses that need a different pace and sometimes a different place of care. Invasive bacterial infection produces fever, cramping pain, a constant urge to pass stool, and visible blood or mucus, and it is the reason a patient with diarrhea can become seriously ill quickly. The same picture may come from an inflammatory disease of the bowel, from a parasite, from a sexually transmitted infection in the rectum or from a tumor, and the history of travel, sexual history, medicines and previous episodes is what orders them. A patient with bloody diarrhea and a fever should not be treated as a simple upset stomach, and the decision about antibiotics rests on the likely organism, the severity of the illness and the local pattern of resistance, which is a decision for the current guideline rather than for memory. The safe rule is that blood, fever and a patient who looks unwell together change the plan.

Constipation is the symptom most easily dismissed and most easily misread, because most people who report it have a habit rather than a disease. The clinician therefore asks what has changed, since a lifelong pattern of infrequent stool is a different matter from a new inability to pass anything at all. A change that has developed gradually in an older person, especially with weight loss, anemia or blood, raises the possibility of a tumor and deserves examination of the rectum and the colon. Absolute constipation, meaning that neither stool nor gas is passing, with distension, vomiting and colicky pain, is obstruction until proven otherwise. The patient's own meaning of the word should be sought, because some mean infrequency, some mean straining, and some mean a sensation of incomplete emptying.

The gut has its own nervous system and its own rhythm, and it also lies under the influence of the autonomic nerves, hormones, electrolytes and the blood supply, so a disturbance anywhere along that chain can present as nausea, vomiting, diarrhea or constipation. This is why the four symptoms so often appear together, and why one cause can produce a mixed picture, since an infection that empties the bowel quickly may be followed by a quiet, distended abdomen, while an obstruction may begin with diarrhea as the bowel empties behind the block. The medicine history belongs to the gastrointestinal history, because many common drugs alter gut motility and a symptom that began with a new prescription should be treated as linked until proven otherwise, and alcohol or its withdrawal is an easily missed cause of both vomiting and diarrhea. Diabetes can damage the nerves that control the stomach and the bowel, and a diabetic crisis itself presents with vomiting and abdominal pain, while thyroid disease, adrenal insufficiency, a high calcium level, a low sodium level and kidney failure each disturb the gut and change the whole patient. The clinician who considers the whole body before settling on a gastrointestinal cause will recognize the patient whose gut symptoms are the first sign of a metabolic problem.

The history is taken in a fixed way so that nothing important is left to chance, and the Oldcarts framework for the history of the present illness gives that structure without dictating the conversation. Onset, location, duration, character, aggravating and relieving factors, timing and severity are the questions, and applied to the gut they become asking when the symptom began, where it is felt, what it looks like, what makes it better, what makes it worse, and what comes with it. The listener should not march through the list like a form but should use it to notice the gap the patient has left. Bowel habit before the illness is the baseline against which every change is judged, and it must be asked explicitly. Diet, travel, water source, contact with others who are ill, recent antibiotics and the menstrual history in a woman of childbearing age each change the differential, and the patient's own fear about what this might be often points to the answer the clinician has not yet considered.

The examination follows the history and is aimed at the questions the history has raised. The general impression comes first, including whether the patient is alert, drowsy or confused, whether the skin is dry or clammy, whether the eyes are sunken, and whether the breathing is normal or deep and sighing. The abdomen is then examined in the sequence of inspection, auscultation, percussion and palpation, with the painful area left until last, and the hernial orifices and the rectum are examined deliberately rather than hopefully. A rectal examination is the only way to feel a low tumor, a loaded rectum or blood that has not yet appeared on the surface, so it is not omitted because the patient has not mentioned bleeding. In a woman, a pelvic examination answers related questions, and a tender mass beside the uterus or pain when the cervix is moved redirects the assessment toward the pelvis. Vital signs are repeated rather than recorded once, because the direction in which the pulse and the pressure are moving often says more than any single value.

Dehydration is assessed by signs that are available everywhere, and its severity is read from several of them together rather than from any one alone. Thirst, a dry mouth and tongue, reduced skin turgor, sunken eyes, a pulse of 110 beats per minute, a pressure of 90 millimeters of mercury, cool hands and feet, reduced urine and a change in alertness are the findings, and no single one of them is reliable by itself; those two figures are illustrations of the reasoning, so confirm the values your service treats as abnormal against the current guideline and the local protocol. In an infant the soft spot, the tears, the wetness of the mouth and the number of wet diapers are the practical measures, and the weight is the most objective of all when a scale exists. A loss of a few per cent of body weight is a useful gauge of fluid deficit, but that figure must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol rather than remembered, because the threshold differs for infants, children and adults. Urine output can be measured when a container exists, and the simplest monitoring is to ask the patient or the family to record how often and how much urine is passed.

The decision to rehydrate is a reasoning decision before it is a prescribing decision. A patient who is alert, drinking, passing urine and making tears can usually be managed by mouth, with small frequent amounts of a solution whose composition follows the current guideline and the local protocol. A patient who is vomiting everything, who is drowsy, whose pulse is rising and whose pressure is falling needs fluid into a vein, and the choice of solution, the rate and the total belong to the protocol of the unit and not to this book. The finding that makes oral rehydration the right answer is a patient who can swallow and keep fluid down, and the finding that makes it the wrong answer is one who cannot, or who already shows the signs of shock. Rehydration is also a diagnostic act, because the patient who improves quickly was probably volume-depleted alone, while the patient who does not improve needs the other causes reconsidered.

Diabetic ketoacidosis sits at the front of the list whenever vomiting and abdominal pain meet a patient with diabetes, and it can appear in someone not previously known to have the disease. The findings are deep sighing breathing, a characteristic odor on the breath, drowsiness, thirst and heavy urination before the illness, and sometimes abdominal pain and tenderness severe enough to suggest a surgical abdomen. A glucose measurement changes the picture at once where a meter exists, and where it does not, the history of weight loss, thirst and passing large volumes of urine in a young person is the substitute. The patient can be profoundly dehydrated, and the dehydration and the acid disturbance together explain much of how ill they look. This is not a condition to be managed with reassurance and a wait, and recognizing the pattern is the contribution of the clinician who meets it first.

Vomiting that comes from the brain has a different shape from vomiting that comes from the gut, and the difference is worth learning because missing it is severe. It tends to occur in the early morning, it may be projectile and without preceding nausea, and it is often accompanied by headache that is worse on waking, by blurred or double vision, and by a change in consciousness or behavior. In an infant the equivalent findings are a bulging soft spot, a rapid increase in head size, and refusal to feed with irritability that does not settle. A head injury, a known tumor, a recent infection of the brain or its coverings, or the use of a blood-thinning medicine all raise the probability. The clinician who finds these features should not treat the vomiting as a stomach problem, and should look for the neurological signs and seek urgent help.

Mesenteric ischemia is the vascular catastrophe that presents through the gut, and it is named again here because it is so easily missed. The pain is typically severe, continuous and poorly localized, while the abdomen is soft and the examination seems far too benign for the complaint, and that contrast is the classic warning. The patient may be older, may have an irregular heart rhythm or a recent heart attack, may have narrowing of the arteries elsewhere, and may describe pain after meals with weight loss from fear of eating. Blood in the stool, a rising pulse and a patient who is deteriorating despite a soft abdomen make the picture more urgent. The decision is surgical and immediate, and waiting for the picture to become classical is a mistake, because by then the bowel may already be dead.

Bowel obstruction is a pattern assembled from the history and the examination before any image is seen. Colicky pain in waves, vomiting, distension and the failure to pass stool or gas are the four features, and their presence together is more convincing than any one alone. A previous operation raises the probability of adhesions, a hernia at any orifice raises the possibility of strangulation, a palpable mass raises the possibility of a tumor, and an older patient with weight loss and anemia raises it further. Bowel sounds are high-pitched and rushing early and may become quiet as the bowel tires, so a quiet abdomen does not exclude obstruction. Fever, localized tenderness and a rising pulse suggest that the bowel is already losing its blood supply and that the problem has become a surgical emergency.

A perforated viscus is recognized by the sudden onset of severe pain, a rigid abdomen, and the loss of the normal movement of the wall with breathing. The pain may begin in one region and spread as the contents of the gut irritate the whole peritoneum, and the patient often lies very still because any movement hurts. Percussion tenderness and pain on coughing are useful when deep palpation is too distressing, and the loss of liver dullness is suggestive though unreliable. The patient may be pale, sweaty and tachycardic, and the pressure may fall as inflammation and fluid loss take their toll. An ulcer, a tumor, an inflamed diverticulum and a foreign body are the usual sources, and the decision is surgical and is taken on the clinical picture rather than on a scan.

The older patient and the patient whose immune defenses are suppressed present the same diseases through a quieter channel, and both deserve a lower threshold for investigation and help. Fever may be absent or blunted, pain may be reported late or not at all, and the first sign may be confusion, a fall or a general decline that the family has noticed. Constipation in an older person can be the presenting feature of obstruction, a tumor or a metabolic disturbance, while diarrhea can be the first sign of an infection that will become serious quickly. A patient taking a blood-thinning medicine may bleed into the gut with little warning, and the resulting diarrhea or vomiting may be attributed to something else. The clinician who asks the family what has changed, and who repeats the examination rather than trusting the first one, will catch what the textbook picture hides.

Children lose fluid faster than adults and show it differently, so the assessment in a child weighs the same signs with a different emphasis. An infant with diarrhea may lose a large share of body fluid before the pulse changes noticeably, and the soft spot, the tears, the mouth, the skin and the wet diapers become the working measurements. Vomiting in a newborn that is bile-stained is a surgical emergency, and green vomit in an infant is never treated as a feeding problem until obstruction has been excluded. Projectile vomiting in a young infant, especially with poor weight gain, suggests a narrowed outlet from the stomach, and the decision is surgical rather than dietary. In an older child, vomiting with abdominal pain and a rash should raise the possibility of a serious infection, and the pattern of the rash matters as much as the gut symptoms.

Constipation that is not a habit deserves a deliberate search, guided by what makes it different from the usual complaint. A new change in bowel habit in an older person, with weight loss, anemia, a family history of bowel cancer or blood in the stool, is a reason to examine the rectum and to arrange the appropriate test according to the local protocol. Constipation alternating with diarrhea, a sensation of incomplete emptying, and a stool that has become narrow suggest a narrowing of the bowel that must be explained. Constipation with severe pain, distension, vomiting and the passage of no gas is obstruction, and constipation after abdominal surgery may mean a bowel that has not woken up or a collection of pus. A neurological cause should be considered when there is weakness, numbness or difficulty passing urine, because the bowel and the bladder travel together.

Imaging and laboratory tests are chosen to answer the questions the history has raised rather than to survey the patient. A blood count, electrolytes, kidney function, glucose and a measure of acid base balance are the common starting point, and the stool is examined when infection or blood loss is suspected. A chest radiograph is useful when aspiration, pneumonia or free gas under the diaphragm is possible, and an ultrasound can show free fluid, an obstructed kidney, gallstones or a pregnancy inside or outside the uterus. Computed tomography, or CT, answers questions about obstruction, perforation, inflammation of the pancreas and masses, and it is chosen when its result would change the plan. The clinician should decide in advance what each result would mean, because a test whose answer changes nothing is a cost, a delay and a false reassurance.

Where there is only a stethoscope, a torch and a thermometer, the same reasoning is performed with fewer instruments, and the discipline is to extract more from each. The thermometer turns a vague impression of illness into a number that can be followed, the stethoscope brings the bowel sounds and the chest, and the torch examines the mouth, the skin, the eyes, the hernial orifices and the anus. A watch allows the pulse and the respiratory rate to be counted rather than guessed, and a scale, when one exists, allows weight to be tracked and fluid loss to be estimated. The urine can be inspected for color and volume, the stool for blood, and the abdomen felt for tenderness and distension by hand alone. The decision that follows is rarely a diagnosis but a plan: whether the patient can be watched safely, whether treatment can begin on the clinical picture, and whether the journey to a hospital must start now.

A constructed illustration may fix the reasoning. Imagine an older woman who has vomited repeatedly, whose abdomen is distended and tympanic, who has passed no stool or gas, and who had an operation years before; the working diagnosis is obstruction, and the questions are whether the bowel is compromised and how quickly she must travel. Now imagine a young man with diarrhea and fever who looks well enough to be sent home, in whom blood in the stool and a pulse that has risen since the morning change the decision, so that infection with an invasive organism becomes the leading concern. Neither patient is real, and neither should be read as a portrait, for they are constructed only to show how one further finding can reverse the order of a differential. The value of the illustration is the habit it builds, not the diagnosis it displays.

The patient who is sent home needs a plan as clear as the one made for the patient who stays. The clinician states what should improve, what should prompt an immediate return, and the patient or the family repeats it back so that misunderstanding is found. The findings that should bring a patient back at once are blood in the vomit or stool, a rigid or newly painful abdomen, the passage of no gas, a fever with a patient who looks worse, a failure to keep any fluid down, reduced urine, drowsiness, and pain that is far worse than the examination suggests. The plan should name a person and a place, because advice to return if worse is useless without somewhere to return to. Follow-up is a diagnostic instrument, since the patient whose symptoms resolve confirms one hypothesis and the patient who returns with new findings corrects another, and the written record should show the reasoning and the plan so that the next clinician can continue rather than begin again.

Pregnancy changes both the list of causes and the urgency of the assessment, and vomiting in a woman of childbearing age should always include it in the reasoning. Vomiting in early pregnancy is common and usually manageable, but severe vomiting with weight loss and dehydration requires assessment and support rather than reassurance. Diarrhea and vomiting in pregnancy can also be the first sign of an infection that threatens both mother and fetus, and the threshold for seeking help is lower. Constipation is common in pregnancy and usually harmless, but abdominal pain with bleeding or a missed period raises the possibility of a pregnancy outside the uterus, which is a surgical emergency. The clinician asks about the last period before deciding that the symptom is gastrointestinal, because that one question can redirect the whole encounter.

To summarize, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea and constipation are read as one disturbance of the gut and of the whole patient, interpreted through mechanism, timing, content and duration rather than through severity alone. The dangers are named as the symptoms are met, among them bowel obstruction, a perforated viscus, mesenteric ischemia, severe dehydration, invasive bacterial infection, diabetic ketoacidosis and raised intracranial pressure, each with the finding that would reveal it and each demanding a decision rather than a wait. Every number, score, cut-off, reference range, temperature, rate, pressure, volume and interval in this chapter is an illustration of reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between countries, between editions and between laboratories. Rehydration belongs to the reasoning even though its amounts belong to the protocol, and the same assessment is performed with a laboratory and with only a scale, a watch and clinical judgment. This recording is educational material and does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment or the protocol of your own service. The patient who is deteriorating is treated while the reasoning continues, because no explanation is worth the delay of someone who is becoming more ill.
