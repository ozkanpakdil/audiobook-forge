# Chapter 45 — Chronic Disease Care at a Distance

The diseases that fill this book are infectious, and that is exactly why the chronic diseases of the same communities are so easily missed. A clinic organized around fever and the next outbreak records a blood pressure only when somebody complains, while a person whose arteries are quietly narrowing feels nothing at all. Hypertension, diabetes, epilepsy, asthma and heart failure now cause a large and growing share of illness and death in low-income and middle-income countries, alongside malaria and tuberculosis rather than instead of them. The World Health Organization has described this double burden for years, and the trend continues as populations age, as people move into cities and as diets shift toward processed food and added salt. The work does not end when the fever breaks, because the treatment begun today will still matter in ten years, and its success will be invisible for most of that time.

Hypertension is called the silent epidemic because it produces no dependable symptom until it has damaged something that cannot be repaired. Headache, dizziness and nosebleeds are common in everyone and therefore useless as a screening question, so the only reliable approach is to measure the blood pressure of adults who did not ask for it. The World Health Organization and most national protocols advise measuring every adult seen at a health facility, with particular attention to people over the age of forty, people with diabetes, people with a body mass index above 25, tobacco users, people with a family history of early heart attack or stroke, and women whose pregnancy was complicated by high blood pressure. Where human immunodeficiency virus is common, some treatment regimens themselves raise blood pressure and add a further reason to measure at every refill visit. Waiting for the patient to raise the subject means the diagnosis will be made in an emergency room.

The technique decides whether the number you write is real. The cuff must fit the arm, because one that is too small inflates the reading and one that is too large lowers it, and the arm should be bare, supported and level with the heart. Let the patient rest quietly for five minutes without talking, then take two readings a minute apart and average them. A single high reading is not a diagnosis and a single normal one does not exclude it, so where possible the diagnosis rests on repeated measurements on separate days. The threshold for treatment is usually a systolic pressure at or above 140 millimeters of mercury or a diastolic pressure at or above 90 millimeters of mercury, confirmed on separate occasions, while many protocols treat at 130 over 80 for people with diabetes, chronic kidney disease or established heart disease.

The World Health Organization package of essential noncommunicable disease interventions sets out these thresholds and the accompanying drug choices, but national protocols differ and the version in force where you work is the one that governs your practice. You must verify the current guideline before you label anyone hypertensive or begin a medicine, because thresholds for older adults, for pregnant women and for people with other illnesses are revised as evidence accumulates. Nothing in this chapter should be applied from memory, and the same caution applies to every dose named in it. Where your national protocol and this chapter disagree, the protocol wins.

For nearly everyone the first prescription is not a tablet. Reducing salt is the single most effective dietary change, and it means less salt in cooking, fewer bouillon cubes and less processed food rather than a vague instruction to eat less salt. Modest weight loss, regular walking, less alcohol and stopping tobacco all lower pressure and, more importantly, lower the risk of the stroke and heart attack that hypertension is really about. Because food insecurity and the price of vegetables make this advice harder to follow than the prescriber assumes, the plan must be built from what is available and affordable. The usual target is a pressure below 140 over 90 and below 130 over 80 for people with diabetes or kidney disease, with a gentler target in the very old to avoid falls and dizziness.

When a medicine is needed, the World Health Organization recommends starting with one of four classes, and the choice depends on what is on the shelf, what the patient can afford and what other conditions are present. A thiazide-type diuretic such as hydrochlorothiazide at 25 milligrams once a day is cheap and effective. A calcium channel blocker such as amlodipine at 5 to 10 milligrams once a day works equally well, is well tolerated and needs no laboratory monitoring. An angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor such as enalapril at 5 to 20 milligrams twice a day or lisinopril at 10 milligrams once a day is preferred when diabetes or protein in the urine is present, but it must be avoided in pregnancy. If the first drug does not reach the target after a month, add a second class rather than push the first to its maximum. One tablet containing two classes and taken once a day is the simplest regimen of all.

The schedule a nurse can keep alone is the schedule that fits how people actually live. A common approach is to review the patient every month until the pressure is at target and every three months afterward, with an annual check of kidney function, electrolytes, urine protein and cardiovascular risk where the laboratory allows it. Each visit should be short and standardized: measure, compare with the target, ask about side effects and missed doses, and refill enough to last until the next appointment plus a small margin. Give the patient a card bearing the diagnosis, the medicines, the doses and the next date, because that card survives a fire, a flood or a move. If the patient cannot come, a community health worker can carry the refill and take a measurement, and that visit is worth more than a perfect one that never happens.

Some pressures cannot wait for next month. A systolic pressure above 180 millimeters of mercury together with chest pain, breathlessness, severe headache, visual disturbance, confusion, weakness on one side or a seizure suggests that an organ is being damaged now, and that patient needs urgent referral rather than an extra tablet in the clinic. The distinction between a very high pressure causing no harm and one injuring the brain, heart or kidneys depends on symptoms and examination rather than on the number alone. Where transfer is impossible, treat what you can, document the reasoning and the discussion with the family, and remember that lowering a pressure too quickly can itself cause harm.

Diabetes arrives in the same clinics and is recognized far later than it should be, because thirst, frequent urination and weight loss are blamed on heat, work or age until the disease is advanced. Diagnosis rests on a fasting plasma glucose at or above 126 milligrams per deciliter, or a random glucose at or above 200 milligrams per deciliter with typical symptoms, or a glycated hemoglobin at or above 6.5 per cent, each confirmed where possible by a second test. In a remote clinic the glucose meter and the urine dipstick are often the only tools, and a dipstick showing glucose and ketones in a thin, thirsty patient is a warning of imminent danger rather than a curiosity. Every person with diabetes needs a plan for the day when tablets no longer control the glucose, decided calmly in advance instead of improvised during a crisis. These criteria come from the World Health Organization and widely used national guidance and are revised periodically, so verify the current version before you diagnose.

The complications of diabetes are what make it lethal, and each has a screening test a nurse can perform. The eyes should be examined at least once a year, and any sudden change in vision is an emergency. The kidneys are followed with urine protein and, where available, a creatinine measurement, because early kidney disease is silent. The nerves are tested with a fine filament at several points on each foot or, where no filament exists, with light touch and vibration sense, since loss of protective sensation is the finding that predicts ulceration. Tuberculosis is more common and more severe in people with diabetes, so a cough lasting more than two weeks deserves investigation rather than reassurance.

Metformin remains the first medicine in most protocols, started at 500 milligrams once or twice a day with food and increased slowly to as much as 1,000 milligrams twice a day as tolerated, because the gradual increase prevents the nausea and diarrhea that make patients abandon it. It is cheap, it rarely causes low blood sugar when used alone, and it is safe in most people, though it is avoided in advanced kidney disease, in severe liver disease and during acute illness with dehydration. A sulfonylurea such as glibenclamide at 2.5 to 5 milligrams once or twice a day or gliclazide at 40 to 80 milligrams once a day can be added, but these medicines do cause low blood sugar, especially in older people and in those who eat irregularly. When tablets no longer hold the glucose down, or when the patient is losing weight and ketones are present, insulin is required and should be started rather than delayed. These doses come from the World Health Organization and national protocols and are revised from time to time, so confirm them against the guideline currently in force.

Insulin frightens nurses and patients alike, and much of that fear is about storage rather than the injection. The common preparations are a rapid-acting analog used at meals, a short-acting soluble insulin, an intermediate-acting isophane insulin that is cloudy and usually given once or twice a day, and a long-acting analog given once a day. An unopened vial or pen must be kept cold between 2 and 8 degrees Celsius, but the vial in use may be kept at room temperature for about four weeks and must never be frozen. Where there is no refrigerator, an evaporative cooler made from two clay pots with wet sand between them keeps insulin usefully cool, and a shaded hole in damp ground or a box wrapped in wet cloth can serve the same purpose. Teach the patient to look at the vial before every dose and to discard one that has been frozen, has turned cloudy or contains particles, because that glance protects more doses than any rule in a book.

The foot examination is the piece of nursing work in this chapter that prevents the most amputations. At every visit, and at least once a year for everyone, look at both feet with the shoes and socks off, in good light, and compare them. Ask about numbness, burning and cramps, test sensation, feel the pulses, and inspect the skin between the toes, on the heels and across the soles for cracks, calluses, blisters and ulcers. Cut the nails straight across rather than into the corners, treat fungal infection because broken skin is the doorway, and examine the shoes, since a tight shoe or a foreign object inside it produces an ulcer faster than any other cause. Give the patient a mirror and teach a daily self-examination, because a small ulcer seen this week is a saved leg next year.

Education matters more than the prescription, and it has to be concrete. Teach the patient to recognize low blood sugar, the sweating, trembling, hunger and confusion that come when glucose falls, and to treat it at once with sugar, honey or a sweet drink followed by a meal. Teach the sick day rules, that illness raises glucose, that fluids must continue, that some medicines are paused and insulin often is not, and that vomiting or drowsiness means urgent travel to the clinic. Teach the family to give the injection, to rotate sites so that lumps do not form and to dispose of the needle safely, and write the whole plan on a card to rehearse at least once a year.

Epilepsy is among the most treatable conditions in this book and among the most neglected, largely because of what people believe about it. In many communities a seizure is read as possession, contagion, a curse or a punishment, and the consequences fall on the whole family as well as on the person, so that children are withdrawn from school and adults are barred from work and marriage. The treatment gap is enormous, and its main cause is not the absence of effective medicine but the distance between the diagnosis and a clinic that stocks it. Your first clinical act is often not a prescription but a sentence spoken to the family that separates the illness from the meaning they have attached to it. That sentence, repeated by a trusted nurse, changes what happens to the patient more than any tablet.

For most people with repeated seizures, a single medicine controls the attacks, and the first-line options are old, cheap and well understood. Phenobarbital at 60 to 100 milligrams taken at night is the least expensive and is widely used, though it causes sedation and can affect behavior and learning in children. Phenytoin, carbamazepine, valproate and levetiracetam are alternatives, and the choice depends on the seizure type, the age, the possibility of pregnancy and what the shelf contains, while valproate must not be given to a woman who may become pregnant because it damages the developing fetus. The principle that matters most is to start low, increase slowly to the lowest dose that controls the seizures, and stay with one effective medicine rather than adding a second because the first was never pushed to an adequate dose. Doses vary with age and weight, come from the World Health Organization and national protocols, and are revised from time to time, so check the current guidance before you prescribe.

A person who has been free of seizures for years will eventually ask whether the medicine can stop, and the answer must be careful. Most protocols suggest considering withdrawal only after a seizure-free interval of at least two years, and often five in adults, weighing the seizure type, the cause, the electroencephalogram and the consequences of a recurrence. Withdrawal is never abrupt, because stopping suddenly can provoke a seizure, so the dose is reduced in steps over weeks or months under supervision. The person who drives, works at heights, swims or operates machinery carries a different risk from the person whose seizures were nocturnal and infrequent. Give the patient a specific plan and a date to return, and never let a request to stop become an unplanned stopping.

The emergencies of epilepsy are few but they must be recognized within minutes. A seizure lasting more than five minutes, or repeated seizures without recovery between them, is status epilepticus and threatens the brain, so protect the airway, place the person on the side, give oxygen if it exists, and give a benzodiazepine by whatever route you have, such as diazepam rectally or midazolam into the cheek, then arrange urgent transfer. Never put anything into the mouth of a seizing person and never restrain the limbs. Between attacks, counsel on the ordinary dangers that kill people with epilepsy, which are burns from cooking fires, drowning in rivers and wells, falls from roofs and trees, and injury on the road.

Asthma is often missing from registers in tropical clinics because the diagnosis is recorded as recurrent bronchitis, pneumonia or a cough that never quite resolves. The pattern that should raise suspicion is wheeze, cough and breathlessness that come in attacks, worse at night or early morning and provoked by dust, smoke, cold air, exercise or infection. Many people living with untreated asthma receive repeated courses of antibiotics that do nothing, while the inflammation in the airway is never addressed. The inhaled route is what makes asthma treatment both effective and safe, because the medicine reaches the airway directly at a dose far too small to cause the side effects of the same drug taken by mouth. Where no inhaler can be found, that is a supply failure to be solved rather than a reason to give oral medicines indefinitely.

Treatment rests on two kinds of inhaler. A reliever, usually albuterol, known in many countries as salbutamol, is taken when symptoms occur, one or two puffs through a spacer, and repeated during an attack. A preventer, an inhaled corticosteroid such as beclomethasone or budesonide, is taken every day, often twice a day, whether or not the person feels unwell, and it is the medicine that reduces attacks, hospital admissions and deaths. The most common reason preventer treatment fails is not the drug but the technique, so every visit is a chance to watch the patient use the device and correct it, and if a spacer is unavailable, a clean plastic bottle with a hole cut in the base for the inhaler works as a holding chamber and is far better than an inhaler used badly. Where inhaled steroids are genuinely unavailable, a low dose of oral prednisolone on the worst days may be used under a written plan, but this is a compromise, because long-term oral steroids cause diabetes, thin bones and infection.

Every patient with asthma should leave the clinic with a written plan that says what to do when control is good, when it is worsening and in an emergency. In an acute attack, give the reliever repeatedly, one or two puffs through a spacer every twenty minutes during the first hour if needed, give oxygen where available, and add oral prednisolone at about 40 milligrams once a day for five days for a moderate or severe attack, checking the current guideline for the dose appropriate to age and weight. Signs of a life-threatening attack are a silent chest, exhaustion, drowsiness, difficulty speaking and low oxygen, and these require immediate transfer. A peak flow meter, where one exists, turns the plan into numbers, but the clinical picture remains the final authority. After the attack settles, review the preventer dose, because an attack is evidence that the maintenance treatment was not enough.

Heart failure in tropical practice has a different set of causes from the ones taught in wealthy countries. Untreated hypertension, rheumatic heart disease, cardiomyopathy after pregnancy, severe anemia, Chagas disease, tuberculosis of the pericardium and the late effects of other infections all produce a heart that cannot keep up, and the patient presents with breathlessness on effort and when lying flat, swollen ankles, an enlarged tender liver, a raised neck vein and weight gain from fluid. An electrocardiogram, the electrical recording of the heart, shows the rhythm, the rate and evidence of previous injury, and it is one of the few investigations worth carrying to a remote post, after which the short form ECG may be used. Point-of-care ultrasound, where it is available, answers the questions that matter most, which are whether the heart is beating well, whether the left side is full or empty and whether there is fluid around the heart. Without these tools the diagnosis rests on the history and the examination, and the response to treatment becomes the test.

Treatment begins with a diuretic, usually furosemide at 20 to 40 milligrams by mouth once or twice a day, adjusted to symptoms, weight and urine output, with potassium watched where it can be measured. An angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor and a beta blocker are added once the patient is stable, because they change the course of the disease rather than only the fluid, and spironolactone is added in selected cases. Digoxin at 0.125 to 0.25 milligrams once a day remains useful for rate control in atrial fibrillation and for symptom relief, but its margin between the therapeutic and the toxic dose is narrow, so toxicity must be suspected when nausea, vomiting, visual disturbance, confusion or a new slow rhythm appears. Salt restriction and daily weighing are as important as any drug, and a weight gain of two kilograms in three days is a signal for action. The trap that kills is the breathless patient with pulmonary edema who is given fluid into a vein, so in heart failure the needle stays out.

A chronic clinic is not mainly a series of diagnoses but a relationship maintained across years, and the advanced practice nurse carries it alone in most remote posts. You are the pharmacist who explains the tablets and counts them, the counselor who hears that the medicine was shared with a relative or stopped after a dream, the clinician who adjusts the dose under protocol and the record keeper who notices that somebody has not come back. These roles are performed in the same ten minutes, often in a language that is not your first, and each demands a different kind of attention. The strain is not dramatic but cumulative, and it shows up as a register that has not been updated, an angry exchange about a wait or a decision made too fast because the queue is long. Recognizing that strain for what it is, and building small routines that protect the quality of the visit, is part of competent practice rather than a luxury.

Documentation is what turns individual visits into continuity. A chronic disease register, kept on paper where there is no computer, lists each patient, the diagnosis, the current medicines, the last measurement, the next appointment and the outcome, and it is the only way to see who has been lost. A patient-held card duplicates the essentials so that another clinic, another country or a hospital at the end of a long journey can understand what has been tried, and a stock card records what came in, what went out and what remains. Follow-up reminders, delivered by a community health worker on a bicycle or by a telephone message, are what convert a register from a list into a system, and tracing those who default is one of the highest-value tasks in the clinic. Write the date, the dose and the reason for every change, because the note you write today is the only history the next clinician will have.

Monitoring for adverse effects is a scheduled activity, not a reaction to complaints. Thiazide diuretics and furosemide can lower potassium and sodium, so cramps, weakness and confusion deserve a measurement where a laboratory exists. Angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors cause a dry cough that is harmless but intolerable, and they can raise creatinine and potassium and cause swelling of the lips and face, which is a reason to stop the drug at once. Metformin can cause stomach upset and, in severe illness or kidney failure, a dangerous build-up of lactic acid, while insulin and the sulfonylureas cause low blood sugar, with older people and those who eat little at the greatest risk. Phenobarbital sedates, phenytoin can cause gum overgrowth, unsteadiness and blood disorders, and long-term steroids cause weight gain, high glucose, thin bones and infection. Review the whole list at every visit and ask specifically about the effects that people do not volunteer.

Interrupted supply is the defining problem of chronic disease care in remote settings, and it deserves to be treated as a clinical fact rather than an administrative nuisance. A person who takes a blood pressure medicine for eight months and then cannot obtain it for two faces a risk that is not the same as never having started, because the vessels have adapted and the pressure may rebound. An interrupted antiepileptic can precipitate a seizure that costs a job, a driving license or a life, and stopping a beta blocker abruptly can provoke a heart attack. The cause is usually a broken link along a long chain, a delayed order, a currency shortage, an expired batch, a flooded road or a warehouse that sent the wrong strength. The clinical consequence is always the same, and it lands on the patient and the nurse rather than on the supply system.

Several practical strategies stretch a fragile supply, and they begin with knowing your own numbers. Keep a simple consumption record and calculate what a month of treatment actually uses, so that orders rest on data rather than on impressions when the shelves look empty. Hold a buffer stock of the medicines that prevent death, and protect it by rotating older batches to the front and checking expiry dates every month. Choose simple regimens precisely because they survive interruption: one tablet once a day rather than three tablets three times daily, a fixed-dose combination rather than two separate tablets, and a refill that covers the interval to the next visit with a small margin. Where the law permits, let community health workers hold and distribute refills for stable patients, which reduces both travel and the risk of default. Share information with neighboring clinics, because one site's surplus is another site's emergency.

Because interruption will happen, the plan for it must exist before it does, and the patient must be told what the plan is. Discuss at the first visit and again at intervals which medicine matters most, what to do if it runs out, whether a substitute is acceptable and when to return. Some protocols allow a temporary switch within the same class, such as replacing one calcium channel blocker with another or one thiazide with another, and some conditions tolerate a gap far better than others, so the priority list reflects that. Do not silently substitute a different drug, because the patient may later receive the old one from another source and take both. Give the patient a written explanation of any substitution, including the old name, the new name and the reason. Honesty here is not a confession of failure but the clinical intervention that prevents the harm of an unplanned stop.

When the stock truly cannot cover everyone, the nurse faces a rationing decision that no guideline fully resolves. The usual principle is to protect the medicines that prevent immediate death or irreversible injury, to keep the treatment that is longest established and hardest to restart, and to avoid starting a new long-term medicine that cannot be continued. It is fairer and safer to tell the patient the truth, that the medicine is finished and that the next delivery is expected but not guaranteed, and to say what to watch for in the meantime. Document what you did and why, because the record protects the patient and it protects you. Where a decision is genuinely beyond your authority, refer it rather than absorbing it alone, even if the referral is only a telephone call to a supervisor. The moral weight of scarcity belongs to the system, but the responsibility for how you communicate it belongs to you.

Systems matter even when you are working alone, and the World Health Organization has published a package of essential noncommunicable disease interventions designed for primary care in low-resource settings, with protocols for screening, treatment and follow-up that a nurse can carry out. The same organization maintains a model list of essential medicines, and national programs adapt both to local realities, including the specific drugs they will actually procure. Decentralizing refills, training mid-level providers and integrating chronic care into clinics that already see mothers and children are the measures that most reliably improve long-term outcomes. Even a small clinic contributes by reporting its register data upward, because the numbers that show rising hypertension and diabetes are what justify the next order and the next post. Your scope of practice determines what you can do alone, and it varies by country, by employer and by the standing orders you work under, so write your protocols down, agree them with your supervisor and review them when the national guideline changes, because supervision is the mechanism that makes a lone nurse safe to be alone.

To summarize, chronic disease is now a large part of the work of every tropical clinic, and it demands a different discipline from the one that treats infections. Hypertension is found by measuring rather than by asking, its treatment follows the World Health Organization package of essential noncommunicable disease interventions and your national protocol, and its success depends on a follow-up schedule that a lone nurse can keep. Diabetes requires the foot examination that prevents amputation, an insulin supply kept cool without a refrigerator and education the patient carries in hand. Epilepsy and asthma are treatable with inexpensive medicines whose real obstacles are stigma, technique and supply, and heart failure demands that you treat the fluid without adding to it. The nurse is at once pharmacist, counselor and clinician, and the register, the patient card and the stock book are clinical instruments rather than paperwork. The one intervention that must never be left to chance is the honest conversation about what happens when the medicines run out, held early, in advance and in plain language, because a plan shared before the shortage is worth more than a promise made during it. Verify the current guideline before you prescribe, and let every patient leave knowing exactly what comes next.
