# Chapter 8 — Diagnostics Where Resources Are Limited

In tropical medicine the diagnosis is often made or lost in the hour before the laboratory sees the specimen. A clinic may have a working microscope and no incubator, a refrigerator that fails when the generator runs out of fuel, and a courier who comes once a week. The clinician who knows what each test can and cannot do, and what each specimen needs to stay valid, can still practice well inside those limits. This chapter follows that chain from collection to understanding, and it keeps returning to the nursing work that holds it together.

Everything begins with the specimen, and a specimen is only as good as the minute in which it was taken. Blood for a malaria film drawn after a dose of antimalarial medicine may be clear when it should have been full of parasites, and blood for a culture drawn after antibiotics may grow nothing. A stool left warm overnight loses the fragile motile forms that would have made the diagnosis, and urine collected at the wrong hour may hold too few eggs to find. Timing is not a detail of technique; it is part of the test itself, and the nurse who controls the timing controls much of the result.

The practical sequence is easiest to remember as four questions. What does this test need in order to work, and does this patient meet that condition? Has the patient already been treated, and how long ago? Is the specimen the right one, at the right volume, labeled at the bedside with the name and the time? Will it reach the laboratory inside the window the test allows, and if not, how will it be stored until it does? Each question has a failure mode that produces a result which looks like an answer and is not one.

Behind those questions lies a biological clock that no amount of care can hurry. Every infection has a prepatent period, the interval between infection and the moment the organism becomes detectable, and a test done before that interval closes is negative in a person who is genuinely infected. A malaria film may be negative on the first day of fever and positive 12 hours later, while a blood culture in typhoid fever is most often positive in the first week. Serology runs on its own clock, since antibodies take days to weeks to appear and then persist long after recovery, so the date of the specimen must always be read with the date the illness began.

Some organisms appear in the blood or the skin only at particular hours, and a specimen taken at the wrong time is read as normal. The microfilariae of Wuchereria bancrofti, the worm of lymphatic filariasis, circulate mainly at night, so a midday sample may show nothing while one taken after ten in the evening shows many. The microfilariae of Loa loa do the opposite and are easier to find during the day. Urine for the eggs of Schistosoma haematobium is best collected around midday, when the bladder releases the eggs that accumulated overnight. Skin snips for Onchocerca volvulus ignore the clock but must be incubated in saline before they yield their microfilariae.

The malaria film remains the foundation of tropical parasitology, and it is two tests in one. The thick film is a drop of blood spread over a small area and stained without fixation, so the red cells burst and release their parasites into a dense layer that can be scanned quickly, which makes it the more sensitive test. The thin film is a single layer of intact red cells, fixed and stained, and it preserves the shape of the parasite, which is how the species is identified. Giemsa stain is the standard, and its quality depends on the pH of the buffer and the freshness of the stain, both of which fail quietly in a hot clinic. A film too thick is unreadable, one too thin misses a low density, and one not fully dry before staining washes away the cells.

Quantifying the parasites matters as much as finding them, because the density changes what the clinician does. The usual approach is to count parasites while counting a fixed number of white blood cells, commonly 200, and then to express the result against an assumed count of 8,000 per microliter. A density above 100,000 per microliter in a non-immune patient, or above 250,000 in a patient living in a transmission area, marks severe disease and demands parenteral treatment and close observation. The count is also a baseline for judging the response, since a density that has not fallen after 24 to 48 hours of effective therapy suggests resistance or a failure of absorption from vomiting. Density belongs in the nursing assessment beside the temperature and the level of consciousness.

One negative film is never enough to exclude malaria, because parasite density fluctuates through the day and can fall below the threshold of the microscope. The standard practice is a film every 12 to 24 hours, three films over 48 to 72 hours, before malaria is set aside, with the patient observed rather than sent home. The same rule applies to a patient who has already taken antimalarial treatment, since a partially treated infection can be suppressed without being cured. The slides should be kept, because a second reader can settle a difficult species call and a slide outlasts the electronic record. Where the laboratory can do it, a polymerase chain reaction test detects very low densities, but it rarely answers in time to change the first decision.

The rapid diagnostic test for malaria changed what a rural clinic could do, because it needs no microscope, no electricity, and no stain, and it answers in 15 to 20 minutes. Most of these tests detect a parasite antigen in the blood, and the antigen most commonly used is histidine-rich protein 2, which is produced by Plasmodium falciparum. A test that detects only histidine-rich protein 2 will not reliably detect Plasmodium vivax, Plasmodium ovale, Plasmodium malariae, or Plasmodium knowlesi, so infection with one of those species can be missed. Antigen can persist for days to weeks after successful treatment, so a positive result in a patient treated last month does not prove that the present fever is malaria. The test is not interchangeable with a film, and the differences decide how a result may be used.

Some parasites carry deletions in the gene for histidine-rich protein 2, produce no antigen at all, and give a negative test while the film is positive. Those deletions were first described in South America and have since been reported in parts of Africa and Asia, and a negative rapid test in a severely ill patient must never overrule the clinical picture. A test combining histidine-rich protein 2 with an enzyme called lactate dehydrogenase, or with aldolase, detects more species and is less affected by deletions, at a higher cost. Where both a rapid test and a microscope exist, the film should confirm and quantify what the rapid test suggests. Where only the rapid test exists, the negative result must be carried with an honest awareness of what it cannot exclude.

Blood cultures remain the reference method for typhoid fever, brucellosis, and bacterial sepsis, and they are among the first casualties of a resource-limited setting. A culture needs enriched medium, a clean venipuncture site, an adequate volume of blood, an incubator that holds a steady temperature for days, and a laboratory that can identify whatever grows. Volume is the strongest determinant of yield, and many systems ask for 10 milliliters per bottle in an adult, more than a hurried collector usually draws. Cultures taken before the first antibiotic dose are far more likely to be positive, and the two or three sets that would raise the yield are rarely affordable. Without an incubator, the honest response is to draw cultures only where the result will be acted upon, and to treat empirically.

Contamination is the other constraint, because a culture that grows an organism from the skin tells the clinician something false but persuasive. Coagulase-negative staphylococci, diphtheroids, and other skin organisms appear when the site was not properly disinfected or the bottle was handled carelessly, and each such result may lead to unnecessary antibiotics and unnecessary cost. The nurse controls the antiseptic, the drying time before puncture, and the transfer of the specimen, and these steps sit within nursing practice in a way that laboratory quality control does not. A culture is only as clean as the moment of collection, and that moment is a nursing moment.

Serology detects the immune response rather than the organism, and that single fact determines both its usefulness and its traps. Because antibodies appear only after the organism has been present for days, a specimen taken early in an illness is often negative and means only that the body has not yet answered. The reliable approach is paired sera, one in the acute phase and a second two to four weeks later, with a fourfold rise between them accepted as evidence of recent infection. A single high level cannot separate a current infection from one acquired years ago, so a positive result on one specimen in a person from an endemic area proves very little, and where paired sera are impossible the clinician must reason from the clinical picture.

Cross-reactivity is the second trap, and it is nowhere more consequential than among the flaviviruses. Dengue, Zika, yellow fever, West Nile, and Japanese encephalitis are closely related, and antibodies against one frequently react in a test designed for another. The problem is acute in a person who has had dengue before, because a second infection with a different serotype produces a rapid and very high antibody response that several flavivirus tests will detect at once. This means a single positive dengue antibody test in a previously infected person may reflect the old infection, the new one, or a cross-reaction, and a high level makes secondary dengue more likely, which is the infection that carries the greatest risk of severe disease. Where only antibody testing exists, the timing of the specimen relative to the day of illness is what makes the result interpretable.

Similar caution applies elsewhere. Rickettsial infections cross-react with one another and with some other bacteria, so a positive test may identify the group without the species. Leptospirosis serology can react with syphilis and other spirochetes, and the microscopic agglutination test, the reference method, is performed in very few laboratories. Brucellosis serology can react with other gram-negative organisms, and antibody tests for tuberculosis have performed so poorly that they should not replace sputum examination and culture. The World Health Organization and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention both caution against basing treatment on a single serological result, and that caution is worth carrying into every clinic.

Stool examination is the workhorse of intestinal parasitology, and it is done in three different ways for three different purposes. The Kato-Katz method uses a measured template and cellophane soaked in glycerol and malachite green, producing a thick smear in which the eggs of Ascaris lumbricoides, Trichuris trichiura, hookworm, and Schistosoma mansoni can be counted. Because the volume of stool under the template is known, the count converts into eggs per gram, which is how the intensity of infection is graded and how a program measures its effect over time. The method is cheap, needs only a microscope, and is the standard for surveys and mass treatment programs. Its weakness is that it samples very little stool, so light infections are missed and sensitivity falls when a specimen sits at room temperature.

The formol-ether concentration method exists to offset that weakness. A portion of stool is emulsified in formalin, strained, shaken with ether or ethyl acetate, and centrifuged, which separates the fatty debris and leaves the eggs and cysts in a small sediment examined in a few fields. It recovers more eggs and cysts than a direct smear and is the better test for light infections, for protozoan cysts, and for specimens that must travel before examination, though it needs a centrifuge and cannot measure intensity. Neither method performs well on a single specimen, so at least two or three stool specimens collected on different days should be examined before a negative result is accepted.

Strongyloides stercoralis, the threadworm that causes strongyloidiasis, needs a method of its own, because it sheds larvae rather than eggs and those larvae are easily missed. The Baermann method places fresh stool on gauze over a funnel of warm water, so the larvae migrate down and can be collected from the bottom after an hour or two. A single Baermann examination detects perhaps one half of infections, and the yield rises with repetition, which is why a patient with a compatible history and a negative test may still need treatment. An agar plate culture is more sensitive in experienced hands. Because strongyloidiasis can persist for decades and can produce overwhelming hyperinfection when the patient later receives corticosteroids, the screening question matters as much as the test.

Urine filtration is the standard method for Schistosoma haematobium, the species that settles in the veins around the bladder. A measured volume of urine, usually 10 milliliters, is drawn through a membrane filter, and the filter is examined under the microscope where the terminal-spined eggs are counted. Collecting the specimen around midday raises the yield, because eggs accumulate in the bladder overnight and are released during the day, so a first-morning specimen may be misleadingly empty. The count is reported as eggs per 10 milliliters and grades the intensity of infection just as the stool egg count grades intestinal schistosomiasis. Visible blood in the urine, or a positive dipstick for blood, is a useful clue in a high-prevalence setting and can guide which children to examine when laboratory time is scarce.

Onchocerciasis, the infection caused by Onchocerca volvulus, is diagnosed by finding microfilariae in the skin. A skin snip is taken with a small punch or a razor, usually from the shoulder, the buttock, or the calf, and a bloodless piece of skin is placed in saline and incubated for several hours so the microfilariae emerge and can be seen moving under the microscope. The procedure must be clean and explained to the patient, because it leaves a small scar. Two snips from different sites raise the yield, though the test cannot detect infection before the adult worms release microfilariae and is unhelpful after ivermectin. Palpating for subcutaneous nodules is a complementary skill, and the nurse who finds a nodule has found the adult worms themselves.

Lymph node aspiration is a small procedure that answers several tropical questions at once. In human African trypanosomiasis, fluid aspirated from an enlarged cervical node can be examined directly for trypanosomes moving among the cells, and a positive result is diagnostic without further tests. In leishmaniasis, aspirate from a node or from the edge of a skin lesion can show amastigotes inside the cells, and the same material serves for culture or a polymerase chain reaction test where available. In tuberculosis, a node aspirate sent for microscopy, culture, or a molecular test can confirm a diagnosis that sputum examination would never reach. Aspiration is also part of the assessment of suspected plague, and the nurse prepares the patient, labels the slides at the bedside, and makes sure the material reaches the laboratory fresh.

The polymerase chain reaction, which everyone calls PCR, detects the genetic material of an organism rather than the organism itself, and it can find a pathogen when very few copies are present. In tropical medicine it can identify a malaria species from a dried blood spot, confirm a dengue or chikungunya diagnosis in the first days of illness, detect Mycobacterium tuberculosis, and distinguish the subspecies that cause human African trypanosomiasis. Its strength is also its demand, because it needs a clean laboratory, a stable supply of reagents, reliable electricity, trained staff, and a specimen that arrives before the nucleic acid has degraded. Dried blood spots on filter paper help, since they can be stored and shipped at room temperature and processed much later. Where PCR exists it is usually at a provincial or national laboratory and answers questions asked days earlier.

When no molecular test exists, the clinician is not without options, but the options change shape. The syndromic approach replaces the organism-specific diagnosis, so a patient with fever and an eschar is treated for a rickettsial infection without waiting for serology, and a patient with bloody diarrhea and tenesmus is treated for shigellosis on the strength of the clinical picture. Empirical treatment becomes a deliberate and documented decision, with a reason recorded, a review date set, and a plan for what to do if the patient does not improve. Specimens that can be sent should still be sent, because the result may guide the next patient even when it is too late for this one. What must never happen is that the absence of a laboratory becomes the absence of a decision.

Point-of-care testing moves part of the laboratory to the bedside, and its value is measured by how quickly an answer changes management. The best-known example in tuberculosis is the GeneXpert system, a cartridge-based molecular test that takes a sputum specimen and reports within about two hours whether Mycobacterium tuberculosis is present and whether the gene conferring resistance to rifampicin is mutated. That second result is why the test has been so widely adopted, because it turns a diagnosis and a drug-susceptibility signal into a single morning's work instead of a wait of weeks. The cartridge has a shelf life and a temperature range, and the machine needs stable power and occasional calibration. A negative result does not exclude tuberculosis when the clinical picture is convincing, particularly in a child who cannot produce good sputum, and a positive result does not say whether the disease is active or merely old scarring.

Other point-of-care tests are so familiar that they are easy to take for granted. A glucose meter answers in seconds and is essential in severe malaria, in the malnourished child, and in any patient with impaired consciousness, but strips expire, the meter must be coded to the strip, and a reading from a cold or wet finger can be wrong. A hemoglobin measurement, from a portable device or a centrifuge, guides transfusion and detects the anemia of hookworm and malaria. A urine dipstick detects blood, protein, glucose, ketones, nitrite, and leukocyte esterase, and it is one of the most useful and least expensive tests in a tropical clinic, provided the strip is read at the correct time. A pregnancy test changes treatment choices in malaria and in leishmaniasis, and rapid tests for HIV, for hepatitis B, and for syphilis extend the same principle to the bedside.

Understanding sensitivity and specificity is the arithmetic that turns a result into a decision. Sensitivity is the proportion of people with the disease who test positive, so a highly sensitive test misses few cases and is used when missing a diagnosis is costly. Specificity is the proportion of people without the disease who test negative, so a highly specific test produces few false positives and is used when a false diagnosis would be harmful. No test is perfect, and the two properties trade against each other when the threshold moves, so a test with 95 per cent sensitivity and 95 per cent specificity is excellent yet still depends entirely on who was tested. The missing piece is prevalence, the share of the tested group who actually have the disease, so a positive rapid test is usually true where malaria is common and mostly false where it is rare.

Every diagnostic procedure carries its own risk, and the nurse who performs or supports it must know what to watch for. A skin snip bleeds and can become infected, so the site is cleaned and the patient is told how to care for it. A lumbar puncture, performed to stage human African trypanosomiasis or diagnose meningitis, can be followed by a headache worse on sitting up, and rarely by bleeding or infection, so the patient is observed and hydrated afterward. Venipuncture can cause a hematoma, and a patient with a bleeding disorder or a low platelet count may bleed far more than expected, which is a particular concern in dengue and in viral hemorrhagic fevers. The monitoring is not incidental to the diagnosis; it is part of the care that makes the diagnosis possible.

Testing and treating are intertwined where the result may not return in time. A patient treated empirically for a rickettsial infection might receive doxycycline 100 milligrams by mouth twice a day, a patient treated empirically for strongyloidiasis might receive ivermectin 200 micrograms per kilogram by mouth once, and a patient treated empirically for schistosomiasis might receive praziquantel 40 milligrams per kilogram by mouth in divided doses. Those figures are what current guidance from the World Health Organization says at the time of writing, and regimens are revised, guidance differs between countries, and you must check the current national guideline before you prescribe. The nurse's part is to verify the dose against the guideline in hand, to check the weight, the kidney function, the pregnancy status, and the interacting medicines, and to know the adverse effects that require a call. Doxycycline can cause sensitivity to sunlight, ivermectin a reaction when microfilariae die in large numbers, and praziquantel dizziness and abdominal discomfort.

Quality control is the discipline that keeps a laboratory honest, and in a low-resource setting it is mostly about temperature, dates, and records. Reagents have expiry dates and storage temperatures, and a box of rapid tests left in a metal cabinet in the sun may fail long before the date printed on it. A refrigerator used for specimens, reagents, and vaccines must have a thermometer inside, and the temperature must be read and written down at least once a day, because a refrigerator that cycles between freezing and warm destroys some reagents and preserves others imperfectly. Blood culture media, serology kits, and assay plates all have their own requirements, and a night without power can undo a month of careful storage. Where a cold chain cannot be guaranteed, the test that does not need cold storage is often the better choice.

Supply chains decide which tests a clinic can offer, and a test that is unavailable leaves a gap nobody has planned for. Stockouts happen for reasons that have nothing to do with the patient, because a national tender is delayed, a shipment is held at a border, or a road washes out in the rainy season. The practical responses are to keep a buffer stock of the tests that change management most, to record consumption honestly, and to know the lead time for each item. Expiry dates must be checked on receipt and again on use, and stock must be rotated so the oldest is used first. Transport is the quiet half of the same problem, because a specimen bound for a district laboratory may travel by motorcycle, bicycle, boat, or on foot, packaged in a leakproof primary container, a leakproof secondary container with absorbent material, and a rigid outer container with the paperwork.

All of this comes back to the nurse, because the nurse usually decides that a test is needed, prepares the patient, takes or supervises the specimen, labels it, stores it, and sends it. At the front of that chain the nurse asks whether the patient has already taken medicine, whether the timing is right, and whether the patient understands what is being collected. At the back of the chain the nurse protects the specimen from heat, labels it correctly, and hands it to the right courier at the right time. Documentation holds the process together, so the record should carry the date and time of collection, the site and method, the test requested, any treatment already given, and the result when it arrives. When a result is unexpected the record should show that it was noticed, questioned, and reconciled with the clinical picture, because a test nobody can find or interpret has achieved nothing.

Another discipline is choosing the tests that will change what happens and declining the ones that will not. Each specimen costs money, time, and the attention of the person who processes it, and where those are scarce the marginal test is expensive even when its price is low. A test whose result will not alter treatment, or will not reach the clinician in time, is better not requested. Results must then reach the patient, since a patient told only that the test was normal may not return for the repeat that was planned, while a patient told that a result is positive without explanation may stop treatment as soon as they feel better. The nurse should explain in concrete terms what was tested, what the result means, and what happens next, and where a result is uncertain, saying so honestly is better than offering false certainty.

The single most important idea in this chapter is that a negative test is not the same as the absence of disease. A test can be negative because the organism is present in numbers below the detection limit, because the specimen was taken at the wrong time in the illness, because it was handled badly, because the test itself is insensitive, or because the patient was treated before collection. Malaria is the classic example, since a single film can be negative in a patient who is genuinely infected and a rapid test can be negative in a patient whose parasite carries a histidine-rich protein 2 deletion. Tuberculosis is another, because a sputum smear may be negative in active disease, especially in a child or a person with HIV. The way to weigh such a result is to keep the pre-test probability in view and to decide in advance what a negative result will change, so that high suspicion with an insensitive test prompts a repeat, a different test, or a documented decision to treat on clinical grounds.

To summarize, diagnostics in a resource-limited setting are a chain of decisions in which the specimen, the timing, the technique, the storage, the transport, the arithmetic of the result, and the communication of that result each carry as much weight as the test itself. The thick and thin malaria films, the rapid test with its histidine-rich protein 2 deletions, the blood culture with its volume and its contamination, the paired sera with their cross-reactivity and their dengue problem, the Kato-Katz and formol-ether and Baermann methods, the urine filtration, the polymerase chain reaction where it exists, and the point-of-care cartridge for tuberculosis all answer different questions and fail in different ways. Sensitivity and specificity describe a test, but prevalence and the pre-test probability decide what a result means for this patient. Quality control, refrigeration, expiry dates, and supply chains are not administrative housekeeping but the conditions under which a result can be believed at all. The habit of asking what a result can prove, what a negative result could still hide, and what will be done differently either way is what makes a limited laboratory safe, and a negative test is never absence of disease but the absence of evidence.
