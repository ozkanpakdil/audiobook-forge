# Chapter 2 — Epidemiology and the Determinants of Tropical Disease

Epidemiology is the study of how disease is distributed in populations and of what determines that distribution, and for a nurse practitioner it is a set of working instruments rather than an abstraction. Almost every measurement in this chapter answers one of two questions, which are how many people are becoming ill and who is becoming ill. The first question describes the pressure of transmission in a community, and the second describes where that pressure is concentrated and which of your patients is most exposed. When a child arrives with fever, the pattern of disease around that child belongs in the assessment alongside the temperature and the respiratory rate. This chapter moves from the measures themselves, through the biology of climate and vector survival, into the social conditions that decide who is at risk, and finally to the surveillance systems that produce the numbers you are asked to trust. The aim is to make you a clinician who can hear a number, ask what it means, and use it.

Incidence is the number of new cases of a disease that appear in a defined population during a defined period. You express it as a count or as a rate, and the rate requires two things that are easy to leave out, which are the size of the population at risk and the length of time over which you counted. If 40 people in a village of 2,000 develop malaria in 4 weeks, the incidence is 40 cases among 2,000 people over 4 weeks. That figure is useful because it measures the force with which infection is being transmitted right now. Prevalence tells you how much disease exists, but incidence tells you how fast people are being infected, and it is incidence that changes first when an intervention begins to work.

Prevalence is the proportion of a population that has the disease at a particular moment or during a particular period. Point prevalence counts everyone who has the condition on one day, and period prevalence counts everyone who had it at any time during a window such as a year. Prevalence depends on how many people develop the disease and on how long each of them remains a case. A disease that is easy to acquire and slow to resolve can produce a large burden of illness even when the number of new infections is modest. This is why lymphatic filariasis and Chagas disease generate such heavy caseloads in a clinic while causing comparatively few new infections each year.

The relationship between incidence and prevalence is one of the most practical things a nurse can carry into a clinic. When a disease lasts a long time, prevalence is high and incidence may be low, and your work becomes long-term management of chronic problems such as lymphedema or cardiac disease. When a disease is short and transmission is intense, incidence is high and prevalence may be low, and your work becomes rapid recognition and treatment. A single number cannot tell you which situation you are in, and asking for both numbers is the first habit of an epidemiologically literate clinician. Service planning follows directly from this distinction, because a clinic full of chronic cases needs different staffing from a clinic full of acute ones.

An attack rate is a special kind of incidence used when a group is exposed to a common source over a short period. If 30 of 120 people who attended a wedding become ill with diarrhea within 2 days, the attack rate is 30 cases among the 120 people exposed. The attack rate is powerful during an outbreak because it lets you compare those who ate a particular food with those who did not, and the difference between the two attack rates points toward the source. A secondary attack rate does the same work inside a household, measuring how many susceptible contacts of a known case become ill. For a nurse responding to an outbreak, the attack rate is the arithmetic that turns a list of patients into a hypothesis.

Case fatality is the proportion of people diagnosed with a disease who die from it. This measure is stated constantly and understood less often than it should be, because its value depends entirely on who is counted in the denominator. Case fatality in cholera can approach 50 per cent when patients reach care late and cannot be rehydrated, yet it can fall below 1 per cent when rehydration begins early. The organism has not changed between those two settings, and what has changed is access to treatment and the speed of recognition. A case fatality rate is therefore as much a measurement of a health system as it is a measurement of a pathogen, and reading it that way is a clinical skill.

Comparisons between settings are treacherous unless the definitions match. One country may count only laboratory-confirmed cases, another may count every patient who fits a clinical case definition, and a third may count only patients who reach a hospital. Test positivity adds another layer of confusion, because the proportion of tests that are positive rises when testing is restricted to the sickest patients and falls when testing is widened to everyone. A fall in reported incidence can be a genuine victory or simply a shortage of test kits. Before you believe any trend, ask who was counted, how they were identified, and whether the testing itself changed.

The basic reproduction number is the average number of people who will be infected by one infectious person in a population that is entirely susceptible. In plain language, it is the answer to the question of how many new cases one case creates when nobody has any protection. Measles has a very high basic reproduction number, which is why it sweeps through unvaccinated groups so quickly, while many vector-borne infections have lower numbers and spread more slowly. The value is not a fixed property of a germ, because it depends on crowding, behavior, and the density of mosquitoes. When immunity reduces the number below one, each case creates fewer than one new case and the epidemic shrinks.

Herd immunity describes the protection that a population gives to people who are not themselves immune. If enough people are immune, an infectious person is unlikely to meet a susceptible one, and transmission cannot sustain itself. The proportion who must be immune depends on the basic reproduction number, and it becomes very high for a disease that spreads easily. This is why measles programs aim for coverage above 90 per cent while some other diseases can be interrupted at lower levels. Vector-borne infections complicate the idea, because a mosquito can carry infection past a wall of immune neighbors and reach a susceptible household. Coverage is measured among people, but protection is delivered by a system.

A reproduction number does not describe everything that matters. Two diseases can share the same number and behave very differently, because one may pass through long chains of quiet transmission while the other produces explosive clusters from a few highly infectious events. The interval between one case and the next, called the generation time, decides how fast an epidemic grows even when the reproduction number is unchanged. Contact patterns are also uneven, since a few crowded households, schools, or workplaces do much of the work of transmission. For a nurse, this means that the average is never the whole story and that the local social map is where the average becomes real.

Temperature governs whether a vector can survive and whether an infection can mature inside it. Mosquitoes and the parasites they carry are cold-blooded in effect, so their internal processes speed up as the air warms and slow as it cools. Below a threshold that differs by species, development stops altogether, and above another threshold the insect dies faster than it can reproduce. The mosquito Anopheles gambiae, which carries most malaria in Africa, generally transmits most efficiently in warm and humid conditions. The mosquito Aedes aegypti, the urban vector of dengue, tolerates a wider range of temperature and thrives in cities. A temperature that suits one vector may suppress another, which is why the details of place matter more than the general rule.

The extrinsic incubation period is the time an infection needs inside the vector before that vector can pass it on. A mosquito that takes up malaria parasites is not immediately infectious, because the parasite must develop through several stages in the insect gut and salivary glands. Warmth shortens that period, which means that a mosquito in a hot place may become dangerous within a week, while the same mosquito in a cooler place may die of old age before it ever becomes infectious. Transmission therefore requires a fortunate overlap, a vector that lives long enough and an environment warm enough. This is why small changes in temperature can produce large changes in transmission, and why vector survival matters as much as vector abundance.

Rainfall creates breeding sites, and the timing of rain shapes the timing of disease. Standing water in containers, ditches, puddles, and rice fields gives larvae somewhere to develop, and a rise in adult mosquitoes usually follows 2 to 4 weeks after the rains begin. River-breeding mosquitoes respond differently from container breeders, because a flood can flush larvae away even while it creates new pools elsewhere. In dry seasons, many species persist in small refuges such as wells, tree holes, and stored water, and they expand again when the rains return. Seasonality is therefore not a calendar but a pattern that differs from one vector and one region to the next.

Altitude sets a ceiling on some diseases because temperature falls as you climb. Malaria has historically been rare in highland areas above roughly 1,500 meters, a limit often called the malaria line, although the exact height differs with latitude, season, and local weather. When warm spells occur at the edge of that line, epidemics can follow in populations with little acquired immunity, and these outbreaks can be severe because adults are as susceptible as children. A patient's village altitude is worth asking about, because a fever acquired in the highlands carries a different differential from a fever acquired in the lowlands. The line is a tendency rather than a wall, and it moves with the climate around it.

The El Niño and Southern Oscillation cycle is a large-scale pattern of ocean warming and cooling that shifts rainfall across continents. In some regions it brings drought, in others heavy rain, and these anomalies precede outbreaks of malaria, cholera, and Rift Valley fever. Because the ocean signal appears months before the epidemics, it can be used for early warning, and programs in some countries have used forecasts to position supplies and intensify vector control in advance. The strong event of 1997 and 1998 was followed by unusually severe epidemics in several regions, which is part of why the connection is taken seriously. Climate patterns do not cause disease directly, and they change the odds across a landscape of vulnerability.

Extreme weather produces outbreaks along pathways that a nurse can anticipate. Floods displace families, contaminate wells, and force people to store water in containers that become breeding sites. Drought concentrates people and animals around scarce water, which can amplify both diarrheal disease and zoonotic transmission. Cyclones destroy clinics and roads, interrupt treatment, and leave crowded shelters behind. Leptospirosis may follow flooding when people wade through water contaminated by rodent urine, and cholera may follow when sanitation collapses. The pattern to remember is that the first disaster is followed by a second wave of infectious disease, and the second wave is often the one that can be prevented.

Climate change is expected to alter the range and season of transmission, though the honest statement is that the size of the effect remains uncertain and differs by disease and place. Warmer highlands may become suitable for malaria, longer warm seasons may extend the period of vector activity, and changing rainfall may create both new habitat and new droughts. Sea level rise and salt water intrusion can displace communities into crowded conditions that favor transmission. At the same time, economic development, housing, water supply, and health care often influence risk more powerfully than a change of a degree or two. Climate acts on transmission through the same social channels that poverty has already opened.

Poverty, not climate, is the fundamental determinant of tropical disease. The tropics contain many poor countries, and the correlation between warmth and disease is largely a correlation between warmth and deprivation. When a tropical country becomes wealthy, the same climate supports dramatically less disease, as the histories of Singapore, northern Australia, and the southern United States show. Money buys screened windows, piped water, reliable electricity, refrigeration, transport, and paid time to seek care. A pathogen may be tropical in its biology, but its burden is distributed by income, and the geography is often a proxy for the conditions of life.

Housing is where the social determinants become physical. Thatch, mud walls, open eaves, and gaps around doors give mosquitoes and triatomine bugs a way in, and sleeping without a net or a screen turns the evening hours into exposure. Crowding within a household increases the transmission of tuberculosis and other infections spread by respiratory droplets. Pigs kept under or beside a house link the pork tapeworm cycle to human disease, and unventilated cooking fires fill the home with smoke that damages lungs already stressed by infection. Asking how a patient sleeps, where the animals are kept, and how the house is built is not small talk, because it is a description of exposure.

Clean water and basic sanitation remove whole categories of disease from a community. Water contaminated with human waste carries cholera, typhoid, and a long list of diarrheal infections, and the absence of latrines keeps that fecal material circulating in the environment. Soil-transmitted helminths depend on the same cycle, since their eggs and larvae reach people through contaminated soil and water. Schistosomiasis requires contact with fresh water that contains the parasite released by snails. Trachoma spreads through face-to-face contact and shared cloth in conditions where washing is difficult. The evidence that these interventions reduce disease is strong, and the obstacles are usually political and financial rather than biological.

Nutrition and infection reinforce each other in a loop that a nurse sees constantly. A malnourished child has a weaker barrier against infection and a slower immune response, and an infected child loses appetite, absorbs less, and burns more energy. Measles, tuberculosis, and diarrheal disease are all more severe in a child who is already thin, and repeated infections produce the stunting that lasts a lifetime. Hookworm causes chronic blood loss and iron deficiency anemia, and malaria destroys red cells and further erodes the blood count. Screening nutrition and treating anemia as a clinical finding, rather than as a background condition, changes outcomes.

Access to care turns a treatable infection into a fatal one. Distance, the cost of transport, fees at the clinic, lost wages, drug stockouts, and the timing of the market and the harvest all decide whether a patient arrives in the first days of illness or the second week. Case fatality for a disease is therefore a measure of how quickly and how reliably care is delivered, and it is the part of the picture that nursing practice can change most directly. The World Health Organization, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control all publish treatment guidance, and that guidance is revised as evidence accumulates. Regimens are revised, guidance differs between countries, and the current guideline must be checked before prescribing.

Migration and displacement place people into conditions that amplify transmission. Families displaced by conflict, drought, or flood often settle in camps where shelter is crowded, water is shared, and latrines are insufficient. Such settings favor measles in unvaccinated children, cholera and other diarrheal diseases, tuberculosis, and the mosquito-borne infections that follow standing water. Displacement also interrupts treatment for malaria, HIV, and tuberculosis, which can lead to relapse and to the emergence of resistance. Migrant workers and seasonal laborers move between areas of different transmission and carry infection with them. The determinants of camp disease are planning failures as much as they are biological ones.

Urban growth in low-income settings has produced the conditions in which Aedes mosquitoes flourish. These mosquitoes breed in domestic containers, discarded tires, water tanks, flower pots, and the small volumes of water that collect around a crowded settlement. Because they bite during the day, insecticide-treated bed nets offer little protection, and because they live closely with people, a single water shortage can create thousands of breeding sites. Dengue has become a disease of cities for exactly these reasons, and its epidemics follow the rainy season and the pattern of water storage. Urban dengue is a disease of infrastructure, and its control belongs as much to water management as to the clinic.

Land-use change creates new habitat and new human contact with vectors. Clearing forest for timber, farmland, or mining exposes workers and settlers to mosquitoes that previously fed on animals, and the forest edge becomes a zone of intense malaria transmission. Roads cut into remote areas bring people, standing water, and commerce together. Dams and irrigation schemes hold water for long periods and can expand the habitat of the snails that carry schistosomiasis, while rice paddies support large mosquito populations. Rift Valley fever has followed irrigation and heavy rain, and gold mining has produced malaria outbreaks in several countries. Development projects are necessary, and their health consequences must be planned for rather than discovered.

Mobile populations and their animals carry infections across the distances that separate ecosystems. Pastoralists move with herds in search of pasture and water, sleeping outdoors and sharing water sources with settled communities. Seasonal farm workers move at harvest, and traders move between market towns and villages. These movements maintain transmission between places that might otherwise be separated by distance or season, and they make a purely local malaria program insufficient. Where people and animals share water and shelter, zoonotic infections find opportunities, and the pattern of movement is the map along which they travel.

Surveillance data come from several different kinds of system, and each has its own biases. Passive surveillance counts the cases that reach health facilities and are reported upward, which means it captures the visible part of the burden and misses the rest. Sentinel sites are selected clinics that report more detail and often provide laboratory confirmation, and they give a consistent signal even when national reporting is uneven. Demographic surveillance platforms follow whole populations over years and can measure incidence directly, which is expensive but unusually informative. Entomological surveys count mosquitoes and measure the infection rate inside them, and household surveys estimate prevalence and the coverage of interventions in the community.

The quality of surveillance data is limited by several predictable problems. Cases are not reported when staff are absent, forms are lost, or reporting is not valued, and laboratory confirmation is missing wherever tests are unavailable. The population denominator may be a projection from an old census, and it may be badly wrong after conflict or displacement. Delay means that the numbers describe the epidemic of last month, and a late report can be worse than no report if it creates false confidence. In fragile states, the reported fraction of the true burden can be very small. Reading a surveillance report honestly means reading its limitations as carefully as its totals.

Case definitions are the foundation on which all of this rests, because a number is meaningless unless everyone is counting the same thing. A case definition states the clinical features, the time frame, the place, and the laboratory evidence that qualify a patient, and it changes as knowledge changes. An epidemic threshold is the level above which the observed number of cases exceeds what is expected for that place and season, and it is the trigger for investigation. The nurse is usually the first person to write the name of a disease on a form, which means the accuracy of the entire system begins at the bedside. Notification is also a legal and ethical duty, and it exists to protect populations rather than to audit individuals.

Some determinants can be named clearly and cannot be fixed by any clinician alone. Land tenure, water rights, migration policy, trade rules, debt, conflict, and the global climate are decided far above the clinic, and they shape exposure for every patient who walks through the door. Naming them is not an excuse for inaction, because naming is the first step in advocacy and it keeps the clinical encounter honest. A nurse who understands that a patient's repeated malaria reflects a broken irrigation scheme rather than a failure of personal discipline will counsel differently. The structural determinants require collective action, and the clinical encounter is where their consequences become visible and countable.

What a nurse can do within that landscape is concrete. You can take a structured exposure history that records where a patient lives, how the house is built, where the water comes from, whether a bed net is used, and what the season has been. You can recognize the syndrome in front of you and connect it to the local pattern of disease. You can record accurately, notify promptly, and teach the specific behavior that interrupts transmission in that setting. You can ask for the data you need and question a number that does not fit what you see in the clinic. Each of these acts is small, and together they are the point at which epidemiology becomes care.

A worked example shows how the measures fit together. Suppose a district reports 300 cases of dengue in 6 weeks, the population is 150,000, and 3 patients have died. The incidence is 300 cases among 150,000 people over 6 weeks, the attack rate in the most affected neighborhood may be far higher, and the case fatality is 1 per cent. If deaths rise while cases stay flat, the problem may be access to care rather than transmission, and if cases rise while deaths stay flat, the problem may be reporting or a milder circulating strain. Each number sends you to a different question, and the questions are more useful than the totals. The clinical response follows the World Health Organization guidance on fluid management, and that guidance is updated as evidence changes, so the current version must be consulted before treatment is prescribed.

Vector control programs are judged by the same measures as clinical care. Coverage of insecticide-treated bed nets is reported as the proportion of households with at least one net and the proportion of people who slept under one the previous night, and those two numbers can differ substantially. Indoor residual spraying requires a high proportion of structures to be treated and repeated at the correct interval to remain effective. Larval source management reduces the number of breeding sites and is measured by the reduction in positive containers. Insecticide resistance can quietly remove the benefit of all of these tools, which is why susceptibility testing belongs in the monitoring plan. Coverage is measured in people and structures, and transmission is what the program is actually trying to change.

Mass drug administration applies a population measure to a population problem. When the prevalence of a helminth infection in a community exceeds a defined threshold, treatment is offered to everyone in the eligible group rather than only to those who test positive, because the tests miss many infections and because reducing the reservoir of infection protects the whole community. Coverage must be high and repeated over several years for transmission to be interrupted, and partial coverage allows the parasite to return. The drugs are given at standard doses under national program guidance, and the regimens and the eligibility criteria differ between countries and are revised over time. Monitoring for adverse effects and for coverage is part of the intervention, not an afterthought.

Translating epidemiology into nursing practice means asking a few questions in a fixed order. What is the likely diagnosis given this patient, this place, and this season. How common is it here, and how severe is it likely to be. What in this patient's home, water, work, or travel increases the risk. What intervention will actually be delivered, and who will deliver it. What must be recorded so that the next patient benefits from this one. These questions take seconds to ask, and they connect the individual in front of you to the population behind them, which is the whole purpose of the discipline.

To summarize, epidemiology gives you four measures that answer different questions, which are incidence, prevalence, attack rate, and case fatality, and each one sends you toward a different clinical and public health response. The basic reproduction number and herd immunity explain why coverage thresholds differ between diseases and why a population can protect an individual. Temperature, rainfall, altitude, and large climate cycles shape where and when vectors can transmit, and they act always through the conditions of housing, water, sanitation, and work. Poverty is the fundamental determinant, and migration, urbanization, deforestation, and irrigation redistribute risk across the landscape. Surveillance data come from several imperfect systems, and reading them honestly is part of clinical judgment. The nurse who can measure, question, record, and teach at the bedside is the point at which all of this becomes care.
