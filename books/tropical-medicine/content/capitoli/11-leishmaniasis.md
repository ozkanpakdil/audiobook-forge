# Chapter 11 — Leishmaniasis

Leishmaniasis is caused by protozoan parasites of the genus Leishmania, transmitted by the bite of infected female sand flies, and it appears in three clinical forms so different that a listener may wonder why they share a chapter. The World Health Organization counts it among the neglected tropical diseases and estimates between 700,000 and 1 million new cases each year, with the visceral form alone causing an estimated 50,000 to 90,000 deaths annually when untreated. The parasites occur in more than 90 countries, and the burden falls hardest on people in poverty, in poor housing, and in conflict zones where the vector and the animal reservoir meet human beings. The three forms are visceral leishmaniasis, which attacks the internal organs and kills if untreated; cutaneous leishmaniasis, which produces skin ulcers that heal slowly and scar; and mucocutaneous leishmaniasis, which destroys the nose, the mouth, and the throat. Which form appears is determined above all by the species of parasite and secondarily by the immune response of the host. This chapter follows the parasite from the sand fly into the macrophage, through the three forms, through the medicines and their dangers, and finally to the nursing work that carries a patient through treatment and recovery.

The parasite has two forms, and the listener needs both to understand the diagnosis. In the gut of the sand fly it exists as a promastigote, an elongated cell with a free flagellum that propels it forward, and when the female takes a blood meal she injects these forms into the skin with saliva that dilates the small vessels and suppresses the local immune response. The promastigotes are taken up by macrophages, the cells whose ordinary work is to engulf and destroy invaders, and inside the macrophage they transform into amastigotes. The amastigote is round or oval, has no free flagellum, and divides until the macrophage bursts, and it is the amastigote that the microscopist looks for in a stained aspirate. More than 20 species are recognized, each with its own geography and host. Some are maintained in people, so that an infected person is the reservoir from which sand flies become infected, which is the pattern of Leishmania donovani in the Indian subcontinent, while others are maintained in animals, with dogs as the reservoir for Leishmania infantum around the Mediterranean and in Latin America. The distinction matters, because a parasite held in people can be interrupted by treating every human case, while one held in animals cannot.

The vector is a sand fly, an insect of the genus Phlebotomus in the Old World and of the genus Lutzomyia in the New World. Sand flies are small, 2 to 3 millimeters long, with hairy bodies and wings held in a shape like the letter V at rest. They fly silently, they are most active in the evening and through the night, and their bite is often not felt. The female, which needs blood for her eggs, breeds in moist organic material, in the cracks of mud walls, in animal sheds, in rodent burrows, and in leaf litter around houses. Sand flies are weak fliers that stay close to the breeding site, so the disease is intensely focal, sometimes appearing at one end of a village and not the other. They can pass through the mesh of a bed net that is untreated or coarsely woven, so the net that protects against malaria is not automatically enough. Control rests on the measures that reduce other vector-borne diseases, which are indoor residual spraying, insecticide-treated bed nets, screening of windows and doors, removal of organic waste and rubble, treatment of animal sheds, and repellents on exposed skin.

Which of the three diseases emerges depends on where the parasite can survive, and that is largely a property of the species. Species that multiply in the macrophages of the spleen, the liver, the bone marrow, and the lymph nodes produce visceral disease, and the two most important are Leishmania donovani, which causes human-maintained disease in the Indian subcontinent and East Africa, and Leishmania infantum, which causes animal-maintained disease around the Mediterranean, in the Middle East, and in Latin America. Species that remain in the skin produce cutaneous disease, including Leishmania major and Leishmania tropica in the Old World and Leishmania mexicana in the Americas. Species that leave the skin, travel through the lymph vessels, and settle in the mucosa of the upper airway produce mucocutaneous disease, and the leading species are Leishmania braziliensis, Leishmania panamensis, and Leishmania guyanensis. The host contributes as much as the parasite in some patients, because a person who is malnourished, who is infected with HIV, who takes corticosteroids, or who is otherwise immunosuppressed is more likely to develop severe and disseminated disease and less likely to respond to treatment. 

Visceral leishmaniasis, also called kala-azar, is the form that kills, and its course is insidious. The incubation period is usually 2 to 8 months, but it can be as short as 10 days or as long as several years, which makes the exposure history essential. The patient begins with fever, often twice in 24 hours, with drenching night sweats, progressive weakness, loss of appetite, and weight loss. The spleen enlarges steadily and is usually the most striking finding, followed by the liver, while in East Africa the lymph nodes may also enlarge and the classic Indian picture is fever with a large spleen and little else. The mechanism behind the fever and the wasting is a heavy parasite load in the network of macrophages that fills the spleen, the liver, and the bone marrow. The spleen removes blood cells faster than the marrow can replace them, the marrow itself is crowded with infected macrophages, and the result is pancytopenia, a fall in red cells, white cells, and platelets together. Untreated, the patient becomes profoundly anemic, bleeds easily, and cannot fight bacteria, and those three deficits rather than the parasite usually cause death within 2 years.

One complication of visceral disease is easy to miss and matters to whole communities. Months to years after apparently successful treatment, some patients develop post-kala-azar dermal leishmaniasis, a skin condition of pale or hypopigmented macules, small papules, and nodules, most often on the face, the trunk, and the limbs. In the Indian subcontinent it appears most often 6 months to 3 years after treatment and is reported in perhaps 5 to 10 per cent of patients treated for visceral disease, while in East Africa it tends to appear earlier, sometimes while treatment is still being given, and it often resolves on its own. The lesions contain amastigotes, and the sand fly that feeds on them can become infected and then infect other people, so a person who looks cured and feels well can sustain transmission in a community that believes visceral leishmaniasis has been eliminated from its villages. Treating these lesions is not cosmetic care, and the nurse who notices new pale patches or nodules on the face of a former patient and reports them is closing a loop that vertical programs most often leave open.

Cutaneous leishmaniasis is the form that scars, and its natural history is measured in months. The lesion begins as a small papule at the site of a fly bite, usually on an exposed part of the body such as the face, the forearm, the wrist, or the calf, enlarges into a firm nodule, and after several weeks to months breaks down into an ulcer with a raised and indurated border and a crusted base. The lesion is painless, which allows patients to ignore it for a long time. In the Old World the wet ulcer of Leishmania major develops quickly and heals sooner, while the dry ulcer of Leishmania tropica enlarges slowly and lasts longer, and both have carried local names such as the Aleppo boil and the Baghdad boil. Most lesions heal on their own over 6 months to 2 years, leaving a depressed, pale, permanent scar, and the scar is the true burden of this form, because it falls on the face of a child or the hand of a young person and it carries stigma, exclusion from marriage, and interruption of schooling and work. The differential diagnosis includes cutaneous tuberculosis, sporotrichosis, squamous cell carcinoma, myiasis, and a foreign body reaction, so a lesion that does not heal must be sampled rather than watched indefinitely.

Mucocutaneous leishmaniasis is the most destructive form, and it is the reason that New World cutaneous lesions are treated more aggressively than Old World lesions. It is caused chiefly by Leishmania braziliensis and its close relatives, and it ordinarily appears months to years after a cutaneous lesion has healed, although the original ulcer may still be active or may never have been noticed. The parasite spreads through the lymph vessels from the skin to the mucosa of the nose, the pharynx, the larynx, and the mouth. The earliest symptoms are nasal stuffiness, crusting, and nosebleeds, which are easily mistaken for allergy or a common cold, and destruction follows. The nasal septum perforates, the palate erodes, the turbinates are consumed, and the collapse of the nasal bridge produces the characteristic tapir nose deformity. Laryngeal involvement can obstruct the airway, and the loss of the palate allows food and liquid to escape into the nose and the airway, which leads to aspiration and repeated pneumonia. The disfigurement is severe, progressive, and permanent, and the psychosocial damage is often worse than the physical damage, so every skin lesion acquired where Leishmania braziliensis circulates deserves serious consideration for systemic treatment.

Diagnosis depends on demonstrating the parasite, its genetic material, or a specific immune response. In visceral disease the specimen of choice is a bone marrow aspirate examined for amastigotes inside macrophages, while splenic aspiration gives a higher yield but risks hemorrhage and is reserved for centers that can manage it. Microscopy is the foundation, but its sensitivity ranges from about 50 to 85 per cent and depends on the skill of the microscopist, so a negative smear does not exclude the diagnosis. Testing for parasite DNA by PCR is the most sensitive method, it can be applied to blood, bone marrow, lymph node, skin, or mucosal tissue, and it can identify the species, which increasingly guides the choice of drug. The rK39 rapid test is an immunochromatographic strip that detects antibodies against a parasite antigen, with a sensitivity above 90 per cent in the Indian subcontinent and roughly 60 to 80 per cent in East Africa. The test remains positive for years after cure, so it cannot prove cure or confirm relapse, and it fails more often in patients with HIV, in whom the parasite must be found directly.

Treatment depends on the form of the disease, the species, the country, the immune status of the patient, and the medicines available, and no single regimen fits every setting. Four drugs carry most of the work, which are liposomal amphotericin B, miltefosine, sodium stibogluconate, and paromomycin, each with a different mechanism, route, and set of dangers. The World Health Organization publishes guidance for each region, and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention publishes guidance for the United States, but the regimens differ between countries and change as new trials and resistance data appear. The nurse who gives any of these medicines must work from the current national protocol and product label rather than from memory or from a course such as this one, because a printed dose becomes outdated while a patient is still receiving it. The dose is calculated on the weight measured that day and adjusted for the renal and hepatic function measured that week.

Liposomal amphotericin B is the preferred treatment for visceral leishmaniasis in most settings where it can be afforded, because the medicine is packaged inside lipid spheres that concentrate in the liver, the spleen, and the bone marrow, exactly where the parasite lives. It binds to ergosterol in the parasite membrane and opens holes in it, which kills the amastigote. For visceral disease in the Indian subcontinent, current guidance supports a single intravenous dose of 10 milligrams per kilogram, which is remarkable for a disease that once required a month of injections. Around the Mediterranean and in South America a total dose of 3 milligrams per kilogram is often given as several doses over several days, and in patients with HIV co-infection the total dose is higher and the course longer. The infusion is given intravenously in a fluid volume over about 2 hours, and the nurse watches for fever, chills, shivering, and back pain during the infusion. The toxicities that matter are renal, with a rise in creatinine and a loss of potassium and magnesium, and the liposomal form is far gentler on the kidney than the older deoxycholate preparation. Infusion reactions are common and unpleasant but manageable with a slower infusion, hydration, and premedication.

Miltefosine is the only oral medicine for leishmaniasis in routine use, so a patient can complete treatment at home. It disrupts the phospholipid metabolism of the parasite membrane, and it is used for visceral, cutaneous, and post-kala-azar dermal disease. For visceral disease the dose is 2.5 milligrams per kilogram per day by mouth for 28 days, usually given as 50 milligrams twice a day for patients above 25 kilograms and 50 milligrams once a day for those below. The most important property of miltefosine is that it causes severe harm to a developing fetus. It is contraindicated in pregnancy, every woman of childbearing age must have a documented negative pregnancy test before the first dose, and contraception must continue for 5 months after the last dose because the drug remains in the body. The common adverse effects are vomiting, diarrhea, nausea, and loss of appetite, and treatment is often accompanied by a rise in creatinine and in liver enzymes. Adherence to a 28-day oral course is the central clinical challenge, and the nurse who explains why the full course matters, counts the remaining tablets at each visit, and treats vomiting as a threat to cure holds the difference between cure and relapse.

Sodium stibogluconate and meglumine antimoniate are pentavalent antimonials, long the standard treatment for leishmaniasis and still used in many countries, particularly for cutaneous and mucocutaneous disease, at 20 milligrams per kilogram per day given intravenously or intramuscularly for 28 days. Antimonials prolong the interval between the Q wave and the T wave on the electrocardiogram, and that prolongation can progress to a dangerous ventricular rhythm and to sudden death, which is rare but real. They cause pancreatitis with abdominal pain and a rise in amylase and lipase, and they cause liver injury, renal injury, joint pain, and reactions at the injection site. Risk factors for sudden death include increasing age, heart disease, and renal impairment, and the drug is avoided in patients with HIV because the pancreatic and cardiac risks are greater. Every patient receiving an antimonial needs a baseline electrocardiogram, repeated electrocardiograms during treatment, and periodic measurement of pancreatic enzymes, liver enzymes, renal function, and blood counts.

Paromomycin is an aminoglycoside antibiotic that is also active against Leishmania, and it is given intramuscularly at 15 milligrams per kilogram per day for 21 days. Its main dangers are damage to the ear and damage to the kidney, and hearing loss may begin with ringing in the ears or difficulty hearing high tones and may become permanent, so audiometry before and during treatment is the safeguard. The nurse must ask the patient directly about ringing or muffled hearing at every visit, because the damage can begin before the patient notices it. The World Health Organization supports combination regimens to shorten treatment and to protect the existing drugs from resistance, including paromomycin with sodium stibogluconate in East Africa and liposomal amphotericin B with miltefosine in the Indian subcontinent.

The treatment of cutaneous leishmaniasis depends on whether the lesion is likely to heal safely on its own or to leave unacceptable damage. A single small lesion caused by Leishmania major in the Old World will usually heal without treatment, and the decision may reasonably be to keep it clean and wait, while lesions that are multiple, large, on the face, over a joint, or caused by a species with the potential to spread to the mucosa are treated systemically. Local options include the injection of an antimonial directly into the lesion, heat therapy that raises the lesion to 50 degrees Celsius for about 30 seconds, and freezing with liquid nitrogen. Systemic options include miltefosine, sodium stibogluconate, and liposomal amphotericin B, and the choice is dictated by the species, the number and the site of the lesions, the pregnancy status of the patient, and the national protocol. Mucocutaneous disease requires systemic treatment, often with a higher cumulative dose of liposomal amphotericin B or with an antimonial, and it requires prolonged follow-up because relapse after apparent cure is common and because the destruction can resume quietly in the nose while the patient feels well.

Monitoring is where nursing practice carries the greatest weight, and it should be planned before the first dose rather than improvised afterward. The baseline assessment includes the weight, a complete blood count, liver enzymes, creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, electrolytes, pancreatic enzymes, an electrocardiogram for any patient who will receive an antimonial, a pregnancy test for every woman of childbearing age, and audiometry for any patient who will receive paromomycin. During treatment the nurse records the temperature, the symptoms, the weight, and the fluid intake and urine output, and ensures that the laboratory tests are drawn on schedule rather than when the patient becomes visibly ill. For antimonials the nurse watches for palpitations, dizziness, fainting, and chest discomfort and reports them immediately, because the first sign of a dangerous rhythm may also be the last. For miltefosine the nurse watches the gastrointestinal symptoms, tracks adherence by counting the tablets, and reminds the patient of the contraception rule at every visit. For any patient receiving a potentially nephrotoxic drug the nurse keeps the patient hydrated and watches the urine output, and for any patient receiving an aminoglycoside the nurse asks about hearing at every visit. None of these tasks is glamorous, and all of them are the reason a treatment which can kill a parasite can be given without killing the patient.

HIV infection and visceral leishmaniasis form one of the most difficult combinations in tropical medicine. HIV raises the risk that an infection with Leishmania will progress to visceral disease many times over, while visceral leishmaniasis accelerates the decline of the immune system. The central clinical problem is relapse. Without effective antiretroviral therapy, relapse occurs in a large proportion of patients, often within a year. Current guidance from the World Health Organization recommends a combination of liposomal amphotericin B and miltefosine for HIV co-infection in the Indian subcontinent, followed by preventive treatment given at intervals until the immune system recovers on antiretroviral therapy. Drug interactions must be checked, because amphotericin and tenofovir together increase renal risk, antimonials and didanosine together increase pancreatic risk, and rifampicin lowers the concentration of miltefosine. Some patients develop a paradoxical worsening of inflammation as the immune system recovers, the immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome, and the nurse must not mistake it for treatment failure.

The elimination program in South Asia is one of the clearest examples of a disease brought under control by deliberate effort. The governments of Bangladesh, India, and Nepal, working with the World Health Organization, set a target of fewer than 1 case per 10,000 people per year at the level of the block or the subdistrict. The region had recorded more than 77,000 cases in the early 1990s, most of them in the state of Bihar in India, where mud walls, cattle in the yard, and damp organic waste provided ideal sand fly habitat. The strategy combined indoor residual spraying twice a year, active case detection through house-to-house visits, free diagnosis with the rK39 rapid test, free treatment with a single intravenous dose of liposomal amphotericin B, cash incentives for early reporting, and the training of community health workers. Reported cases fell by more than 90 per cent, and the program became a model for how a vector-borne parasitic disease can be driven down when the tools, the money, and the political commitment arrive together. The last stretch has proved the hardest, because post-kala-azar dermal leishmaniasis keeps a reservoir in cured skin, asymptomatic infections are invisible, sand flies have developed resistance, and the pandemic that began in 2020 interrupted spraying and case finding.

The nursing assessment begins with a question that many clinicians forget to ask, which is where this person has lived, worked, or traveled, and when. The incubation can be months and the bite is usually not felt, so patients rarely volunteer the information that would make the diagnosis obvious. The nurse should ask about residence in a village rather than only about travel to a city, about sleeping arrangements and animals near the house, about season, about agricultural or forest work, and about previous skin lesions that healed with a scar. The physical examination records the spleen and the liver in centimeters below the costal margin, the weight, the nutritional state, the temperature pattern, and the condition of the skin, the nose, and the mouth. A patient with cutaneous disease needs wound care that keeps the lesion clean, covers it with a non-adherent dressing, and protects it from trauma and scratching, because secondary bacterial infection is what makes these lesions painful and slow to heal. A lesion near the eye, on the nose, or over a joint needs referral rather than local wound care, because the cosmetic and functional consequences of waiting are permanent.

Leishmaniasis carries a stigma that outlasts the infection, and the nurse is positioned to reduce both the disease and its social cost. A healed facial scar may keep a child from school, and nasal destruction may drive a man from work and family life. Explaining that the disease is caused by a fly bite and is not contagious from person to person removes a fear that leads to isolation of the patient. Prevention at the household level includes sleeping under an insecticide-treated bed net of fine mesh, screening windows and doors, using repellent on exposed skin in the evening, keeping animals out of the sleeping room, clearing organic waste and damp vegetation from the surroundings, and cooperating with indoor residual spraying. There is no licensed vaccine against any form of leishmaniasis, so prevention rests entirely on reducing contact between the sand fly and the human being. The nurse who explains why the spraying team must enter the house, why the net must be used every night, and why a new skin lesion must be shown to a health worker rather than hidden is doing prevention in its most practical form.

Follow-up after treatment is part of the treatment, and it requires an organized record rather than a good memory. Clinical cure in visceral disease is defined by the disappearance of fever, the return of appetite and weight, and the gradual reduction in the size of the spleen, and laboratory cure follows with the recovery of the blood counts. The rK39 rapid test remains positive for years after cure and must not be used to judge it, so cure is judged clinically and, where it is available, by repeated PCR. Relapse is the return of signs and symptoms with the reappearance of the parasite, and it is far more common in patients with HIV. Follow-up visits are usually scheduled at 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months, to confirm cure, to catch relapse or post-kala-azar dermal leishmaniasis early, and to reinforce the prevention advice that protects the household.

No regimen in this chapter should ever be given from memory. Doses, durations, and total cumulative amounts change as trials are published and as national programs revise their protocols, and the same disease is treated differently in the Indian subcontinent, in East Africa, around the Mediterranean, and in Latin America. Guidance from the World Health Organization and from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention is revised on its own schedule and differs between the countries in which a nurse may practice, so the current national guideline and the current product label must be checked before any antileishmanial medicine is prescribed, dispensed, or administered. The dose is calculated from the weight actually measured that day and adjusted for the renal and hepatic function actually measured that week, because a drug that damages the kidney, the pancreas, and the heart cannot be dosed on a number remembered from a previous admission. A patient who has gained or lost several kilograms during a long illness may need a different dose from the one written at the start, and the nurse who reweighs the patient at every visit is supplying the number that keeps the prescription correct.

Special populations require separate thought. Miltefosine must not be given in pregnancy and should be avoided in women who may become pregnant, and liposomal amphotericin B is generally the preferred treatment for visceral disease in pregnancy because it does not carry that risk and has a long record of safe use. Pentavalent antimonials are avoided in pregnancy, and their cardiac and pancreatic risks make them a poor choice in advanced age and in renal disease. Children tolerate liposomal amphotericin B and miltefosine reasonably well when the dose is calculated per kilogram of body weight, and children with visceral disease often recover faster than adults once treatment begins. Patients with renal impairment need lower doses, closer monitoring, and a preference for liposomal amphotericin B over paromomycin and the antimonials. Patients who are immunosuppressed for any reason should be treated as being at high risk of relapse and followed for longer.

To summarize, leishmaniasis is a protozoan infection transmitted by the bite of a sand fly, and its three forms, visceral, cutaneous, and mucocutaneous, are determined chiefly by the species of Leishmania and secondarily by the immune response of the patient. Visceral disease produces prolonged fever, an enlarging spleen, pancytopenia, and wasting, and it kills within 2 years if it is not treated, while post-kala-azar dermal leishmaniasis can keep a silent reservoir of infection in skin that appears cured. Cutaneous disease is chronic and scarring, mucocutaneous disease destroys the nose, the palate, and the airway, and both cause a stigma that outlasts the physical lesion. Diagnosis rests on microscopy of an aspirate, on PCR, and on the rK39 rapid test, whose result must be interpreted with its regional limits and its inability to prove cure. Treatment draws on liposomal amphotericin B, miltefosine, sodium stibogluconate, and paromomycin, each with cardiac, pancreatic, renal, otologic, or teratogenic dangers that the nurse monitors through scheduled laboratory work, electrocardiograms, audiometry, and pregnancy testing. The nurse's work of assessment, wound care, adherence support, toxicity monitoring, and follow-up is what turns a cure that exists into a cure that reaches the patient.
