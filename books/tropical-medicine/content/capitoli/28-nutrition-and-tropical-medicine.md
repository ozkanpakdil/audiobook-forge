# Chapter 28 — Nutrition and Tropical Medicine

Malnutrition is not a separate subject from tropical medicine. It is the ground on which many tropical infections grow and the residue that many of them leave behind. A child who is undernourished has thinner skin and mucosa, a weaker cell-mediated immune response, and smaller reserves of every nutrient the body needs to survive an acute illness. An adult with a chronic parasitic infection loses nutrients continuously through blood loss, poor absorption, and the metabolic cost of inflammation. Nutrition and infection therefore form a loop, and the loop turns in both directions at once, which is why feeding is a clinical intervention and not a background condition. This chapter follows that loop from the most severe forms of undernutrition to the deficiencies that still cause blindness, heart failure, and bleeding gums, and it ends with the work the nurse actually does, which is measuring, feeding, teaching, and following up.

Malnutrition in the tropics takes two broad shapes that are not opposites. Undernutrition is the shortage of energy, protein, and micronutrients that leaves a person small, thin, and vulnerable to infection. Overnutrition is the excess of energy that produces obesity, diabetes, and their complications. Both can exist in the same village, the same street, and sometimes the same household, so a clinic that screens only for thinness will miss half of the problem. Growth failure is described with three measurements that a voice can carry: stunting, which is low height for age and reflects long-standing deprivation; wasting, which is low weight for height and reflects recent, severe deprivation; and underweight, which is low weight for age and blends the two. The World Health Organization attributes roughly 45 per cent of deaths among children under 5 years of age to undernutrition, acting through diarrhea, pneumonia, measles, and malaria. The nurse should therefore read every growth chart as a record of both nutrition and infection.

The two classic severe forms of protein-energy malnutrition are marasmus and kwashiorkor, and the fastest way to tell them apart at the bedside is to press a thumb against the child's lower leg. Marasmus is severe wasting: the child has lost fat and muscle, the ribs and the bones of the face are visible, the skin is loose over the buttocks, and the child is often alert, hungry, and irritable. Kwashiorkor is the edematous form: the child retains fluid, the legs and feet swell, the belly may be distended, the hair becomes sparse and pale, and the skin may peel in patches. A child with kwashiorkor may look well fed on a scale, because the edema adds kilograms that the body does not have. Many children have both problems at once, and this mixed picture is called marasmic kwashiorkor, with edema layered on top of severe wasting.

The edema of kwashiorkor is bilateral, pitting, and dependent, beginning in the feet and lower legs and moving upward as it worsens. It does not respond to diuretics, and giving a diuretic to a child with nutritional edema is a serious error that can cause circulatory collapse. Nutritional edema is driven by low plasma protein, altered sodium handling, and inflammation, not by a failing heart. The nurse must distinguish it from nephrotic syndrome, in which protein appears in the urine, and from cardiac failure, in which the neck veins are raised and the liver is enlarged. Edema also inflates the weight-for-height measurement, so a child with kwashiorkor can be scored as only moderately wasted while actually being severely depleted of tissue. This is why edema is recorded as a separate finding on every assessment form and not folded into the weight.

Marasmus and kwashiorkor share a common background of poverty, food insecurity, repeated infection, and interrupted feeding. What separates them is partly the pattern of the diet and partly the body's inflammatory response, and the exact mechanism remains debated. Children with either form are at high risk of low blood sugar, low body temperature, infection, and electrolyte disturbance, and they can die within the first days of treatment if feeding is handled carelessly. Both forms impair the immune system, delay wound healing, and reduce the response to vaccines and to treatment of tuberculosis. The child who survives may carry stunting, cognitive delay, and a lifelong tendency toward metabolic disease, so recovery is measured in years and not in weeks.

Severe acute malnutrition is diagnosed in a child 6 to 59 months of age when any one of three findings is present. The first is a weight-for-height that falls far below the median, conventionally more than three standard deviations below it. The second is a mid-upper arm circumference of less than 115 millimeters, measured on the left arm at the midpoint between the shoulder tip and the elbow. The third is bilateral pitting edema of any grade, which by itself defines severe acute malnutrition regardless of the weight. A mid-upper arm circumference between 115 and 125 millimeters identifies moderate acute malnutrition, which carries less immediate risk but still needs a feeding program. In older children and adults the same logic applies with different thresholds, and a body mass index below 16 in an adult signals severe depletion.

Assessment begins with the measurement and never with the impression. The nurse needs a scale that is calibrated and zeroed, a length board for children under 2 years of age and a height stand for older children, and a tape that does not stretch. The child is weighed without shoes and in light clothing, the length is taken lying down for infants and standing for children old enough to stand, and the mid-upper arm circumference is taken three times and recorded. The appetite test, in which the child is offered ready-to-use therapeutic food and observed, separates those who can be treated at home from those who need admission. The history should record breastfeeding, the timing and content of complementary feeds, recent diarrhea or fever, immunizations, and whether food is actually available in the household. The examination should look for pneumonia, tuberculosis, malaria, skin infection, anemia, and dehydration, because each of these changes the plan.

Management of severe acute malnutrition proceeds in phases, and the World Health Organization organizes it into ten steps that can be remembered as the order of danger. The first steps address the immediate threats to life: low blood sugar, low body temperature, dehydration, and electrolyte imbalance. The next steps address infection and micronutrient deficiencies, and only then does the plan turn to the food that will rebuild the child. The phases that carry this sequence are stabilization, transition, and rehabilitation, and each has its own feeding formula, its own monitoring, and its own risks. Trying to rush from admission to catch-up growth is the single most common way to kill a child who was expected to survive.

During stabilization, which covers roughly the first week, the child is kept warm, given glucose if the blood sugar is low, and rehydrated cautiously with oral rehydration salts or, when oral intake fails, with fluids given intravenously. Potassium and magnesium are replaced even when the blood levels look near normal, because total body stores are depleted, and sodium is restricted because the edematous child cannot excrete a load. Broad-spectrum antibiotics are given routinely, because the signs of infection are blunted and the risk of death from unrecognized sepsis is high. Vitamin A is given, along with a multivitamin and folate, but iron is withheld at this stage because free iron can feed bacteria and malaria parasites. Feeding is frequent and small, using a low-energy, low-protein therapeutic milk often labeled F-75, and the child is watched for signs of fluid overload.

Refeeding too fast produces a recognizable syndrome, and the nurse who knows it will prevent most of it. As cells suddenly receive glucose and phosphate, they pull phosphate, potassium, and magnesium out of the blood, and the resulting low phosphate can cause muscle weakness, respiratory failure, heart failure, and seizures. Thiamine is consumed rapidly during this shift, so thiamine deficiency can appear or worsen when feeding begins. The warning signs are a rising pulse, a falling blood pressure, new edema, breathlessness, and lethargy in a child who had been improving. Prevention consists of starting with small volumes, advancing slowly, giving potassium, magnesium, and thiamine, and checking electrolytes when they can be checked. Feeding is advanced only when the child is truly stable, and the appetite is allowed to set the pace.

The transition phase begins when the child's appetite returns and the edema is resolving, usually somewhere between the seventh and fourteenth day. Feeding switches to a higher-energy therapeutic milk often labeled F-100, given in larger volumes at longer intervals, and the child is watched closely for the reappearance of edema or heart failure. Once the child is taking the higher-energy milk without difficulty, the diet widens to include ready-to-use therapeutic food and then ordinary family foods. The rehabilitation phase is the long stretch of catch-up growth, during which the child gains weight quickly and needs more energy and protein than a healthy child of the same age. This phase also includes stimulation, play, and emotional care, because recovery from severe malnutrition is neurological and developmental as much as it is physical.

Ready-to-use therapeutic food has changed the logistics of treating malnutrition more than any other product of the past generation. It is an energy-dense paste, usually based on peanut, fortified with vitamins and minerals, that needs no water, no cooking, and no refrigeration. It comes in sealed sachets with a long shelf life, so it can be stored in a health post or carried home in a bag, and it can be eaten directly by the child from the packet. Because it tolerates heat and poor roads, it allows children without medical complications to be treated at home through community-based programs rather than occupying a hospital bed for weeks. The nurse's role in this system is to decide who qualifies, to teach the caregiver exactly how much to give and when, and to schedule the return visit that catches failure early.

The child who cannot be treated at home must be admitted, and the reasons are specific. Admitted care is needed when the child fails the appetite test, when there is severe edema, when there is an obvious infection or danger sign, when the child is under 6 months of age, or when the caregiver cannot manage the feeding or cannot return for follow-up. Discharge criteria usually combine a good appetite, resolving edema, a rising weight, and a caregiver who has been taught what to do. After discharge the child returns regularly for weighing, and any loss of weight triggers reassessment and possible readmission. Relapse is common in the months after discharge, especially where food insecurity, tuberculosis, or HIV is present in the household.

Vitamin A deficiency remains one of the great preventable causes of blindness in the world. Vitamin A is needed to make rhodopsin in the retina and to maintain epithelial surfaces, and its absence first causes night blindness, then dry conjunctiva, then the foamy patches known as Bitot spots, and finally corneal ulceration and softening that can dissolve the eye within days. The same deficiency weakens the immune response, so children with vitamin A deficiency are more likely to die of measles, diarrhea, and respiratory infection. Measles itself depletes vitamin A stores, which is why the two problems travel together and why supplementation after measles is not optional. The World Health Organization recommends a dose of 200,000 international units by mouth for children 12 months of age and older, 100,000 international units for infants 6 to 11 months of age, and 50,000 international units for infants under 6 months, repeated the next day and again at two to four weeks in the treatment of clinical deficiency.

Preventive supplementation is delivered in campaigns, often alongside immunization and deworming, and it must be repeated every four to six months to maintain protection in places where dietary sources are scarce. Toxicity is real but uncommon: a single large dose can cause vomiting, headache, and a bulging fontanelle in an infant, and repeated very high doses over months can cause liver injury and bone changes. The nurse should therefore never repeat a campaign dose early because a caregiver asks for it, and should record each dose in the child's card. Pregnant women need a much lower dose, because high doses of vitamin A early in pregnancy can harm the fetus, and this is one of the places where national guidance differs sharply from country to country. Food-based approaches, including eggs, liver, orange-fleshed sweet potato, dark green leaves, and fortified oils, are the sustainable complement to campaigns.

Iron deficiency anemia is the commonest nutritional deficiency in the tropics, and hookworm infection is one of its commonest causes. Hookworms attach to the small intestine and feed on blood, and each worm removes a small but continuous volume every day, so a heavy burden over months can empty the body's iron stores. The anemia is compounded by malaria, by schistosomiasis, by other intestinal worms, and by diets that are rich in fiber and phytate and poor in absorbable iron. The World Health Organization recommends treating anemia with 2 to 3 milligrams of elemental iron per kilogram per day for children, given in divided doses by mouth, and with 60 milligrams of elemental iron plus 400 micrograms of folic acid daily for pregnant women, usually with deworming after the first trimester in areas where hookworm is common. Iron therapy should be continued for several months after the hemoglobin returns to normal, because stores refill slowly.

The timing of iron where malaria exists is a genuine clinical problem and not a superstition. Iron is needed by the malaria parasite as much as by the child, and the body responds to infection by raising hepcidin, which locks iron inside cells and lowers its level in the blood. Giving iron to a child during an acute malaria episode, or to a child with untreated infection, has been associated with worse outcomes in some trials, and a large trial in a malaria-endemic area found increased serious adverse events among children who received iron without adequate malaria treatment and prevention. The practical rule that follows is to screen and treat malaria and other infections first, to avoid iron during the acute illness, and to ensure that malaria prevention is in place before supplements are given to children in endemic areas. National policies differ on this point, and the current national guideline must be checked before iron is started in any child in a malaria zone.

Iodine deficiency is quieter than the other deficiencies and its consequences last a lifetime. Iodine is required to make thyroid hormone, and when it is scarce the thyroid enlarges into a goiter, the person becomes hypothyroid, and in pregnancy the developing brain is damaged. The severe end of the spectrum includes miscarriage, stillbirth, cretinism with deafness and intellectual disability, and a measurable loss of cognitive capacity across whole populations. The World Health Organization and the United Nations Children's Fund promote universal salt iodization, which is the cheapest and most durable public health measure in this chapter and requires no individual behavior change. Monitoring uses urinary iodine concentration in school-age children and thyroid stimulating hormone screening in newborns, and the nurse may be the person who collects those samples and explains the result. Iodine excess is also possible, particularly where salt is over-iodized or where iodized salt and supplements are combined, so the program must be monitored rather than simply expanded.

Zinc is not a cure for diarrhea, but it shortens it, and that is enough to matter. Zinc is involved in the repair of the intestinal lining and in immune function, and deficiency is common in the same children who have repeated diarrheal illness. The World Health Organization and the United Nations Children's Fund recommend 20 milligrams of zinc per day by mouth for 10 to 14 days in children 6 months of age and older, and 10 milligrams per day for 10 to 14 days in infants under 6 months, given alongside oral rehydration salts and continued feeding. The effect is a shorter episode, less stool volume, and fewer episodes in the following months. Zinc can cause nausea and vomiting if given on an empty stomach, so it is given with food where possible, and it should not be used as a substitute for assessment of the child's hydration and for the treatment of dysentery or cholera.

Thiamine deficiency still causes beriberi in the twenty-first century, and it appears wherever polished rice or white flour dominates the diet and fresh foods are scarce. Wet beriberi is high-output heart failure with edema, a fast pulse, and breathlessness, and it can kill quickly if it is mistaken for cardiac disease and treated only with diuretics. Dry beriberi is a peripheral neuropathy with numbness, weakness, and loss of reflexes that begins in the feet and rises. Wernicke encephalopathy adds confusion, eye movement abnormalities, and unsteadiness, and it becomes Korsakoff syndrome with permanent memory loss if thiamine is not given promptly. Severe cases are treated with thiamine given intravenously or intramuscularly, and mild cases with thiamine by mouth, with the exact dose and schedule taken from current national guidance. Thiamine is given before glucose in any malnourished or confused patient, because a glucose load in a thiamine-depleted person can precipitate the neurological syndrome.

Pellagra is the deficiency of niacin and it still appears among people who eat maize as their staple and little else. Its features are remembered as the four Ds, which are dermatitis, diarrhea, dementia, and death, and the dermatitis is the one that points to the diagnosis. The rash is photosensitive, appearing on skin exposed to sun such as the face, the neck in a band known as Casal's necklace, the forearms, and the feet, and it looks like a burn that later thickens and darkens. Niacin can also be lost in the urine in carcinoid syndrome and can fail to be absorbed in Hartnup disease, so the same picture can appear without a diet of maize. Treatment uses nicotinamide, which avoids the flushing that niacin causes, usually in divided doses by mouth for several weeks together with a balanced diet and other vitamins.

Scurvy appears wherever fresh fruit and vegetables disappear, and that happens in refugee camps, conflict zones, famine, long sea voyages, prisons, and in children whose diets are restricted by illness or by poverty. Vitamin C is required to build collagen, so its absence causes blood vessels to leak, gums to bleed, hair follicles to bleed under the skin, and wounds to heal badly. The classic signs are corkscrew hairs, perifollicular hemorrhage on the legs, swollen and bleeding gums, and easy bruising, and in infants the condition causes painful swelling of the limbs with pseudoparalysis. Treatment is ascorbic acid by mouth for at least one month, with the dose set by current guidance, and the response is usually dramatic within days. Scurvy is a reminder that a deficiency can appear wherever the diet loses its variety, and that the diagnosis is often made by a nurse who asks what the person has actually eaten.

Alongside this burden of deficiency, the same communities are now carrying a double burden of obesity and diabetes. The nutrition transition brings cheap energy-dense food, sugary drinks, refined grains, and less physical work, and it arrives in places that still have stunted children and infectious disease. It is common to find a stunted child, an anemic adolescent, and an obese grandmother in one household, each needing a different conversation. Diabetes in a tropical setting is dangerous in a particular way, because high blood sugar worsens tuberculosis, predisposes to skin and soft tissue infection, and makes any infection harder to control. Gestational diabetes produces large babies, birth injury, and a next generation at higher risk of obesity and diabetes themselves. The nurse should therefore screen for both ends of the spectrum in the same visit and avoid the assumption that a thin patient is the only patient with a nutrition problem.

The interaction between infection and nutrition deserves its own place in the nurse's thinking. Tuberculosis causes wasting through reduced appetite, fever, and the catabolic effect of inflammation, and weight loss before treatment predicts a worse outcome. HIV does the same through opportunistic infection, poor intake, diarrhea, and malabsorption, and antiretroviral therapy reverses much of the loss by restoring appetite and health. Intestinal worms take blood directly and nutrients indirectly, and they reduce growth and cognitive development in children who carry them for years. Measles, cholera, and other acute infections drain reserves within days, and each illness makes the next one more likely. The practical consequence is that every nutrition assessment in the tropics is also an assessment for infection, and every serious infection should prompt a plan for feeding during recovery.

Nutritional support in tuberculosis is mostly ordinary food, given in adequate amounts, and the evidence for high-dose single micronutrients is disappointing. Trials of high-dose vitamin A, zinc, and other supplements have generally failed to improve treatment outcomes, and some have suggested harm, so the safe plan is a balanced diet with sufficient energy and protein plus correction of specific deficiencies that are actually documented. Weight gain during treatment is a useful marker of response, and a patient who is losing weight after several weeks of therapy may have drug resistance, poor adherence, another infection, or simply not enough food. Food assistance improves adherence and completion in programs where patients are hungry, and it is a clinical intervention when the alternative is defaulting from treatment. The nurse should ask about food at every visit, because a full blister pack does nothing for a patient with nothing in the pot.

HIV care and nutrition are inseparable, and the nurse is usually the person who holds them together. Food insecurity drives risky behavior, reduces adherence to antiretroviral therapy, and worsens outcomes at every stage, while treatment itself restores appetite and can lead to weight gain that is initially welcome and later becomes obesity. The metabolic complications of long-term treatment, including fat redistribution and disordered blood sugar and lipids, need dietary counseling rather than a change in the regimen. Infants born to mothers with HIV require a clear feeding plan built around the mother's treatment and viral suppression, and staff must give consistent advice rather than mixing messages about breastfeeding. Every clinic that provides treatment should also screen for food insecurity, because a patient who cannot eat cannot take medicine reliably.

Deworming is the simplest nutritional intervention in tropical practice and one of the most cost-effective. Hookworm causes iron deficiency anemia, Ascaris and Trichuris compete for nutrients and worsen growth, and schistosomiasis causes anemia and chronic inflammation. Periodic treatment of school-age children and at-risk adults reduces worm burden, improves hemoglobin, and modestly improves growth and school performance, and it is usually delivered through schools, child health days, and community programs. Because reinfection follows, treatment is repeated at intervals set by national programs, and the nurse's role is often to explain why the same child is treated again and again. Sanitation, safe water, footwear, and hand washing prevent reinfection, and without them the medicine is a temporary fix that must be repeated forever.

Growth monitoring is the nurse's signature contribution to nutrition, and it only works when the technique is trustworthy. Every child should be weighed at every opportunity, the weight plotted on a chart that shows the trend, and any flattening of the curve treated as a signal to act before the child becomes wasted. The same tape, the same scale, and the same technique must be used each time, and staff should be standardized against each other so that two nurses reading one child agree. Growth faltering in the first two years of life is the point at which stunting becomes irreversible, so the early months carry the greatest weight in the whole program. The nurse also counsels, supporting early and exclusive breastfeeding, timely and diverse complementary feeding, continued feeding during illness, and careful hand hygiene before food preparation.

Counseling must be concrete and affordable, because advice that costs more than the family earns will not be followed. The nurse should learn what food is actually available in the local market and season, and build advice around it rather than around imported products. A discussion about dietary diversity can be as simple as adding a green leaf, an egg, or a spoonful of oil to the usual staple, and a message about frequency can be as simple as feeding a young child five times a day. Kitchen gardens, small animal rearing, and food preservation are legitimate partners of clinical care, and clinics that link to them see nutrition improve. Where food insecurity is the real diagnosis, the honest response is to connect the family to food assistance and cash support and to document that the problem is poverty, not ignorance.

Monitoring and logistics decide whether any of this reaches the patient. Supplies such as therapeutic milk, ready-to-use therapeutic food, vitamin A, zinc, iron and folic acid tablets, and deworming medicine must be forecast, stored, and protected from heat and moisture, and a stockout in the middle of a treatment course is a clinical failure rather than an administrative one. Registers should record admission, weight, mid-upper arm circumference, edema, treatment given, and outcome, and monthly reports should track cure, default, death, and relapse. The nurse should expect supervision and quality checks on measurement, because a program with a perfect cure rate and impossible measurements is a program with a broken scale. Referral links to hospital care must be tested before they are needed, with a plan for transport that a poor family can actually use.

Safety surrounds every one of these interventions, and the nurse should know the adverse effects as well as the benefits. Vitamin A in repeated high doses can cause headache, vomiting, and liver injury, and high doses in early pregnancy can harm the fetus. Zinc commonly causes nausea and may cause vomiting if given without food. Iron given during acute infection or untreated malaria has been associated with harm, and iron overload occurs in people who receive repeated transfusions or unnecessary supplements. Iodine excess can cause thyroid dysfunction, and selenium deficiency can worsen the effects of iodine in some settings. Refeeding syndrome is the most dangerous of all, and the diuretic treatment of nutritional edema is perhaps the most avoidable error in this chapter.

Regimens in nutrition change as often as regimens in infection, and guidance differs between countries. The World Health Organization sets global norms for the management of severe acute malnutrition, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention publishes guidance for micronutrient programs and for nutrition in refugee settings, and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control addresses nutrition in displaced populations arriving in Europe. The doses and schedules quoted in this chapter are drawn from that guidance as it stood when this course was written, and they are quoted so that the listener understands the scale and the logic, not so that they can be copied from memory. Before prescribing or dispensing any product, the nurse must check the current national guideline, the product label, and the supply actually in the clinic, and must confirm the dose with a second qualified colleague when the patient is a child or a pregnant woman. When the local guideline and this recording disagree, the local guideline and the current product label win.

Nutrition work is also where the ethics of tropical medicine are most visible, because the nurse often has food and the patient does not. Screening for food insecurity should be as routine as checking a temperature, and the result should change the plan rather than merely being recorded. Stigma attaches to both thinness and obesity, and a judgmental tone closes the conversation before it begins. Food assistance programs raise difficult questions about targeting, dependency, and sustainability, and no nurse resolves them alone, but silence about hunger is never neutral. The most useful thing a nurse can do is measure honestly, feed safely, teach clearly, and make sure the family is connected to whatever the system actually provides.

To summarize, nutrition and tropical medicine are one subject, joined by the loop in which infection causes malnutrition and malnutrition worsens infection. Severe acute malnutrition appears as marasmus with wasting or kwashiorkor with edema, it is diagnosed with weight-for-height, mid-upper arm circumference, and the presence of edema, and it is treated in phases that stabilize the child before feeding is advanced, because feeding too fast can kill. Specific deficiencies still matter, and vitamin A, iron, iodine, zinc, thiamine, niacin, and vitamin C each produce a recognizable illness with a recognizable treatment that must be checked against current guidance. The same communities now carry obesity and diabetes alongside undernutrition, so screening must look in both directions. The nurse's contribution is measurement that can be trusted, feeding that is safe, counseling that is affordable, and follow-up that does not lose the patient.
