# Chapter 7 — The Syndromic Approach to Tropical Fever

A syndrome is a cluster of findings that occur together often enough to be useful before a diagnosis is known, and in tropical medicine it is the only honest starting point when the laboratory is a day away. You take the presenting pattern, name the small group of organisms that produce it, and arrange that group by what will kill the patient first. Fever itself is a regulated elevation of the body's thermal set point, driven by cytokines released when the immune system recognizes a pathogen, and the shape of the curve is far less reliable than most teaching suggests. Sustained fever, intermittent fever and the stepwise ascent all occur with the same organism, so what matters more than the curve are the associated findings: the rash, the yellow eyes, the altered consciousness, the falling blood pressure, the falling urine output.

Four syndromes carry most of the weight in a febrile patient: fever with rash, fever with jaundice, fever with neurological signs, and fever with an abnormal blood count, of which eosinophilia most often changes the plan. Diarrhea, skin lesions, enlarged lymph nodes and an enlarged spleen are modifiers that narrow the list inside one of the four. A patient may present inside two syndromes at once, and the overlap is itself diagnostic information rather than a problem to be resolved first. The aim is not to name a disease from the doorway but to decide the next hour, the next day, and what finding would make you change your mind.

A rash in a febrile patient usually arises by one of three mechanisms, each with its own appearance and tempo. Direct viral injury to skin cells produces a widespread eruption that follows the viremia, while immune complexes deposited in small dermal vessels produce a maculopapular rash that may later become petechial. Injury to the vessel wall produces lesions that do not fade when pressed, and that bedside test separates the dangerous from the ordinary. The timing of the rash against the fever is your best discriminator, because a rash with or before the fever suggests dengue, chikungunya or measles, while a rash after several days suggests rickettsial disease, typhoid fever or meningococcal infection. Examine the whole skin in good light, including the scalp, palms, soles, mouth and perineum, because distribution is often the diagnosis.

Dengue is the first consideration in a returning traveler with fever and rash, and it is caused by four related viruses transmitted by day-biting mosquitoes of the genus Aedes. The illness begins abruptly with high fever, severe headache, pain behind the eyes, muscle and joint pain, and a rash that may flush early and become maculopapular later. The dangerous transition usually occurs as the fever settles, between the third and the seventh day, when plasma leaks from the smallest vessels into the tissues. Warning signs include abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, bleeding from the gums or nose, lethargy, restlessness and fluid accumulation in the chest or abdomen. A rising hematocrit with a falling platelet count during this window signals that the patient has entered the critical phase.

Current guidance from the World Health Organization recommends crystalloid solutions, given by mouth when the patient can drink and intravenously when they cannot, with the rate adjusted to urine output, hematocrit and clinical signs rather than fixed in advance. Aspirin and the nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs are avoided because they increase bleeding risk. Acetaminophen is used for fever, commonly 10 to 15 milligrams per kilogram every six hours in a child and 500 to 1000 milligrams every six hours in an adult, never exceeding the daily maximum in the current guideline. Verify every dose against the patient's weight and the current national guideline before it is given, and record the urine output hourly during the critical phase.

Chikungunya is carried by the same mosquitoes and begins in a similar way, but its signature is joint pain so severe that patients often cannot walk or grip. The fever is usually shorter than in dengue, and the rash is more often itchy. Most people recover, yet a substantial minority continue to have joint pain for months, and the nurse's role in that phase is realistic counseling, pain relief and referral for rehabilitation rather than repeated courses of antibiotics. Neonates and older adults are at higher risk of severe disease, and infection around the time of birth can produce serious neonatal illness.

Measles remains a major cause of fever with rash in unvaccinated populations, and its pattern is one of the most reliable in medicine. After an incubation of roughly 10 to 14 days comes a prodrome of high fever, cough, runny nose and red eyes, often with small white spots on the inside of the cheek called Koplik spots. The rash then begins behind the ears and along the hairline and spreads downward over the face, trunk and limbs, fading in the same order. Pneumonia, diarrhea, ear infection and encephalitis are the complications that kill, and malnutrition magnifies every one of them. Current guidance from the World Health Organization supports vitamin A, given as 200,000 international units on two consecutive days for a child older than one year, with the schedule confirmed against the current guideline because the dose differs by age.

Rickettsial disease belongs on the list for any febrile patient with a rash and a history of insect or mite exposure, and its mechanism explains its danger. These organisms multiply in the cells lining small blood vessels, producing a vasculitis that leaks fluid, consumes platelets and can injure brain, lungs and kidneys. The rash may be maculopapular, may become petechial, and may involve the palms and soles, and an eschar at the bite site strongly suggests scrub typhus or a tick-borne spotted fever. Because serology returns too late to guide the first decision, current guidance from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention supports beginning doxycycline, commonly 100 milligrams by mouth twice a day in an adult, on suspicion rather than waiting for confirmation.

Typhoid fever, caused by the bacterium Salmonella Typhi, produces a stepwise fever that climbs over the first week, a relatively slow pulse for the height of the fever, and a faint rash of rose-colored spots on the trunk that is easily missed in dark skin or poor light. Constipation often precedes diarrhea, and the patient may look deceptively well before deteriorating suddenly. The intestinal lymphoid tissue can ulcerate and perforate, which is the complication that kills, and the diagnosis rests on blood culture taken before antibiotics. Resistance has removed fluoroquinolones from most of the world, and current guidance favors ceftriaxone or azithromycin, with the exact dose and duration confirmed against the national guideline before prescribing.

The organism invades the bloodstream, releases endotoxin and damages the vessel wall, so the rash begins as tiny petechiae and can progress to large purpuric areas with skin necrosis, a picture called purpura fulminans. The patient may have fever, headache, neck stiffness, vomiting and drowsiness, or may present with shock and a rash alone. Immediate treatment is ceftriaxone, commonly 1 gram intravenously every 12 hours in an adult and 100 milligrams per kilogram per day in a child, together with fluid resuscitation and urgent referral. Close contacts need prophylaxis, usually rifampicin or a single dose of ciprofloxacin according to local guidance, and the case must be notified to public health authorities.

Jaundice is yellowing of the skin and the whites of the eyes caused by bilirubin accumulation, and it helps to sort the mechanism before sorting the diseases. Prehepatic jaundice comes from excessive breakdown of red cells, hepatic jaundice from injured liver cells that cannot process or excrete bilirubin, and posthepatic jaundice from obstruction of the bile ducts. In obstruction the urine is dark, the stool is pale and the patient often itches, while in liver injury the liver may be tender and the enzymes are raised. Naming the mechanism tells you what to look for next, and in a limited setting a urine dipstick and a careful history can separate red cell destruction from obstruction before any laboratory result arrives. Where malaria is endemic, a rapid diagnostic test or a blood film takes priority, because treatable severe malaria is common and fast.

Hepatitis A and hepatitis E spread by the fecal-oral route, usually through contaminated water or food, and they produce an acute illness with fever, nausea, poor appetite, abdominal discomfort and then jaundice. Hepatitis A is usually self-limiting in children, who may have no symptoms at all, while adults tend to be sicker. Hepatitis E is particularly dangerous in pregnancy, where it can cause fulminant hepatic failure and high mortality in the third trimester. Treatment is supportive, with attention to hydration, nutrition and drugs that burden the liver, and the nurse also teaches household hygiene and traces a common water source when several cases appear together.

Leptospirosis is caused by spirochetes of the genus Leptospira, shed in the urine of rodents and other animals and entering the body through cuts, mucous membranes or waterlogged skin. The illness begins with fever, headache and muscle pain, but two signs matter: conjunctival suffusion, a reddening of the whites of the eyes without discharge, and severe calf tenderness. In its severe form, called Weil's disease, jaundice appears with kidney failure and bleeding, and the jaundice has a distinctive orange tint. Current guidance supports treatment with doxycycline or a penicillin, and the dose and route must be checked against the current guideline.

Severe malaria from Plasmodium falciparum is a leading cause of fever with jaundice in endemic areas, and it arises when a heavy parasite load destroys red cells, obstructs small vessels and consumes glucose. The patient may be jaundiced from red cell destruction and liver injury, and the urine may turn dark from hemoglobin, a picture historically called blackwater fever. Hypoglycemia is common, especially in children and in pregnancy, and it can mimic worsening cerebral malaria, so glucose belongs at the bedside within minutes of arrival. Current guidance from the World Health Organization treats severe malaria with intravenous artesunate, commonly 2.4 milligrams per kilogram at the start and again 12 hours and 24 hours later, then once a day, and pre-referral rectal artesunate is recommended for young children who cannot reach a treatment center quickly.

Yellow fever is a viral hemorrhagic illness carried by mosquitoes in parts of Africa and South America, and it produces a biphasic illness in which an initial febrile phase may be followed by a brief remission and then a severe phase with jaundice, bleeding, kidney failure and shock. The diagnosis is confirmed by laboratory testing, and there is no specific antiviral treatment, so care is supportive and rapid referral matters more than any drug. Ascending cholangitis, an infection of an obstructed biliary tree, can present in a similar way, with fever, jaundice and right upper abdominal pain, sometimes with confusion and low blood pressure. The obstruction is usually a stone or, in some regions, a parasite or a tumor pressing on the duct, and the laboratory shows a cholestatic pattern with a raised white cell count. Antibiotics are given promptly, but the definitive treatment is drainage of the obstruction, by endoscopy or surgery, and antibiotics alone will not cure an obstructed duct.

Fever with neurological signs most often requires immediate action, and the first steps are always the same: secure the airway, check the glucose, and look for a treatable cause before assuming the worst. Assess consciousness with a simple scale, examine the pupils, look for neck stiffness, test for focal weakness, and ask the family when the change began. A seizure is common in this group and must be stopped and its cause investigated. Hypoglycemia, low sodium, high fever and drug effects can all mimic encephalitis, so they are excluded at the bedside first. Only then does the differential move to cerebral malaria, meningitis, encephalitis and the slower infections that invade the nervous system.

Cerebral malaria is a diffuse brain dysfunction caused by Plasmodium falciparum, and it occurs when infected red cells stick to the lining of small cerebral vessels, obstructing flow and provoking inflammation. The patient, most often a child in an endemic area, becomes drowsy and then unrousable, sometimes after a seizure, and may have abnormal posturing. A normal cerebrospinal fluid with a positive malaria test and no other explanation defines it, and retinal changes on examination support the diagnosis. Treatment is intravenous artesunate as described earlier, with close attention to glucose, fluid balance, temperature and seizures. Mortality remains high, and survivors may carry neurological sequelae, so monitoring and rehabilitation planning begin on the first day.

Bacterial meningitis produces fever, headache, neck stiffness, vomiting and a change in consciousness, and early on it may look like any other febrile illness. The organism is linked to the patient's age, with different bacteria dominating in neonates, older children and adults. A lumbar puncture gives the answer, but it must not delay antibiotics when the patient is unstable or has signs of raised pressure, because treatment within the first hour changes the outcome. Current guidance recommends ceftriaxone, with or without vancomycin depending on local resistance, and dexamethasone in certain age groups, with every dose checked against the guideline and the patient's weight. The nurse watches for seizures, unequal pupils, falling consciousness and shock, and reports each change at once.

Japanese encephalitis is a mosquito-borne viral infection of the brain that occurs mainly in rural Asia, where it affects children and can leave severe neurological damage. It begins like a nonspecific febrile illness and may progress to headache, vomiting, drowsiness, seizures and coma. There is no specific antiviral treatment, so care is supportive, with attention to the airway, seizures, fluids and nutrition, and vaccination remains the main preventive measure in affected countries. Survivors may have movement disorders, weakness or cognitive change, so the nurse's long-term work is rehabilitation and support for the family.

Human African trypanosomiasis, also called sleeping sickness, is caused by the parasite Trypanosoma brucei and spread by the tsetse fly in parts of sub-Saharan Africa. A painless chancre may appear at the bite site, followed by fever, enlarged lymph nodes and a rash. Later the parasite enters the central nervous system, producing sleep disturbance, behavioral change, tremor and progressive neurological decline. The stage of disease is determined by examination of the cerebrospinal fluid, because the drugs that reach the brain differ from those that do not, and using the wrong one leaves the patient untreated. Current guidance includes oral fexinidazole and combinations such as eflornithine with nifurtimox, and the regimen must be confirmed with the current guideline and the national program.

Typhoid fever can produce an encephalopathy that looks like meningitis but is not, with drowsiness, confusion, tremors and sometimes rigidity, while the cerebrospinal fluid is usually unremarkable. The mechanism is thought to involve systemic inflammation and toxin effects rather than direct invasion of the brain, so it responds to treatment of the underlying typhoid rather than to a separate neurological regimen. In every neurological case the nursing work is observation repeated often enough to catch deterioration before it becomes irreversible. Record consciousness, pupils, breathing pattern, temperature and seizure activity at every round, and use the same scale each time so the trend is visible. Keep the airway clear, position the patient to protect it, and have oxygen and suction ready, and measure glucose whenever consciousness falls.

Eosinophilia is a rise in the number of eosinophils, the white cells that defend against parasites and also drive allergic inflammation, and it is usually defined as a count above 500 cells per microliter. The mechanism is a response to signals released by immune cells when they meet a parasite they cannot easily kill, so it marks chronic tissue invasion more than acute bloodstream infection. The count rises slowly and falls slowly, so a measurement taken on the first day may be normal even in a disease that will later produce a high count. Steroids, severe acute illness and some infections suppress the count as well. Repeat the count after a week or two when the clinical picture suggests a helminth, because a normal value never excludes a parasite.

The organisms that most often raise the eosinophil count in tropical practice are the soil-transmitted helminths, including Ascaris lumbricoides, the large roundworm, and the hookworms Necator americanus and Ancylostoma duodenale, which cause chronic blood loss as well as eosinophilia. Schistosomiasis raises the count while the parasite migrates through the body and may be normal later in established bladder or intestinal disease. Filarial infection, especially lymphatic filariasis from Wuchereria bancrofti and onchocerciasis from Onchocerca volvulus, raises the count particularly in its acute phases. Strongyloidiasis matters because its autoinfection cycle can persist for decades, and trichinellosis from undercooked meat produces marked eosinophilia with muscle pain and edema.

Strongyloides stercoralis deserves special attention because of its autoinfection cycle, in which larvae mature within the same host and reinvade the bowel or the skin, allowing infection to persist silently for decades after a person leaves an endemic area. When the immune system is suppressed, particularly by corticosteroids, the parasite multiplies without restraint, producing hyperinfection with severe diarrhea, abdominal pain, lung symptoms, sepsis and high mortality. This is why screening for strongyloidiasis before starting corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive treatment is a rule rather than a preference, even in a patient with no symptoms now. Current guidance treats it with ivermectin, commonly 200 micrograms per kilogram by mouth once a day for 2 days, with the guideline and the patient's weight checked before administration.

The evaluation of eosinophilia begins with a careful exposure and travel history, followed by stool examination using methods that will actually find the organism. A single stool sample is unreliable, so current guidance calls for repeated samples, and for the Baermann method or agar plate culture when strongyloidiasis is suspected. Serology helps for some parasites, and imaging helps when liver, lungs or bowel are involved, while empirical treatment without examination is sometimes justified in endemic settings according to national guidance. Explain to the patient why several samples are needed, because the delay feels like indifference unless it is explained.

Diarrhea in a febrile patient is classified by duration and character, and the classification points directly at the mechanism. Acute watery diarrhea lasts less than 14 days and is usually caused by a toxin or by an organism that does not invade the bowel wall, which is why there is no blood and why dehydration is the main threat. Dysentery means diarrhea with visible blood and indicates invasion and inflammation of the bowel lining, most often by Shigella and sometimes by Entamoeba histolytica, Campylobacter or a nontyphoidal Salmonella. Persistent diarrhea lasts 14 days or more and usually reflects malabsorption, repeated infection or an underlying condition such as HIV or tuberculosis, and it is a major cause of malnutrition in children.

The most important measurement in any diarrheal illness is the degree of dehydration, assessed from the patient's general condition, the eyes, the mouth, the skin, the pulse and the urine output rather than from a single number. Current guidance from the World Health Organization classifies dehydration as none, some or severe, and treatment follows directly: oral rehydration salts by mouth in small frequent sips for some dehydration, and rapid intravenous fluid with Ringer's lactate for severe dehydration until pulse and perfusion improve. The standard low-osmolarity oral rehydration salts mixture contains sodium chloride 2.6 grams, glucose 13.5 grams, potassium chloride 1.5 grams and trisodium citrate 2.9 grams dissolved in 1 liter of clean water, and the nurse should confirm the sachet instructions before mixing. Zinc, commonly 20 milligrams by mouth once a day for 10 to 14 days in a child, shortens the episode. Verify the dose against the current guideline and the child's weight, and record the urine output as the measure of success.

Bloody diarrhea changes management because the target is the invasive organism rather than the fluid loss alone, and current guidance supports antibiotics for shigellosis, usually ciprofloxacin or azithromycin depending on local resistance, and metronidazole for amoebic dysentery followed by a luminal agent to clear the cysts. Antimotility drugs are avoided in bloody diarrhea because slowing the bowel can worsen invasion and, with Shiga toxin, increase the risk of kidney injury. When diarrhea persists beyond 14 days, the approach shifts to nutrition, weight monitoring and the search for HIV, tuberculosis or another chronic infection. The nurse monitors stool frequency, abdominal pain, temperature and urine output.

An ulcer is a break in the skin with loss of the surface, and its edge, base, tenderness and discharge all carry information. A nodule is a solid raised lesion deeper than a centimeter, a plaque is a broader raised area, and a migratory swelling moves from place to place over days. The eschar is a black, crusted, painless lesion with a rim of redness that marks the site where an arthropod fed. Certain lesions narrow the diagnosis immediately: an eschar with fever suggests scrub typhus from Orientia tsutsugamushi or a tick-borne spotted fever and justifies empirical doxycycline, while a chronic painless ulcer with a raised edge on an exposed limb suggests cutaneous leishmaniasis and a painless swelling with sinuses discharging grains suggests mycetoma. A migratory swelling under the skin, sometimes with a worm visible in the eye, suggests loiasis from Loa loa, and a serpiginous itchy track suggests cutaneous larva migrans. A painless ulcer with a rolled edge and sensory loss suggests leprosy, and multiple ulcers in a child in the tropics should raise the question of yaws, so read these patterns against the exposure history and confirm them with an appropriate test when available.

Enlarged lymph nodes and an enlarged spleen are general clues that become specific when read with the syndrome. Tender matted nodes suggest infection with a spreading organism, while a single fluctuant node suggests a bubo, as in plague or lymphogranuloma venereum. Generalized lymphadenopathy with fever and weight loss requires consideration of HIV, tuberculosis and lymphoma, so the nurse examines the neck, axillae, groin and abdomen rather than only the symptomatic site. An enlarged spleen accompanies malaria, visceral leishmaniasis, typhoid fever, brucellosis and schistosomiasis, and it is found by careful palpation rather than by imaging when imaging is unavailable.

The chain from syndrome to organism to test to treatment is the spine of this chapter and should become a habit. The syndrome gives you a list, the exposure history reorders it, the examination looks for the discriminating sign, and the test confirms what you have begun to treat. Choose the test that changes your management, not the test that is easiest to send, and understand its window of positivity so a negative result is read correctly. The greatest danger here is anchoring on the first plausible diagnosis and bending every later finding to fit it, because a patient with a positive malaria film may also have meningitis. Protect yourself with a written differential, a scheduled re-examination and a deliberate question at every round: what have I not explained, and would a colleague see something I have missed?

The syndromic approach depends on logistics as much as on knowledge, and a brilliant diagnosis is worthless if the drug or the sample cannot move. Know what your clinic holds, including antibiotics, antimalarials, intravenous fluids, oral rehydration salts, testing supplies and personal protective equipment, and know the expiry dates and cold chain requirements. Plan how a specimen reaches the laboratory, how long it takes, and what you will do with the result when it arrives after the patient has gone home. Decide in advance where a patient with suspected meningitis or a viral hemorrhagic fever will be isolated, and who will be notified. Standing orders and clinical algorithms written for your setting are the instruments that make this safe when no physician is present.

Monitoring a febrile patient is a scheduled activity, not a reaction to a crisis. Record temperature, pulse, breathing rate, blood pressure, consciousness and urine output at intervals set by the severity of the illness, and use an early warning score if your setting has one. Look at the patient between measurements, because the nurse who notices the change in breathing or the new confusion is often the one who saves the life. Repeat the malaria film over 48 to 72 hours when the first is negative and suspicion remains, and repeat the hematocrit in dengue as the fever settles. Write down what you see when you see it, because memory is a poor record and the trend is the diagnosis.

Doxycycline causes nausea, irritation of the esophagus and sensitivity to sunlight, so it is given with water and the patient is warned about the sun. Ceftriaxone can cause rash, diarrhea and, rarely, a serious allergic reaction, so the first dose is given where resuscitation is possible. Artesunate can be followed by delayed destruction of red cells several days after treatment, so a falling hemoglobin after apparent recovery requires attention rather than reassurance. Document every suspected reaction and report it through the pharmacovigilance system. Your role is wider than the drug, because you take the exposure history, decide who is seen first, isolate the infectious, teach the family what to watch for at home, and explain in plain words why treatment continues after the fever settles.

Pregnancy, infancy, advanced age and immunosuppression each change the probabilities and the thresholds for action in every syndrome. Malaria in pregnancy is more severe and threatens the fetus, and the choice of treatment depends on the trimester, so the current guideline must be consulted rather than recalled. A neonate with fever may have a serious bacterial infection with almost no localizing signs, so the threshold for investigation and treatment is low, and an older adult may have a blunted fever response and still be gravely ill. The regimens named here are the ones current guidance supports today, and they will not remain unchanged, because recommendations are revised as evidence appears and national programs adapt them to local resistance patterns and to the drugs they can obtain. Before you prescribe, verify the dose, the interval and the duration against the current national guideline and the patient's weight, and have a second clinician check the arithmetic when a child is involved.

To summarize, the syndromic approach to tropical fever is a disciplined way of acting before certainty arrives. You recognize the pattern, whether rash, jaundice, neurological change, eosinophilia, diarrhea or a skin lesion, and you let the pattern name the small group of organisms that matter. You use the exposure history and the examination to reorder that group, you choose the test that will change what you do, and you treat when waiting would be more dangerous than the drug. You monitor on a schedule, you watch for adverse effects, and you keep supply, specimen transport and isolation working in the background. Above all, you hold your first diagnosis loosely, because the patient who does not fit it is telling you something, and the next observation may be the one that saves a life.
