# Chapter 52 — Drug and Supply Management

The pharmacy of a remote clinic is often a cupboard, a metal cabinet, or one locked room with a bench, and yet it is the place where most of the clinic's promises are kept or broken. A consultation can be careful, the diagnosis correct, and the advice sound, and all of it comes to nothing if the shelf is empty when the patient reaches it. The pharmacy is therefore not a storeroom that merely supports the clinical work; it is part of the clinical work, and the nurse who manages it is practicing medicine with every decision about what to hold, what to order, and what to give when the supply is short. This chapter treats drug and supply management as a clinical skill rather than an administrative chore, because that is what it becomes the moment the first patient depends on it.

When a clinic lies far from the district store and the road is long or impassable for part of the year, the pharmacy stops being a convenience and becomes the boundary of what care is possible. The nurse decides which conditions can be treated on site and which must be referred onward, and that decision is often made weeks earlier, at the moment of ordering, rather than at the bedside. A medicine that is not on the shelf on the day of the emergency cannot be prescribed no matter how thoroughly it is understood. The nurse who controls the order form therefore controls the scope of the clinic, and this is why the whole chain from the patient's need back to the manufacturer deserves attention.

The organizing idea behind most national supply systems is the concept of essential medicines, which the World Health Organization describes as those medicines that satisfy the priority health care needs of the population. The organization publishes a model list that is revised periodically, and each country adapts that list into a national essential medicines list shaped by its own disease pattern, its own prescribing rules, and its own budget. The concept is not a collection of cheap drugs for poor places; it is a discipline of choosing a small number of well-understood medicines that together cover the great majority of what people actually need. A clinic that stocks 200 items badly will treat fewer people well than a clinic that stocks 40 items reliably, and the second clinic is the safer one.

For a remote post the list should be short, and every item on it should justify its place. Antimalarials, a small range of antibiotics, an analgesic and an antipyretic, oral rehydration salts with zinc for childhood diarrhea, anthelmintics, oxytocin and magnesium sulfate for obstetric emergencies, epinephrine for anaphylaxis, a vaccine set, and the medicines for whatever chronic diseases the community carries are the usual core. Each addition brings a cost that is not only financial, because every extra item consumes shelf space, attention, and the time needed to track its expiry. The nurse should be able to name why each item is present and what would be lost if it were removed.

Where specific medicines are named in this chapter, the doses are offered as examples of current guidance and not as settled truth. The World Health Organization model list and the national protocol that adapts it are the authorities, and the current version of each, together with the product label in the box, must be checked before anything is prescribed or dispensed. To make that concrete, oral rehydration salts are dissolved in 1 liter of clean water and given by mouth in small sips, zinc is given at 20 milligrams a day for 10 to 14 days in a child older than 6 months, and paracetamol is given at 15 milligrams per kilogram every 6 hours by mouth to a maximum of 60 milligrams per kilogram a day. Oxytocin for postpartum hemorrhage is commonly given at 10 international units intramuscularly, and epinephrine for anaphylaxis at 0.5 milligrams intramuscularly into the outer thigh in an adult, with 0.01 milligrams per kilogram in a child. Every one of those numbers belongs to a guideline with a date on it, and the guideline in force where you work is the one that governs.

Ordering begins with the clinic's own data rather than with a feeling about what looks low. The most reliable starting point is average monthly consumption, calculated from the records of what was actually dispensed over the previous 6 to 12 months and adjusted for a change in the population or in the season. If the clinic treated 300 cases of malaria in the last rainy season and 60 in the dry season, the annual average hides a pattern that will empty the shelf in July if it is not planned for. Consumption data are imperfect, especially in the first year of a new post, and the nurse should say so plainly rather than pretend to a precision the records do not support.

Onto that average the nurse adds a buffer, which is the quantity held to absorb the ordinary surprises of a place where transport fails and demand shifts. The buffer must cover the lead time, which is the interval between placing an order and receiving it, and that interval is usually far longer than anyone new to the post expects. A district store two days away in the dry season may be unreachable for 6 weeks in the rains, and the order must be written with the worst month in mind rather than the best. The mistake of optimism is to order exactly what was used last month and assume the next delivery will arrive on time; the mistake of panic is to order a year's supply at once and watch half of it expire on the shelf. Both errors are common, and both are prevented by the same discipline of counting what is there and knowing how long the gap can be.

The consumption method can be sharpened with a second method, which is to estimate need from the number of cases the clinic expects to see and the standard treatment for each. That morbidity method is valuable when the clinic is new and has no reliable consumption history, and it is also a check on the first method when the two disagree. A large gap between what was used and what the case numbers suggest should be used usually means that stock was lost, that treatment was withheld, or that the records are incomplete, and each of those deserves an investigation. Neither method removes the need for judgment, and the nurse should document the reasoning behind the order so that the next person can follow it.

The stock card is the memory of the pharmacy, and it is only as useful as the habit of writing on it. For each item it records the date, the quantity received, the quantity issued, the balance remaining, the batch number, and the expiry date, and it is updated at the moment of the transaction rather than at the end of the week. A stock card filled in from memory on Friday evening is a work of reconstruction and will not agree with the shelf. The card answers the questions that matter most and that a busy clinic forgets to ask, which are how much is left, how quickly it is moving, and when it will run out at the present rate.

The stock card and the physical count must agree, and the only way to know that they do is to count the shelf. A monthly count, performed item by item and written down, is the single most useful routine in the whole system, because it catches the leakage, the miscount, and the quiet theft before they become a stock-out. A discrepancy is not a scolding; it is a finding, and it should be investigated with the same curiosity as an abnormal laboratory result. Counting should include opened containers, loose tablets, and anything stored outside the main cupboard, because those are precisely where the record and the reality drift apart.

Alongside the count, the nurse should keep a simple list of what will expire in the next 3 to 6 months, drawn from the batch entries on the stock cards. That list converts expiry from a vague worry into a plan, because it can be reviewed at every order and used to decide what must be used first. Batch numbers matter for another reason as well, since they allow a specific lot to be traced if a quality problem or an adverse reaction appears. A pharmacy that cannot say which batch a patient received has lost the ability to answer the question that a recall will ask.

Expiry is a slow loss that is easy to ignore because it happens quietly, one box at a time, in a cupboard no one is watching. The rule that prevents most of it is simple to state and requires discipline to keep, which is that the stock with the earliest expiry date is placed at the front and issued first. On a shelf where the newest delivery is stacked in front because it was easiest to put there, the oldest stock sits at the back and expires unseen. Rotating the stock each time a delivery arrives takes a few minutes and saves more medicine than any single economy the clinic can make.

When stock is close to expiry and cannot be used in time, the nurse has several honest options and should use them early rather than late. It can be transferred to a busier clinic that will consume it, returned to the district store if the system allows, or used in a targeted way within its remaining shelf life for the patients who need it. What it must not become is medicine that is quietly issued after the date on the label, because potency falls and the patient is treated with something less than a full dose while believing otherwise. A short-dated list circulated to neighboring posts is a small act of cooperation that prevents a great deal of waste.

Stock-outs happen for reasons that are mostly predictable, and naming them removes some of their power. The order may have been placed late, the quantity may have been too small, the supplier may have had nothing to send, the road may have closed, the drug may have been consumed faster than expected because of an outbreak, or the stock may have been diverted or lost. At a clinic that sees a sudden influx of patients with diarrhea, the consumption of oral rehydration salts can double in a week, and an order built on the previous month will be gone before the next one arrives. A stock-out is therefore a signal about the system, and the nurse who records why it happened is helping the next order to be better.

When the shelf is empty, the response must be clinical rather than merely apologetic. The first question is whether a therapeutic alternative exists that is genuinely equivalent for this patient, because substitution with a drug that is convenient rather than appropriate is not a solution. The second is how to ration fairly, which means deciding in advance who must be treated first and applying that rule consistently rather than by who asks loudest. The third is whether the patient can be referred to a place that has the medicine, and whether the journey is realistic for that person on that day. Rationing is uncomfortable, and it is far better done according to a written priority than improvised at each encounter.

Honesty with patients when the shelf is empty is a clinical intervention, not a courtesy. A patient who is told clearly that the medicine is not available, why it is not available, what is being done to obtain it, and what to do in the meantime is far safer than one who is given a substitute and left to believe it is the same. Trust built in those conversations is what brings people back when the stock-out ends and the vaccine or the antimalarial is on the shelf again. A vague answer or an invented explanation does more damage to a clinic than the shortage itself, because it teaches the community that the clinic cannot be believed.

The cold chain is the part of supply management where a failure of a few hours can destroy a product completely, and it deserves its own discipline. Vaccines and some other biological medicines lose their potency when they are too warm, and some lose it when they are frozen, so the requirement is not simply cold but a narrow band of temperature that must be held at every step. The World Health Organization guidance places most vaccines between 2 and 8 degrees Celsius from the manufacturer to the child, and the nurse is the last person in that chain before the dose is given. A refrigerator that is working but unmonitored is not a cold chain; it is a hope.

The vaccine refrigerator should be used for vaccines and for nothing else, because every extra item changes the temperature and competes for space. It should be loaded with space between the boxes so that air can move, with no vaccine stored in the door where the temperature swings, and with nothing placed on the floor of the unit where it may freeze. A freeze indicator or a vaccine vial monitor, where it is supplied, gives a visible answer about whether the product has been exposed to damaging heat, and the nurse should read it rather than assume. Freeze-sensitive vaccines such as those against diphtheria, tetanus and pertussis, and hepatitis B, must never be allowed to freeze, and a vial that has frozen is discarded rather than used.

The temperature log is the evidence that the cold chain held, and it is kept by hand twice a day, in the morning and in the afternoon, at the same times each day. The reading is written down with the date and the time, and any excursion beyond the permitted range is recorded with what was done about it, not hidden to keep the page tidy. A log with gaps is a log that cannot be trusted, and gaps appear exactly when the clinic is busiest, which is when the risk is highest. Some systems add a continuous temperature recording device, and where one exists its trace should be reviewed rather than merely filed.

Because the refrigerator depends on power, the cold chain depends on a plan for the day the power fails. The plan names who checks the unit, how long the vaccine will hold its temperature with the door closed, where the cold boxes and conditioned ice packs are kept, and who will move the stock if the outage is expected to be long. Ice packs must be conditioned, which means allowed to warm until they no longer crackle and then wrapped, because a pack straight from the freezer can freeze the vaccine it is meant to protect. A generator that is never tested is not part of the plan, and a cold box with no ice packs is only a box.

Where a freeze indicator is not available, the shake test can suggest whether an adsorbed vaccine has been frozen, since a frozen and thawed vial may form a sediment that does not disperse with shaking when it is compared with a vial known to be unfrozen. The test is an aid to judgment rather than a laboratory result, and the safe rule when in doubt is to discard the vial and record the loss. Discarding a doubtful vial is expensive, and it is also the correct decision, because a vaccine that has been damaged gives no protection while appearing to have been given. The nurse who documents the reason for the loss protects the clinic from the suspicion that follows unexplained disappearance.

A small number of medicines in any clinic are controlled because they can be diverted for nonmedical use, and these need a level of record keeping beyond the ordinary stock card. Opioids for severe pain, some sedatives, and certain other agents fall into this group according to national law, and the exact list is set by the country rather than by the clinic. The purpose of control is not to make pain relief difficult; it is to make the movement of a dangerous medicine visible from the moment it enters the clinic to the moment it is given or destroyed. In a remote post with one nurse and no pharmacist, that visibility depends almost entirely on the nurse's own discipline.

The controlled substances register records every receipt, every dose given, the patient's name, the prescriber, the balance remaining after each transaction, and the signature of the person who made the entry. Where two staff are present, a second signature at the periodic count is the strongest protection the clinic can have, and it protects the nurse as much as it protects the stock. The register is counted against the physical stock at least weekly and at every handover, and the count is witnessed and dated. Good records are what allow a nurse to answer an inspector's question calmly and to demonstrate that nothing is missing.

The concern about diversion must never be allowed to become a reason to undertreat pain, and the nurse has to hold both truths at once. A patient dying of cancer or recovering from a severe injury deserves adequate analgesia, and the fear of scrutiny should not leave that person suffering while the register stays clean. The answer is a system that is tight enough to make diversion difficult and simple enough to be used every day, with a register that takes a minute to complete rather than an hour. When a discrepancy appears, the response is a careful investigation rather than an accusation, because clerical error is far more common than theft.

Donations are one of the most common sources of both help and harm in a remote pharmacy. A well-intentioned shipment that arrives unrequested may contain medicines the clinic does not use, labels in a language the staff cannot read, quantities too small to treat anyone, or products already close to expiry, and all of it consumes time and shelf space. Some donations are worse than useless, because a substandard or unregistered product can harm a patient and discredit the clinic. The nurse who accepts everything out of gratitude will eventually be managing a room full of unusable stock while the essential medicines run out.

A clinic should have a written donation policy, and the nurse is often the person who must explain it to a well-meaning visitor. The policy says that donations are welcome when they match the national essential medicines list, arrive with at least a year of shelf life remaining, carry labels and instructions in a language the staff can read, and come in a quantity that is clinically useful. It also says that the clinic reserves the right to decline, and that medicines received will be entered into the same stock records as any other supply. Saying no to an inappropriate donation is not ingratitude; it is stewardship of a small space and a large responsibility.

Procurement is the process that connects the clinic's needs to the national supply system, and its defining feature is time. Orders travel up a chain that may include the health center, the district store, the regional store, and a national medical supply agency, and each step adds delay and each step can lose the request. The lead time is therefore not a single number but a range, and the nurse should learn the range for each item by watching what actually happens rather than by trusting the schedule on paper. Items that are ordered once a year, such as certain vaccines or program medicines, require planning on an annual horizon, and a request made late may simply be absent for 12 months.

The order itself should be written early, checked twice, and copied before it leaves the clinic. Quantity, strength, dosage form, and pack size all need to be explicit, because an order for a drug without its strength may return as the wrong one, and an order for a syrup may return as tablets. The nurse should record the date the order was sent, the date it was acknowledged, and the date the stock arrived, because that history is the only reliable guide to future lead times. When a delivery is short or wrong, the discrepancy should be reported in writing on the day it is discovered, while the evidence is still in hand.

Not every medicine that arrives is what its label claims, and the problem of substandard and falsified products is real in every supply chain, including the ones that reach remote clinics. Warning signs include packaging that looks different from the last batch, print that is blurred or misspelled, tablets that are crumbled or discolored, an unexpected taste or smell, and a price that is too good to be true. The nurse cannot test a product at the bench, but the nurse can compare it with a known good sample, check the batch number and the expiry date, and report any suspicion to the district level rather than use it. A medicine of uncertain quality should never be given to a patient while its status is being checked.

Storage conditions decide how long a medicine remains the medicine that was purchased. Most drugs want a dry, cool, dark place, away from direct sunlight and away from the heat that builds in a metal-roofed room in the afternoon, and a thermometer in the store is a useful instrument rather than a decoration. The room should be clean, free of pests, and secure, because a pharmacy with a broken lock is a pharmacy that will be emptied. Stock should be kept off the floor on shelves or pallets, away from the wall, and never in a place where water can reach it in the rainy season. Good storage is not a luxury of well-funded clinics; it is the cheapest form of inventory protection available.

Documentation in the pharmacy is clinical documentation, and it should be treated with the same seriousness as a patient record. The stock card, the requisition, the temperature log, the controlled substances register, the short-dated list, and the report of losses together tell the story of what the clinic had, what it gave, and what happened to the difference. That story is what allows a nurse to answer a supervisor, an auditor, or a district officer without anxiety, and it is also what protects the nurse when a shortage is blamed on mismanagement. Records kept only for the inspector are a burden; records kept because they make the work possible are a tool.

Every medicine the clinic gives can cause harm, and the nurse is usually the only person positioned to notice it. Adverse effects that matter in this setting include the rash that signals a drug allergy, the vomiting that means a dose was not absorbed, the jaundice or dark urine that points to liver injury, the ringing in the ears or the dizziness that marks toxicity, and the severe skin reaction that requires the drug to be stopped at once. The nurse should counsel the patient about the effects that are expected so that the dangerous ones are distinguished from the ordinary, and should record what was observed and what was done. Reporting a suspected adverse reaction to the national pharmacovigilance system is part of the nurse's responsibility, and it is what turns individual experience into knowledge that protects other patients.

Monitoring is the scheduled observation that catches a problem before it becomes an injury, and it is different from the passive noticing of adverse effects. A patient on a drug that can damage the kidney needs the urine output and, where available, the creatinine watched; a patient on a drug that can suppress the bone marrow needs the blood count; a patient on an injectable medicine needs the injection site examined. The intensity of monitoring should match the risk of the drug and the ability of the clinic to act on what it finds, because a test that no one can follow up is of limited value. Where the test cannot be done, the nurse relies on clinical observation and on a low threshold for referral.

In most remote posts there is no pharmacist, and the nurse is the pharmacist, the storekeeper, the dispenser, and the person who explains the medicine to the patient. That concentration of roles is efficient and it is also risky, because the same person who orders, receives, stores, dispenses, and counts has the opportunity to conceal an error or a loss. The protections are transparency and routine, which means records that anyone can read, counts that someone else witnesses, and a standing agreement that the district supervisor will visit and check. A nurse who invites scrutiny is a nurse who is trusted, and a nurse who resists it invites suspicion even when nothing is wrong.

The nurse is also a teacher, and the supply system only works when others understand their part in it. Community health workers who carry medicines to villages, cleaners who move boxes, drivers who transport vaccine carriers, and the patients themselves all need clear instruction about what to keep cool, what to keep locked, and what to report. A short written standard for each task and a periodic refresher are more effective than a single long training, because memory fades and staff change. The nurse should check understanding by watching a task performed rather than by asking whether it was understood.

Drug and supply management cannot be separated from the quality of prescribing, because the pharmacy responds to the prescription. If clinicians treat viral illness with antibiotics, the clinic will run out of antibiotics and will also breed resistance that makes them useless when they are truly needed. If malaria is diagnosed without a test wherever a fever appears, the antimalarial stock will vanish and patients without malaria will be exposed to a drug they do not need, which is the subject of the chapter on antimicrobial stewardship. The nurse who manages the shelf sees the pattern of prescribing in the consumption data and is well placed to raise it, which is one more reason to keep the numbers honest.

At the end of the chain lies disposal, and it must be planned rather than improvised. Expired or damaged medicines should be separated from usable stock at once, labeled clearly, and stored in a locked place until they can be destroyed or returned through the proper channel, because a drug left loose in the clinic will eventually be used. The method of destruction is set by national rules and by environmental sense, and medicines must never be poured into a river, a latrine, or an open dump where they can enter the water or be recovered. Every destroyed item is recorded with its quantity, its batch, and the reason, so that the loss is visible and the process can be audited.

The nurse does not manage the pharmacy alone even when no one else is present, because the pharmacy sits inside a system that includes the district store, the national program, and the community it serves. Communication with that system is part of the job, and it means reporting consumption, forecasting needs, and raising a shortage before it becomes a crisis rather than after. A clinic that reports honestly is more likely to be resupplied than one that hides its problems, because the district cannot allocate what it does not know is needed. The relationships behind those reports, built over time and maintained in calm periods, are what make the system work when the pressure is greatest.

There is a moral dimension to the shelf that is easy to lose in the logistics. A clinic that has the right medicine on the day it is needed treats not only the infection but also the fear that comes with being far from help. A clinic that is out of stock teaches the community that the journey was wasted, and repeated disappointment turns into the decision not to come at all. The nurse who keeps the stock in order is therefore protecting something larger than inventory, which is the community's belief that the clinic can be relied upon. That belief is the clinic's most valuable supply, and like every other supply it must be maintained deliberately.

To summarize, drug and supply management is a clinical discipline in which the pharmacy is the place where the clinic's promises are kept or broken. Forecasting begins with the clinic's own consumption data and the average monthly use, to which a buffer and lead time stock are added, and the errors of optimism and panic are avoided by counting what is on the shelf. The stock card and the physical count must agree, batch numbers and expiry dates are recorded, and stock is rotated so that the earliest expiry is issued first, which keeps expiry from becoming a slow and silent loss. Stock-outs are answered by rationing according to a written priority, by genuine substitution, and by honest explanation to patients when nothing remains. The cold chain holds vaccines between 2 and 8 degrees Celsius and is protected by the vaccine refrigerator, the freeze indicator, a power failure plan, and a temperature log kept twice a day. Controlled substances require a register that satisfies the inspector and protects the patient's access to pain relief, and an unrequested donation that does not match the essential medicines list, carries a short shelf life, or arrives unreadable should be declined. Procurement is governed by lead times that shape every order, and the nurse as pharmacist carries the ordering, storage, dispensing, monitoring, documentation, adverse effect reporting, and teaching that hold the system together. Because regimens are revised, because guidance differs between countries, and because the current guideline must always be checked before prescribing or dispensing, the practitioner who verifies the protocol and the product label before acting is the one who practices safely.
