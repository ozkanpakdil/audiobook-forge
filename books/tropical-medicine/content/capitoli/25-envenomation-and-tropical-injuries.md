# Chapter 25 — Envenomation and Tropical Injuries

Envenomation and traumatic injury sit at the edge of tropical medicine, where the biology of venom meets the economics of poverty. A snakebite is rarely a random accident, because it is the predictable consequence of walking barefoot to a field, sleeping on a floor at ground level, storing grain beside the house, or reaching under a rock for fuel. The World Health Organization added snakebite envenoming to its list of neglected tropical diseases in 2017, and its own estimates place the global burden at roughly 4.5 million bites each year, with 1.8 to 2.7 million cases of envenoming and 81,000 to 138,000 deaths. Those figures are widely believed to undercount the true toll, because most bites happen where no one is counting. This chapter follows the guidance of the World Health Organization, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control, and it treats every regimen as current guidance rather than settled truth. Regimens are revised as evidence changes, guidance differs between countries, and the current guideline must be checked before anything is prescribed.

The burden falls hardest on South Asia, sub-Saharan Africa, Latin America, and parts of Southeast Asia and Papua New Guinea, where agriculture, herding and hunting place people in contact with venomous animals every day. Farmers, plantation workers, fishermen, children and women collecting water or firewood carry most of the risk. Children are overrepresented among the severely envenomed, partly because a smaller body receives a larger dose of venom relative to its weight, and partly because a child cannot describe what happened. Distance is a second wound, since many victims live hours from a hospital that stocks antivenom, and the journey is made at night, on foot or on the back of a motorcycle, along roads that flood in the rainy season. The cost of transport, of the antivenom itself and of lost working days can push a family into debt. Envenomation is therefore as much a social disease as a toxicological one.

Two great families of venomous snakes account for nearly all serious bites, and the distinction matters because their venoms injure the body in different ways. The Viperidae, the vipers, pit vipers and adders, have long hinged fangs that inject deeply, and their venoms are chiefly destructive to tissue and to the clotting system. The Elapidae, the cobras, kraits, mambas, coral snakes and taipans, have short fixed fangs and venoms that attack the nervous system. Knowing the snake guides expectations, but treatment is usually decided from the clinical syndrome rather than from the species, because most patients cannot identify the animal and most hospitals cannot test the venom. Bring the dead snake only if it can be carried safely, and never touch a snake that appears dead, because a severed head can still bite.

The viper venoms combine enzymes that digest tissue with proteins that switch on clotting. Metalloproteinases and phospholipases break down the walls of capillaries and the matrix between cells, so plasma and blood leak into the limb and the skin blisters and dies. Other components activate the clotting cascade directly, converting prothrombin to thrombin or triggering factor activation without the control that the body normally applies. The result is a paradoxical state in which clotting is switched on throughout the circulation, fibrin is laid down, the clotting factors and platelets are used up, and the patient then bleeds because nothing is left to clot with. A patient with a viper bite may thus arrive with a swollen, bleeding limb, blood oozing from the gums, and laboratory tubes that simply will not clot.

Different vipers carry different emphases. The lance-headed pit vipers of the genus Bothrops, the fer-de-lance and its relatives, cause severe local destruction across Latin America, often with coagulopathy and kidney injury. The saw-scaled vipers of the genus Echis, found from West Africa through the Middle East to India, produce one of the most potent consumptive coagulopathies known, with spontaneous bleeding and a high risk of bleeding into the brain. Russell's viper, Daboia russelii, adds kidney failure and damage to the pituitary gland to its coagulopathy, and the puff adder, Bitis arietans, is notorious for the depth of tissue loss it leaves. These patterns are generalizations rather than rules, because the same species can behave differently with age, season and the amount of venom injected.

Elapid venoms act at the junction between nerve and muscle. Postsynaptic neurotoxins, common in cobras, bind the acetylcholine receptor on the muscle side and block the signal that tells the muscle to contract. Presynaptic neurotoxins, common in kraits, taipans and coral snakes, damage the nerve terminal itself so that it can no longer release acetylcholine, and this injury is slower to reverse because the terminal must regrow. Either way the muscle receives no instruction, and the patient develops a descending, flaccid paralysis that begins with the eyelids and the muscles of the face. Cobra venoms often add local tissue destruction, so an elapid bite can produce both a dying limb and a failing airway. The krait deserves special warning, because its bite often leaves almost no local mark and the paralysis can appear many hours later, when the patient is already asleep.

The local syndrome begins with pain at the bite, followed by swelling that advances up the limb over hours. The skin becomes tense, shiny and discolored, blisters form, and in severe cases the underlying muscle and fat die, leaving a black eschar that later separates. Swelling inside a closed fascial compartment can raise the pressure enough to stop the blood supply to nerve and muscle, producing a compartment syndrome with pain on passive stretch, weakness, numbness, and a limb that is cold and pulseless. The venom itself, the pressure and the inflammation combine to cause this injury, and the resulting disability can be permanent even when the patient survives. Necrotic tissue is also a perfect culture medium, so secondary bacterial infection, including tetanus and gas-forming organisms, is a constant threat.

The coagulopathy syndrome is less visible and more lethal than the local injury. Bleeding may begin as oozing from the gums, the nose or the puncture sites of intravenous lines, and progress to blood in the urine, vomit or stool. The most feared consequence is bleeding into the brain, which can begin with headache, drowsiness or confusion and end in death within hours. Laboratory confirmation includes the platelet count, the prothrombin time, the activated partial thromboplastin time, fibrinogen and the products of fibrin breakdown, but in a district hospital these may take a day to return. The bedside clotting test fills that gap. A few milliliters of venous blood are placed in a clean, dry glass tube, left undisturbed for 20 minutes, and then the tube is gently tipped; if the blood has not clotted, the patient has a severe coagulopathy and needs antivenom without further delay. The test is repeated every six hours, or before each additional dose, because the result guides how much antivenom is given.

Neurotoxic envenoming announces itself with the eyelids. Ptosis, or drooping of the upper lid, is often the first sign, followed by double vision, difficulty in swallowing, pooling of saliva, and weakness of the neck and limbs. The patient may remain fully alert while becoming unable to breathe, which is a terrifying and dangerous combination. Bedside monitoring therefore includes the single breath count, in which the patient takes the deepest breath possible and counts aloud on one exhalation, and the measurement of vital capacity with a simple spirometer if one is available. A falling count, a weak cough, an inability to lift the head or a rising respiratory rate demands preparation for airway support before the patient stops breathing. Never leave a patient with a neurotoxic bite alone to sleep, because respiratory arrest is quiet and can happen within minutes.

Other systemic effects deserve their own attention. Cardiovascular collapse can follow massive venom injection, internal bleeding or an allergic reaction to the venom itself, and it can also be caused by the venom's direct effect on the heart and blood vessels. Acute kidney injury is common after viper bites, from poor perfusion, from the breakdown products of muscle and blood, and from direct injury to the kidney tissue. Some patients develop an immediate hypersensitivity reaction to the bite, with generalized itching, hives, wheezing and low blood pressure, even before any treatment is given. A bite to the head or neck, a bite into a vein, and a bite in a small child are all associated with faster and more severe poisoning. The nurse who records these observations in sequence, rather than as isolated numbers, is building the picture that tells the team how fast the venom is working.

What happens in the first hour shapes the outcome, and most of the useful actions are simple. Lay the patient flat, keep them calm and warm, and immobilize the bitten limb in the position found, using a splint or a sling as if the limb were fractured. Remove rings, watches, bracelets and tight clothing before the swelling makes removal impossible or dangerous. Do not let the patient walk if they can be carried, and do not let them eat or drink, because vomiting and the need for anesthesia are both possible. Bring the patient to a facility that stocks antivenom, and send word ahead by telephone or radio so that the drug and the team are ready on arrival. Record the time of the bite, the site, the symptoms and any first aid already applied, because that timeline guides treatment.

One technique requires judgment rather than habit. Pressure immobilization, in which a firm bandage is wrapped over the whole bitten limb and the limb is splinted, is recommended for bites by neurotoxic elapid snakes in some national guidelines, where it may slow the movement of venom to the bloodstream. The bandage must be snug but must never stop the pulse, and it must not be used when the venom destroys tissue, because a tight bandage over dying muscle worsens the injury. In most of Africa and Asia the evidence for the technique against viper venoms is weak, and the guidelines differ. This is a decision to make from the current national guidance and the identity of the snake, not from memory. The dose verification habit you apply to drugs applies equally to first aid.

The list of traditional first aid measures that cause harm is long, and each one has a mechanism you can explain to a frightened family. A tourniquet stops the blood supply, so the limb can die from lack of blood before the venom does its work, and when the tourniquet is finally released the venom that has pooled behind it enters the circulation in a single surge that can kill. Cutting the wound opens vessels and nerves, causes bleeding and infection, and removes almost no venom. Suction, whether by mouth or by a device, extracts a negligible amount and introduces bacteria from the mouth. Ice and freezing damage the tissue further and have never been shown to help. Electric shock, drinking alcohol, applying dung or herbs, and delaying transport in order to try them all waste the hours in which antivenom works best.

On arrival, assessment and treatment begin together, and no test should delay the first dose of antivenom in a patient who clearly needs it. Take a focused history of the time, the place, the snake if known and the first aid used, and ask about bleeding, difficulty in swallowing, drooping eyelids, weakness and pain. Examine the whole patient, measure the circumference of the bitten limb at a marked point every hour, and look inside the mouth and at the skin for signs of bleeding. Record the blood pressure, pulse, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, temperature, urine output and level of consciousness. Send blood for the clotting tests, the blood count, the electrolytes and the kidney and liver function, and start the bedside clotting test immediately. Draw the limb and the mark or photograph them, because the next clinician needs to see the change rather than hear a description of it.

Antivenom is the only treatment that neutralizes venom, and everything else in this chapter is support. It is made by immunizing horses or sheep with venom and harvesting the antibodies, which bind the venom proteins and mark them for removal. Antivenom can only reach venom that is still in the blood or the tissues; it cannot undo damage already done to a nerve terminal, a blood vessel or a kidney cell, which is why it must be given early. The indications are systemic envenoming, meaning a coagulopathy, neurotoxicity, cardiovascular instability or spontaneous bleeding, and progressive local swelling that crosses a joint or involves more than half the bitten limb. Severe local necrosis, a bite in a child, and a bite to the head or neck also justify treatment. Antivenom is not given for a dry bite with no signs of envenoming, and it is not a vaccine or a preventive.

The dose is measured in vials rather than milligrams, and it is not adjusted for body weight in the way most drugs are, because the same amount of venom must be neutralized whatever the size of the patient. The initial number of vials depends on the product, the species and the severity, and that figure belongs to the package insert and the national guideline rather than to memory. The contents of the vials are diluted in normal saline and infused intravenously, usually over about one hour, with the patient watched continuously for the first minutes. Antivenom is a foreign protein, so a history of previous antivenom, of asthma or of allergy raises the risk of a reaction and is recorded before the infusion starts. Antivenom must be kept cold, between 2 and 8 degrees Celsius, and never frozen. An expired or heat-damaged vial is a clinical emergency in itself, because it may be powerless when it is most needed.

Before any antivenom is given, the current guideline should be opened and the dose confirmed against it, and a second clinician should independently verify the product, the number of vials and the route. Regimens are revised as new products are licensed and new evidence appears, guidance differs between countries and even between regions of the same country, and the antivenom in your refrigerator may cover a different set of snakes from the one in the guideline you remember. The same caution applies to every other drug in this chapter. A dose that was correct at your last posting, or in the last outbreak, may be wrong here and now. Write the source and the date beside the order, and ask rather than guess. A wrong dose of antivenom can mean a dead patient, or a patient who received a scarce medicine to no purpose.

Anaphylaxis during an antivenom infusion is common enough that it must be anticipated on every occasion. Early signs include itching, flushing, hives, a feeling of heat, restlessness, cough, wheeze, swelling of the lips or tongue, vomiting, abdominal cramping and a falling blood pressure. The infusion is stopped at once, the airway is assessed, oxygen is given, and epinephrine is injected. The adult dose is 0.5 milligrams of a 1 in 1000 solution intramuscularly into the outer thigh, and the pediatric dose is 0.01 milligrams per kilogram of the same solution, also intramuscularly, up to a maximum of 0.5 milligrams, repeated every 5 to 15 minutes if the reaction continues. Intravenous fluids are given for low blood pressure, and an antihistamine and a corticosteroid may be added as adjuncts, but they never replace epinephrine. Verify these doses against the current guideline before the event, not during it.

Delayed reactions are easy to miss because the patient has often gone home. Between 5 and 14 days after the infusion, a patient may return with fever, hives, itching, swollen joints, muscle pain, enlarged lymph nodes and sometimes swelling of the face. This is serum sickness, an immune complex reaction to the foreign proteins in the antivenom, and it is uncomfortable rather than dangerous. It is treated with an antihistamine, an analgesic and, for more severe cases, a short course of a corticosteroid, with the dose and duration taken from the current guideline. The patient must be warned before discharge that this reaction can occur, and told where to return if it does. Document the antivenom product and the batch, so that a later reaction can be linked to the supply that caused it.

The nurse's role during antivenom treatment is the difference between a controlled procedure and a catastrophe. Two large intravenous lines are secured before the infusion begins, one for the antivenom and one for fluids and emergency drugs. Resuscitation equipment, oxygen, a suction device, a bag and mask, and a drawn-up dose of epinephrine are at the bedside and checked by a second person. Vital signs, oxygen saturation, the level of consciousness and the infusion site are recorded every few minutes for the first quarter of an hour and then at a fixed interval for the rest of the infusion. The patient is never left alone, and the reason is written in the notes so that the next shift honors it. Any change in breathing, voice, skin or blood pressure is reported immediately, and the infusion rate is adjusted rather than abandoned until the reaction is under control.

Repeat dosing is guided by the patient's response and by the bedside clotting test rather than by a fixed schedule. If bleeding continues or the clotting test remains unclotted six hours after the first dose, a further dose is given, and the guideline for the product states how much. Swelling that continues to advance, worsening neurotoxicity and persistent cardiovascular instability are also reasons to repeat, but a patient whose coagulopathy has corrected and whose bleeding has stopped does not need more antivenom simply because a vial remains. The monitoring record should carry the limb circumference, the bleeding sites, the clotting test result, the urine output and the neurological observations at each time point, so that the trend is visible. A nurse who documents that the limb grew by 3 centimeters in two hours has made a diagnosis, not just a note. The team makes the decision together, and the nurse is often the person who first sees the reason to make it.

Airway management in neurotoxic envenoming is a nursing responsibility that begins before the crisis. Position the patient upright or in the recovery position according to their breathing and their level of consciousness, keep the mouth clear, and have suction ready because pooled secretions can drown a patient who cannot swallow. Give oxygen for low saturation, and use a bag and mask to assist ventilation when the respiratory effort is failing. When the single breath count falls below about 10, or when the patient can no longer cough or speak clearly, prepare for intubation and mechanical ventilation, and call the person skilled in it rather than waiting for respiratory arrest. Some national guidelines use an anticholinesterase drug as a test in cobra bites, because postsynaptic blockade can sometimes be reversed, and the response is judged at the bedside. Assume that ventilation may be needed for days, and plan the shifts, the equipment and the staff accordingly.

Supportive care carries the patient through the hours that antivenom cannot cover. Pain is treated with acetaminophen or an opioid, while aspirin and the other non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs are avoided because they worsen bleeding and injure the stomach. Fluids are given by mouth when the patient can drink and intravenously when they cannot, with the volume judged against the blood pressure, the urine output and the signs of fluid overload, because the injured limb and the leaking capillaries make overload easy. The bitten limb is cleaned, dressed, elevated and splinted, and the wound is examined daily for infection. Antibiotics are given when there is established infection or extensive necrosis, not routinely as a substitute for wound care, and the choice follows the local resistance pattern and the current guideline. Blood products are reserved for active bleeding or for a procedure, because the coagulopathy of snakebite responds to antivenom rather than to transfused clotting factors.

Surgical decisions around a snakebitten limb must be made with restraint. A fasciotomy performed on a limb full of venom and leaking capillaries can convert a viable limb into an amputated one, because the surgeon opens the compartments into a wound that cannot be closed and the tissue dies anyway. Antivenom is given first, and the limb is reassessed after it has had time to work, because much of the apparent pressure is caused by venom-induced swelling that will settle. Debridement of dead tissue is usually delayed until the boundary between living and dead is clear, often a week or more after the bite. Amputation becomes necessary when a limb is already dead or infected beyond salvage, and it is a devastating outcome for a subsistence farmer or a child. Rehabilitation, including physiotherapy, wound care and attention to the patient's mood, begins early and continues after discharge.

The logistics of antivenom and referral decide who lives, and they are nursing concerns as much as medical ones. Many district hospitals have no antivenom at all, and a nurse who knows the stock, the cold chain and the referral pathway is the person who can save a patient by moving them early. Pre-referral antivenom, given before transport when it is available, prevents the venom from working during the journey, and it is often the highest-value intervention in the whole episode. The receiving facility is notified by telephone or radio with the patient's age, the time of the bite, the signs of envenoming and the antivenom already given, so that the dose is neither lost nor duplicated. Transport is arranged with a vehicle, fuel, a driver and an escort who can monitor and give oxygen and fluids on the way. Antivenom that sits in a refrigerator no one can reach, or that expires because no one tracks the stock, has saved no one.

Scorpion sting is a different emergency with a different venom, and in children it is one of the most dangerous events in tropical medicine. Scorpions live in dry, hot environments, under stones, in rubble, in firewood and inside shoes and bedding, and stings happen at night, often to a person walking barefoot or reaching into a dark corner. Most species cause intense local pain and little else, but a minority carry venom that attacks the nervous system and the autonomic nervous system. The dangerous genera include Leiurus in North Africa and the Middle East, Androctonus across the same region, Tityus in South America and the Caribbean, Centruroides in Mexico and the southern United States, and Parabuthus in southern Africa. The severity of envenoming increases as the patient gets smaller, which is why a stung child can deteriorate within an hour while an adult with the same sting only complains of pain.

The venom opens sodium channels and releases a flood of acetylcholine and catecholamines, producing what is often called an autonomic storm. Excess acetylcholine causes sweating, vomiting, diarrhea, excessive salivation, tearing, blurred vision, small pupils and a slow heart rate, while excess catecholamines cause agitation, muscle twitching, a fast heart rate, high blood pressure, and in boys a painful erection that families often report as the first strange sign. The heart muscle itself can be injured by the catecholamine surge, so the patient may develop heart failure and fluid in the lungs, with a rapid breathing rate, crackles in the chest, and pink frothy sputum. Children are also at risk of dangerously high blood pressure, convulsions and coma. The picture can change from minute to minute, which is why continuous observation rather than hourly rounds is the standard of care.

Treatment is directed at the storm. Prazosin, an alpha blocker, is the mainstay for severe autonomic symptoms, given by mouth at about 30 micrograms per kilogram per dose, repeated every six hours, with the dose increased step by step according to the response and the guidance in force. Scorpion antivenom, where it is available, reduces the circulating venom and shortens the illness, and it is given intravenously with the same vigilance for reactions that snake antivenom requires. Oxygen, careful fluid management and, in selected cases, a drug that supports the failing heart are used for fluid in the lungs and injury to the heart muscle. Atropine is generally avoided except for a dangerously slow heart rate, because it can worsen the autonomic imbalance. Every dose in this paragraph is product-specific and must be checked against the current national guideline and verified by a second clinician before it is given, because regimens differ between countries and are revised.

The nursing care of a stung child is mostly vigilance. Record the heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, temperature, level of consciousness and urine output at short and regular intervals, and note the character of the breathing, because the earliest sign of fluid in the lungs may be a cough or a reluctance to lie flat. Weigh the child and calculate every dose by weight, and double-check the arithmetic with a colleague, because the margin between a therapeutic and a dangerous dose in a small body is narrow. Keep the child calm and the room quiet, treat pain, and involve the parent, who is often the person who can best say what has changed. Arrange follow-up, because some children develop delayed heart failure or high blood pressure after apparent recovery. Document the species if it was caught, the time of the sting and the response to each drug, so that the next child in the same village benefits from what was learned.

Spider bites are far less dangerous than their reputation, but two genera produce recognizable syndromes. The widow spiders, Latrodectus, live worldwide in sheds, outhouses, woodpiles and under furniture, and their venom is neurotoxic. The bite itself is often felt as a sharp pinprick and may leave two small marks, and the pain then spreads from the site to the chest, the back and the abdomen. Within an hour or two the patient develops severe muscle cramps, abdominal rigidity that can mimic an acute abdomen, sweating, restlessness, agitation, a fast heart rate and high blood pressure. The syndrome is called latrodectism, it is extremely painful and frightening, and it is rarely fatal, even in children. Treatment is analgesia, with an opioid for severe pain, a benzodiazepine for muscle spasm and agitation, and, in the most severe cases, a specific antivenom where one is available. Antivenom decisions and doses come from the current guideline and are verified before use, and calcium gluconate, once used widely, has weak evidence behind it.

The recluse spiders, Loxosceles, are brown spiders that live in dry, quiet places, including behind pictures, in closets and in stored clothing and bedding. Their venom destroys tissue, and the bite is often not felt at the time. Over the next several hours a red, painful area appears, sometimes with a pale center and a ring of inflammation, and a blister forms that breaks down into an ulcer with a dark, necrotic base. Most lesions heal slowly over weeks or months, leaving a scar that can be disfiguring. A minority of patients, especially children, develop systemic loxoscelism, in which the venom destroys red blood cells, producing fever, jaundice, dark urine from hemoglobin, a falling blood count and acute kidney injury. The nurse watches for the color of the urine, the urine output, the blood count and the kidney function, because those signs mark the transition from a skin problem to a systemic emergency.

Management of a recluse bite is mostly wound care and observation. The limb is rested and elevated, the wound is kept clean with a simple dressing, and pain is treated with an analgesic, while early surgery is avoided because it enlarges the ulcer and delays healing. Antibiotics are given for proven infection, not as a routine, and tetanus prevention is given for the bite. Dapsone has been used to limit necrosis, but the evidence is uncertain and it can cause a dangerous destruction of red cells, so it is used only within a protocol and with monitoring. Antivenom exists in some countries, particularly in South America, and its use is governed by the national guideline and by the availability of the product. Systemic loxoscelism is managed with fluids, close monitoring of urine output and kidney function, and blood transfusion when the anemia becomes severe, and the patient may need renal support.

A few other spiders warrant mention. The Brazilian wandering spiders of the genus Phoneutria produce intense local pain, sweating, agitation, vomiting, high blood pressure and, in men, a painful erection, and a specific antivenom is used in Brazil for severe cases. The Australian funnel-web spiders of the genus Atrax, now treated with a specific antivenom, can cause a rapidly progressive and potentially fatal syndrome with sweating, agitation, muscle twitching, high blood pressure and fluid in the lungs, and their bites are a medical emergency that requires immediate antivenom. The tarantulas kept as pets can cause irritation from their hairs as well as a painful bite, and a bite from any spider that is followed by severe pain, systemic symptoms or a spreading wound should be assessed in a facility. In every case the care of the wound, the assessment for tetanus and the treatment of pain and infection follow the same principles as for any other penetrating injury.

Bees, wasps, hornets and ants of the family Hymenoptera cause more deaths than snakes in some countries, almost all of them through anaphylaxis. A single sting in a person who has been sensitized by previous stings can trigger a reaction within minutes: itching, hives, swelling of the face and throat, wheezing, vomiting, a sense of impending doom, and a rapid fall in blood pressure. The reaction can progress to cardiac arrest, and the speed is the danger. Epinephrine is the first and most important treatment, and it is given intramuscularly into the outer thigh at 0.5 milligrams of a 1 in 1000 solution for an adult and 0.01 milligrams per kilogram for a child, up to 0.5 milligrams, repeated every 5 to 15 minutes while the reaction continues. It is given before antihistamines and corticosteroids, which are adjuncts and not substitutes, because only epinephrine reverses the airway swelling and the circulatory collapse. Any patient who has had such a reaction needs a written plan, two auto-injectors, training in their use and referral for possible desensitization.

Mass attacks by swarms of bees, particularly the Africanized honeybee in the Americas, produce a different and often fatal illness from the sheer quantity of venom. The patient may have hundreds or thousands of stings, and the venom causes destruction of muscle and red blood cells, with fever, vomiting, dark urine, a falling blood count, high potassium and acute kidney injury. Death can follow from kidney failure, bleeding, liver injury or collapse, and the danger continues for days after the attack. Treatment is supportive: fluids to maintain the urine output, monitoring of the creatine kinase, the blood count, the electrolytes and the kidney function, and dialysis when it is needed. The stingers embedded in the skin are removed by scraping rather than squeezing, so that no more venom is injected. The nurse who follows the urine color and the urine volume hour by hour is watching the organ most likely to fail.

The sea holds its own envenoming, and the treatment is governed by the species and by the physics of the venom, not by the habits of land based first aid. Jellyfish stings are caused by capsules called nematocysts that fire on contact, and the rule of first aid is to prevent them from firing again. Rinse the area with seawater, never with fresh water, because a change in salt concentration triggers the discharge of more capsules. Remove any visible tentacles with a gloved hand or forceps, and, for the box jellyfish of the Indo-Pacific and Australia, pour vinegar over the area; the acetic acid does not stop the pain but it prevents further firing, and antivenom is given for severe stings. For most other jellyfish and for the Portuguese man-of-war, the treatment is immersion in hot water at about 45 degrees Celsius for 20 minutes, after the tentacles are removed, because heat destroys the venom proteins and relieves pain. Do not rub the skin, do not apply ice, do not use alcohol, and do not let the patient walk if a large sting has caused collapse.

Some marine envenomings require their own mention. The small Irukandji jellyfish of northern Australia causes a delayed and severe syndrome with back pain, muscle cramps, vomiting, sweating, agitation, high blood pressure and fluid in the lungs, and management is supportive with close observation. The stonefish lies on the seabed and its dorsal spines inject a venom that causes extreme pain and, occasionally, paralysis and cardiovascular collapse; the treatment is hot water immersion for pain and a specific antivenom for severe cases. Cone snails fire a venomous tooth that can paralyze the muscles of breathing, and there is no antivenom, so the treatment is airway support and ventilation, often for a prolonged period. Stingrays inflict a deep laceration with a barbed spine, so the wound is explored for the retained spine, cleaned, and treated with hot water for pain as well as surgical care. Every marine wound needs careful irrigation, removal of foreign material, assessment for deep infection and tetanus prevention.

Marine wounds deserve particular attention for infection, because the sea carries organisms that are aggressive and unfamiliar to the land hospital. Vibrio species, including Vibrio vulnificus, can cause rapidly spreading infection and sepsis in people with liver disease, diabetes or a weakened immune system, and the warning signs are severe pain out of proportion to the wound, swelling, discoloration and fever. The wound is cleaned and debrided, and the choice of antibiotic follows the current guideline, the local resistance pattern and the severity of the infection. Tetanus prevention accompanies care of every marine wound, because the same puncture that delivered the spine or the bite has also delivered soil or water organisms. The patient with a limb wound should be examined for loss of sensation, weakness and loss of the pulse, because the injury may have damaged a nerve or a vessel beneath a small surface mark.

Tetanus prevention is the unglamorous duty that accompanies every penetrating injury in this chapter, and it is the one intervention that is almost always within reach. Tetanus is caused by Clostridium tetani, a bacterium whose spores live in soil, dust, feces and rusty metal, and which produces a toxin that blocks the nerves that restrain muscle contraction. The wound most likely to cause tetanus is a deep puncture, a wound contaminated with dirt or manure, a wound with dead tissue, a burn, or a wound neglected for days, and a snakebite, a scorpion sting, a spider bite or a marine wound fits that description. A patient with an up to date immunization history, meaning a complete primary series and a booster within the last 10 years, needs a booster only if the wound is tetanus prone and more than 5 years have passed. A patient who is unvaccinated, incompletely vaccinated or of unknown status needs both a tetanus toxoid containing vaccine and tetanus immunoglobulin, given at different sites, together with thorough cleaning and debridement of the wound. The dose of the vaccine and of the immunoglobulin, and the choice between the two, are set out in the current national guideline and should be verified before they are given.

Tetanus itself is a disease of muscle spasm, and when it appears it is a medical emergency. The incubation period is usually 3 to 21 days, and a shorter interval means a worse prognosis. The first signs are stiffness of the jaw, called trismus, and difficulty in swallowing, followed by a fixed smile, stiffness of the neck and back, and then generalized spasms that may be triggered by light, sound or touch. The spasms can arch the body, close the airway and cause fractures, and the patient remains conscious throughout. Treatment is wound debridement, a course of metronidazole, human tetanus immunoglobulin, control of the spasms with a benzodiazepine or magnesium, and mechanical ventilation in a quiet, darkened room, with the doses taken from the current guideline and verified before use. The mortality of severe tetanus remains high, which is the argument for preventing it at the wound rather than treating it in the intensive care unit.

Wound care in tropical practice is a discipline of its own, and it determines whether a bite becomes a scar or a disability. A bite or sting wound is cleaned with clean water and a mild antiseptic, and devitalized tissue is removed so that infection has nowhere to grow. Dressings are kept simple, changed regularly, and chosen to keep the wound moist enough to heal and dry enough not to macerate the surrounding skin. The wound is measured or photographed at each dressing change, because a wound that stops shrinking after 2 weeks needs a new assessment, a biopsy or a referral, not another dressing. Antibiotics are used for established infection, for deep or contaminated wounds, and for patients with diabetes, immunosuppression or a prosthetic joint, and the choice follows the local guideline rather than habit. The nurse who keeps the wound clean, the tetanus record complete and the follow-up appointment in the patient's hand has prevented the complications that fill surgical wards.

Prevention is the part of this chapter that costs the least and saves the most. Most snakebites happen at night in the dark, so lighting, a torch and a companion reduce the risk, and most happen to the feet and ankles, so closed shoes or boots are protective where people can afford and tolerate them. Sleeping off the floor, using a bed net tucked under the mattress, shaking out bedding and clothing before use, and storing grain away from the house reduce the chance of a scorpion or a snake in the bed. Clearing rubbish, firewood and tall grass from around the dwelling removes the shelter that attracts rodents and the snakes that follow them, and rodent control in the store and the kitchen is snakebite prevention by another route. Education must displace the harmful first aid that families believe in, and that work is best done by people the community trusts, in the local language, before the bite happens. Teaching a village health worker to immobilize a limb, arrange transport and call ahead is one of the most effective public health acts available.

Envenomation also has to be visible to be managed, and that is a systems task. Bites and stings should be reported to the public health authority under the national surveillance rules, because the numbers determine where antivenom is stocked, which species are covered and which training is funded. Recording the species, the time to treatment, the antivenom product and batch, and the outcome builds the local evidence that a national program needs. The antivenom supply chain must be mapped from the manufacturer to the district store, with named people responsible for the cold chain, the stock count and the expiry dates, because an antivenom that is out of reach is indistinguishable from no antivenom at all. Nurses should be at the table when those decisions are made, because they know which facility runs out first and which road floods in June. The cost of treatment, the lost income and the permanent disability that follows a severe bite are arguments for public funding and for rehabilitation services, and they belong in the nursing advocacy that goes with this work.

The human cost continues long after the antivenom has been given. A farmer who loses a foot to necrosis loses the ability to work, and a child who survives a neurotoxic bite may still face weakness, difficulty in swallowing or disruption of schooling for months. Survivors need wound follow-up, physiotherapy, pain control, mental health support and a realistic conversation about what they can and cannot do, and they need it delivered by someone who will not disappear after the acute ward. Families carry the cost of transport, food and lost labor, and they often sell livestock or land to pay for treatment, which deepens the poverty that placed them in danger. Stigma can follow a disfiguring wound, and a young woman with a scarring bite on a visible limb may face marriage and employment consequences that no clinical note captures. The nurse who asks what the patient does for a living, and who writes the answer in the plan, is practicing tropical medicine at its most complete.

Across the whole episode, the nurse is the person who watches, measures, documents, teaches and advocates. In the field, that means correcting harmful first aid, immobilizing the limb and getting the patient moving. In the emergency area, it means two lines, a checked resuscitation trolley, a drawn-up dose of epinephrine, observations at a fixed interval, and a telephone call that prepares the receiving team. In the ward, it means the limb measurements, the bedside clotting test, the neurological checks, the fluid balance and the wound care that turn a protocol into a recovery, and the warning signs that turn a quiet shift into an escalation. After discharge, it means the tetanus record, the follow-up appointment, the warning about serum sickness and the honest conversation about rehabilitation. None of this is glamorous, and all of it is what separates a patient who walks out of the hospital from one who does not. The knowledge in this chapter matters only when someone is at the bedside to use it, and that someone is usually you.

To summarize, envenomation and tropical injury are diseases of poverty, distance and timing, and the nurse stands at the point where all three can be changed. A snakebite kills through tissue destruction, a consuming coagulopathy or a quiet paralysis, and antivenom is the only treatment that neutralizes venom, so it must be given early, at the right dose, by the right route, with the guideline open and a second clinician verifying. The bedside clotting test guides repeat dosing, and the airway, the circulation, the urine output and the wound must be watched continuously while the antivenom runs. Scorpion stings cause an autonomic storm that is most dangerous in small children, spider bites divide into the painful neurotoxic and the quietly necrotic, and the stings of bees and wasps kill through anaphylaxis that only epinephrine reverses. Marine wounds demand species-specific first aid, careful wound care and tetanus prevention, and every penetrating injury in this chapter earns a tetanus assessment. Keep the antivenom cold, the stock counted, the first aid honest and the follow-up real, and check the current guidance before every dose, because the figures are revised, they differ between countries, and the patient in front of you deserves the version that is correct today.
