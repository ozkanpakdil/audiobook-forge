# Chapter 31 — Vaccination for Tropical Settings

Vaccination is the intervention that prevents tropical disease before the encounter with the health system ever happens, and in settings where diagnostic capacity is thin and referral is difficult, that prevention carries more weight than it does anywhere else. A child who never develops measles never needs a hospital bed, and a traveler who never develops yellow fever never needs a bed in an intensive care unit. The advanced practice nurse working in tropical medicine therefore handles vaccines on three levels at once: the individual traveler who needs protection for a specific itinerary, the resident population that depends on routine immunization for its baseline safety, and the community whose unvaccinated members determine whether an outbreak can spread. This chapter explains how vaccines produce protection, walks through the vaccines that matter most in tropical and travel practice, and then turns to the unglamorous parts of the work that decide whether any of it succeeds, which are the cold chain, the monitoring, the contraindications, the adverse effects, and the daily nursing tasks of counseling, administration, and documentation.

How a vaccine works is worth understanding before the products themselves, because the mechanism explains nearly every practical rule that follows. The immune system learns by encounter, and a vaccine arranges a safe encounter with an antigen, the part of an organism that the immune system recognizes. When that antigen arrives with the right signals, the B lymphocytes produce antibodies that bind the organism and block it, while the T lymphocytes coordinate the killing of infected cells and support the antibody response. A small number of those cells remain afterward as memory cells, and they are the point of the whole exercise, because they turn a first infection that might take a week into a second encounter that is stopped within a day. Some vaccines need repeated doses because the first dose only primes the response and later doses deepen and lengthen it. Some need periodic boosters because antibody levels fall over years, and some, particularly the live attenuated products, produce such durable protection that a single course lasts a lifetime.

Vaccines come in families, and the family determines whether the product may be given to a pregnant woman or to an immunosuppressed patient. Live attenuated vaccines contain organisms that have been weakened so that they multiply enough to provoke immunity without causing disease, and they include the vaccines against yellow fever, measles, mumps, rubella, chickenpox, oral typhoid, and BCG. Because they multiply, they are the products that can cause serious infection when immunity is impaired, and they are the ones generally avoided in pregnancy. Inactivated vaccines contain killed organisms or purified pieces of them, and they include the vaccines against hepatitis A, hepatitis B, rabies, the inactivated Japanese encephalitis vaccine, influenza, and the injectable typhoid vaccine, none of which can replicate, so they are safe in principle for immunocompromised patients even though the response may be weaker. Toxoid vaccines such as those against tetanus and diphtheria use a chemically modified toxin that the immune system can recognize without suffering its effects. Conjugate and subunit vaccines attach a weak antigen to a carrier protein or use a single purified protein, which is how the pneumococcal, meningococcal, Haemophilus influenzae type b, and human papillomavirus vaccines work. The newer platforms, including messenger RNA, deliver genetic instructions for a single antigen rather than the antigen itself.

What makes tropical practice different is not the vaccines but the arithmetic of risk that surrounds them. The same product given to a child in a temperate city and to a child in a village where hepatitis A is universal has a different value, because the chance of encounter is different, and the same logic governs every recommendation in this chapter. The World Health Organization, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control all publish immunization guidance that reflects this arithmetic, and they do not always agree with one another, because each weighs local epidemiology, cost, and program capacity differently. Guidance also changes as evidence accumulates, and a schedule that was standard a decade ago may no longer be current. Every dose, interval, and age limit described here is therefore a summary of what current guidance says rather than a settled fact, and the current national guideline and the product insert must always be checked before a vaccine is prescribed or administered.

Hepatitis A is the vaccine most travelers genuinely need, because the virus spreads by the fecal-oral route, it is common wherever sanitation is unreliable, and an infected adult can become seriously ill. The illness produces fever, nausea, abdominal discomfort, and jaundice, and while young children often have a mild or silent infection, adults can be unwell for weeks and occasionally develop acute liver failure. The vaccine is an inactivated product given by injection into the muscle, and the standard schedule is 2 doses separated by 6 to 12 months, with the second dose never given sooner than 6 months after the first. Protection begins within about 2 to 4 weeks of the first dose and is expected to last for decades once the series is complete. A combined vaccine against hepatitis A and hepatitis B exists and follows a similar principle using 3 doses. Where a traveler must depart within days, a single dose still provides substantial short-term protection, and the second dose can be completed after return.

Hepatitis B deserves more attention than it usually receives in travel clinics, because it is transmitted through blood, sexual contact, and contaminated needles rather than through food and water, so its risk follows behavior and occupation more than itinerary. The virus causes acute hepatitis that may be silent, and it can persist as a chronic infection that leads to cirrhosis and liver cancer decades later. The vaccine is given as 3 doses, commonly at the first visit, 1 month later, and 6 months later, and an accelerated schedule at the first visit, 1 month, and 2 months followed by a final dose at 12 months is used when protection is needed quickly. In countries where the virus is common, the birth dose given within 24 hours of delivery is the single most valuable dose in the entire program, because it prevents transmission from mother to newborn. Health care workers, people living with HIV, household contacts of infected people, and anyone likely to need transfusions or injections abroad should be vaccinated. After a needlestick or sexual exposure, immunoglobulin and vaccine are both given, and the interval and dose must be confirmed against the current guideline.

Typhoid fever is caused by the bacterium Salmonella enterica serovar Typhi, it spreads through food and water contaminated by human waste, and it produces a prolonged fever with abdominal pain, constipation or diarrhea, and sometimes intestinal hemorrhage. Three vaccine options exist and they differ in age limits, duration, and whether they are live. The injectable polysaccharide vaccine is a single dose given into the muscle, licensed from about 2 years of age, with a booster every 2 to 3 years for continued protection. The oral live attenuated vaccine is given as 4 capsules on alternate days across one week, with a booster every 5 years, and because it is live it is avoided in pregnancy and in immunosuppression. The newer conjugate vaccine is a single dose that can be given from 6 months of age, and it is being introduced into routine childhood programs in countries where the disease is common. None of the three is fully protective, so vaccination never replaces safe food and water, and hand hygiene remains the core of prevention.

Cholera vaccine has a narrower role than many travelers assume, because the disease is rare in ordinary tourist itineraries and the vaccine is most valuable as a public health tool. The oral killed whole-cell vaccine is given as 2 doses between 1 and 6 weeks apart, and protection lasts roughly 2 years, after which a booster is needed. A live attenuated single-dose vaccine is available in some countries. In outbreak settings and in humanitarian emergencies, the killed vaccine is deployed in reactive campaigns that target entire affected communities rather than individual patients, and the World Health Organization maintains a global stockpile for that purpose. Aid workers, health staff, and people living in camps or areas with poor sanitation may reasonably be vaccinated before deployment. As with typhoid, the vaccine supplements clean water, safe sanitation, and prompt rehydration, and it does not replace them.

Rabies is the vaccine that is most clearly a matter of life and death, because once symptoms begin the disease is almost always fatal, and prevention is therefore everything. The virus travels from a bite or scratch along the nerves toward the brain, and the interval between exposure and illness is usually weeks to months, which is the window that treatment uses. Pre-exposure vaccination uses 3 doses given on days 0, 7, and 21 or 28 by injection into the muscle, and it does not remove the need for treatment after a bite, but it simplifies that treatment and buys time in places where vaccine is scarce. After an exposure, the wound must be washed immediately with soap and running water for at least 15 minutes, and then a vaccine course is given on days 0, 3, 7, and 14, with a fifth dose on day 28 for people who are immunosuppressed. Category 3 exposures, which include bites that break the skin and contamination of mucous membranes or broken skin, also require rabies immunoglobulin infiltrated into and around the wound. Intradermal regimens that place a small volume of vaccine at several sites are used in many countries to stretch limited supplies, and the exact regimen and immunoglobulin dose must be verified against the current guideline.

Japanese encephalitis is a mosquito-borne viral infection of rural Asia, where the virus circulates between pigs, wading birds, and the mosquitoes that breed in flooded rice fields. Most infections are silent, but roughly 1 in 250 produces illness, and among those who develop encephalitis the death rate is high and a large share of survivors are left with weakness, seizures, or cognitive change. Two vaccine types are in common use: an inactivated vaccine given as 2 doses 28 days apart with a booster after 1 to 2 years, and a live attenuated vaccine given as a single dose in some countries. Vaccination is recommended for travelers who will spend a month or more in rural endemic areas, particularly during the transmission season, and for anyone whose work takes them into rice-growing districts, pig farms, or long stays in endemic countries. A short visit confined to a major city rarely justifies it. The older mouse brain derived vaccine is still used in a few settings but has been replaced almost everywhere by safer products.

Tick-borne encephalitis is the other viral encephalitis that tropical and travel nurses should recognize, and its geography is European and central Asian rather than tropical, which is exactly why it is easy to overlook. The virus is spread by the bite of an infected tick and, less commonly, by drinking unpasteurized milk or eating unpasteurized dairy products from infected goats, sheep, or cows. Illness begins like a flu, and after a quiet interval a proportion of patients develop meningitis or encephalitis with headache, neck stiffness, confusion, and sometimes paralysis, and the neurological consequences can be permanent. Vaccination follows either a rapid schedule with doses on days 0, 7, and 21 or a conventional schedule with the second dose 1 to 3 months later and the third dose 5 to 12 months after the first, with boosters every 3 to 5 years. Travelers to forested areas of central and eastern Europe and to parts of Asia during the warmer months should consider it. Avoiding unpasteurized dairy during those trips is a simple protective measure that costs nothing.

Yellow fever is the vaccine with the most complicated rules, and the nurse must know both the medical and the regulatory dimensions. The disease is a viral hemorrhagic illness transmitted by mosquitoes in parts of Africa and South America, and it can cause fever, jaundice, bleeding, kidney failure, and death. The vaccine is a live attenuated product, a single dose confers lifelong protection, and since 2016 the World Health Organization considers one documented dose sufficient for the international certificate for the whole of a person's life. Under the International Health Regulations, certain countries require a certificate of vaccination against yellow fever for entry, and the certificate becomes valid 10 days after vaccination, which is why it can never be given at the airport gate. The vaccine is contraindicated in severe immunodeficiency, in symptomatic HIV infection, in thymus disease, and in infants under 9 months of age, and it is used in pregnancy only when the risk of disease clearly outweighs the risk of the vaccine. Rare but serious reactions include yellow fever vaccine associated viscerotropic disease and neurotropic disease, both of which require prompt recognition and reporting.

Meningococcal disease is a bacterial infection that produces meningitis and a rapidly progressive bloodstream infection with rash and shock, and the organism Neisseria meningitidis has several serogroups with different geographic patterns. The quadrivalent conjugate vaccine covers serogroups A, C, W, and Y and is the product used for most travel indications. Saudi Arabia requires pilgrims traveling for the Hajj and Umrah to show a certificate of vaccination with a quadrivalent vaccine given at least 10 days before arrival and within the previous 5 years, and this requirement is enforced at the points of entry. Across the African meningitis belt, mass campaigns with a conjugate vaccine against serogroup A have substantially reduced epidemic disease, and routine childhood vaccination is expanding there. Close contacts of a patient with meningococcal disease require preventive antibiotics, and the choice of drug follows the national guideline.

Influenza is included here because it is the vaccine that tropical programs most often neglect and most often need. Influenza circulates throughout the year in tropical countries rather than following the sharp winter season of temperate regions, so a single annual dose in the autumn does not map onto local epidemiology. The vaccine is inactivated and given by injection into the muscle, and it is recommended from 6 months of age for everyone, with the strongest recommendation for pregnant women, young children, older adults, people with chronic heart, lung, kidney, or metabolic disease, and people living with HIV. In tropical settings the World Health Organization advises vaccinating before the main local peak rather than assuming the temperate calendar applies. Health care workers should be vaccinated partly to protect themselves and largely to avoid carrying the virus into wards where vulnerable patients are clustered. The strain composition changes each year, which is why the dose is repeated annually.

The vaccines against coronavirus disease 2019 belong in this chapter for the same reason, because the pandemic reshaped travel medicine and because several products were designed with resource-limited settings in mind. The available platforms have included inactivated whole virus vaccines, protein subunit vaccines, viral vector vaccines, and messenger RNA vaccines, and they differ in storage requirements, in the number of doses, and in how quickly their protection wanes. The inactivated and protein subunit products generally tolerate ordinary cold chain conditions between 2 and 8 degrees Celsius, which made them usable in places where ultra-cold storage was impossible. Older adults, pregnant women, and immunocompromised patients remain the groups for whom booster doses matter most, and the virus continues to evolve, so the recommended composition is revised periodically. The same rule that governs every vaccine in this chapter applies here, which is that the current national guidance and the product insert must be consulted rather than a schedule remembered from an earlier year.

BCG is the oldest vaccine in this chapter, a live attenuated strain of Mycobacterium bovis given by injection into the skin, and its reputation exceeds what it actually does. It protects infants and young children against disseminated tuberculosis and tuberculous meningitis, which are the forms that kill quickly, and it does not reliably protect adults against pulmonary tuberculosis and does not prevent infection in the first place. The World Health Organization recommends a birth dose in countries with a high burden of disease, and in low-incidence countries the vaccine is reserved for infants at specific high risk. The scar that often follows is not proof of protection, and its absence is not proof of failure, because the immune response does not depend on the scar. BCG is contraindicated in infants with symptomatic HIV infection or other severe immunodeficiency, and the decision in a child with HIV exposure requires the current guideline. Because the chapter has emphasized mechanism, this is the clearest example of a vaccine whose benefit is real but strictly bounded.

The dengue vaccine is the one product whose use depends entirely on a laboratory result, and this makes it a test of how well a program can establish prior infection status. The first licensed dengue vaccine is a live attenuated product given as 3 doses across 12 months, and it is approved only for people with documented prior dengue virus infection. In a person who has never been infected, the vaccine acts like a silent first infection and increases the risk of severe dengue during a later natural infection, which is why serological testing before vaccination is mandatory rather than optional. The difficulty is that the available tests cannot always distinguish a past dengue infection from a past infection with a related virus, and test performance varies between laboratories, so the result must be interpreted with care. A second dengue vaccine has since been licensed in some countries with a different approach to pre-vaccination testing, and its indications continue to evolve. Because the product is live, it is avoided in pregnancy and in immunosuppression, and vector control remains the primary defense against the disease.

The malaria vaccines are the newest products in this chapter and the ones most easily misunderstood, so they must be described honestly rather than as a solution. The first, known as RTS,S and marketed as Mosquirix, targets a protein on the surface of the parasite Plasmodium falciparum, and the second, known as R21, uses the same target with a different immune-stimulating adjuvant. The RTS,S schedule gives 4 doses to young children beginning at about 5 months of age, with the first 3 doses given monthly and the fourth at about 18 months, and some programs add a fifth dose about a year later, while the R21 schedule uses 3 doses with a booster. Both reduce clinical malaria by roughly 30 to 40 per cent in the first year, that protection wanes over time, and the reduction in severe malaria and death becomes substantial when the vaccine is combined with other measures. The evidence base came from pilot implementation in Ghana, Kenya, and Malawi beginning in 2019, which followed children through routine health systems rather than through trial conditions. The World Health Organization now recommends these vaccines for children in areas of moderate to high transmission, and seasonal schedules are used where transmission is concentrated in a few months, but they do not replace insecticide-treated bed nets, indoor residual spraying, prompt diagnosis, and effective treatment.

The routine childhood schedule is the backbone of prevention in every tropical country, and the travel or tropical medicine nurse should be able to read it and explain it to a parent. A typical schedule includes BCG at birth, a birth dose of hepatitis B vaccine, oral and injectable polio vaccines, a combined vaccine against diphtheria, tetanus, and pertussis, Haemophilus influenzae type b vaccine, pneumococcal conjugate vaccine, rotavirus vaccine, and later measles and rubella vaccine followed in adolescence by human papillomavirus vaccine. What limits these programs is rarely the existence of the vaccines and almost always the coverage, because a child who receives the first dose and misses the third is not protected in the way the schedule intends. The nurse who checks a child's card during an unrelated visit, notices a missed dose, and offers it before the family leaves is doing more for that child than a specialist consultation would. The same principle applies to adults, whose immunity against tetanus, diphtheria, measles, and hepatitis B is often simply unknown.

The cold chain is the part of vaccination that fails quietly, and its failure is invisible at the moment of injection. Most vaccines must be kept between 2 and 8 degrees Celsius from the factory to the patient, and the chain is only as strong as its weakest link, which is usually the last one, the health post with an unreliable refrigerator and no one assigned to watch it. Several vaccines are damaged by freezing, including hepatitis B vaccine, the diphtheria and tetanus containing vaccines, and pneumococcal conjugate vaccine, and a vial that has been frozen may look perfectly normal while providing no protection at all. The messenger RNA vaccines against coronavirus disease 2019 require much colder storage, which is why their introduction depended on equipment that many tropical programs did not have. Temperature must be monitored at every level with a logger or a thermometer read twice each day, and a break in the chain requires immediate assessment rather than guesswork. The nurse who records those readings and reports excursions is protecting the entire program, not completing a formality.

The vaccine vial monitor is a small square printed on the label that darkens with cumulative heat exposure, and it is the simplest tool available for deciding whether a vial may still be used. It answers a question that no thermometer can answer after the fact, which is how much heat the vial has already absorbed across its journey. The rule is to compare the inner square with the outer circle: if the inner square is lighter than the outer circle, the vaccine may be used, and if the inner square is the same shade or darker, the vial must be discarded. The monitor does not detect freezing, so freeze-sensitive vaccines require the separate shake test, in which a suspect vial and a known unfrozen control vial are shaken side by side and compared for the granular sediment that indicates damage. Any vial that fails either check is discarded and the loss is documented, because unexplained wastage is itself a signal that the chain has a problem.

Multi-dose vials create a decision that has to be made correctly every single day. The World Health Organization multi-dose vial policy allows a vial that has been opened to be kept and reused for up to 28 days when four conditions are met: the vaccine is one of the products covered by the policy, it has been stored correctly between 2 and 8 degrees Celsius, it has not been contaminated, and the vaccine vial monitor has not reached its discard point. Vials that contain no preservative, such as reconstituted BCG vaccine and reconstituted measles vaccine, must be discarded 6 hours after reconstitution or at the end of the session, whichever comes first, because bacterial contamination can multiply in them. Discarding a partly used vial feels wasteful, and reusing one past its limit feels thrifty, but the second choice can cause a fatal infection, so the rule is not negotiable. The nurse should also track how much vaccine is wasted and why, because a sudden rise in wastage usually points to a fixable problem in session size, refrigeration, or technique.

Pregnancy changes the vaccination decision, and the reason is the same mechanism described at the opening of this chapter. Live vaccines are avoided as a rule, which includes yellow fever vaccine, oral typhoid vaccine, live Japanese encephalitis vaccine in most circumstances, live dengue vaccine, and the vaccines against measles, mumps, rubella, and chickenpox. Yellow fever is the difficult exception, because in a pregnant traveler going to an area of active transmission the risk of the disease may be greater than the risk of the vaccine, and that judgment belongs to the current guideline and to a clinician experienced in travel medicine. Inactivated vaccines may be given when the risk justifies them, and hepatitis A vaccine, hepatitis B vaccine, and rabies vaccine fall into that group. Influenza vaccine and the combined tetanus, diphtheria, and pertussis vaccine are actively recommended during pregnancy, because they protect both the woman and the newborn during the first months of life. Every one of these decisions should be documented with the reasoning behind it.

Immunosuppression is the other great divider, and it appears in tropical practice more often than anywhere else because of HIV, malnutrition, cancer treatment, transplantation, and the biologic agents used for inflammatory disease. Live vaccines are contraindicated in anyone with severely impaired immunity, because a weakened organism can still cause disseminated disease when the defenses that contain it are absent. BCG is contraindicated in symptomatic HIV infection, yellow fever vaccine is contraindicated in advanced HIV, and the live oral typhoid, live Japanese encephalitis, and live dengue vaccines are all avoided. Inactivated vaccines are safe in principle, but the response may be blunted, which means the patient may not achieve the protection the record suggests they have. The timing matters enormously, because vaccination given before chemotherapy, transplantation, or the start of a biologic agent produces a far better response than the same vaccine given afterward, and this is why the pre-treatment visit is the right moment to complete the schedule. Household members of an immunosuppressed patient should be fully vaccinated where possible, and the question of live vaccine virus shedding in the home follows the current guidance rather than habit.

Adverse effects after vaccination range from the trivial to the catastrophic, and the nurse must be able to sort them quickly. Local reactions such as pain, redness, and swelling at the injection site are common and expected, and systemic reactions such as fever, muscle aches, headache, and irritability usually appear within a day and resolve within 2 to 3 days. Serious allergic reactions are rare, occurring at roughly 1 per million doses, and they begin within minutes, which is why every vaccination session needs a period of observation and a plan. Anaphylaxis is treated with epinephrine at 0.5 milligrams given intramuscularly into the outer thigh for an adult, and at 0.01 milligrams per kilogram for a child, repeated every 5 to 15 minutes as needed while help is summoned. Fainting is far more common than anaphylaxis and is managed by laying the patient flat with the legs raised, but it must never be assumed when the airway or the blood pressure is in question. Every serious event must be reported to the national authority and into the international monitoring system that the World Health Organization maintains, because rare effects are only detected when reports accumulate across many countries.

Counseling is where most of the nursing value in vaccination lies, and it is also the part that gets squeezed when a clinic is busy. Before recommending anything, ask what the patient already believes and what they have heard, because a concern that is never spoken cannot be answered. Address the specific worry rather than reciting a list of facts, and use the conversation to find out whether the barrier is fear, cost, distance, religious belief, or a previous bad experience with a vaccine or with the health system itself. Explain what to expect after the injection, including the timing of fever and the fact that a sore arm is a sign of an immune response rather than a complication. Explain the intervals honestly, because a traveler who learns at the airport that a course needs weeks will not return for the next dose. Where a patient declines, document the conversation and leave the door open, since a respectful refusal today often becomes acceptance at the next visit.

Administration technique determines whether the dose that was given actually works, and the details are not optional. The deltoid muscle is the site for adults and older children, and the vastus lateralis of the thigh is the site for infants, while the needle length must match the muscle mass of the patient, because a short needle that deposits vaccine into fat produces a weaker response. The skin is cleaned and allowed to dry, the needle is inserted at the angle the product requires, and the vaccine is injected without aspiration, since aspiration is no longer recommended for any of these products. The site is not massaged afterward. A sterile needle and syringe must be used for every injection and discarded immediately into a sharps container, because reuse is one of the ways blood-borne infection spreads in exactly the settings this book describes. The patient should be positioned safely before the injection, particularly a child or a nervous adult, so that a faint does not become a fall.

Documentation is the thread that holds the whole program together, and it is the part most easily rushed. The record should carry the date, the vaccine and its presentation, the dose volume, the route, the site, the lot number, and the expiry date, together with the name of the person who administered it. The patient should leave with a personal record in hand, because in many countries that card is the only evidence of protection that will ever exist, and it must survive moves, floods, and changes of clinic. For yellow fever and for meningococcal vaccination before the pilgrimage, the official international certificate must be completed correctly, with the product details and the signature of the authorized provider, since an incomplete certificate can stop a traveler at a border. Documentation is also how the program learns, because coverage figures, dropout between doses, and adverse events are all built from the same entries the nurse makes at the end of a session.

Finally, the nurse's role extends beyond the individual encounter into the life of the program, and this is where vaccination succeeds or fails. Tracing children who have missed a dose, running outreach sessions in the communities farthest from the clinic, monitoring stock and cold chain, reporting adverse events, and keeping coverage records accurate are all nursing tasks, and they determine whether a vaccine that exists in the refrigerator becomes protection in a body. Integrating vaccination with other services, such as growth monitoring, antenatal care, and HIV care, brings people in and reduces the number of visits a family must make. The communities that return for the second and third doses are usually the ones where a trusted health worker explained the schedule in a language the family speaks.

To summarize, vaccines are the most powerful preventive tools in tropical medicine, they work by teaching the immune system through a safe encounter with a weakened organism or a purified antigen, and they span a wide range from the lifetime protection of a single live yellow fever dose to the modest and waning protection of the newest malaria products. The choice of product depends on the patient's age, pregnancy status, immune status, and itinerary, and the cold chain, the vaccine vial monitor, and the multi-dose vial policy decide whether the product that was chosen still works when it reaches the arm. The monitoring that follows administration, from the observation period to the report of a serious adverse event, protects both the individual and the program. None of these vaccines is a substitute for clean water, vector control, sanitation, prompt diagnosis, or effective treatment, and each of them is only as good as the schedule that is completed. The nurse who understands the mechanism, the schedule, the cold chain, the contraindications, the monitoring, and the conversation is the person who turns a fragile supply chain into actual immunity for a traveler, a child, or a whole village.
