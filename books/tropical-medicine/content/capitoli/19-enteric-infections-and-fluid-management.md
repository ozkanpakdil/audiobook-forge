# Chapter 19 — Enteric Infections and Fluid Management

Enteric infections spread by the fecal-oral route and remain a leading cause of death in young children across tropical settings. The organisms differ widely, from bacteria that invade the bloodstream and seed distant organs to bacteria that never leave the gut lumen yet pour out fluid fast enough to kill within hours. They share a transmission pathway through water, food, hands, and flies, so the same measures interrupt all of them at once. Because that pathway is ordinary, these illnesses are often accepted as normal life, and that acceptance is why they stay lethal. This chapter takes typhoid and paratyphoid fever, cholera, shigellosis, and traveler's diarrhea in turn, and treats fluid management as a clinical skill in its own right. Rehydration decides whether most of these patients live, and the nurse usually delivers it.

Typhoid fever is caused by Salmonella Typhi, and paratyphoid fever, similar but milder, by Salmonella Paratyphi A, B, or C. The organism infects only humans, so every case comes from another person through contact or contaminated water and food. It enters by mouth, survives stomach acid when the dose is large, and behaves differently from bacteria that stay in the lumen. The World Health Organization estimates roughly 9 million illnesses and 110,000 deaths each year, heaviest in South Asia, much of Africa, and wherever water and sewage systems are inadequate. Clean water, sanitation and hygiene would prevent most of it, so typhoid marks failing infrastructure as much as disease. The incubation period is usually 7 to 14 days but ranges from 3 days to more than a month.

After being swallowed, the organisms cross the small intestine lining, are taken up by immune cells in lymphoid tissue, and reach the bloodstream in a silent first phase. During incubation they multiply inside the liver, spleen, bone marrow, and gallbladder, and that multiplication produces the illness. A second wave entering the blood coincides with the onset of fever, when the organism can be recovered from blood reliably. The gallbladder becomes a site of continued seeding, explaining both the prolonged fever and the later risk of carriage. The intestine is injured again when the organism returns to the terminal ileum, and that injury can ulcerate through the bowel wall. This explains why stool culture is often negative early and why blood culture is the test of choice in the first week.

The fever of typhoid is called stepped because it climbs in stages over the first week, each afternoon a little higher, until it settles near 39 to 40 degrees Celsius. The pulse is often slower than the fever would predict, a finding called relative bradycardia, and it is a useful clue in prolonged fever without an obvious source. Rose spots appear in the first week in a minority of patients, as faint salmon-colored marks 2 to 4 millimeters across, in crops on the trunk, blanching when pressed and easily missed on darker skin. Constipation is common early and is followed later by diarrhea, the opposite of what most clinicians expect from an intestinal infection. The patient looks increasingly unwell, with headache, a coated tongue, loss of appetite, abdominal pain, and an enlarged tender liver or spleen. By the second week the fever persists and the patient may become confused or drowsy.

The complications of typhoid cluster in the second and third weeks and are what make it dangerous. The most feared is perforation of the terminal ileum, where lymphoid tissue has ulcerated, presenting as sudden severe abdominal pain, guarding, a rising pulse, and shock. Perforation is a surgical emergency with high mortality, and it must be suspected whenever a patient with typhoid develops abrupt abdominal pain or a rigid abdomen. Intestinal hemorrhage is the other bowel complication, and it can be massive and fatal. Beyond the gut, typhoid can cause encephalopathy, inflammation of the heart muscle, pneumonia, hepatitis, and, rarely, abscesses in the liver or spleen. Untreated, it kills roughly 10 to 20 per cent of those it infects, and relapse occurs in a small proportion after recovery.

Diagnosis rests on culture, and the specimen depends on how long the patient has been ill. Blood culture is most useful in the first week, when the organism is circulating, and it becomes less sensitive later as antibiotics clear the bloodstream. Bone marrow culture is the most sensitive test at any stage, but it requires a procedure many clinics cannot perform. Stool culture becomes positive in the second and third weeks, and urine culture can also yield the organism. The Widal test measures antibodies but is poorly specific, because previous infection, vaccination, and other infections raise the same antibodies, so a positive result never proves current typhoid. Molecular testing by PCR on blood answers within hours, though it does not replace culture, which provides the organism for susceptibility testing.

Treatment of typhoid has been reshaped by resistance. Fluoroquinolones were once the treatment of choice, but resistance spread through South Asia and much of Africa until they could no longer be relied on, and in many regions they have been removed from first-line use. Current guidance from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention favors ceftriaxone at 2 grams intravenously once daily for 7 to 14 days, or cefixime by mouth, or azithromycin at 1 gram by mouth once daily for 5 to 7 days, with ciprofloxacin only where the organism is susceptible. Dexamethasone is sometimes given for severe typhoid with shock or encephalopathy, but it is not routine and can worsen other infections. Supportive care with fluids, fever control, and nutrition runs alongside the antibiotic. Before prescribing, verify every regimen against current guidance, because these regimens are revised and they differ between countries, programs, and product labels.

Some patients do not clear the organism and become chronic carriers, excreting Salmonella Typhi in stool for more than a year after the acute illness resolves. Carriage usually reflects colonization of the gallbladder, and it is more common in women, in older adults, and in people with gallstones. A carrier may feel entirely well and may have no memory of a significant illness, which is why carriers seed outbreaks through the food they prepare. Historical investigations traced single asymptomatic cooks to dozens of cases in households and institutions, and those findings are why food handlers are screened in many countries. Current guidance offers prolonged ciprofloxacin for several weeks, or ampicillin with probenecid, and removal of the gallbladder when medical treatment fails. Public health follow-up matters as much as the prescription, because an unmonitored carrier keeps infecting others while feeling healthy.

Prevention rests on clean water, safe food, sanitary disposal of human waste, and vaccination, in that order of importance. The typhoid conjugate vaccine is given as a single dose and can be used in children from 6 months of age, and older polysaccharide vaccines remain available in some programs. The World Health Organization recommends introducing typhoid conjugate vaccine in the countries with the highest burden, and campaigns have reduced illness where water and sanitation improvements lag behind. Vaccination does not replace safe water, because it reduces the chance of illness without stopping transmission. For the traveler, the advice is to drink boiled, filtered, or bottled water, avoid ice from uncertain water, eat food that is cooked and still hot, and peel fruit personally. When a case is confirmed, contact tracing, food handler investigation, and notification to public health authorities are part of the clinical response.

Cholera is caused by Vibrio cholerae, and epidemic strains belong mainly to serogroup O1, with serogroup O139 appearing in some regions. The organism lives in brackish water and with small aquatic animals, and it has produced pandemics that began in the Ganges delta and spread along trade and pilgrimage routes since the nineteenth century. Cholera is endemic across much of South Asia and parts of Africa, and it flares into epidemics wherever people are crowded without safe water or sanitation, which is why war, flooding, and displacement often precede outbreaks. The incubation period is short, from a few hours to 5 days, so an outbreak can grow explosively within a week. A small number of people carry the organism without symptoms and shed it for a few days. Severity ranges from mild diarrhea to a loss of fluid that kills a healthy adult within hours.

The reason cholera is so violent is a single toxin, and its mechanism explains every clinical feature of the disease. Vibrio cholerae does not invade the bowel wall; it attaches to the lining of the small intestine and releases a toxin made of two parts, one that binds to a receptor on the cell surface and one that enters the cell and does the damage. Once inside, the toxin chemically modifies a regulatory protein by attaching a small sugar and phosphate unit, and that modification locks the protein into its active state permanently. The locked protein drives an enzyme called adenylate cyclase without pause, and the level of cyclic adenosine monophosphate inside the cell rises far above normal. High cyclic adenosine monophosphate forces the chloride channels to remain open, chloride pours into the gut lumen, and sodium and water follow passively. The result is a massive secretion of isotonic fluid that overwhelms the colon's ability to reabsorb it.

The clinical picture of severe cholera is unmistakable once seen. Profuse, painless, watery diarrhea begins suddenly, and the stool is pale, cloudy, and flecked with mucus, which is why it is called rice-water stool. There is typically no fever, no tenesmus, and no blood, and the patient may vomit repeatedly, making oral replacement difficult. Fluid loss can reach 1 liter per hour in an adult, and a previously healthy person can lose more than 10 per cent of body weight within a few hours. The signs are those of profound dehydration and shock: sunken eyes, a hollowed face, dry mouth, skin that stays pinched when released, wrinkled hands, a weak rapid pulse, low blood pressure, and almost no urine. Muscle cramps are common from loss of sodium and chloride, and potassium loss causes weakness and dangerous heart rhythms. Without treatment the patient becomes unconscious and dies, and with prompt rehydration the same patient can be sitting up and drinking within hours.

Assessing the degree of dehydration determines everything that follows, and the World Health Organization classification is reliable enough to use in a tent or a hallway. A patient with no dehydration has no signs, is alert, drinks normally, and passes urine. A patient with some dehydration has two or more of restlessness, sunken eyes, thirst and eagerness to drink, a skin pinch that returns slowly, and reduced urine output. A patient with severe dehydration has two or more of lethargy or unconsciousness, very sunken eyes, inability to drink, and a skin pinch returning very slowly, along with a weak pulse and low blood pressure. The skin pinch is performed on the abdomen, and in an older person or a severely malnourished child it may retract slowly even without dehydration, so the whole picture must be read together. Weigh the patient whenever a scale is available, because weight change is the most honest measure of fluid balance, and reassess at short intervals, because dehydration can worsen within an hour.

Oral rehydration salts are the single most important medicine in this chapter, and their composition is not arbitrary. The low-osmolarity formulation recommended by the World Health Organization contains 75 millimoles of sodium, 75 millimoles of glucose, 20 millimoles of potassium, 65 millimoles of chloride, and 10 millimoles of citrate in each liter of clean water. The glucose is present at a concentration that matches the sodium, and that match is the whole trick of the solution. When glucose and sodium reach the intestinal surface together, a transporter carries them into the cell as a pair, and water follows the absorbed sodium by osmosis. This route works even while the intestine is pouring fluid outward, which is why a solution that seems too simple can rescue a patient minutes from death. One sachet is dissolved in 1 liter of clean water, because a solution that is too strong worsens dehydration and one that is too weak fails to replace sodium.

Giving oral rehydration salts well is a skill, and it is often performed badly. A child with some dehydration should receive 75 milliliters per kilogram over 4 hours, by cup and spoon in small frequent sips, and a patient who vomits should wait 10 minutes and resume more slowly. Replace ongoing losses separately, with about 10 milliliters per kilogram after each loose stool in a child under 2 years, 50 to 100 milliliters for an older child, and 200 to 250 milliliters for an adult. If a patient cannot drink because of vomiting, lethargy, or a swollen abdomen, a nasogastric tube allows the same solution to be given safely while intravenous access is arranged. Never give a soft drink, fruit juice, or sports drink instead, because the sugar is too high and the sodium too low. Teach the family to prepare and offer the solution themselves, because the person who keeps the cup filled usually decides the outcome.

For severe dehydration, fluids must go directly into the vein, and Ringer's lactate is the fluid of choice because its composition is close to the fluid being lost. Current guidance from the World Health Organization suggests 100 milliliters per kilogram over the first 3 hours, with 30 milliliters per kilogram in the first 30 minutes and the remainder over the following two and a half hours. An adult typically needs 2 to 4 liters in the first 3 hours, and the rate is adjusted continuously according to the pulse, the blood pressure, the skin pinch, and the urine output. If Ringer's lactate is unavailable, normal saline can be used, but it does not correct the acidosis and potassium loss of cholera as well. Potassium is often added to the intravenous fluid once the patient is passing urine, and low blood sugar must be anticipated in children. Reassess every 30 minutes, because both too little and too much fluid are dangerous.

Antibiotics play a supporting role in cholera, and they are not the treatment. A single dose of doxycycline at 300 milligrams by mouth for an adult shortens the diarrhea, reduces the volume of fluid lost, and shortens the time the patient sheds the organism, which limits transmission. For children the dose is 2 milligrams per kilogram by mouth as a single dose, and azithromycin at 20 milligrams per kilogram, or 1 gram for an adult, is an alternative where doxycycline resistance or pregnancy makes it preferable. Antibiotics must never delay rehydration, because no antibiotic has saved a patient whose circulation had already collapsed. Resistance to doxycycline has appeared in several outbreaks, so local susceptibility patterns and current guidance should be checked before the drug is chosen. In the nineteenth century cholera killed more than half of those it struck, and with rehydration alone the case fatality falls below 1 per cent. That change came from water, salt, and sugar, not from a new drug.

Managing a cholera outbreak is a logistical exercise as much as a clinical one, and the nurse usually holds it together. A cholera treatment center should separate triage from rehydration from the ward, so that patients can be sorted by dehydration category and treated without crowding. Oral rehydration corners stocked with sachets, clean water, cups, and spoons can treat some dehydration before a patient needs a bed, and they free the ward for those who need intravenous fluids. Supplies to count and restock daily include oral rehydration salts, Ringer's lactate, intravenous tubing and catheters, nasogastric tubes, chlorine, soap, and gloves and gowns for personal protection. Stool and vomit are highly infectious, so surfaces must be disinfected, bedding handled carefully, and hands washed with soap and running water after every contact. A patient who dies should be handled with respect and safe burial practices, and staff who become ill must be removed from duty immediately.

Shigellosis is caused by Shigella species, chiefly Shigella dysenteriae, Shigella flexneri, Shigella boydii, and Shigella sonnei, and it is the classic cause of bacillary dysentery. The organism is extraordinarily infectious, with as few as 10 organisms able to cause illness, which is why it moves so quickly through households, schools, camps, and institutions. It spreads by the fecal-oral route, and because the infecting dose is so low, contaminated fingers and food are enough even where water is safe. Shigella invades the cells lining the colon rather than remaining in the lumen, multiplying inside those cells and spreading sideways. Shigella dysenteriae type 1 produces Shiga toxin, a potent poison that blocks protein production inside cells by damaging the larger ribosomal subunit, and this toxin causes the most severe disease. Direct invasion combined with toxin production explains why shigellosis produces bloody, small-volume stools rather than the massive watery loss of cholera.

The illness begins 1 to 3 days after exposure with fever, cramping abdominal pain, and watery diarrhea that becomes bloody within a day or two. Stools are small, frequent, and composed largely of blood, mucus, and pus, and the patient has tenesmus, an intense painful urge to pass stool that produces almost nothing. Children may develop seizures, and severe cases can progress to toxic megacolon, intestinal perforation, or a profound inflammatory state. Dehydration is usually less extreme than in cholera, because the colon is inflamed rather than freely secreting, so the danger lies in the systemic effects of the toxin. Shigella dysenteriae type 1 is particularly associated with hemolytic uremic syndrome, the triad of anemia, a low platelet count, and acute kidney injury, which appears about a week after bloody diarrhea begins. Children are affected most often, and the illness can require dialysis, though many recover kidney function. Giving antibiotics to a patient with Shiga toxin-producing infection remains debated, because killing the organism may release more toxin, but avoiding drugs that slow the bowel and avoiding dehydration are always right.

Treatment of shigellosis shortens illness, reduces shedding, and prevents complications, but it must be accompanied by the same fluid discipline as every other enteric infection. Current guidance favors ciprofloxacin at 500 milligrams by mouth twice a day for 3 days, or azithromycin at 1 gram by mouth as a single dose, or azithromycin at 500 milligrams once daily for 3 days, with ceftriaxone reserved for severe or hospitalized patients. Resistance is a growing problem, particularly to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole and to older agents, so the choice should follow local susceptibility data and current guidance rather than habit. Antibiotics are not indicated for every watery diarrhea, but they are valuable in confirmed or suspected bloody diarrhea and are most effective when started early. Antimotility drugs are dangerous in dysentery and must be withheld, because slowing the bowel traps the organism and toxin and can precipitate toxic megacolon. Continued feeding is part of recovery, and reporting matters because shigellosis spreads easily and resistance must be tracked.

Traveler's diarrhea is the enteric syndrome most often seen in clinics in wealthy countries, and it follows a predictable pattern. It is defined as three or more loose stools in 24 hours, with or without cramps, nausea, or fever, beginning during travel or within about 10 days of returning. The commonest cause is enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli, which produces toxins that drive secretion without invading tissue, and other causes include Campylobacter, Salmonella, Shigella, and several viruses and parasites. The illness is usually self-limited, lasting 3 to 5 days, and the main risk is dehydration rather than death, so the clinician's task is to identify the minority who need antibiotics or who have a more serious illness. Loperamide has a real but narrow place. It is reasonable in watery diarrhea without fever or blood, at 4 milligrams by mouth at the start and 2 milligrams after each loose stool, up to 8 milligrams in a day. It must not be used with fever or bloody diarrhea, because in dysentery it traps the organism and toxin and has been associated with toxic megacolon, and it should be avoided in children and in anyone significantly dehydrated.

The nurse's central role in enteric disease begins with assessment, and it is more consequential than any prescription. Look for dry mucous membranes, sunken eyes, absent tears in a child, reduced skin turgor, a rapid weak pulse, low blood pressure, cool extremities, delayed capillary refill, and lethargy, and record each finding rather than a general impression. Ask about thirst, vomiting, stool volume and frequency, and the ability to drink, because a patient who cannot keep fluid down needs a different plan. Weigh the patient at admission and at intervals afterward, since a falling weight is the clearest evidence of continuing loss and a rising weight may signal over-hydration. Measure urine output, and in a severe case record it hourly, because urine is the body's own report on whether circulation has been restored. Pay particular attention to the malnourished child, the older adult, and the pregnant patient, in whom the same loss produces far greater danger. Document everything you observe and everything you give, because in a busy outbreak the chart is the only memory the team has.

Monitoring during treatment is continuous and concrete. In severe cholera, urine output should be at least 30 milliliters per hour in an adult and at least 0.5 to 1 milliliter per kilogram per hour in a child, and a falling output despite fluids calls for faster infusion and a search for another problem. Follow the pulse, the blood pressure, the respiratory rate, and the level of consciousness, because they change before the laboratory does, and check the skin pinch and the eyes at each review. Measure electrolytes where possible, especially potassium, sodium, bicarbonate, and magnesium, since low potassium causes weakness and heart rhythm disturbances while low bicarbonate reflects acidosis. Check the blood sugar in children, because low blood sugar is common and dangerous. Replace ongoing losses according to measured stool and vomit volumes rather than a fixed rule. A patient who is improving passes urine, becomes alert, drinks eagerly, and has a skin pinch that returns promptly.

Getting fluid into a patient fast enough is the technical heart of this chapter, and it requires choosing the right route for the degree of illness. A patient who can drink, is alert, and has some dehydration should be rehydrated by mouth with oral rehydration salts, in small sips by cup and spoon, and watched closely for 4 hours. A patient who is vomiting repeatedly or too weak to drink should have a nasogastric tube placed, and the same solution can be given by that route in measured amounts. A patient with severe dehydration, shock, or an inability to take anything by mouth needs an intravenous line immediately, and two lines may be needed if the pulse is weak and the veins are collapsed. Do not wait for laboratory results before starting fluids, because in cholera the diagnosis is clinical and the treatment is time-critical. The commonest error in these patients is giving fluid too slowly, and the second commonest is stopping it too soon.

Special populations need modified plans, and the general rules must be adapted rather than applied mechanically. Children dehydrate faster than adults, because more of their body is water, and they are prone to low blood sugar. Older adults tolerate both dehydration and rapid rehydration poorly, and heart or kidney disease limits how fast fluids can be given, so smaller volumes are used. Pregnancy adds risk in both directions, because dehydration can precipitate preterm labor while over-hydration can cause fluid in the lungs, so treatment is planned with obstetric support. Severe acute malnutrition changes the signs of dehydration, since the skin pinch and the sunken eyes may mislead, and the World Health Organization recommends a specific low-sodium rehydration solution for these children. Continue feeding throughout, because withholding food prolongs the illness and worsens malnutrition, and keep breastfeeding a sick child. Zinc supplementation at 20 milligrams per day for 10 to 14 days reduces the duration and severity of diarrhea and lowers recurrence, and iron and vitamin A deficiencies should be treated after the acute episode.

The adverse effects of the drugs used in this chapter are mostly predictable, and knowing them lets you prepare and protect the patient. Doxycycline commonly causes nausea, vomiting, and sun sensitivity, and it should be avoided in pregnancy and in young children where alternatives exist, because it can stain developing teeth. Azithromycin is generally well tolerated but can cause nausea, abdominal discomfort, and diarrhea, and rarely it prolongs a portion of the heart rhythm in a way that can trigger dangerous arrhythmias. Ceftriaxone can cause diarrhea, rash, and allergy, and it can precipitate in the gallbladder or the kidney, so the patient should be watched for abdominal pain or reduced urine. Ciprofloxacin can cause nausea, headache, dizziness, tendon rupture, and peripheral neuropathy, and it is used cautiously in children and pregnancy because of effects on growing cartilage. Loperamide causes constipation, bloating, drowsiness, and, in excess or in the wrong patient, ileus and toxic megacolon. Ask about every drug the patient takes, because interactions are common, and report severe reactions promptly.

Infection control in enteric disease is unglamorous, cheap, and decisive, and the nurse sets the standard for the whole team. Hand hygiene is the foundation, and it must be washing with soap and running water at every opportunity, because alcohol rubs do not reliably kill all of the organisms discussed here. Separate patients with the same infection when possible, keep food preparation away from patient care, and exclude symptomatic staff and food handlers from work until they are cleared. Clean surfaces, bedding, and shared equipment with an appropriate disinfectant, and treat stool and vomit as infectious material at all times. Improving water, sanitation and hygiene in a community prevents more cases than any treatment program, and the World Health Organization and the United Nations Children's Fund treat these measures as the foundation of diarrheal disease control. Surveillance and antimicrobial stewardship are part of clinical care, so cholera is reported promptly, often within 24 hours, because a single confirmed case signals a possible outbreak. Shigellosis and typhoid should also be reported according to national rules, and a simple line list of dates, ages, locations, and outcomes reveals a common water source or a failing latrine. Use antibiotics only when indicated and complete the course, because every unnecessary prescription selects for resistance.

To summarize, enteric infections share a fecal-oral route, and the nurse's response is built on assessment, rehydration, and infection control rather than on antibiotics alone. Typhoid fever is a systemic illness of the bloodstream with stepped fever, relative bradycardia, rose spots, and the early constipation that later becomes diarrhea, and its blood culture confirms what its complications, especially intestinal perforation, make urgent. Resistance has removed fluoroquinolones from use in much of the world, and the chronic carrier must be found and treated because a well person can seed an outbreak. Cholera is a toxin-driven secretion in which cyclic adenosine monophosphate locks the intestinal cell into pouring out water, producing rice-water stool and losses that kill within hours. Oral rehydration salts with the correct sodium and glucose concentrations replace those losses, Ringer's lactate is used intravenously for severe dehydration, and a single dose of doxycycline is an adjunct that must never delay fluids. Rehydration turned cholera from a disease that killed most of its victims into one that kills fewer than 1 in 100. Shigellosis and Shiga toxin cause bloody dysentery and hemolytic uremic syndrome, while traveler's diarrhea is usually self-limited and loperamide has a narrow place with clear cautions; assess dehydration carefully, monitor urine output and electrolytes, and verify every regimen against current guidance from the World Health Organization, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, or the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control, because doses differ between countries. Treat the patient in front of you, and treat the water everyone else will drink.
