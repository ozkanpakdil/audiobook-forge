# Chapter 34 — Mass Drug Administration and Elimination Programs

Mass drug administration is the practice of offering a medicine to every eligible person in a defined community, whether or not that person has been diagnosed with the infection the medicine is meant to treat. The World Health Organization more often uses the term preventive chemotherapy when it speaks of the neglected tropical diseases, and the two phrases describe the same act of repeated population-level treatment delivered on a fixed schedule. The idea is uncomfortable at first hearing, because it reverses the ordinary order of clinical medicine, in which a diagnosis precedes a prescription. In these programs the unit of care is not the patient in front of you but the entire community, and the aim is to reduce the amount of infection circulating in that community until transmission can no longer sustain itself. A nurse who understands why that reversal is necessary will run a campaign far better than one who only follows the calendar.

The population principle rests on the biology of these parasites and on plain arithmetic. For lymphatic filariasis, onchocerciasis, schistosomiasis, the soil-transmitted helminths, trachoma and yaws, a large proportion of infected people carry the organism silently and pass it on while feeling entirely well. Many of these infections can be found only by tests that are unavailable, slow or too costly for a district clinic, and no campaign can examine a million people one at a time. The medicines used are inexpensive or donated, safe enough for wide use, and effective against the stages of the parasite that keep transmission going. Treating everyone therefore drains the reservoir of infection far more completely and far more quickly than treating only the people who happen to be diagnosed.

The strategy was assembled over the twentieth century and refined in its final decades. Campaigns against yaws after the Second World War treated millions of people with penicillin and drove the disease down, only to see it return when the programs were stopped, a lesson that shaped everything that followed. The eradication of smallpox proved that a population-wide effort joined to strong surveillance could end a disease permanently. The campaign against Guinea worm showed what village-level work, a cloth filter and careful case finding could accomplish when the goal was absolute. In West Africa, programs against river blindness combined spraying of the rivers that bred the blackfly with community distribution of ivermectin, and the discovery that trained community members could give the medicine safely changed the scale of what was possible. Donated medicine programs, in which manufacturers provide the tablets free of charge for as long as they are needed, made repeated mass treatment affordable in countries that could never have paid for it.

The diseases that dominate this work are lymphatic filariasis, onchocerciasis, schistosomiasis, the soil-transmitted helminths, trachoma and yaws. Each is a candidate for population treatment because it is common, because it causes avoidable suffering, because a safe single-dose or short-course medicine exists, and because the parasite cannot easily hide in an animal reservoir or multiply freely in the environment. The World Health Organization gathers most of them under the heading of preventive chemotherapy, while yaws is approached through a different design that treats whole communities at the start and then narrows to cases and their contacts. Other conditions enter this field as well, including scabies and leprosy, and the organization now counts 20 neglected tropical diseases, having most recently added noma, the destructive infection of the mouth and face. What unites them is not a single parasite but a single logic, which is that the medicine is safe enough and the burden large enough that treating the many protects the many.

Lymphatic filariasis is the infection for which the modern mass campaign was designed, and it remains the largest of these programs. The World Health Organization estimates that about 120 million people are infected and that hundreds of millions more live in areas where transmission continues. The parasites Wuchereria bancrofti, Brugia malayi and Brugia timori, the worms that cause lymphatic filariasis, live for years in the lymphatic vessels, and the microscopic offspring they release circulate in the blood, where mosquitoes pick them up and pass them to other people. The strategy has two pillars, and both matter to nursing. The first is repeated treatment of the whole community to wipe out the microfilariae that feed transmission, and the second is care of the people already damaged, through hygiene of the limb, treatment of skin infection and surgery for hydrocele. A program that treats but does not care for the swollen leg has done only half of its work.

Onchocerciasis, also called river blindness, is caused by the worm Onchocerca volvulus and transmitted by blackflies that breed in fast-flowing rivers. Adult worms gather in nodules beneath the skin and release microfilariae that migrate through the skin and the eye, producing intense itching, disfiguring skin disease and irreversible blindness. Because the adult worms live for more than a decade and the microfilariae cause the damage, the treatment is not curative in one course but suppressive, and it must be repeated for many years. Community-directed treatment with ivermectin, in which the village itself chooses the distributors and manages the distribution, has been the engine of control in Africa, and it proved durable where externally run campaigns faltered. In the Americas, a combination of repeated treatment and vector control interrupted transmission across the whole affected region, and the goal in Africa has now shifted from control toward the elimination of transmission in selected countries.

Schistosomiasis, caused by blood flukes including Schistosoma haematobium and Schistosoma mansoni, follows a different route, because the parasite passes through fresh-water snails and infects people who wade, swim, wash or work in contaminated water. The worms live in the blood vessels around the bladder or the intestine, release eggs that damage those organs, and cause blood in the urine or stool, anemia, poor growth in children and, over years, bladder and liver disease. The medicine praziquantel kills the adult worms effectively but does not prevent reinfection, so a single round buys relief rather than freedom, and repeated annual treatment is needed while exposure continues. School-age children carry the heaviest burden in most settings, which is why schools are the natural platform, but fishermen, farmers, irrigation workers and women who wash clothes at the water's edge are also heavily exposed and are often missed by a campaign that counts only children. Treatment must therefore be paired with safe water, latrines and explicit advice about which contact with water carries the greatest risk.

The soil-transmitted helminths are the most numerous of these infections and the easiest to treat. Ascaris lumbricoides, the large roundworm, Trichuris trichiura, the whipworm, and the hookworms Necator americanus and Ancylostoma duodenale infect more than a billion people, and they are transmitted by eggs or larvae in soil contaminated with human waste. Hookworm causes chronic intestinal blood loss and iron deficiency anemia, Ascaris can obstruct the bowel or the bile ducts, and whipworm can cause dysentery and rectal prolapse in heavy infection. The World Health Organization recommends periodic treatment of preschool children, school-age children and women of reproductive age without individual diagnosis, with albendazole or mebendazole, and the purpose is not to cure every worm but to keep the burden low enough that growth, learning and hemoglobin are protected. Because reinfection follows quickly where sanitation is poor, deworming is deliberately coupled with water supply, latrines, hygiene education and footwear rather than presented as a cure on its own.

Trachoma is caused by the bacterium Chlamydia trachomatis, and its path to blindness is a story of repetition. Repeated episodes of conjunctivitis in childhood scar the inner surface of the eyelid, the scarred lid turns inward, and the lashes scrape the cornea until it clouds and sight is lost. The World Health Organization strategy combines surgery for the inturned lashes, antibiotics for the community, facial cleanliness and environmental improvement, and the four parts are meant to work together rather than as alternatives. Mass treatment with azithromycin reduces the bacterial load across the district, facial cleanliness reduces the transmission that happens from face to face and from eye-seeking flies, and better water supply makes washing possible. The elimination threshold is demanding: the prevalence of active trachoma in young children must fall below 5 per cent, and the prevalence of the blinding complication in adults must become very low, and both must then stay there under surveillance.

Yaws is caused by the bacterium Treponema pallidum subspecies pertenue, a relative of the organism that causes syphilis, and it spreads by direct skin contact among children in warm, humid and crowded conditions. It begins as a painless ulcer on the leg or elsewhere, then produces widespread skin lesions and, after years, destruction of the bones of the nose, the palate and the legs. The disease is exquisitely sensitive to azithromycin, and the eradication strategy begins with total community treatment of every person in an affected district, followed by a second round months later, and then by total targeted treatment of cases and their contacts as the disease recedes. Where azithromycin is unavailable, benzathine penicillin by intramuscular injection remains effective. Yaws is one of the two diseases the world currently aims to eradicate, and its history warns that the last cases are the hardest, because the disease becomes rare and invisible just when surveillance needs to be most active.

The medicines themselves are few, and the combination chosen depends on which diseases circulate in the district. For lymphatic filariasis where onchocerciasis is absent, the usual regimen is diethylcarbamazine at 6 milligrams per kilogram of body weight together with albendazole at 400 milligrams, both by mouth as a single dose, repeated once a year. Where onchocerciasis is present, ivermectin at 150 to 200 micrograms per kilogram of body weight replaces diethylcarbamazine, because diethylcarbamazine can provoke dangerous reactions in people who carry the eye worm. A three-drug combination of ivermectin, diethylcarbamazine and albendazole has been introduced in settings where it is safe, and it can interrupt transmission in fewer annual rounds than the older two-drug regimens. The number of rounds is therefore no longer fixed at 4 to 6 everywhere, and the duration must be read from the current national protocol rather than assumed.

Onchocerciasis is treated with ivermectin at 150 micrograms per kilogram of body weight by mouth, repeated once or twice a year for many years, because the drug kills microfilariae but does not reliably kill the adult worms. Schistosomiasis is treated with praziquantel at 40 milligrams per kilogram of body weight as a single dose by mouth for the urinary and intestinal forms, while the species found in parts of East Asia are treated with 60 milligrams per kilogram divided into 2 or 3 doses given within one day. In young children the tablet is crushed or a pediatric formulation is used, and the bitter taste is a practical problem that the nurse should anticipate rather than discover at the doorway. A preschool child with schistosomiasis now can and should be treated, and the older habit of excluding all young children from praziquantel is no longer correct.

The soil-transmitted helminths are treated with albendazole at 400 milligrams or mebendazole at 500 milligrams as a single dose by mouth, repeated once or twice a year according to the intensity of transmission, with heavy whipworm infection often treated with albendazole at 400 milligrams a day for 3 days because a single dose is less effective against that worm. Trachoma is treated with azithromycin at 20 milligrams per kilogram of body weight by mouth, to a maximum of 1 gram, given once a year to the whole district for 3 to 5 years, while an individual with active infection may also receive tetracycline eye ointment at 1 per cent applied twice a day for 6 weeks. Yaws is treated with azithromycin at 30 milligrams per kilogram of body weight by mouth, to a maximum of 2 grams, and benzathine penicillin at 1.2 million units intramuscularly for an adult or 600,000 units for a young child is the alternative. Ivermectin is also used at 200 micrograms per kilogram for scabies in population treatment, and each of these schedules belongs to a specific disease and setting rather than to a general rule.

None of these doses should be given from memory, and the nurse is the person who keeps that rule alive in a campaign. Antiparasitic regimens are revised as new evidence appears, guidance differs between countries, and the same infection may be treated with one schedule in a national program and another in the clinic next door. The World Health Organization, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control each publish their own documents, updated on their own schedules and written for different populations, so no single number can be treated as settled truth. The current national protocol, the current product label and the local program instructions must all be checked before a medicine is given, and the check is repeated at every round because the protocol may have changed since the last one. The weight is measured on the day of treatment rather than recalled from a previous visit, the child stands straight against the dose pole, and the tablet handed out is compared with the strength written on its label. A dose that was correct at the last campaign may be wrong at this one, and the guideline is the authority rather than the habit of the team.

The delivery platform decides whether a campaign reaches the people who need it, and the model that transformed this field is community-directed treatment. In that model the community itself selects its distributors, decides how and when the medicine will be given, and keeps its own records, while the health system supplies the medicine, the training and the supervision. The distributors are usually farmers, teachers or local leaders who receive a few days of instruction and a small allowance, and they reach households that a clinic could never reach on foot. Schools are the natural platform for deworming school-age children, because the children are already gathered, the teachers can be trained to use the dose pole and the register, and treatment becomes part of the school day. Community health workers and midwives carry the campaign for preschool children and women of reproductive age, often on child health days that also deliver vaccination and vitamin supplements, and fixed posts at a market or a health center serve the towns. House-to-house visiting remains essential for the people who never come to either.

Where several diseases overlap, the programs are integrated so that one visit can address lymphatic filariasis, onchocerciasis, soil-transmitted helminths and schistosomiasis at once, with trachoma included where azithromycin is also being distributed. Integration reduces the number of visits to the same village, the number of registers, the number of community meetings and the fatigue of the volunteers, and it makes better use of the community's patience. It also creates a new risk, because a single round now carries several medicines and every eligibility rule for each medicine must be applied at the same doorway. The nurse who supervises an integrated round has to know which drug is withheld from whom and why, and has to be able to explain that clearly to a distributor standing in front of a family. Integration that is only a matter of convenience, without that knowledge, produces errors that damage the whole program.

The unglamorous work of logistics begins months before the first tablet is swallowed. Districts must be mapped so that every eligible community is named, and a census or an agreed population estimate must be settled so that the denominator for coverage is honest rather than convenient. The quantity of each medicine is forecast from that population, ordered through the national program, moved to the district store and then broken down into smaller packets for each distribution team. Storage is simpler than for vaccines because these tablets tolerate heat, but they still need a dry place, protection from direct sun and stock records that match what is on the shelf. Registers, tally sheets, pens, weighing scales, height poles, megaphones and bicycles all belong on the supply list, and a campaign that runs out of tally sheets in the third village will never know what it achieved.

A day of distribution begins with a short team meeting, and the routine of that meeting protects the whole round. The team checks the stock against the register, agrees the route, reviews the exclusions and confirms who will carry the medicines and the records. Each distributor carries only the quantity needed for the day, because a large supply in a single bag invites loss and diversion. At the end of the day the unused tablets are counted back, and any discrepancy is investigated the same day, since a missing box is either a recording error or a theft and both matter. The nurse supervisor visits a sample of distribution points rather than trusting the reports, watches a distributor measure a dose, asks a treated person what he was told about possible reactions and checks that refusals and exclusions were written down with a reason. Supervision that only counts tablets will miss the household that was skipped because the mother was away, and it is exactly that household that will keep transmission alive.

Interrupting transmission requires a high proportion of the community to be treated, and the threshold differs by disease. For lymphatic filariasis and onchocerciasis the World Health Organization advises that at least 65 per cent of the total population in an implementation unit must take the medicine in each round. For the soil-transmitted helminths and schistosomiasis the target is at least 75 per cent of the at-risk group, usually school-age children, and for trachoma the azithromycin campaign aims at 80 per cent of the total population, because the antibiotic must reach enough of the children who carry the infection. These figures are not aspirations but biological requirements, because the parasite reproduces rapidly enough that a smaller share of untreated people can sustain transmission between rounds. Coverage is measured in every implementation unit separately, and a low-coverage unit is treated as a failing unit rather than hidden inside a flattering national average.

A campaign that reaches 60 per cent of a community may feel like a triumph and may still fail to interrupt transmission. Each round kills a large share of the parasites in the people who are treated, but the untreated continue to produce the stages that infect mosquitoes or contaminate water, and by the time the next round arrives the parasite population has partly recovered. Coverage that is high in the towns and low in the remote hamlets leaves a refuge from which infection spreads back into the treated areas. This is why repeated rounds are needed rather than one heroic effort: the first round reduces the burden, and the later rounds drive it below the level at which transmission can continue. The nurse should explain this to the community plainly, because the idea that a single dose would last forever is one of the most common reasons for poor participation in the second round.

The distribution of non-participation matters as much as its amount. If the same people refuse every year while others always accept, the effective coverage is far lower than the reported figure suggests, because a stable untreated group becomes a stable reservoir. This pattern is called systematic non-participation, and it is often concentrated among people who are absent for work, who fear the medicine, who belong to a group that has been poorly engaged, or who were badly treated at an earlier campaign. A district that counts only the tablets given cannot see this pattern, and it needs registers that follow households across rounds and a nurse who asks why the same names are missing each year. Reaching those households is slower and more expensive than reaching the willing ones, which is precisely why programs tend to fail near the end rather than at the beginning.

Coverage figures themselves must be checked against reality rather than accepted as record. Reported coverage can be inflated when a tablet is recorded as swallowed although it was dropped, when the same person is counted twice, or when the denominator is taken from an old and generous population estimate. Small cluster surveys, in which a set number of villages and households are sampled and people are asked whether they took the medicine, are the honest check on the register, and they can be reinforced by looking for the bitter taste of the drug or by counting remaining tablets. A program that reports 90 per cent coverage and cannot demonstrate it under survey has a measurement problem that will eventually become a transmission problem. The nurse who keeps records carefully and welcomes the survey is protecting both the community and the program's reputation.

The most important safety problem in this field arises where onchocerciasis and loiasis overlap, and every campaign team must know whether its district lies in that zone. Ivermectin kills the microfilariae of Loa loa rapidly, and in a person who carries a very heavy load the dying parasites provoke an inflammatory reaction in the brain that can cause confusion, coma and death. The danger rises sharply when the count of microfilariae in the blood exceeds roughly 30,000 per milliliter, and it is greatest in people who have lived for years in the forest where the deerfly transmits the worm. A program that simply adds ivermectin to a campaign in such a district may cause encephalopathy in people who were entirely well before the campaign. Diethylcarbamazine is also hazardous in heavy loiasis, so the whole treatment combination, and not only one drug, has to be designed around the local geography of this infection. Several strategies exist, including counting microfilariae in a drop of blood at the point of treatment and withholding the drug from anyone above the threshold, and mapping that excludes whole villages where the risk is too high. The nurse's responsibility is to know the risk zone and to refuse to improvise.

Reactions to treatment are common and should be expected rather than treated as failure. When a person who carries microfilariae receives ivermectin or diethylcarbamazine, the rapid death of the parasites releases antigens and bacterial products and produces the Mazzotti reaction, with fever, headache, itching, rash, muscle and joint pain, dizziness and tender lymph nodes beginning within a day of treatment. Praziquantel commonly causes abdominal discomfort, nausea, headache and drowsiness that settle within a day or two, and azithromycin may cause nausea, abdominal cramping and, rarely, an irregular heartbeat. Management is supportive, with rest, fluids by mouth, acetaminophen for fever and pain, and an antihistamine when itching is severe. The single most valuable nursing act is to warn the community beforehand that some people feel unwell for a day or two, because an unexpected reaction frightens far more people than the reaction itself.

The team must also recognize the rare event that is not ordinary and must act on it immediately. Danger signs include difficulty breathing, swelling of the face or throat, loss of consciousness, a seizure, severe eye pain and a rapidly spreading skin infection. A person who becomes confused or cannot be woken after treatment in an area where loiasis is present may have encephalopathy and needs urgent transfer, with the drug, the dose and the time recorded and sent with the patient. Any death, hospitalization or persistent disability after treatment must be reported to the national program within the required time, whatever the suspected cause. The nurse should not wait for certainty before reporting, because a report can always be revised while a case that is never reported cannot be investigated at all.

Every campaign needs a working pharmacovigilance system, not merely a reaction register kept in a drawer. An adverse event report should name the medicine, the dose, the route, the time, the symptoms and the interval before they began, the treatment given and the outcome. The national program forwards these reports to the drug regulatory authority and to the World Health Organization system for international drug monitoring, where signals from many countries are compared and rare harms become visible. Active surveillance, in which a sample of treated people is followed deliberately rather than waiting for complaints, detects reactions that passive reporting misses. The nurse is usually the first and often the only person able to complete such a report properly, and a program that hides its adverse events eventually loses both its credibility and its community.

Training is a nursing function even where the nurse never gives a tablet, and the quality of that training sets the ceiling on the campaign. Before a round, the nurse teaches the distributors how to greet a household, how to explain the purpose of treatment, how to apply the exclusion rules, how to read the dose pole and measure weight, how to record every person treated, refused or excluded, how to manage a mild reaction and when to send someone to the health center. The teaching uses the materials of the national program and the local language, and it is repeated rather than delivered once, because volunteers change and memory fades between rounds. Supervision continues throughout the campaign, as the nurse observes, corrects gently, answers questions and confirms that each team has enough tablets and enough light to write by. The nurse also protects the volunteers themselves, because a distributor blamed for a reaction that was unavoidable will not return for the next round.

A campaign is a social event before it is a clinical one, and trust is its currency. Rumors about infertility, about poison and about a hidden agenda spread quickly, and they are answered best by a known local person who listens first and then speaks plainly. Permission must be sought from community leaders and the purpose explained in open meetings, but permission is not the same as consent, and every person retains the right to refuse. Ethical programs explain the purpose, the possible reactions and the alternatives, do not pressure people, do not tie treatment to the receipt of food or other services, and record refusals without punishment. Where a serious risk is known, as with ivermectin in an area of heavy loiasis, the duty to avoid harm outweighs the ambition of coverage, and the nurse should say so openly rather than treat hesitation as resistance.

Eligibility rules protect the people in whom the medicine might do harm, and they must be applied without shortcuts. Pregnant women are usually excluded and offered treatment after delivery, and women who might be pregnant are handled according to the national protocol, which varies between countries. Very young children are excluded from some medicines, with the age or weight threshold set by the program and confirmed rather than remembered. People who are acutely ill, and those whose chronic illness is not controlled, are usually deferred until they are stable, and anyone with a history of a severe reaction to the medicine is excluded permanently. In areas where a medicine is given for one disease but is risky for another, the local protocol determines the choice, and people living with HIV who are taking antiretroviral therapy are generally eligible but the current interaction guidance should be checked. Every exclusion is written with a reason so that the next round can find the person, and the excluded person is told when to return.

Monitoring during a campaign has two faces, one measuring the work and the other measuring the effect. The process indicators are coverage, the number treated, the number who refused, the number excluded, the stock consumed and the completeness of the registers, and they tell the supervisor whether the round was delivered as planned. The impact indicators are prevalence and intensity of infection, antigen prevalence in children, egg counts in stool or urine and the detection of new cases, and they tell the program whether the parasite is actually retreating. Programs also monitor the quality and potency of the donated medicines by sampling them in the field, because a substandard tablet wastes a round and destroys confidence. Therapeutic efficacy is watched as well, since a falling egg reduction rate after albendazole or a poor response in a treated person may be an early sign of reduced drug effectiveness. The nurse who records accurately at the point of care is supplying the raw material for every one of these judgments.

The decision to stop treatment is not the end of the program but the beginning of a different kind of work. After several annual rounds, the program conducts a transmission assessment survey in which children born after the campaigns began are tested for the parasite antigen, and if very few or none are positive, transmission is judged to have been interrupted and mass treatment may stop. Mosquitoes are collected and tested for parasite DNA, an approach called xenomonitoring, to look for the parasite in the vector when the human survey is reassuring but not conclusive. For onchocerciasis the evidence comes from the blackfly and from children, since antibody in a young child shows that transmission is still occurring. For schistosomiasis the snails themselves are examined, and for trachoma and yaws repeated surveys of prevalence and of new cases take the place of the antigen test. Each of these methods is a way of asking the same question, which is whether the parasite is still finding new hosts.

Surveillance after treatment stops must continue for years, and sometimes for decades, because the last infections are the ones nobody sees. In lymphatic filariasis a second assessment several years after the first confirms that the parasite has not returned, and in onchocerciasis the watch is longer still, because adult worms live for more than 10 years and can resume producing microfilariae long after the final round. A single imported case or a small hidden focus can restart transmission, and the surveillance system must be able to find it while it is still small and containable. Programs therefore keep their registers, their trained distributors and their laboratory links alive during the quiet years, even when the visible disease has disappeared from the clinic. The endgame is not a campaign at all but a standing watching brief, and it is the least dramatic and most easily abandoned part of elimination.

The words control, elimination and eradication describe different promises, and a program is judged by which promise it made. Control means reducing the burden of a disease to a level the health system can manage and keeping it there, with treatment or another intervention continuing indefinitely. Elimination means reducing new locally acquired cases to zero in a defined area, with continued measures to stop the disease from being reintroduced. Eradication means reducing new cases to zero everywhere in the world, permanently, so that intervention can stop altogether. Only smallpox and rinderpest, a disease of cattle, have been eradicated, and Guinea worm is close but unfinished, partly because the worm has found a reservoir in dogs. A program that uses the strongest of these words to describe the weakest of these achievements misleads both its funders and its community.

Even the word elimination needs a qualifier, because there are two different goals hidden inside it. Trachoma and lymphatic filariasis are eliminated as a public health problem when the prevalence of the disease and of its complications falls below defined thresholds, even though the organism may still be present at very low levels. The World Health Organization uses the word validation for this kind of achievement, and the proof rests on surveys repeated over years. Onchocerciasis and, in some countries, schistosomiasis aim at the stronger goal of elimination of transmission, in which no new infections occur and the evidence must come from vector and child surveillance as well as from patients. Verification and certification are reserved for the formal, independent confirmation of elimination of transmission and of eradication respectively. The distinction is not pedantry, because the two goals require different budgets, different surveillance and different timelines.

Certification is a formal and independent judgment, and it is deliberately difficult to obtain. For Guinea worm, an international commission reviews the evidence country by country and certifies a country as free of transmission only after years without an indigenous case and after surveillance has been shown to work. For onchocerciasis, the World Health Organization verifies the elimination of transmission through a documented dossier and expert review, and for lymphatic filariasis it validates the elimination of the disease as a public health problem through its own process. For yaws, eradication requires that the world be free of the disease and that surveillance be able to prove it, which is one reason the target is so demanding. The nurse may never sit on such a committee, but the data the nurse records are the raw material of the decision, and inaccurate registers can delay a country's case by years.

The current frame for all of this work is the World Health Organization road map for neglected tropical diseases for the years 2021 to 2030. Its headline goals are a 90 per cent reduction in the number of people needing treatment for these diseases, a 75 per cent reduction in the disability-adjusted life years they cause, the elimination of at least one neglected tropical disease in 100 countries, and 2 eradications, Guinea worm and yaws. Lymphatic filariasis, onchocerciasis, schistosomiasis and trachoma are each targeted for elimination as a public health problem, or in some settings for elimination of transmission, by 2030. Guinea worm is the closest to eradication, with fewer than 20 human cases reported worldwide in recent years, down from millions in the 1980s. The road map follows the London Declaration of 2012 and the Kigali Declaration of 2022, in which governments, donors, manufacturers and international organizations pledged to sustain the effort for a decade at a time.

Progress is real and it is fragile, and the threats to it are mostly not biological. Armed conflict and displacement scatter the populations that campaigns are built to reach, and they destroy the registers and the supply chains on which coverage depends. Funding gaps and the diversion of health workers to other emergencies interrupt rounds, and the pause that occurred during the pandemic of the coronavirus disease of 2019 showed how quickly a parasite recovers when treatment stops. Climate change is shifting the range of the mosquitoes, blackflies and snails that carry these infections, and rapid urbanization is creating new habitats and new concentrations of susceptible people. Complacency is a quieter threat, because a disease that is no longer visible stops attracting money, and the last cases are always the most expensive to find. Reduced drug effectiveness is watched closely, particularly in the worms treated repeatedly with albendazole and in the bacteria exposed to repeated azithromycin, and it is one reason that coverage and dose accuracy are treated as resistance issues as well as delivery issues.

Mass treatment also carries a stewardship obligation that belongs in every training session. Giving an antibiotic to a whole population selects for resistance in the bacteria that are not the target, and repeated azithromycin campaigns for trachoma and yaws are monitored for exactly that reason. This is a serious concern but it is not a reason to withhold treatment where blindness and disfiguring disease are common, and the honest position is that the benefit and the risk must both be measured and reported. The nurse should record and report treatment failures and unexpected lack of response, because those are the earliest signals of reduced efficacy. Medicines must also be stored and dispensed legitimately, and leftover tablets must never be diverted into informal markets, where casual use breeds resistance and harms patients.

Mass treatment is also only one component of a program, and it must be paired with the work that prevents reinfection. The control of the soil-transmitted helminths and schistosomiasis cannot be sustained by tablets alone, because people are reinfected when they drink contaminated water, wade in infected lakes or walk barefoot, so treatment is paired with latrines, safe water, hygiene education and shoes. Lymphatic filariasis programs pair repeated treatment with limb hygiene, skin care and hydrocele surgery, and trachoma programs pair azithromycin with facial cleanliness and environmental improvement. The nurse who understands the full package can explain to a community why the tablets alone will not be enough and why a latrine, a clean face and a pair of shoes are part of the cure rather than a distraction from it. A program that delivers only the medicine may lower the burden for a season and will watch it return.

As prevalence falls, the cost of finding each remaining case rises, and the last mile is the hardest part of any elimination effort. A campaign that once treated a million people for a few cents each must later map, train, distribute and report in a handful of remote villages with the same apparatus and a smaller budget. Programs must decide when to stop mass treatment and move to targeted treatment and surveillance, and that decision has to rest on survey evidence rather than on the calendar or on convenience. Donor fatigue and competing health priorities threaten the endgame precisely when the investment is closest to paying off. The nurse who has kept good records, who knows the missing households and who has earned the community's trust is often the person who makes the final phase possible at all.

The tools themselves continue to change, and a chapter like this one is a snapshot rather than a settlement. A longer-acting drug against the worms of onchocerciasis is being introduced, a child-friendly formulation of praziquantel has made treatment of preschool children practical, and better point-of-care tests for loiasis are reducing the guesswork in co-endemic areas. Research continues into new medicines that kill the adult worms rather than only their offspring, and into vaccines against schistosomiasis and leishmaniasis, though none is yet part of a routine campaign. Diagnostics for trachoma and yaws are being simplified so that surveillance can be done closer to the village. The nurse should expect the protocols to change and should treat each new recommendation as something to verify rather than something to assume.

The nurse stands at the center of this entire field, and the role is broader than any single task. Training and supervising community distributors, measuring and verifying every dose, monitoring adverse events and completing pharmacovigilance reports, engaging the community and answering its fears, managing stock and records, applying the exclusion rules and protecting the people they exclude, and explaining why the second round matters as much as the first are all nursing work. The nurse is the bridge between the national protocol and the household doorway, and the strength of that bridge decides whether a round of treatment actually protects a community. That work is unglamorous, it is repetitive, and it is the work that ends diseases.

To summarize, mass drug administration, which the World Health Organization also calls preventive chemotherapy, treats whole eligible populations rather than diagnosed individuals in order to reduce the reservoir of infection below the level that sustains transmission. Its principal targets are lymphatic filariasis, onchocerciasis, schistosomiasis, the soil-transmitted helminths, trachoma and yaws, and the medicines are few and well established, including diethylcarbamazine, ivermectin, albendazole, mebendazole, praziquantel and azithromycin, each given at a defined dose by mouth or by injection and repeated on a schedule set by the current national protocol. Success depends on coverage above a biological threshold, on community-directed distributors, schools and community health workers, on reliable logistics and honest records, and on reaching the households that refuse or are absent year after year. The gravest safety problem is ivermectin in areas where loiasis is also transmitted, where a heavy microfilarial load can lead to encephalopathy, and the ordinary reactions of treatment must be anticipated, explained, managed supportively and reported. Control, elimination and eradication are different promises, and validation, verification and certification are the formal processes that confirm them, with surveillance continuing long after treatment stops. The nurse carries the training, the supervision, the dose verification, the adverse event monitoring and the community trust on which every one of these programs ultimately rests. Because regimens are revised, because guidance differs between countries and because the current guideline must always be checked before prescribing or dispensing, the practitioner who verifies the protocol at every round is the one who practices safely.
