# Chapter 9 — Malaria

Malaria is the disease that tropical medicine is built around, and it remains a leading cause of death from infection in African children. A protozoan parasite of the genus Plasmodium causes it, a female Anopheles mosquito transmits it, and it can move from headache to coma within 24 hours. It is preventable, diagnosable, and curable, yet it still kills hundreds of thousands of people every year. This chapter follows the parasite from the salivary gland to the bedside, using guidance from the World Health Organization, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control, and treating every dose as current guidance rather than permanent truth.

Five species of Plasmodium cause almost all human malaria, and each has its own habits, geography, and clinical signature. Plasmodium falciparum is the most dangerous and the most common in Africa, and it causes nearly every death. Plasmodium vivax is the most widespread, dominant across much of Asia and Latin America, and able to hide in the liver and cause repeated relapses. Plasmodium ovale is uncommon and rarely severe, and Plasmodium malariae is the rarest and can persist in the blood for years. Plasmodium knowlesi comes from macaque monkeys in Southeast Asia and can multiply fast enough to become severe.

Plasmodium falciparum dominates the mortality figures because of what it does to the blood vessel rather than at first contact. Its infected red cells grow sticky knobs that make them adhere to the lining of small vessels in the brain, the gut, the kidney, the lung, and the placenta. This process, called sequestration, hides the parasites from the spleen and lets them consume the local oxygen, so the microcirculation is obstructed and the tissue becomes acidotic. The other species mostly stay in circulation and cause disease by destroying red cells, which is why falciparum produces cerebral malaria and organ failure while the others rarely do.

The life cycle begins when a female Anopheles mosquito, feeding mostly between dusk and dawn, injects saliva containing sporozoites into the skin. The sporozoites reach the liver within minutes and enter liver cells, where the parasite grows into a schizont that divides without cell division. After roughly 1 to 2 weeks the schizont ruptures the liver cell and releases thousands of merozoites, and the interval between the bite and that release is the prepatent period. In Plasmodium vivax and Plasmodium ovale, a fraction of the sporozoites become hypnozoites, dormant liver forms that reactivate weeks to months later and cause a relapse without any new bite.

Once merozoites enter red cells, the parasite repeats a cycle of growth and division lasting about 48 hours for most species and 72 hours for Plasmodium malariae. The red cell becomes a trophozoite, then a schizont, and finally bursts, releasing a new generation of merozoites with fragments of parasite and red cell debris. That burst is what the body recognizes as a fever, which explains why the temperature may rise in a regular rhythm. Some merozoites become gametocytes, the sexual forms taken up by the next mosquito, and in falciparum malaria the infected cells may never circulate freely, so the peripheral count may understate the true burden.

Each antimalarial drug acts at one point in the life cycle and is blind to the others. Chloroquine concentrates in the parasite's digestive vacuole and blocks the detoxification of heme, so the parasite is poisoned by its own waste, but that mechanism fails wherever resistance has fixed itself. The antifolate drugs sulfadoxine with pyrimethamine block 2 steps in the parasite's folate pathway, atovaquone disrupts its mitochondrial electron transport, and doxycycline acts on its residual plastid, which is why antibiotics are slow and useful mainly for prevention. Primaquine and tafenoquine act on the dormant liver forms and the gametocytes, so a complete treatment is often 2 drugs rather than 1.

The incubation period from an infective bite to the first fever is usually 7 to 30 days for Plasmodium falciparum, and it can be much longer after partial prevention or infection with a species that has dormant liver forms. The classic teaching that malaria produces a fever every 48 or 72 hours is true only in the minority of patients with synchronized cycles and no prior immunity. In practice the fever is often continuous, irregular, or absent, and a patient may present with headache, malaise, nausea, and body aches rather than a dramatic rigor. Anyone who waits for the textbook pattern will miss cases, so fever after exposure in a malarious area is malaria until the films prove otherwise.

Uncomplicated malaria is defined by what it lacks rather than by what it has. The patient has fever or a history of fever, often with chills, sweats, headache, muscle and joint pain, nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea, and the illness may be indistinguishable from influenza. The examination may show pallor, a palpable spleen, mild jaundice, or nothing at all, and there is no impaired consciousness, no respiratory distress, no bleeding, no shock, and no evidence of organ dysfunction. Because the patient can take medication by mouth, outpatient treatment is possible when someone reliable can observe the doses and the patient can return if things worsen.

The nonspecific nature of uncomplicated malaria is why clinicians in areas of high transmission no longer treat every fever as malaria without a test. Decades of presumptive treatment produced wasted drugs, missed diagnoses of pneumonia and meningitis, and pressure that selected for resistance. The World Health Organization now asks for confirmation by a rapid diagnostic test or by microscopy before treatment, except where no test is possible and suspicion is high. A returning traveler with fever presents the opposite problem, because there the test is always available and the clinician must still remember that malaria can be present.

Severe malaria is a medical emergency, and it is defined by criteria that describe what the parasite has already done to the body. Children and adults fail differently, so a child may present with convulsions, profound anemia, hypoglycemia, and acidosis, while an adult may present with renal failure, pulmonary edema, jaundice, and impaired consciousness. Any 1 of the criteria is enough to make the diagnosis and to change treatment from a tablet by mouth to a drug given intravenously. Several criteria together raise the risk of death sharply, and the trajectory over the first hours matters more than the initial number.

Impaired consciousness is the criterion most people associate with severe malaria, and cerebral malaria is its most feared expression. A patient who cannot localize a painful stimulus, who is drowsy and difficult to rouse, or who has had a generalized seizure may have parasites sequestered in the cerebral microcirculation. In children the diagnosis is supported by a Blantyre coma score below the normal range, and in adults by a Glasgow coma scale score below it, together with a blood film that shows the parasite. Seizures may appear only as twitching of a limb or deviation of the eyes, and reversible causes such as hypoglycemia and meningitis must be excluded at the bedside.

Acidosis is a powerful predictor of death in severe malaria, and it usually signals impaired tissue perfusion and increased anaerobic metabolism rather than a single organ failure. The patient breathes deeply and rapidly, often without obvious lung disease, and the bicarbonate and lactate measurements confirm the picture. In children, deep breathing is one of the most reliable bedside signs of severe disease and one of the easiest to overlook as anxiety or pneumonia. The acidosis is worsened by anemia, low blood volume, hypoglycemia, and the parasite's own consumption of glucose and oxygen, so the response is to correct those causes, give oxygen, and restore the circulation rather than to give bicarbonate.

Hypoglycemia is one of the most common and most reversible causes of deterioration in severe malaria. The parasite consumes glucose, the liver makes less of it, the patient may not be eating, and quinine stimulates the pancreas to release insulin, and young children and pregnant women are at particular risk. Sweating, tremor, and fast heart rate may be absent, so a low reading must be treated at once with an intravenous bolus of glucose followed by a continuous infusion and repeated measurement. A nurse who measures glucose on arrival, after any treatment that stimulates insulin, and at regular intervals prevents a large share of the deaths on the ward.

Severe anemia in malaria comes from the destruction of infected and uninfected red cells, from suppression of the bone marrow, and from repeated infection. The threshold differs by age and is more strict in young children, and a child below it may need a transfusion even before other criteria appear. Pallor of the conjunctivae, the palms, and the tongue is a useful bedside sign, and a rapid hemoglobin measurement should be obtained whenever it is available. The anemia develops over days, so the nurse watches the trend rather than a single number and weighs the severity against the real risks of blood that is not screened well.

Renal impairment in severe malaria ranges from a modest rise in creatinine to complete absence of urine, and it is more common in adults than in children. The mechanisms include reduced blood flow to the kidney, sequestration of parasites in the renal microcirculation, and the release of free hemoglobin when red cells are destroyed inside the vessels. Blackwater fever is the dramatic form, with massive destruction of red cells, dark urine, jaundice, and acute kidney injury, and it has been associated with quinine and with repeated infection. Dialysis is often needed when available, so the nurse watches the urine output hourly and reports a fall before the creatinine rises.

Pulmonary edema in severe malaria usually follows increased capillary permeability and fluid overload rather than heart failure, and it can appear even in a young patient. The patient becomes breathless and short of oxygen, the chest examination reveals crackles, and treatment is oxygen, careful fluid restriction, and ventilatory support where it exists. Bleeding from the gums, the nose, the gut, or the sites of venipuncture reflects disseminated intravascular coagulation and a low platelet count. Shock with cold peripheries and a prolonged capillary refill is a late and ominous finding, because too much fluid in severe malaria is as dangerous as too little.

A high parasite density is itself a criterion of severe disease, usually defined as more than 5 per cent of red cells parasitized or a count above the accepted threshold in each microliter, and it should be read with the clinical picture rather than in isolation. Prostration, meaning the patient cannot sit or walk unaided, counts as severe even when consciousness is preserved, and jaundice, hemoglobinuria, a high lactate level, and a low urine output each add weight. In pregnancy, severe malaria carries additional risk to the fetus, so any pregnant woman with malaria deserves rapid assessment and treatment. A patient who meets no criterion on arrival can still deteriorate, so the reassessment is as important as the initial classification.

The diagnosis rests on demonstrating the parasite in the blood, and the thick and thin films remain the reference method when they are done well. The thick film concentrates the parasites and is used to detect them and estimate the density, while the thin film shows the species and the stage. The films are stained with Giemsa, and the quality of the stain, the thickness of the smear, and the reader's experience matter more than the laboratory's equipment. A falling density over 24 to 48 hours is reassuring, while a rising density demands reassessment of the drug, the dose, and the diagnosis, and a single negative film does not exclude malaria, so films should be repeated over 48 to 72 hours when suspicion remains.

The rapid diagnostic test has changed malaria care where microscopy is not available, and it detects a parasite protein in a drop of blood within minutes. Most of these tests detect histidine-rich protein 2, which Plasmodium falciparum produces, and some detect a second enzyme that identifies the other species. The test is easy to use and needs no electricity, but it stays positive for days to weeks after a treated infection has cleared, so a positive result in someone treated recently does not prove a new episode. Some parasites have lost the ability to make histidine-rich protein 2, and these deletions produce a false negative in a patient who is genuinely infected, so a negative rapid test in a severely ill patient must be followed by microscopy.

The World Health Organization and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention publish guidance on classification and treatment, and that guidance is revised as resistance patterns and drug availability change. Regimens differ between countries and between age groups, and no chapter can substitute for the current national guideline, so the doses here are current guidance rather than a permanent standard. National programs adapt the recommendations of the World Health Organization to local resistance and local supply, so verify every dose against the current guideline and the product label before it is given. The nurse is often the last person who can catch a decimal error, a wrong weight, or a drug unsafe in pregnancy, and asking a second clinician to check unusual arithmetic is the standard of care.

Uncomplicated falciparum malaria is treated with an artemisinin-based combination therapy, meaning a fast-acting artemisinin derivative paired with a slower partner drug that clears the parasites the artemisinin leaves behind. The pairing is deliberate, because the short-acting component kills most parasites quickly while the partner eliminates the residual ones, which reduces the chance that resistant parasites survive. The common combinations include artemether with lumefantrine, artesunate with amodiaquine, dihydroartemisinin with piperaquine, and artesunate with mefloquine or with sulfadoxine and pyrimethamine, and every regimen is given for 3 days. Artemether with lumefantrine is the most widely used, given as 6 doses over 3 days, and an adult typically takes 4 tablets per dose, each containing 20 milligrams of artemether and 120 milligrams of lumefantrine, with the pediatric dose calculated by weight.

Two practical points determine whether the treatment works, and both belong to the nurse. The first is food, because artemether with lumefantrine is absorbed much better with fat, such as milk, breast milk, or a fatty meal, so tablets taken on an empty stomach may fail even with perfect adherence. The second is vomiting, because any dose vomited within 30 minutes should be repeated in full, and a dose vomited later may need repeating as well, since an unabsorbed dose equals no dose. The full 3 days must be completed even after the fever settles, because stopping early selects for the parasites that were hardest to kill.

Non-falciparum malaria is treated differently because the aim includes preventing relapse as well as curing the current illness. Chloroquine remains effective for Plasmodium vivax in most places, given as a total dose of 25 milligrams of base per kilogram over 3 days, although resistance rules it out in parts of Oceania and Southeast Asia, and Plasmodium ovale and Plasmodium malariae also respond. For Plasmodium vivax and Plasmodium ovale, primaquine is added to kill the dormant liver forms, usually 0.25 to 0.5 milligrams per kilogram by mouth once a day for 14 days, while tafenoquine offers a single dose of 300 milligrams for adults. Both drugs can destroy red cells dangerously in a person with glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency, so the enzyme status must be checked before either is given.

Severe malaria is treated with a drug given intravenously, and the drug of choice is artesunate. The World Health Organization recommends 2.4 milligrams per kilogram given intravenously at the start, again after 12 hours, again after 24 hours, and then once every 24 hours until the patient can take an oral artemisinin-based combination therapy. Quinine is the alternative where artesunate is not available, given as a loading dose of 20 milligrams per kilogram infused over 4 hours and then 10 milligrams per kilogram every 8 hours, and it demands close attention to hypoglycemia and the heart rhythm. For a child who cannot reach a clinic in time, a single rectal dose of artesunate at 10 milligrams per kilogram before referral reduces the risk of death and disability, and that pre-referral treatment is one of the most important practical advances in the field.

Once the patient can swallow and the parasite density is falling, treatment is completed with a full course of an artemisinin-based combination therapy by mouth. Artesunate is generally well tolerated, but it can cause a delayed destruction of red cells 1 to 3 weeks after treatment, when the cells that were once infected are removed by the recovering immune system. Any patient treated with artesunate who becomes anemic weeks later should have a hemoglobin measurement and a reticulocyte count, because the cause is often missed. The nurse also watches for the return of fever, a falling urine output, and fluid overload, and follow-up after discharge matters because the delayed complications declare themselves after the acute illness.

Resistance is the force that keeps rewriting malaria treatment, and its history is a lesson in why combination therapy matters. Chloroquine was the mainstay of treatment and prevention for decades, and it was lost first in Southeast Asia in the middle of the twentieth century and then across Africa in the last 2 decades of that century, while sulfadoxine with pyrimethamine followed the same path. Artemisinin partial resistance, seen first in the Greater Mekong region early in the present century, does not abolish the drug but slows the clearance of parasites, so the partner drug must finish the job. When the partner also fails, treatment failure becomes common, and partial resistance has now been detected in parts of East Africa.

Chemoprophylaxis is for people who are not immune and are going to a place where malaria is transmitted, and it works only when the right drug is chosen and taken as prescribed. The choice depends on the destination and its resistance pattern, the length of the trip, and the traveler's medical history, pregnancy status, and age. Mefloquine, 250 milligrams by mouth once a week, suits long stays but is avoided in people with a history of psychiatric illness or seizures. Doxycycline, 100 milligrams by mouth once a day, is effective and inexpensive but can cause sensitivity to sunlight, and it must be continued for 4 weeks after leaving the area. Atovaquone with proguanil, 1 tablet by mouth once a day, needs only 7 days of continuation but is expensive, and chloroquine is used only where the parasite remains fully sensitive.

The failure of chemoprophylaxis is almost always a failure of adherence rather than of the drug, because travelers forget doses, stop early when they leave the area where malaria is transmitted, or never start. The continuation after departure is the part most often abandoned, and it is exactly the part that prevents the illness appearing weeks later. Tafenoquine, taken as 200 milligrams by mouth once a day for 3 days before travel and then once a week, offers a simpler schedule for some adults, but it carries the same glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase caution as primaquine. Mefloquine has been linked to effects on mood and sleep, which are uncommon but serious, and the nurse's role in the pre-travel visit is to make the schedule concrete for the traveler.

Malaria control is not a single intervention but a package, and drugs alone will never eliminate it. Insecticide-treated bed nets reduce bites during the hours when Anopheles mosquitoes feed, through both a physical barrier and an insecticidal effect. Indoor residual spraying kills mosquitoes that rest on the walls of a dwelling after feeding, and larval source management removes or treats the standing water where the mosquito breeds. Seasonal chemoprevention in children during the months of highest transmission, and intermittent preventive treatment in pregnancy, reduce illness in the groups at greatest risk. The malaria vaccines now recommended for children in high transmission areas add a further layer, though they reduce illness rather than prevent infection.

Pregnancy changes malaria in both directions, and the guidance must be applied with the trimester in mind. The placenta concentrates parasites, and the result is maternal anemia, impaired fetal growth, premature delivery, and stillbirth, often without the fever that would bring a patient to care. In the first trimester, uncomplicated infection is usually treated with an artemisinin-based combination therapy, because the risk of untreated malaria outweighs the concern about the drug. In the second and third trimesters, the same combinations are used, and intermittent preventive treatment with sulfadoxine and pyrimethamine is given at scheduled antenatal visits in areas of stable transmission. Severe malaria in pregnancy is treated with intravenous artesunate at the standard dose regardless of trimester, and prevention with an insecticide-treated bed net begins before conception where possible.

Infants and young children carry the greatest burden of severe malaria, and their treatment is a matter of arithmetic as much as pharmacology. Doses are calculated by weight, and the dispersible pediatric formulations of the artemisinin-based combinations have made accurate dosing much easier than splitting adult tablets. A child's dose of artemether with lumefantrine is based on a weight band and dispersed in a small amount of water, and children lose fluid and become hypoglycemic quickly and may convulse before any other sign appears. Malaria in the newborn is uncommon but occurs, transmitted from an infected mother, so a child who has been treated and remains febrile needs a repeat film, a glucose measurement, and a careful search for another cause.

Two words describe 2 different returns of malaria, and confusing them leads to the wrong treatment. Recrudescence is the reappearance of parasites from the same blood-stage infection that was never fully cleared, and it usually appears within weeks and reflects treatment failure, an inadequate dose, or resistance. Relapse is the reactivation of dormant liver forms, and it occurs only with Plasmodium vivax and Plasmodium ovale, often months after the original illness and after a period with no parasites at all. Recrudescence is managed by reviewing the dose and the drug, while relapse requires primaquine or tafenoquine to kill the hypnozoites, and reinfection is a third possibility that is common where transmission continues.

The nursing care of a patient with malaria is organized around a small number of things that determine survival, and they are glucose, fluid, drug timing, temperature, and neurological status. Each of them can deteriorate silently, and each can be corrected by a nurse who is watching. The ward may be crowded, the monitors few, and the laboratory far away, but these 5 observations need only a glucose meter, a clock, a thermometer, a fluid balance chart, and a working pen. Written down and handed over, they become the patient's continuity of care.

Glucose monitoring is the first nursing priority because hypoglycemia is common, dangerous, and reversible. Measure the blood glucose on arrival in every patient with severe malaria, then at intervals determined by the patient's condition, and always after a dose of quinine or a period without food. In children, check it whenever the child becomes drowsy, convulses, or stops feeding, because those signs may be hypoglycemia rather than cerebral malaria. Treat a low reading at once with an intravenous bolus of glucose, then start a continuous infusion and recheck within the interval your protocol specifies, and never assume a normal reading will stay normal.

Fluid balance in severe malaria is a narrow path between dehydration and overload, because the patient is often short of fluid from fever and poor intake while the capillary leak of severe infection means too much fluid collects in the lungs. Give fluids to restore the circulation, using a balanced crystalloid, then reassess the perfusion, the breathing rate, and the urine output before giving more. Measure the urine output hourly and report a fall below the expected rate at once, because the kidney is often the first organ to declare that the circulation is inadequate. Keep a strict intake and output chart, because the nurse who notices crackles at the lung bases after a bolus has prevented pulmonary edema.

Drug timing is a nursing responsibility in severe malaria, because the schedule for artesunate is based on hours and a missed dose is measured in parasite growth. Record the exact time of every dose, keep the intervals as prescribed, and prepare each dose freshly according to the instructions. Temperature control matters for comfort and metabolic demand, so use paracetamol for fever, avoid aspirin in children, and use tepid sponging when the temperature is high. Watch for the adverse effects of each drug, including delayed red cell destruction with artesunate, hypoglycemia and ringing in the ears with quinine, effects on mood and sleep with mefloquine, and sensitivity to sunlight with doxycycline, and report suspected reactions through the national system for monitoring drug safety.

Neurological checks turn the observation chart into an early warning system. Record the level of consciousness with a formal score, examine the pupils, check for stiffness of the neck, and look for weakness on one side at every shift and after every seizure. A seizure may be the only sign of cerebral malaria in a child, and it may be brief and easy to miss, so a nurse who is present when it happens has made the diagnosis. Protect the airway during a seizure, position the patient on the side, give oxygen, check the glucose, and give the prescribed anticonvulsant, then document the event because a patient whose consciousness deepens needs immediate reassessment.

The logistics of malaria care decide outcomes as much as the pharmacology does, and the nurse is usually the person who manages them. Stock must be counted, expiry dates checked, and a supply of artesunate and of the oral combination kept where severely ill patients arrive, because a stockout at the wrong moment is a clinical event. The cold chain matters for some products and not others, so know which drugs need refrigeration and protect them accordingly. Referral requires a plan covering who decides, who transports, what treatment is given before departure, and what documentation travels with the patient, and rectal artesunate given before referral is one of the most effective links in that chain.

Community work closes the loop, and it belongs to nursing practice as much as bedside care does. Teaching a household how to hang and use an insecticide-treated bed net, when to seek care for a fever, and why a full course of tablets must be finished prevents more malaria than any single prescription. Follow-up after treatment catches recrudescence and relapse, while campaigns of seasonal chemoprevention and visits for intermittent preventive treatment depend on nurses to screen and record the doses. Reporting cases to the surveillance system is a professional duty, because the national program cannot respond to a rise it cannot see, and these unglamorous tasks decide whether a program works.

To summarize, malaria is a protozoan infection transmitted by the female Anopheles mosquito and caused by 5 species of Plasmodium, of which Plasmodium falciparum causes nearly all the deaths. The life cycle moves from the salivary gland to the liver, then to the red cells, then to the gametocytes that return to the mosquito, and each class of drug acts at a different point in that sequence. Fever is unreliable in its pattern, so any compatible exposure requires a blood film or a rapid diagnostic test, and severe malaria is defined by impaired consciousness, acidosis, hypoglycemia, severe anemia, renal impairment, pulmonary edema, bleeding, shock, and high parasite density. Uncomplicated falciparum malaria is treated with an artemisinin-based combination therapy for 3 days, and severe malaria is treated with intravenous artesunate, with rectal artesunate given before referral when a child cannot reach care in time. Chloroquine has been lost across most of the world and artemisinin partial resistance is established in Southeast Asia, so regimens must be checked against current national guidance rather than remembered. Prevention rests on chemoprophylaxis for travelers, insecticide-treated bed nets, indoor residual spraying, and chemoprevention in the groups at highest risk, and the nursing priorities are glucose, fluids, drug timing, temperature, and neurological checks. The nurse who performs those checks is the reason most patients with severe malaria survive.
