# Chapter 18 — Intestinal Protozoa

The intestinal protozoa are single-celled parasites that live in the human gut, and together they cause a large share of the chronic and recurrent diarrheal illness seen in tropical practice. They are not bacteria, so antibacterial drugs do nothing for them, and they are not worms, so the medicines that empty the bowel of helminths leave them untouched. What unites them is a life cycle built around a tough cyst or oocyst that survives in water, on food, and on hands long enough to reach a new host, together with a growing form that multiplies in the intestine and produces the symptoms. Some are cosmopolitan and turn up in childcare centers, swimming pools, and municipal water systems in wealthy countries, while others cluster where water is unsafe and sanitation is poor. This chapter works through them in the order a clinician is most likely to meet them, amoebae first, then Giardia, then the coccidia and the microsporidia, and it closes with the laboratory work, the treatment you must verify, and the nursing care that usually decides the outcome.

Amoebiasis is caused by Entamoeba histolytica, whose name means tissue-destroying amoeba and describes exactly what it does. The parasite exists in two forms, a motile feeding trophozoite that lives in the colon and a round cyst that is passed in the stool and is the infectious stage. Infection follows the fecal-oral route, when cysts from contaminated water, food, or hands are swallowed, and as few as 10 cysts can establish an infection. In endemic areas most infections are asymptomatic, which is why the burden of infection is far larger than the burden of disease. The World Health Organization estimates that invasive amoebiasis causes tens of millions of illnesses and tens of thousands of deaths every year, nearly all of them preventable. It remains one of the most serious parasitic infections of the human intestine, and it is also one of the most treatable when it is recognized. The gap between those two facts is almost entirely a matter of access to clean water, a working laboratory, and a clinician who thinks of it.

Ingested cysts pass through the stomach, excyst in the small intestine, and release trophozoites that travel to the colon and multiply by simple division. There they live on the mucosal surface, feeding on bacteria and debris, and as they move toward the descending colon they encyst and are passed in formed stool. Trophozoites do not survive long outside the body, so they are not an important source of transmission, whereas cysts survive days to weeks in cool, moist conditions. Invasion happens when trophozoites breach the mucus layer, adhere to the epithelial cells, and release enzymes that dissolve tissue, producing the characteristic flask-shaped ulcers of amoebic colitis. Not every strain and not every host reaches that point, and the decisive variable is the species, because Entamoeba dispar looks identical under the microscope but never invades tissue and never causes disease. Distinguishing the two reliably requires antigen testing or molecular testing by PCR, since morphology alone cannot separate them. This single fact explains why a report that says only that amoebae were seen is not yet a diagnosis.

Several other amoebae and flagellates live harmlessly in the human intestine, and recognizing them matters more than treating them. Entamoeba coli, Endolimax nana, Iodamoeba buetschlii, and Entamoeba hartmanni are common commensals, especially where sanitation is poor, and their presence is a marker of fecal exposure rather than a disease. Entamoeba coli cysts contain up to 8 nuclei, while Entamoeba histolytica cysts contain up to 4, and that difference on a stained smear is often the only thing standing between a correct report and an unnecessary prescription. Blastocystis species, discussed later, belong to the same gray zone of uncertain pathogenicity. Treating every organism seen on a stool report is a familiar and costly error, because it exposes patients to drugs they do not need, wastes scarce medicines, and can delay the real diagnosis sitting behind the finding. The rule to carry into practice is that you treat a patient with symptoms, not a laboratory result on a page. When a report names an organism you do not recognize, the correct next step is to ask the laboratory what it means, not to reach for a prescription pad.

Amoebic colitis usually begins 1 to 4 weeks after the cysts were swallowed, and it starts gradually rather than explosively. The patient reports cramping abdominal pain, often in the lower abdomen, with tenesmus, urgency, and a growing number of small stools that contain blood, mucus, and little fecal material. Fever is absent or low, which distinguishes it from the bacillary dysenteries that produce a similar picture with higher fever and more systemic illness. The diarrhea can persist for weeks with remissions and relapses, and a chronic form exists in which pain, flatulence, and altered bowel habit dominate without visible blood. Severe disease occurs in a minority and includes fulminant colitis with widespread ulceration, toxic megacolon, and perforation with peritonitis, all of which carry high mortality and require surgical assessment. A mass of inflamed tissue and granulation called an ameboma can form in the colon and be mistaken for cancer on imaging. Because the presentation overlaps with inflammatory bowel disease, giving corticosteroids to a patient whose colitis is amoebic can precipitate fulminant disease, so the stool studies must be completed before immunosuppression is started. This is one of the few places in tropical medicine where a delay of a day or two in an unproven diagnosis is safer than a rapid assumption.

Amebic liver abscess develops when trophozoites enter the portal circulation and settle in the liver, and it can appear without any preceding diarrhea. It typically presents 2 to 4 weeks after infection but sometimes months later, with fever, rigors, night sweats, weight loss, and a dull pain in the right upper abdomen that worsens on deep breathing. The liver is enlarged and tender, and the patient may have a dry cough or right shoulder pain from irritation of the diaphragm. Most abscesses are single and in the right lobe, and they are filled with a thick reddish-brown material rather than pus, which is why the old description of anchovy paste is still heard. Mild jaundice is common and deep jaundice is not, so a very high bilirubin should prompt you to look for another cause. Rupture into the chest or abdomen is the feared complication, and it presents as sudden severe pain, breathlessness, or shock. Imaging with ultrasound or computed tomography shows the lesion, while serology usually confirms the cause. A patient with fever, right upper quadrant pain, and a liver lesion who has lived in or traveled to an endemic area should be treated for amebic abscess until proven otherwise.

The diagnosis of amoebiasis rests on finding the parasite, but the stool examination has to be requested correctly. Trophozoites are fragile and degenerate quickly, so a fresh specimen or a promptly preserved one is essential, and at least 3 stool samples collected on separate days are needed before a negative result means anything. A stained smear, with iron hematoxylin or trichrome, is far better than a wet mount for identifying trophozoites, and the single most useful finding is a trophozoite that has ingested red blood cells, which no harmless amoeba does. Antigen detection in stool is more sensitive than microscopy, and molecular testing by PCR is the most sensitive of all where it is available. Serology is a valuable adjunct, because antibody is present in roughly 70 per cent of patients with amoebic colitis and in more than 90 per cent of those with liver abscess, but it is often negative in asymptomatic cyst passers and it can remain positive for years after a cured infection, so a positive result does not prove active disease. For suspected liver abscess, ultrasound and computed tomography localize the lesion and guide aspiration, and antibody testing supports the diagnosis. Repeated negative stool studies in a patient with continuing bloody diarrhea should push you toward colonoscopy and biopsy rather than another blind course of treatment.

Treatment of invasive amoebiasis is a two-step sequence, and understanding why there are two steps prevents the most common therapeutic mistake. The first step is a tissue-active drug that kills trophozoites in the bowel wall and the liver, and the second is a luminal agent that clears the cysts remaining in the colon so that the patient does not remain a carrier. Current guidance from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention gives metronidazole at 500 to 750 milligrams by mouth three times a day for 7 to 10 days for invasive disease, or tinidazole at 2 grams by mouth once daily for 3 days. Metronidazole is well absorbed and reaches the liver in high concentration, which is why it works so well for abscess, but that same absorption means little of it reaches the colonic lumen, and that is precisely why the second drug is required. The luminal agent may be paromomycin at 25 to 35 milligrams per kilogram per day by mouth divided into three doses for 7 days, or diloxanide furoate at 500 milligrams by mouth three times a day for 10 days, or iodoquinol at 650 milligrams by mouth three times a day for 20 days. Omitting the luminal step leaves the patient passing cysts and able to infect others, and it also leaves them at risk of relapse. Large liver abscesses, those at risk of rupture, or those that fail to improve within about a week may need ultrasound-guided aspiration, which is both diagnostic and therapeutic. An asymptomatic person passing Entamoeba histolytica cysts still needs a luminal agent, because carriage is the mechanism by which the disease reaches the next household.

Before you prescribe any of these regimens, verify them against current guidance, because they are revised as evidence accumulates and they differ between countries, programs, and product labels. Amoebiasis treatment is a good example, since diloxanide furoate is not marketed in the United States, tinidazole availability varies, and pediatric regimens are always weight-based and must be calculated rather than approximated. The World Health Organization, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control publish recommendations that do not always agree, and a national program may follow a different algorithm again. No dose should be given from memory, from an old chart, or from a colleague's recollection, and the current guideline, the current formulary, and the current product label must be reviewed before the first dose. Document the source you followed and the weight you used, so that the next clinician can see the reasoning. This is not bureaucratic caution; it is the difference between treatment and harm, and in a field setting it is also how you detect an error before the pharmacy or the patient does.

The adverse effects of these drugs are mostly predictable, and knowing them lets you prepare the patient instead of alarming them. Metronidazole commonly causes nausea, a metallic taste, headache, and dark urine, and it produces a disulfiram-like reaction with alcohol, so patients must avoid alcohol during treatment and for 3 days afterward. Prolonged use can cause peripheral neuropathy, which is one reason the course is kept short, and it interacts with warfarin and with some anticonvulsants. Tinidazole shares these effects and has a longer half-life, so a single-dose course can cause symptoms that last more than a day. Paromomycin is poorly absorbed, so systemic toxicity is uncommon, but it can cause diarrhea, abdominal pain, and rarely kidney injury or hearing loss, and it should be used cautiously in renal impairment. Iodoquinol can cause optic neuritis, peripheral neuropathy, and thyroid dysfunction when given for long periods. Monitoring after treatment means confirming that symptoms have resolved, repeating stool studies at an appropriate interval, checking liver enzymes in liver abscess, and asking directly whether the patient completed the second, luminal drug, because that is the step most often abandoned once the diarrhea stops.

Giardiasis is caused by Giardia duodenalis, a flagellated protozoan also known as Giardia lamblia and Giardia intestinalis, and it is the most commonly reported intestinal parasite in many high-income countries. The cyst is the infectious form, it is passed in stool, and the infecting dose is remarkably low, with as few as 10 cysts able to cause illness. Giardia cysts tolerate routine chlorination better than most bacteria, so municipal water that meets disinfection standards can still transmit infection, and mountain streams, untreated well water, childcare centers, and swimming pools are classic sources. The incubation period is 1 to 3 weeks. Illness begins with watery diarrhea and evolves into stools that are greasy, pale, floating, and foul smelling, the steatorrhea that reflects malabsorption of fat. Bloating, flatulence, belching with a sulfurous taste, nausea, and weight loss are common, and the malabsorption can extend to fat-soluble vitamins and to lactose, so milk worsens symptoms even after the infection has apparently cleared. Many infections resolve on their own within 2 to 6 weeks, but some become chronic, with intermittent symptoms, fatigue, and failure to thrive in children. Because the cyst is so widely distributed, a negative water history does not exclude the diagnosis.

Stool microscopy can identify cysts and trophozoites, but because excretion is intermittent, 3 samples on separate days are needed, and antigen testing or molecular testing by PCR raises the yield substantially. When stool studies remain negative and suspicion is high, duodenal aspirate or a string test can recover trophozoites, though these are rarely necessary in practice. Treatment is straightforward when the right drug is used. Tinidazole at 2 grams by mouth as a single dose is highly effective, metronidazole at 250 milligrams by mouth three times a day for 5 to 7 days is the traditional alternative, and nitazoxanide at 500 milligrams by mouth twice a day for 3 days is a further option. Children receive weight-based doses of the same drugs, and children with repeated failure may need a longer course or a change of agent. Treatment failure is common enough that it should be expected rather than treated as a mystery, and the usual causes are poor adherence, reinfection from a household member or a water source, and genuine drug resistance. Resistance to the nitroimidazole drugs can be managed by repeating with a higher dose, by combining drugs, or by using a different class, and alcohol must be avoided with tinidazole and metronidazole exactly as in the treatment of amoebiasis. Household members with symptoms should be tested and treated, and the water source should be reviewed whenever reinfection is suspected.

Cryptosporidiosis is caused mainly by Cryptosporidium parvum and Cryptosporidium hominis, and its oocyst is the reason the infection is so difficult to control. The oocyst is immediately infectious when passed, it is small enough to pass many filters, and it resists chlorine at the concentrations used in drinking water and swimming pools, so outbreaks have followed municipal water supplies, recreational pools, childcare centers, and contact with calves and lambs. The infectious dose is very low, and person-to-person spread is efficient, which is why outbreaks move quickly through households and institutions. After an incubation of 2 to 10 days, illness begins with profuse watery diarrhea, cramping, nausea, vomiting, and low-grade fever, and in a healthy adult it usually settles within 1 to 2 weeks, though it can relapse. Blood is not usually present and the stool is not inflammatory, so the picture is one of high-volume water loss rather than dysentery. Diagnosis depends on laboratory methods that specifically look for the organism, because routine stool examination often misses it. A modified acid-fast stain, immunofluorescence, antigen testing, and PCR are the tools that find it, and the laboratory should be told that cryptosporidiosis is suspected so that the correct stain is requested. Dehydration and electrolyte loss, not the parasite itself, are what make this illness dangerous in a small child or an older adult.

In a person with advanced HIV, cryptosporidiosis behaves differently and can become a chronic, wasting illness. The diarrhea is prolonged, high in volume, and often associated with malabsorption, and the organism can spread to the biliary tree and the pancreas, causing pain, cholestasis, and occasionally a picture resembling sclerosing cholangitis. Before effective antiretroviral therapy was available, cryptosporidiosis was one of the leading causes of chronic diarrhea and death in people with advanced HIV, and it remains a marker of severe immunodeficiency. The most important treatment is not an antiparasitic drug at all but antiretroviral therapy, which restores immune function and is usually what stops the diarrhea, so it must be started or optimized promptly. Nitazoxanide at 500 milligrams by mouth twice a day for 3 days is used in immunocompetent patients and may shorten the illness, while in advanced HIV its benefit is limited when the helper T lymphocyte count is very low, and some clinicians use a longer course. Supportive care with fluids, electrolyte replacement, and nutritional support carries the patient until immunity recovers. Antimotility drugs can reduce stool volume but should be used with care, and nitazoxanide should be taken with food to improve absorption. Relapse is common if immune recovery does not occur, which is why the helper T lymphocyte count and the viral load belong in the same conversation as the diarrhea.

Cyclospora cayetanensis is a coccidian that causes a prolonged, relapsing diarrheal illness and is increasingly recognized in travelers and in outbreaks linked to imported produce. Its oocyst is not infectious when passed, because it must spend days to weeks in the environment to mature, which means direct person-to-person transmission is unlikely and the infection usually comes from contaminated food or water. Outbreaks have been traced to raspberries, snow peas, cilantro, basil, and other fresh items that are difficult to wash thoroughly. After about a week of incubation, patients develop watery diarrhea, loss of appetite, fatigue, bloating, and weight loss, and without treatment the illness can last for weeks with a waxing and waning course. Fever is uncommon. Diagnosis uses a modified acid-fast stain, and Cyclospora oocysts autofluoresce under ultraviolet microscopy, which is a useful clue when the stain is equivocal, while molecular testing by PCR is increasingly available. Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole is the treatment of choice, given as a tablet containing 160 milligrams of trimethoprim and 800 milligrams of sulfamethoxazole by mouth twice a day for 7 to 10 days, and patients with HIV often need a longer course followed by maintenance suppression. Sulfa allergy is the main obstacle, and it requires an alternative plan made with an expert rather than a guess.

Cystoisospora belli, formerly called Isospora belli, is a related coccidian that produces a similar but generally less explosive illness. It occurs sporadically in tropical and subtropical settings and is an important cause of chronic diarrhea in people with advanced HIV, in whom it can cause severe watery diarrhea, steatorrhea, marked weight loss, and sometimes blood eosinophilia. The oocyst is large and oval and is found with a modified acid-fast stain, and as with the other coccidia the laboratory must be asked to look for it specifically. Treatment follows the same logic as Cyclospora, with trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole given as a tablet containing 160 milligrams of trimethoprim and 800 milligrams of sulfamethoxazole by mouth twice a day for 7 to 10 days. Relapse after treatment is common in immunocompromised patients, so chronic maintenance therapy is often required until immune function improves with antiretroviral therapy. When sulfa cannot be used, pyrimethamine or a fluoroquinolone has been tried with variable success, and expert advice should be sought. Supportive care, nutrition, and management of the underlying immune deficiency are as important as the antiparasitic drug itself.

The microsporidia are a large group of spore-forming organisms now classified as fungi, and two species cause most human intestinal disease: Enterocytozoon bieneusi and Encephalitozoon intestinalis. Their spores are very small, so they are easily missed on routine stool examination, and they require special stains such as chromotrope or calcofluor white, or molecular testing by PCR, to be identified. They cause chronic watery diarrhea, malabsorption, and wasting, chiefly in people with advanced HIV and in transplant recipients, and they can also infect the biliary tract and the sinuses. Management begins with restoring immunity through antiretroviral therapy, which is the intervention most likely to produce lasting resolution. Albendazole at 400 milligrams by mouth twice a day is effective against Encephalitozoon intestinalis, while Enterocytozoon bieneusi responds poorly to albendazole and has been treated with fumagillin or nitazoxanide, with limited and inconsistent results. Nutritional support and management of fluid and electrolyte losses remain central, because the diarrhea itself can be debilitating. Microsporidiosis is uncommon outside the severely immunocompromised, so a diagnosis in an apparently healthy patient should prompt a search for an underlying cause of immune suppression.

Blastocystis species, long called Blastocystis hominis, is the most common intestinal protozoan found in human stool and the most controversial. It is present in a large proportion of healthy people, it exists in many genetically distinct subtypes, and only some have been associated with symptoms, so the evidence that it causes disease remains weak and inconsistent. Patients in whom it is found may have diarrhea, abdominal pain, and flatulence, but they may equally have another cause that has not yet been found. Some clinicians offer a trial of metronidazole, tinidazole, nitazoxanide, or paromomycin when symptoms persist and no other explanation emerges, and some patients improve, but the improvement is difficult to attribute and relapses are frequent. Treating every Blastocystis finding is wrong, because it exposes patients to unnecessary drugs, reinforces the idea that the laboratory report is the diagnosis, and can delay the search for a treatable condition such as celiac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, or a bacterial infection. Dientamoeba fragilis is a related puzzle, because it has no cyst, only a fragile trophozoite, and while it has been linked to diarrhea and abdominal pain, the evidence for treating it is similarly limited. The honest position is that these organisms are common, that their pathogenicity is uncertain, and that decisions should rest on the whole clinical picture rather than on a single line of a report.

Across this whole group, the laboratory work follows a few practical rules, and the nurse often controls whether those rules are met. Collect at least 3 stool specimens on separate days, because parasite excretion fluctuates and a single sample misses a substantial share of infections. Send specimens fresh or properly preserved, since trophozoites disintegrate within hours and a delayed specimen can turn a positive case into a negative report. Ask for the right test rather than a generic stool examination: trichrome or iron hematoxylin staining for amoebae and Giardia, a modified acid-fast stain for the coccidia, and calcofluor white or chromotrope staining for the microsporidia. Antigen tests for Giardia and Cryptosporidium are rapid, sensitive, and useful in field settings, and multiplex molecular panels that detect several parasites at once by PCR are becoming standard where they are affordable. Concentration techniques such as formalin-ethyl acetate improve recovery of cysts and oocysts, and ultraviolet autofluorescence helps identify Cyclospora. A test of cure is worth arranging 2 to 4 weeks after treatment in persistent or high-risk cases, and it should use the same method as the original diagnosis so that the results can be compared. A negative result never fully excludes these infections when the clinical suspicion is strong.

The nursing work in intestinal protozoal disease begins with hydration, and it is more important than any antiparasitic prescription. Assess the patient for dry mucous membranes, reduced skin turgor, sunken eyes, a rapid heart rate, low blood pressure on standing, and reduced urine output, and weigh the patient accurately, because weight change is the most reliable measure of fluid loss over time. Mild and moderate dehydration is corrected with oral rehydration salts given in small, frequent sips, and the nurse should teach the family to prepare and offer the solution rather than assume it will be done. Severe dehydration, persistent vomiting, or an inability to drink requires fluids given intravenously, and in children zinc supplementation reduces the duration and severity of diarrhea. Continue feeding during illness, because withholding food prolongs recovery and worsens malnutrition, and choose simple, well-tolerated foods. After Giardia and other malabsorptive infections, lactose intolerance may persist for weeks, so a temporary reduction in milk and a gradual reintroduction help, and fat-soluble vitamin deficiency may need specific replacement. Monitor electrolytes, especially potassium and magnesium, and watch for hypokalemia in patients with profuse diarrhea, since low potassium can cause weakness and dangerous heart rhythms. Growth monitoring in children and referral to nutrition services turn a single episode of illness into an opportunity to prevent the next one.

Infection control is where intestinal protozoa punish complacency, and the nurse is usually the person who sets the standard. Hand hygiene is the foundation, and when cryptosporidiosis is possible it must be washing with soap and running water rather than an alcohol rub, because alcohol does not reliably kill the oocyst, while alcohol rubs remain useful for most other organisms. Cohorting patients with the same infection, excluding symptomatic children and food handlers from childcare and food preparation until they are clear, and cleaning surfaces and shared equipment with an appropriate disinfectant all interrupt transmission. Water safety is a clinical intervention here: advise boiling, filtering through a filter rated to remove cysts, or using bottled water, and warn patients not to swallow water in pools, lakes, or rivers, especially during an outbreak. Food safety advice matters more than most clinicians realize, since fresh produce that cannot be peeled or cooked is a well-documented route for Cyclospora and Cryptosporidium. In an outbreak, the nurse coordinates specimen collection, keeps a line list, reports to public health authorities promptly, and communicates clearly with families who may be frightened and may not understand why a child cannot return to childcare. Education should be concrete and repeated, because advice about hand washing is followed far more often when it is tied to a specific moment in the day. Healthcare workers themselves must not work while symptomatic, and staff illness should be reported and managed rather than hidden.

Prevention and special populations deserve their own attention, because the same parasite affects different people very differently. Travelers should be advised to drink boiled, filtered, or bottled water, to avoid ice made from uncertain water, to eat food that is cooked and still hot, and to peel fruit themselves, and these measures also protect against the bacterial causes of traveler's diarrhea. In pregnancy, the treatment of amoebiasis and giardiasis requires judgment, because some clinicians prefer to defer metronidazole in the first trimester and use paromomycin, which is poorly absorbed, while others treat symptomatic disease promptly because the illness itself threatens the pregnancy, and current guidance must be checked before any decision. Children need weight-based doses calculated carefully, and adult tablets must never be split casually to approximate a pediatric dose. In people with HIV, antiretroviral therapy is the definitive management of cryptosporidiosis and microsporidiosis, so adherence support is itself an antiparasitic intervention. Returning travelers and migrants with diarrhea lasting more than 2 weeks should have parasite studies performed before they are labeled as having irritable bowel syndrome, because chronic protozoal infection is easily missed and easily treated. Antimicrobial stewardship applies here as everywhere, since an antiparasitic drug given for a commensal, a colonizer, or a misread report is still an unnecessary drug.

To summarize, the intestinal protozoa are a compact group of parasites with a shared strategy, a resistant cyst or oocyst that travels by water, food, and hands, and a growing form that damages the gut or spreads beyond it. Entamoeba histolytica is the dangerous amoeba, causing bloody colitis and liver abscess that require a tissue-active drug followed by a luminal agent, while Entamoeba dispar and the other harmless amoebae should be left alone. Giardia causes malabsorptive, fatty diarrhea and responds to tinidazole, metronidazole, or nitazoxanide, and treatment failure usually means poor adherence, reinfection, or resistance rather than a wrong diagnosis. Cryptosporidium, Cyclospora, Cystoisospora, and the microsporidia cause prolonged watery diarrhea, resist routine chlorination, and in advanced HIV are managed principally by restoring immunity with antiretroviral therapy alongside targeted antiparasitic treatment. Diagnosis depends on collecting multiple properly handled specimens and asking for the correct stain, antigen test, or molecular test rather than a generic stool examination. The nursing contribution is decisive: assess and correct dehydration, keep the patient fed, monitor electrolytes and weight, teach lactose tolerance, enforce hand washing with soap and water, and coordinate specimen collection, reporting, and family education during an outbreak. Verify every dose against current guidance from the World Health Organization, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, or the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control, because regimens change and countries differ. Treat the patient, not the laboratory report.
