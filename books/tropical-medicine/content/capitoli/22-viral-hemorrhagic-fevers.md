# Chapter 22 — Viral Hemorrhagic Fevers

Viral hemorrhagic fevers are a group of illnesses caused by four unrelated families of viruses that produce a similar clinical picture. The filoviruses include Ebola and Marburg, the arenaviruses include Lassa fever and the New World viruses, the nairoviruses include Crimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever, and the phleboviruses include Rift Valley fever. All can injure small blood vessels, disturb clotting and drive fever toward shock and death within a week or two. Bleeding appears late and is absent in many fatal cases, so it cannot be the sign you wait for. This chapter follows the guidance of the World Health Organization, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control, and it treats every dose as current guidance rather than settled truth.

The filoviruses are long, filamentous viruses whose genetic material is a single strand of ribonucleic acid, and they are among the most lethal pathogens known. Ebola virus disease and Marburg virus disease are the two human illnesses, and Ebola includes the Zaire, Sudan, Bundibugyo and Tai Forest viruses. The natural reservoir is the fruit bat, which carries the virus without illness and sheds it in bodily fluids. Outbreaks begin when a person enters the world of the bat, through mines and caves, through harvesting fruit, or through hunting and butchering infected animals, and non-human primates act as an amplifier rather than as the reservoir.

Once a person is infected, the virus spreads through direct contact with blood, vomit, stool, urine, saliva, sweat, breast milk, semen or tissue. Infectious fluid must reach a break in the skin or a mucous membrane, which is why the person washing the body without gloves is so often infected. Ordinary air does not carry Ebola across a room, but vomiting, diarrhea, suctioning and resuscitation generate sprays that demand fuller protection. The virus survives in body fluids after death, so funeral washing and touching have driven many outbreaks, and sexual transmission from a survivor can occur months after recovery.

The incubation period of Ebola and Marburg disease is 2 to 21 days, and most patients fall ill between 8 and 10 days after exposure. Illness begins abruptly with fever, profound fatigue, muscle pain, headache and sore throat, a picture indistinguishable from malaria, influenza or typhoid fever. Several days later come vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal pain, dehydration and sometimes a rash. Bleeding from the gums, nose, gut or injection sites appears late, usually in the second week and only in a minority of patients. Ask about contact with a case, a funeral, a health facility or an animal, because the history raises the possibility before any test does.

The virus enters through a mucosal surface or broken skin and travels to the lymph nodes, liver, spleen and bone marrow. Its preferred targets are the monocytes, macrophages and dendritic cells, the very cells that should present the infection to the immune system. The virus multiplies inside them, releases inflammatory signals and infects the endothelium lining the blood vessels. Fluid then leaks into the tissues, circulating volume falls and blood pressure drops toward shock. The clotting system is activated and then exhausted, so bleeding reflects a broken clotting system rather than a direct attack on the vessel wall.

Case fatality varies with the virus, the outbreak and the quality of care. The Zaire species of Ebola has killed between 25 and 90 per cent of those infected, the Bundibugyo species has been closer to 25 per cent, and Marburg has ranged from 24 to 88 per cent. Death usually comes between day 6 and day 16 from shock, organ failure or hemorrhage, while a patient who survives the second week usually improves. Bleeding, confusion, low blood pressure, fast breathing and a falling urine output mark a poor prognosis and should prompt the most intensive supportive care available.

Laboratory confirmation rests on the polymerase chain reaction, which detects the genetic material of the virus, and on tests for antigen and for antibodies of the immunoglobulin M and immunoglobulin G classes. The polymerase chain reaction is most reliable from about the third day of illness, while antibodies appear later and may be absent in patients who die quickly. A negative test taken too early does not exclude the diagnosis, and a second sample days later is often needed. Malaria must be excluded in every febrile patient in an endemic area, because the two diseases can coexist and a positive blood film can end the search too soon.

There is no antiviral drug that cures Ebola virus disease, so treatment rests on meticulous supportive care delivered by a trained and protected team. Fluids are given by mouth when the patient can drink and intravenously when they cannot, with the volume adjusted against blood pressure, urine output and signs of overload. Electrolytes, particularly potassium and magnesium, are replaced according to measured losses, oxygen is given for low saturation, and blood products are used for significant bleeding. Two monoclonal antibody products are authorized for Ebola disease caused by the Zaire species, and both are given as a single intravenous infusion. One is ansuvimab at 50 milligrams per kilogram, and the other combines atoltivimab, maftivimab and odesivimab at 50 milligrams of each per kilogram. These products work best when given early, and the guideline must be checked and the dose verified independently by a second clinician, because the figures are revised, differ between countries and age groups, and are easy to misremember under pressure.

Vaccines have changed the shape of Ebola outbreaks. A single dose vaccine built on a weakened vesicular stomatitis virus protects against the Zaire species and is given intramuscularly, usually in ring vaccination around each case. A two dose regimen is available for health workers and responders, with the second dose about 8 weeks after the first, and the vaccine does not protect against Marburg or Lassa fever. Vaccinating the response team, the burial team and the frontline nurses is standard preparedness, and a vaccine left in a broken refrigerator is worthless, so the cold chain and a named person responsible for it are clinical concerns.

Lassa fever is an arenavirus disease carried by the multimammate rat, Mastomys natalensis, which lives in and around houses across much of West Africa. The rat sheds virus in urine and feces, and people are infected when those materials contaminate food, water, utensils or the floor where a child crawls. The disease rises in the dry months, when rats come closer to dwellings, and human to human transmission occurs through blood and body fluids, especially in hospitals where the diagnosis was never considered. Perhaps 80 per cent of infections are mild or silent, so reported cases are only a fraction of the true number.

The severe minority develops fever, sore throat, cough, chest pain, vomiting, diarrhea, facial swelling, bleeding and shock, and death can come within 14 days. Deafness is the signature complication, because the virus damages the hearing nerve and leaves permanent sensorineural hearing loss in roughly one quarter of survivors. Supportive care and infection control are the same as for the filoviruses, and the drug widely used is ribavirin, given by mouth or intravenously. A common oral regimen is a loading dose of 30 milligrams per kilogram, then 15 milligrams per kilogram every six hours for 4 days, then 7.5 milligrams per kilogram every eight hours for 6 days. The intravenous regimen is 30 milligrams per kilogram, then 16 milligrams per kilogram every six hours for 4 days, then 8 milligrams per kilogram every eight hours for 6 days.

The evidence for ribavirin comes largely from a single observational study rather than a large randomized trial, so it is offered as current guidance rather than established certainty, and the current guideline must be checked before it is given. Pregnant women with Lassa fever face a very high risk of death and of losing the fetus, and the virus can be found in the placenta, the amniotic fluid and the newborn. Every survivor needs a follow-up plan, because a hearing loss noticed only when the patient can no longer follow conversation can end a child's schooling and an adult's livelihood. That plan should name the eye and hearing assessments, the mental health review and the person who will pursue the patient if they do not return.

Crimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever is caused by a nairovirus carried by ticks of the genus Hyalomma, and it is the most widely distributed of these diseases, found from Africa through the Balkans and the Middle East to central Asia. The tick lives on livestock, and farmers, shepherds, slaughterhouse workers and veterinarians are exposed through tick bites and through contact with infected animal blood and tissue. Health workers are infected when they care for a patient whose diagnosis was not suspected, and hospital outbreaks have followed needlestick injuries, blood splashes and inadequate protection during procedures. The incubation period is 3 to 7 days after a tick bite and 5 to 13 days after contact with infected blood.

Illness begins with sudden fever, headache, muscle pain, dizziness, neck pain, nausea and vomiting, and a hemorrhagic phase with bleeding from the gums, nose, gut and puncture sites follows. The case fatality has ranged from 10 to 40 per cent. Ribavirin has been used although the evidence for benefit remains uncertain and observational, and treatment is otherwise supportive. Prevention rests on tick avoidance, protective clothing, careful animal handling and prompt recognition of the first hospital case. A vaccine exists only in a few countries, so the nurse who suspects the diagnosis, isolates the patient and reports it without waiting for confirmation is performing the most effective preventive act available.

Rift Valley fever is a phlebovirus transmitted by mosquitoes and also by direct contact with the blood, tissues and fluids of infected animals. An outbreak among people is often announced by a storm of abortions and deaths among sheep, goats and cattle, and some Aedes eggs survive drying to carry the infection into the next rainy season. Most human infections are mild, with fever, headache and muscle pain, but a small proportion develops inflammation of the retina that can leave permanent visual loss, inflammation of the brain, a hemorrhagic form with jaundice and bleeding, or hepatitis with liver failure. There is no specific antiviral treatment for human disease, so control depends on vaccinating livestock, reducing mosquito breeding and protecting the people who handle animals and their products.

The New World arenaviruses cause a set of geographically distinct illnesses in South America, each named for the place where it was first recognized. Junin virus causes Argentine hemorrhagic fever, Machupo virus causes Bolivian hemorrhagic fever, Guanarito virus causes Venezuelan hemorrhagic fever, and Sabia and Chapare viruses have caused smaller outbreaks in Brazil and Bolivia. Each virus has a rodent reservoir, and infection follows the agricultural cycle through inhaled or eaten virus from rodent excreta, or through person to person contact during the hemorrhagic phase. Illness begins after an incubation of 7 to 14 days with fever, headache, muscle pain and dizziness, and it may progress to bleeding, neurological signs and shock, while ribavirin is used in treatment and a live attenuated vaccine against Junin virus is used in Argentina.

The differential diagnosis of a suspected viral hemorrhagic fever is long, and the clinician's task is to consider the dangerous possibilities while treating the likely ones. Malaria remains the most common cause of fever in most of the tropics, and it must be tested for and treated without waiting for any other result. Dengue, yellow fever, typhoid fever, leptospirosis, rickettsial infection, meningococcal disease, viral hepatitis and severe respiratory infections can all present with fever, bleeding or shock. The travel and exposure history is the single most useful tool, and a patient who has fever after travel to an area with active transmission should be isolated while the tests are done, not after the results return.

The case definition published by the national program and the World Health Organization guides the decision, and a suspected case is a clinical category that permits action before confirmation. Screening at the first point of contact protects everyone else in the building, so a sign and a short set of questions about fever, travel and contact should be in place before a patient reaches the waiting room. A suspected case is placed in a single room with its own toilet and hand washing station where one exists, and where single rooms are scarce, patients with the same suspected diagnosis may be cared for together in a designated area. The isolation area should have its own equipment, its own waste route and a defined boundary that visitors and staff cross only with permission. A log at the entrance records who entered, when and what protection was worn, and dedicated staff who do not rotate to the rest of the hospital reduce the chance that an infection travels out on a uniform.

Personal protective equipment is the barrier between the nurse and the virus, and it works only when it is complete, intact and correctly worn. The set includes a fluid-resistant gown or coverall, gloves, a face shield or goggles, a fluid-resistant mask, a head cover and boots or boot covers, and the exact combination depends on the task and the guidance in force. The order of donning matters, and it usually begins with the gown or coverall, then the mask, then the goggles or face shield, then the head cover, then the gloves. A buddy checks every step, looking for a gap at the neck, a torn seam, a mask that does not seal or a glove that has slipped, because the wearer cannot see their own back and neck.

Heat stress is a real hazard, so staff must drink, rest and rotate out of the protective set before exhaustion or fainting causes a breach. Doffing is the most dangerous moment of the shift, because the outside of the equipment is contaminated and the hands must not touch the skin or the clothing beneath. The sequence generally begins with removal of the outer gloves and the outer apron, then the gown or coverall, then the head cover, then the goggles or face shield, then the mask, with hand hygiene at every stage. Each item is removed by rolling or peeling it away from the body, so that the contaminated surface ends up inside. The mask is removed last, by the straps rather than the front, and the hands are washed or rubbed with alcohol based hand rub immediately afterward. Equipment is never reused, never washed for a second wear and never taken home, and every breach, however small, is reported and managed as an exposure the same day.

Waste from the care of a patient with a hemorrhagic fever is infectious, and the rules for it must be simple enough to follow when the unit is busy and the staff are tired. Sharps go into a puncture resistant container that is never filled above the marked line, never recapped by hand and never carried through a crowded corridor. The World Health Organization recommends that sharps and other infectious waste be burned in a high temperature incinerator, and where that is not available, the safe alternatives must be chosen deliberately rather than improvised at the end of a shift. Laundry is bagged at the bedside, never sorted by hand, and injections are kept to the minimum, with an intramuscular injection avoided when an oral or intravenous route will do, and a trained observer should watch the whole doffing sequence and speak only to name the next step.

Safe and dignified burial is one of the most effective interventions in an Ebola outbreak and one of the most difficult to deliver, because it touches grief, religion and community trust at once. The body remains highly infectious, and washing, touching, kissing and dressing the dead are the practices that most reliably transmit the virus to the family. A trained burial team works in full protective equipment, places the body in a leak-proof bag, disinfects the area and limits the number of people who come near. The family should not be excluded but engaged, and where tradition allows, they may watch from a distance, name the deceased, offer prayers and receive the belongings after they have been disinfected. The team should include someone who speaks the local language and can explain each step, because a burial performed without explanation is remembered as a violation and drives the next family to hide a death.

Contact tracing finds the people who have been exposed and watches them through the incubation period, which for Ebola and Marburg is 21 days. A contact is anyone who had physical contact with a suspected or confirmed patient, who touched the body or fluids of a patient, who lived in the same household or who cared for the patient without protection. Each contact is listed by name and address, assigned to a surveillance officer, and visited or telephoned twice a day for temperature and symptoms until the period ends. A contact who develops fever is isolated at once, tested and cared for, and ring vaccination around a confirmed case depends on the same lists that contact tracing produces.

The work demands vehicles, telephones, fuel and a system for recording information, and it fails when the community does not trust the people doing it. Health workers are the highest-risk group in these outbreaks, because they have repeated, close contact with patients who are vomiting and having diarrhea, they perform procedures that generate splashes, and they work long shifts in equipment that is hot and easy to breach. Understaffing multiplies the risk, since one nurse asked to care for more patients than is safe will cut corners on hand hygiene, on waste handling and on the slow, deliberate doffing sequence. An infected health worker can seed an outbreak inside a hospital, terrify the staff, close the facility and destroy the community's willingness to seek care. Every facility should therefore keep a list of isolation staff, monitor them for fever, vaccinate them where a vaccine exists and provide immediate care and compensation if they become ill.

Monitoring a patient with a suspected hemorrhagic fever is nursing work of the highest order, because the disease changes hour by hour and the interventions that save life are adjustments rather than single decisions. Record the temperature, heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, mental state and urine output at a fixed interval, and compare each set with the one before. A rising heart rate with a falling blood pressure and cool hands points to shock before the blood pressure collapses, and a falling urine output is the earliest sign that perfusion is inadequate. Fluid balance must be totaled for every shift, with all intake by mouth and by vein and all urine, vomit and stool recorded, because that arithmetic is the only way to see overload or dehydration developing. In an isolation area the nurse must often decide what can be monitored at the bedside and what can be postponed, and that judgment should be made with the team and written down.

Supportive care is the treatment, and it is delivered largely by nurses. Oral rehydration salts are offered in small, frequent sips to patients who can swallow, and intravenous fluids are used when vomiting, diarrhea or shock prevents oral intake. Potassium and magnesium are replaced when measured losses or laboratory results show the need, with attention to the urine output and the heart rhythm. Fever and pain are treated with acetaminophen, while aspirin and other non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs are avoided because they impair clotting and irritate the stomach. Coexisting malaria must be treated and a bacterial infection must be sought, because a patient who dies of a treatable second disease while the team watches the virus is a preventable death.

Comfort, cleanliness, a cool cloth, a calm voice and honest explanation are not extras; they are the substance of what the nurse provides when there is no cure to give. Every treatment in this chapter also has a cost, and the nurse is the person who sees it first. Ribavirin causes a fall in hemoglobin through the destruction of red cells, so the blood count is monitored and a transfusion is given when the anemia becomes symptomatic or dangerous. Aggressive fluid administration can cause fluid in the lungs and heart failure, so the fluid balance is reviewed with the same care as the dose of any drug. Monoclonal antibody infusions can cause fever, chills, rash, itching, low blood pressure and difficulty in breathing, so the patient is watched closely during the first minutes and the infusion is slowed or stopped if a reaction begins. The protective equipment itself causes heat exhaustion, dehydration, headache and fainting when it is worn too long, and staff must be rotated before those signs appear.

Pregnancy makes these diseases more dangerous for both the woman and the fetus, and it also makes the clinical picture harder to read. Ebola and Marburg infection in pregnancy is associated with a very high rate of death of the mother and of pregnancy loss, and bleeding in pregnancy may be attributed to obstetric causes when the true diagnosis is an infection. Delivery, whether vaginal or by operation, must be conducted with full protection for the team, and the newborn must be cared for as a suspected case until the tests are known. Feeding at the breast is a route of transmission, and the decision about it must balance the risk of infection against the value of breast milk, with current guidance and the mother's wishes taken into account. Children often present with fever, vomiting and diarrhea but may show less obvious bleeding, and every dose for a child is calculated by weight and verified independently.

Orphans and children separated from their families during an outbreak need a named caregiver, a place to stay and someone who explains what is happening, because the measures that protect them can also terrify them. Survivors of these diseases carry consequences that outlast the fever, and many report persistent fatigue, headache, muscle and joint pain, eye pain, sleep disturbance and difficulty concentrating. Inflammation inside the eye, or uveitis, can appear weeks to months after recovery and can cause permanent visual loss if it is not recognized and treated. Hearing loss after Lassa fever and after the other hemorrhagic fevers is often permanent, and it should be managed with hearing aids, sign language or classroom support. The virus can persist in parts of the body the immune system reaches poorly, including the eye, the testes, breast milk and the central nervous system, and relapse with inflammation of the brain has been described months after recovery.

Survivors need a follow-up plan with named appointments, clear instructions about barrier protection with sexual partners and an open door to return if new symptoms appear. Stigma is one of the most damaging consequences of an outbreak, and it is directed at survivors, at their families and at the health workers who cared for them. A survivor may be refused entry to a market, dismissed from work, abandoned by a spouse or feared by neighbors who believe the virus stays in the body forever, and children who lost a parent may be shunned. The answer begins with accurate information given to the community, in the local language, by people it trusts, and it continues with visible reintegration in which leaders, employers and religious figures welcome survivors back. Survivors need practical help with food, housing, schooling and income, and they need mental health support for grief, for survivor guilt and for the fear that the illness will return. Follow-up care should combine the eye examination, the hearing assessment, the mental health review, the sexual health counseling and the management of chronic pain in one place.

Prevention works at the level of the community, the household and the health facility. Avoiding the hunting, butchering and eating of bats and non-human primates removes the most direct route from the reservoir to people, and any animal found dead should be reported rather than handled. Storing food in closed containers, keeping rodents out of the house, trapping them and cleaning surfaces reduces the risk of Lassa fever and of the South American arenaviruses. People who work with livestock should wear protective clothing, use repellents, check themselves for ticks and remove attached ticks with fine tweezers, and livestock should be vaccinated against Rift Valley fever where a program exists. Health facilities need a triage system, a stock of protective equipment, a trained team, a waste route and a plan for safe burial before the first patient arrives, because an outbreak is a bad time to write a protocol.

Community engagement and risk communication must be honest about what is known and what is not, and they must answer the fears that spread faster than the virus, including the fear that the hospital kills people. National and international systems exist to detect these outbreaks early and to stop them, and they depend on clinicians who report suspicion rather than waiting for confirmation. The International Health Regulations require countries to notify the World Health Organization of events that may constitute a public health emergency of international concern, and hemorrhagic fevers are among the events that trigger that obligation. The pillars of a response are case finding, isolation and care, contact tracing, safe burial, infection control, laboratory confirmation, community engagement and logistics, and every one needs people, vehicles, fuel, food, water, communication and a supply chain. Resource allocation can become an ethical problem when beds, vaccines or protective equipment are scarce, so decisions should be made openly by a designated team, with nurses at that table because they see the consequences.

The team itself needs support, because repeated deaths, the fear of infection and the strain of the equipment produce moral distress and exhaustion. Debriefings, rest, mental health care and acknowledgment of what was done well are what allow people to keep working. When death is likely, pain, breathlessness, agitation, thirst and fear can all be relieved, and a dying patient should not be left alone. A nurse who can stay, speak gently and hold a hand provides care that no drug can replace, even through protective equipment that hides the face. Training, drills and a culture in which anyone can stop a procedure to correct a breach are the foundation of a safe unit, and duty of care never means that a nurse must work unprotected.

To summarize, viral hemorrhagic fevers are a group of severe illnesses caused by filoviruses, arenaviruses, nairoviruses and phleboviruses, and they share fever, vascular injury, disordered clotting and a high case fatality. Ebola and Marburg come from the fruit bat, spread through direct contact with blood and body fluids, and cause bleeding only late, so the fever, the exposure history and the contact history are what should raise the alarm. Lassa fever comes from the multimammate rat and leaves deafness in many survivors, Crimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever comes from ticks and infected livestock and spreads inside hospitals, Rift Valley fever follows livestock and mosquitoes, and the New World arenaviruses follow rodents in South America. Treatment begins with supportive care, careful fluids and electrolytes, and disciplined monitoring of a patient who can change within hours, and it now includes monoclonal antibodies for Ebola and ribavirin for Lassa fever and the arenaviral diseases. The nurse's responsibilities are the ones that decide whether an outbreak stays small, and they include correct wearing and removal of protective equipment, safe waste and sharps handling, safe and dignified burial, contact tracing, monitoring and comfort for the patient, and support for survivors who face stigma and long-term harm. Every regimen in this chapter is current guidance rather than settled truth, because it is revised as evidence accumulates, it differs between countries, and the current guideline must be checked, and every dose verified, before any drug, fluid or vaccine is given.
