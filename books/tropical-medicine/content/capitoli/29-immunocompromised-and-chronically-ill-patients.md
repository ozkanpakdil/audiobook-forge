# Chapter 29 — Immunocompromised and Chronically Ill Patients

Immunosuppression changes the rules of infection, and it changes them everywhere in tropical medicine. It changes which organisms are likely, how quickly they move, how loudly they announce themselves, and how much of the ordinary clinical picture you can trust. A patient whose immune defenses are intact usually turns an exposure into either no illness or an illness with recognizable features. A patient whose defenses are damaged can turn the same exposure into disseminated disease with a modest fever and a chest that sounds clear. The advanced practice nurse therefore works from a different set of expectations in this population, and the most dangerous habit is to be reassured by the absence of signs. This chapter describes what immunodeficiency does to the encounter with tropical infection, the specific infections that follow advanced HIV, the chronic illnesses that behave in similar ways, and the practical nursing work of screening, vaccinating, monitoring, and recognizing deterioration early enough to act.

The immune system fails in patterns, and each pattern points to a predictable family of organisms. When the helper T lymphocyte population falls, the body loses its coordinator for organisms that live inside cells, which opens the door to fungi, protozoa, and mycobacteria. When the neutrophil count falls, the body loses its first responder against bacteria and molds, which opens the door to bloodstream infection and invasive fungal disease. When the spleen is absent or no longer functions, the body loses its filter for organisms carrying a polysaccharide capsule, and those organisms can multiply in the blood with startling speed. When the skin and the gut lining are breached by catheters, surgery, or the inflammation of chemotherapy, the barrier that kept the internal environment sterile is gone. Learning this map is not an academic exercise, because it tells you what to look for before any test result returns, and it tells you which prevention belongs in the plan.

The most useful thing to understand is why severe disease can look mild. Inflammation is not the injury itself, it is the response to the injury, and that response requires a working immune system. A person who cannot mount a vigorous inflammatory response may have a normal temperature, a normal white cell count, and unremarkable examination findings while bacteria or fungi are multiplying in the bloodstream. A patient with advanced HIV and cryptococcal infection may complain only of a headache that has been building for two weeks. A patient whose neutrophil count has collapsed after chemotherapy may have nothing more than a feeling of unease and a slight rise in heart rate. The nurse who waits for a high fever, a productive cough, or a purulent wound before escalating care will wait past the point at which treatment changes the outcome.

Because the usual warning signs are unreliable, the reliable ones are trends and function. You watch how the patient looks compared with yesterday, whether they are eating, whether they are walking as far, whether the family says something has changed, and whether the simple measures of circulation and breathing are drifting. A rising respiratory rate, a falling blood pressure, a new confusion, a decreasing urine output, and a temperature that is unexpectedly low all deserve the same attention that a high fever would receive in a healthy adult. Charting those observations at fixed intervals, and acting on a change rather than waiting for the next scheduled round, is the single most valuable nursing contribution in this population. When the numbers are ambiguous, trust the trajectory and trust the person who knows the patient best.

Prevention before suppression is the organizing principle of care for anyone who is about to lose immune function. Chemotherapy, transplantation, high-dose corticosteroids, and the biologic agents that block tumor necrosis factor all create a window of vulnerability, and that window is easiest to influence before it opens. Screening for latent infection, updating vaccination, and treating whatever is found are tasks that must be finished before the suppressive treatment starts, not after. A program that treats this sequence as optional will discover reactivated tuberculosis, disseminated strongyloidiasis, or a hepatitis B flare at the worst possible moment. The nurse who owns this checklist owns the difference between a planned course of treatment and an unplanned catastrophe.

The threadworm Strongyloides stercoralis is the organism that makes this principle concrete, because it is the classic infection that lies quiet for decades and then explodes when immunity falls. The larvae persist in the gut and can complete their life cycle within the host, so infection can continue silently for a lifetime, and it is common across tropical and subtropical regions. When corticosteroids or other suppressive treatment remove immune control, the parasite can accelerate its cycle, producing hyperinfection with massive larval migration through the lungs and the gut, and dissemination to the brain, the liver, and other organs, with Gram-negative bloodstream infection carried on the migrating larvae. The mortality of the disseminated form is high even with treatment, which is why screening is mandatory rather than optional in anyone from an endemic area who is about to be immunosuppressed.

Screening for Strongyloides stercoralis combines a blood test for antibodies with repeated stool examination, and neither test is perfect. Antibody testing is sensitive but can be positive from past exposure and can be falsely negative in people whose immune response is already blunted, while stool microscopy is specific but misses infection when the larval output is low. Where the organism is suspected and the patient is about to be immunosuppressed, many programs treat presumptively rather than wait for a perfect result, because the treatment is safer than the disease. The usual regimen is ivermectin at 200 micrograms per kilogram by mouth once daily for 2 days, with albendazole at 400 milligrams by mouth twice a day for 7 days as an alternative where ivermectin is unavailable, and a follow-up test several weeks later confirms the response. In hyperinfection the treatment is prolonged and repeated, and expert advice should be sought. Because these regimens are revised as evidence accumulates and national guidance differs between countries, the current guideline must be checked before prescribing.

Hepatitis B belongs in the same pre-suppression conversation, because the virus can flare violently when immune control is withdrawn. Anyone facing chemotherapy, transplantation, or a biologic agent should be tested before treatment begins, and those who carry the virus should receive antiviral suppression that continues through the period of risk and for a defined interval afterward. Tuberculosis screening is the other routine step, using symptoms, a chest radiograph, and an interferon gamma release assay or a tuberculin skin test, with preventive treatment given before suppression where infection is documented and active disease has been excluded. Vaccination should also be updated before the window closes, because the response to a vaccine is far better while the immune system is still intact. The practical order is screen, treat, and vaccinate, and only then proceed with the treatment that suppresses.

Advanced HIV remains the most common and most instructive form of acquired immunodeficiency in tropical practice, and the specific infections that appear as the helper T lymphocyte count falls are the core curriculum of this chapter. The count is not a number to file away, it is a map of risk. As the count falls below about 200 cells per microliter, Pneumocystis pneumonia and toxoplasmosis become the concerns. Below about 100 cells per microliter, cryptococcal meningitis and disseminated histoplasmosis move to the front. Below about 50 cells per microliter, the risk of these infections and of chronic cryptosporidiosis is highest. Antiretroviral therapy changes all of this, because restoring immunity prevents most of these infections, and the nurse who pushes for early treatment and sustained adherence is doing opportunistic infection prevention with every visit.

Cryptococcal meningitis is the infection that kills more people with advanced HIV than any other in many tropical settings, and it is deceptive in its ordinariness. The fungus Cryptococcus neoformans is inhaled from the environment, often from soil contaminated by bird droppings, and in a person with advanced immunodeficiency it spreads from the lung to the membranes around the brain. The usual presentation is a headache that has developed over 1 to 2 weeks, sometimes with fever, neck stiffness, confusion, nausea, or visual disturbance, and sometimes with almost nothing else. Diagnosis rests on a lumbar puncture with measurement of the opening pressure and on testing the cerebrospinal fluid with a cryptococcal antigen test, which is rapid and highly sensitive, supported by culture and staining where available. Because the pressure inside the head can rise dangerously, and because that pressure causes much of the blindness and death, repeated lumbar punctures to drain fluid are a treatment in their own right and not merely a diagnostic step.

Treatment of cryptococcal meningitis follows an induction phase, a consolidation phase, and a maintenance phase, and the World Health Organization has revised the preferred regimen in recent years. The current recommendation favors a single high dose of liposomal amphotericin B at 10 milligrams per kilogram intravenously together with flucytosine at 25 milligrams per kilogram by mouth four times a day for one week, followed by fluconazole at 400 to 800 milligrams by mouth daily, although older guidance used liposomal amphotericin B at 3 to 4 milligrams per kilogram intravenously daily for two weeks and many programs still follow it. Where flucytosine is unavailable, amphotericin B is combined with fluconazole. After induction, fluconazole continues at 400 milligrams daily for consolidation and then at 200 milligrams daily as long-term maintenance until immunity recovers. Liposomal amphotericin B damages the kidneys and lowers potassium and magnesium, and flucytosine suppresses the bone marrow, so kidney function, electrolytes, and blood counts must be monitored closely. Antiretroviral therapy is usually delayed by several weeks in this infection, because starting it too early increases death, and the exact interval is set by the current guideline.

Pneumocystis pneumonia is the other classic AIDS-defining infection, caused by the fungus Pneumocystis jirovecii, and it presents with a dry cough, breathlessness on exertion, and a slow decline over days to weeks. The chest radiograph may look normal early, and the oxygen level can fall sharply with exercise before it falls at rest, which is why a walking oxygen measurement is more informative than a resting one. Diagnosis is often clinical in a patient with advanced immunodeficiency and a compatible picture, and where a laboratory is available the organism can be found by PCR or by special staining of induced sputum or of fluid obtained by washing the lung. Treatment is trimethoprim with sulfamethoxazole at 15 to 20 milligrams per kilogram per day of the trimethoprim component, divided and given by mouth or intravenously every six to eight hours for three weeks, and moderate to severe disease also receives a corticosteroid such as prednisone at 40 milligrams by mouth twice a day for 5 days and then tapered, because the inflammatory response to dying organisms can worsen breathing. Adverse effects include rash, fever, low white cell counts, high potassium, and kidney injury, and alternative drugs are used when the reaction is severe. Prophylaxis with the same combination at a lower dose prevents the disease and is continued until immune recovery is secure.

Toxoplasmosis, caused by the protozoan parasite Toxoplasma gondii, is usually a reactivation of a latent infection acquired years earlier from undercooked meat, from soil, or from cat feces. In advanced HIV it presents as encephalitis with headache, fever, confusion, seizures, or weakness on one side of the body, and imaging shows multiple rounded lesions with swelling around them in the deep parts of the brain. Treatment is pyrimethamine given as a loading dose of 200 milligrams by mouth and then 50 to 75 milligrams daily, combined with sulfadiazine at 1000 to 1500 milligrams by mouth every six hours, and leucovorin at 10 to 25 milligrams by mouth daily is added to protect the bone marrow. Where sulfadiazine cannot be used, trimethoprim with sulfamethoxazole is a common alternative. The response is judged clinically and by repeated imaging after 2 to 3 weeks, and treatment is then continued at a lower maintenance dose until immunity recovers. Prophylaxis is indicated for patients with advanced immunodeficiency who carry antibodies to the parasite.

Tuberculosis is the leading cause of death among people living with HIV, and it behaves differently as immunity falls, as the preceding chapter describes in detail. In this chapter the emphasis falls on the nursing tasks around it. Every person with advanced HIV should be screened for tuberculosis symptoms at every visit, and the absence of a cough does not exclude disease. Preventive treatment for latent infection is offered where active disease has been excluded, and the choice of regimen depends on the national guideline and on interactions with antiretroviral therapy. The immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome, the paradoxical worsening that can follow the restoration of immunity, must be anticipated, because it can look like treatment failure when it is in fact a sign that the immune system has returned. Monitoring must include weight, symptoms, and adherence, and the nurse should confirm which medicines the patient is actually taking, because rifampicin and the antiretroviral drugs interact strongly.

Disseminated histoplasmosis is the fungal infection most often mistaken for tuberculosis in tropical practice, and it is caused by Histoplasma capsulatum, acquired by inhaling spores from soil enriched by bird or bat droppings. In advanced HIV it produces prolonged fever, weight loss, an enlarged liver and spleen, low blood counts, and sometimes skin or mouth lesions, while the chest radiograph may be unremarkable. Diagnosis is by antigen testing of blood or urine where available, by culture, or by examination of a bone marrow or tissue sample, and it is frequently missed because it is not considered. Treatment begins with liposomal amphotericin B intravenously for severe disease, followed by itraconazole at 200 milligrams by mouth three times a day for 3 days and then 200 milligrams twice a day for at least a year in the setting of advanced immunodeficiency. Itraconazole absorption depends on stomach acid, so it is taken with food, and its blood level should be measured where possible, because low levels lead to relapse and high levels to toxicity. It also interacts with many other medicines, so the whole medication list must be reviewed before it is started.

Chronic cryptosporidiosis is a protozoal infection of the gut that causes watery diarrhea, cramps, and malabsorption, and in a person with advanced HIV it can persist for weeks or months and waste the patient. It is acquired from contaminated water and food, and it occurs wherever sanitation is fragile. There is no reliably effective antimicrobial cure, though nitazoxanide at 500 milligrams by mouth twice a day for 3 days is often given and may shorten the illness in patients with better preserved immunity, and paromomycin is sometimes used. The decisive treatment is the restoration of immunity with antiretroviral therapy, because the diarrhea often resolves when the helper T lymphocyte count recovers. In the meantime the nursing work is fluid and electrolyte replacement, nutritional support, skin care to protect against excoriation, and the practical hygiene teaching that prevents reinfection within a household. Handwashing with soap, safe drinking water, and careful food handling are the measures that matter most.

The transplant recipient who travels is a distinct patient with a distinct set of questions, and the pre-travel consultation is where most of the risk is managed. Immunosuppressive drugs such as tacrolimus and mycophenolate weaken the response to infection and also to vaccination, so the timing of every vaccine must be planned around the regimen. Live vaccines are generally avoided after transplantation, while inactivated vaccines are safe but may produce a weaker response, and household members should be fully vaccinated to reduce the risk they bring home. Food and water precautions matter more than they do for other travelers, because organisms such as Salmonella, Cryptosporidium, and Listeria cause far more severe disease in this group. Malaria prevention must be chosen with attention to drug interactions, since some antimalarial drugs alter the levels of the immunosuppressive medicines and can threaten the graft, and the choice must be made with the transplant team rather than from a standard travel list. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control both publish guidance for the immunocompromised traveler, and that guidance is revised regularly, so the current version must be consulted before advice is given.

Screening before transplantation reaches beyond the recipient to the donor and to the geography of the infection. Donors and recipients are tested for hepatitis B, hepatitis C, HIV, syphilis, and other transmissible infections, and in tropical settings the assessment also considers Chagas disease, strongyloidiasis, and tuberculosis, because these can be transmitted with the organ or reactivated by the drugs that follow. A recipient who has lived in a region where Chagas disease is endemic should be tested for antibodies to Trypanosoma cruzi, the parasite that causes it, because immunosuppression can reactivate a silent infection into fever, heart involvement, and skin lesions. Strongyloidiasis must be excluded in anyone with past residence in an endemic area, as described earlier, and tuberculosis must be screened for in every candidate. The nurse's part in this work is to take a careful geographic and exposure history, to ensure the tests are actually done and the results acted upon, and to record the plan so that it survives the transfer between teams.

Chemotherapy and corticosteroids suppress immunity in ways that are predictable and therefore partly preventable. A course of prednisone at 20 milligrams or more daily for more than two weeks, or repeated pulses of high-dose corticosteroid, is enough to raise the risk of opportunistic infection, and the risk rises further with age, malnutrition, and diabetes. Chemotherapy that lowers the neutrophil count opens a short but dangerous window in which fever is an emergency, and bacterial infection can progress to shock within hours. Prophylaxis against Pneumocystis pneumonia is standard for many regimens, and antifungal and antiviral prophylaxis is added according to the protocol and the patient's serology. Fever during neutropenia requires blood cultures and broad-spectrum antibiotics within the first hour, without waiting for the results, because delay costs lives. The nurse who monitors the white cell count, teaches the patient to take a temperature at home, and makes sure the emergency plan is written down does more than any single drug.

The biologic agents that block tumor necrosis factor deserve their own warning, because they are among the strongest reactivators of tuberculosis known. These medicines are used for inflammatory bowel disease, rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis, and other conditions that are increasingly common in tropical countries, and they can convert a lifetime of quiet infection into active disease within weeks. Screening for tuberculosis must therefore be completed before the first dose, and preventive treatment must be started and often finished before the biologic agent is given where the infection is documented. The same caution applies to hepatitis B, to histoplasmosis in endemic areas, and to strongyloidiasis, and vaccination should be completed before the regimen begins where possible. The nurse should treat the biologic prescription as a trigger for the entire pre-suppression checklist, not as an isolated decision made by another specialist. Communication between the prescribing team and the primary care team is the mechanism that prevents the gap.

Diabetes is the chronic illness that most changes the course of tropical infection, and its effect is both immunological and mechanical. High blood glucose impairs the function of neutrophils and the migration of immune cells, reduces blood flow through small vessels, and provides a ready fuel supply for organisms, and nerve damage hides the early warning signs in the feet. Tuberculosis is several times more common in people with diabetes, and it is more likely to be severe, to relapse, and to be accompanied by drug resistance. Melioidosis, the infection caused by the bacterium Burkholderia pseudomallei, is strongly associated with diabetes and with exposure to soil and water in Southeast Asia and northern Australia, and it can present as pneumonia, bloodstream infection, or abscesses in many organs, especially during the rainy season. Necrotizing skin and soft tissue infection is another danger, often polymicrobial, spreading faster than the skin changes suggest, and it requires surgical debridement as an emergency. The nurse's contribution is glucose control, careful foot examination at every visit, education about footwear and skin care, and a low threshold for urgent referral when an ulcer looks worse than it should.

Melioidosis is worth describing in detail because its treatment is long and its relapse rate is high when it is cut short. The presentation can imitate tuberculosis, typhoid, or severe pneumonia, and the organism is resistant to many common antibiotics, so the diagnosis depends on the laboratory growing it from blood, sputum, urine, or pus, and on the clinician having considered it. The intensive phase usually uses ceftazidime at 50 milligrams per kilogram intravenously every six hours, or meropenem at 25 milligrams per kilogram intravenously every eight hours for severe disease, for at least 2 weeks and until the patient has clearly improved. The eradication phase then uses trimethoprim with sulfamethoxazole by mouth for 3 to 6 months, because the organism can hide inside cells and return if treatment is stopped too early. The World Health Organization and national programs in endemic countries publish the current regimens for melioidosis, and because these differ between settings and are revised as resistance patterns change, the current guideline must be checked before prescribing. The nurse should prepare the patient for months of treatment, monitor kidney function and blood counts, and explain that feeling well is not the same as being cured.

Chronic kidney disease changes drug handling long before it is severe, and it also changes the risk of infection itself. As kidney function falls, the clearance of many drugs falls with it, so a dose that is safe for one patient becomes toxic for another, and the correct dose depends on the measured kidney function rather than on habit or on weight alone. Some medicines should be avoided altogether, including nitrofurantoin, high-dose trimethoprim with sulfamethoxazole, aminoglycosides, and amphotericin B where an alternative exists, and others such as metformin, tenofovir, and many antibiotics require dose reduction or a longer interval between doses. Dialysis adds another layer, because the timing of a dose relative to the dialysis session changes the blood level that the patient actually experiences, and the medication chart must record that timing explicitly. Kidney disease also blunts the response to vaccination and increases the severity of many infections, so prevention carries extra weight. The practical rule is to check every prescription against the current formulary and the measured kidney function, and to involve a pharmacist wherever one is available.

Sickle cell disease creates a specific and lifelong vulnerability to infection that is easy to forget between crises. The spleen in sickle cell disease is damaged by repeated sickling and often stops working early in childhood, so the patient loses the filtering function that removes encapsulated bacteria from the blood, and severe infection becomes a leading cause of death. The organisms that matter most are Streptococcus pneumoniae, Neisseria meningitidis, Haemophilus influenzae type b, and Salmonella, and infection with Salmonella is a recognized cause of the bone infection that these patients develop. Infection with parvovirus B19 can produce a sudden aplastic crisis with a rapid fall in hemoglobin that requires transfusion, and malaria is more severe and more likely to cause death, although the sickle cell trait offers partial protection while the disease itself does not. Acute chest syndrome, a dangerous combination of infection, infarction, and breathing failure, must be considered whenever a patient with sickle cell disease has fever, chest pain, or breathlessness. For the nurse, fever in a patient with sickle cell disease is an emergency that requires immediate assessment and antibiotics rather than an appointment later in the week.

Asplenia, whether from surgery, trauma, or a congenital absence, concentrates the risk in a small and predictable group of organisms. The spleen filters the blood, removes organisms that carry a polysaccharide capsule, and produces antibodies against them, so a person without a functioning spleen is vulnerable to overwhelming infection with Streptococcus pneumoniae, Neisseria meningitidis, and Haemophilus influenzae type b, which can become fatal within a day. Prevention begins with vaccination against those organisms, ideally at least 2 weeks before a planned splenectomy and with boosters afterward, and with daily prophylactic penicillin at 250 milligrams by mouth twice a day in children and in some adults, according to the current guideline. Patients should carry a card or a medical alert record stating that they have no spleen, so that any clinician meeting them in an emergency understands the risk immediately. Fever in an asplenic patient is never routine, and it requires urgent assessment and empirical antibiotics, because the illness can be overwhelming before a diagnosis is made. Travel advice should also include the higher risk of severe malaria, the danger of animal bites, which can transmit a bacterium that causes fulminant sepsis in this group, and the need to seek care early wherever they are.

Vaccination before suppression is a time-limited opportunity, and it has its own logic. The best response to a vaccine comes when the immune system is intact, so the target is to complete the routine and travel schedule at least 2 weeks before chemotherapy, transplant, or biologic treatment begins, whenever the clinical situation allows. Live vaccines, including yellow fever, measles, mumps, rubella, varicella, and oral typhoid, are generally avoided once immunosuppression is established, because a weakened vaccine organism can cause disease in a patient who cannot control it, and they are also avoided in people with advanced HIV. Inactivated and subunit vaccines are safe, but the response may be poor, so the schedule may need additional doses, higher doses in some cases, or revaccination after immune recovery. Household members should be fully vaccinated, both to protect themselves and to reduce the risk of bringing an infection home, and this is especially important for influenza. BCG vaccination is given at birth in many countries but is avoided in children with symptomatic HIV, because of the risk of disseminated infection, and the decision belongs to the national program. The nurse should hold the vaccination plan alongside the treatment plan, because a vaccine given too late is a missed opportunity that cannot be recovered.

Monitoring in this population has to be deliberate, because the usual symptoms will not call for help. A practical plan names what is measured, how often, and what result triggers action, and it is written where the whole team can see it. Blood counts, kidney function, liver enzymes, blood glucose, and the relevant drug levels are checked at the intervals the national guideline specifies, and therapeutic drug monitoring is used for medicines such as itraconazole and some antiretroviral and antituberculous drugs where it is available. Weight, appetite, and functional status are recorded at every visit, because a decline in any of them is often the first sign of an infection that the patient cannot describe. Adherence is assessed by asking how the medicines are actually being taken rather than by asking whether they are being taken, and a missed dose should lead to a search for the reason. The nurse should also watch for the adverse effects of the treatment itself, because kidney injury, bone marrow suppression, rash, and drug interactions are common and are frequently mistaken for the underlying disease.

Logistics decide whether any of this reaches the patient, and the nurse is usually the person who makes the supply chain work at the point of care. Many of these drugs are expensive, some require refrigeration, and some are subject to stockouts, so the patient's plan must be built with a realistic view of what is available this month and next. Liposomal amphotericin B must be stored and transported correctly, flucytosine and itraconazole may be scarce, and a course that is interrupted by an empty shelf can lead to relapse and resistance. Transport is another constraint, because a patient who must travel a day to reach the clinic cannot return for weekly monitoring, so follow-up must be arranged closer to home, with a community health worker, a local clinic, and a written plan that travels with the patient. Referral letters should state the diagnosis, the current medicines and doses, the monitoring results, and the specific question being asked, because a referral without that information usually produces a delay. Documentation is also a clinical instrument in this population, since the patient may be seen by several teams, and a clear record is what prevents the same test being repeated and the same interaction being missed.

The nursing role in all of this can be stated in three parts, and each part is ordinary work rather than heroism. The first is screening before suppression, which means taking the geographic and exposure history seriously, ordering the tests that the checklist requires, and following up until the results are acted upon. The second is vaccinating before suppression, which means protecting the window of intact immunity and coordinating the schedule with the treatment rather than after it. The third is recognizing that a patient with damaged immunity may have severe disease with few signs, which means trusting trends, function, and family observation over the reassuring absence of fever. Around those three parts sits the work of explanation, adherence support, monitoring, and coordination that determines whether the plan is actually followed. None of it requires a laboratory that a tropical clinic does not have, and most of it requires only attention and consistency.

Caring for the chronically ill and the immunosuppressed also means caring for the person who carries the burden. A patient on lifelong suppressive treatment, repeated clinic visits, and a long list of medicines can lose hope, and hopelessness shows up as missed appointments and stopped treatment. Stigma adds to this, whether it concerns HIV, a transplant, or a diagnosis that the neighbors can recognize, and the nurse shapes whether the clinic feels safe by the words used at the desk and the privacy of the consultation. Depression and anxiety are common in this population and they reduce adherence, so they should be asked about and treated rather than noted and left. Practical support, such as a treatment supporter chosen by the patient, a pill organiser, and a plan for what to do when a dose is missed, is often more effective than encouragement alone. A patient who understands why each medicine matters and what happens if it stops is far more likely to persist, and that understanding is built in conversation, not in a leaflet.

To summarize, immunosuppression changes which infections occur, how severe they become, and how little they may show on examination, so the nurse must work from an expectation of severe disease with few signs. The pattern of immune failure predicts the organisms, with helper T lymphocyte loss opening the door to fungi, protozoa, and mycobacteria, neutrophil loss to bacteria and molds, and loss of splenic function to encapsulated bacteria. The pre-suppression checklist of screening, treating, and vaccinating must be completed before chemotherapy, transplantation, corticosteroids, or biologic agents begin, and Strongyloides stercoralis is the organism that makes that rule unforgettable. Advanced HIV brings cryptococcal meningitis, Pneumocystis pneumonia, toxoplasmosis, histoplasmosis, tuberculosis, and chronic cryptosporidiosis, each with its own treatment, monitoring, and adverse effects. Diabetes, chronic kidney disease, sickle cell disease, and asplenia each add their own vulnerability and their own nursing work, and the transplant recipient who travels needs a plan built around drug interactions and vaccine timing. Because regimens are revised and guidance differs between countries, every dose in this chapter must be verified against the current national guideline and the World Health Organization, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, or the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control before it is prescribed. The nurse who screens before suppression, vaccinates before suppression, and watches the trend rather than the fever is the person who keeps this patient alive.
