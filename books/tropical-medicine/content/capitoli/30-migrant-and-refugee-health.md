# Chapter 30 — Migrant and Refugee Health

Migrant and refugee health is not a specialty at the edge of tropical medicine. It sits at its center, because the movement of people is one of the most powerful forces that distributes infection across the world. The nurse who works in a refugee camp, a border clinic, an urban community health center, or a hospital emergency department meets the same diseases in the same bodies, and only the point on the journey differs. The person in front of you may have walked for weeks, spent years in a settlement, crossed a sea, or arrived by air with a visa and a folder of records. Place that person on a timeline, because the timeline tells you what is possible, what is urgent, and what can wait.

One discipline governs this whole chapter, and it is stated plainly because it is easily lost. Never treat a migrant as a diagnosis rather than a person. The labels migrant, refugee, asylum seeker, and undocumented patient carry legal and social meaning, but none of them describes a body, and each can quietly replace clinical reasoning with assumption. A cough is not automatically tuberculosis, and a headache is not automatically trauma, just as a healthy looking young arrival is not automatically free of chronic infection. Ask, examine, and test when testing changes management, and let the person tell you who they are before the chart does.

Think of migration as four stages, because each produces its own pattern of disease. There is the origin phase, the time before departure in the home country or region. There is the transit phase, the weeks, months, or years spent moving and waiting in camps, border towns, detention centers, or rented rooms. There is the destination phase, the first months and years after arrival, when acute problems surface and chronic ones remain silent. Then there is the ongoing phase, which includes resettlement, family reunion, return visits to the country of origin, and onward movement to another country. Many patients pass through all four, and their health record passes through none of them, because records rarely travel as far as people do.

In the origin phase the exposures are the ordinary exposures of the place. Malaria transmission may be intense and repeated, so the patient arrives with partial acquired immunity that fades once transmission stops. Tuberculosis infection may have been acquired in childhood and now sit latent, waiting for a trigger. Soil-transmitted helminths, schistosomiasis, and other parasitic infections are common where water, sanitation, and hygiene are limited. Hepatitis B is highly prevalent in much of Asia, sub-Saharan Africa, and the western Pacific, and it is usually acquired at birth or in early childhood. HIV, leishmaniasis, Chagas disease, and human African trypanosomiasis each have their own geographic map. The origin history is a geographic exposure history and deserves the care given to any travel history.

The transit phase is where crowding writes the clinical story. Camps and informal settlements group large numbers of people into small areas with shared water points and latrines, and that arrangement transmits whatever is already present. Measles spreads rapidly among unvaccinated children, while cholera, typhoid, and shigellosis travel through contaminated water. Tuberculosis transmission is favored by crowding, poor ventilation, and long contact, so a transit camp can turn a few infectious cases into many new infections. Scabies, lice, and other infestations flourish where washing is difficult, and diphtheria and meningococcal disease can appear in crowded shelters. Density, shelter design, water quantity, and the number of people sharing a latrine are therefore clinical variables rather than a backdrop. Protracted displacement, in which a family lives in a camp for a decade or more, converts acute risks into chronic ones, including malnutrition, anemia, delayed development, and untreated mental illness.

The destination phase is where the tropical medicine curriculum is most often tested. Acute illness in the first weeks may be malaria, dengue, typhoid, or another infection acquired in transit and now incubating. Chronic infection reveals itself more slowly. Latent tuberculosis infection can reactivate months or years after arrival, sometimes when the immune system is stressed by pregnancy, diabetes, HIV, or the hardship of resettlement. Schistosomiasis and strongyloidiasis may have been carried silently for decades, chronic hepatitis B may be found only when the liver is already scarred, and Chagas disease may appear first as a cardiac arrhythmia in middle age. A normal examination at arrival does not close the file.

The healthy migrant effect is the observation that people who migrate are on average healthier than the populations they leave and often healthier than the population they join, at least in the first years. Migration selects for people with the resources, stamina, and determination to move, while the frail and the very ill are less likely to attempt or survive the journey. Early statistics therefore make new arrivals look better than they are, and a clinic that sees only those who come through its door sees a selected sample. The effect fades as that selection is worn down by poor housing, low income, discrimination, and limited access to care. There is a further trap, because some of the sickest people return home, so their deaths are recorded elsewhere and the remaining population looks healthier still. Use population data to set your prior probability, then let the person in the room change it.

Vaccination gaps are almost universal in people who have been displaced, and closing them is among the most valuable things a nurse can do. Routine immunization is one of the first services to break down in conflict, so a child born in a camp may have received nothing, or may have received doses that were never recorded. Records that exist may be written in a script you cannot read or in a numbering system that does not match your national schedule. Do not restart every series blindly; treat the person as susceptible to whatever is not documented and provide catch-up vaccination according to the national schedule. Several vaccines may be given at one visit at separate sites, and inactivated vaccines impose no interval between them, while live vaccines are generally given together or separated by 4 weeks. Where measles is circulating, protect against measles and rubella first.

Some vaccines require a specific judgment. BCG is given at birth in many countries and leaves a scar, but the scar is an unreliable guide to prior protection and revaccination is not generally recommended. Hepatitis B vaccine should be given to anyone who is neither immune nor infected, because the series is safe and effective. Tetanus and diphtheria toxoid should be brought up to date in everyone, and a tetanus-containing vaccine in pregnancy protects both the woman and the newborn. Polio and measles deserve particular attention because they spread widely in under-vaccinated communities, and human papillomavirus vaccine should be offered to eligible adolescents and young adults according to national guidance. Every vaccination encounter is also an opportunity to bring a patient into care, so make it a visit about more than a needle.

Screening programs for migrants are common, well intentioned, and limited, and every nurse should be able to state the limits. Screening applies a test to people without symptoms in order to find disease early enough to change its course. Its value depends on how common the condition is in the group tested, on the accuracy of the test, and on the existence of an effective and acceptable treatment. When the condition is uncommon, even a good test produces many false positive results, and each false positive creates anxiety, further tests, and sometimes harm. Screening must be linked to treatment, because identifying an infection that no one will treat is an ethical failure rather than a public health success. A program that tests a thousand people and follows up fifty has produced paperwork, not health.

The most useful distinction in this field is the difference between testing for infection and testing for disease. Testing for infection asks whether the organism has ever met the person's immune system, and a positive result says only that the meeting happened. Testing for disease asks whether the organism is present now and causing harm. A positive tuberculin skin test or interferon gamma release assay indicates infection with the tuberculosis bacterium, not tuberculosis disease, and most people with that result are neither ill nor infectious. A positive hepatitis B surface antigen test is different again, because it indicates current infection, which may be chronic and may damage the liver even when the person feels well. A positive HIV antibody test indicates infection and requires confirmation and treatment, not a diagnosis of AIDS. Confusing these categories produces two errors: telling a well person that they are ill, and telling a person with treatable disease that they are only a carrier.

Tuberculosis screening in migrant populations shows the distinction at work. A symptom screen asks about cough, fever, night sweats, weight loss, and blood in the sputum, and it is inexpensive and reasonably sensitive when done properly. A chest radiograph adds information but is not diagnostic, and sputum smear, culture, and PCR are what confirm disease. Active tuberculosis must be excluded before latent infection is treated, because giving a single drug while active disease is present can select for resistance. Latent infection may then be treated according to current guidance, and these regimens are revised periodically, differ between countries, and must be checked against the current national guideline before prescribing. What the World Health Organization currently describes includes 6 to 9 months of isoniazid, 4 months of rifampicin, or 12 weeks of weekly isoniazid with rifapentine, with the choice depending on age, pregnancy, drug interactions, and resistance patterns. Isoniazid is commonly given at 300 milligrams by mouth once a day with pyridoxine 25 to 50 milligrams a day to prevent peripheral neuropathy, and these figures illustrate the regimens rather than prescribe them. Doses are verified against the current guideline and against the patient's weight, kidney function, and other medicines before they are given.

Children in migrant families deserve particular attention in tuberculosis screening. A child who is a household contact of an infectious case is at high risk of progression, and young children progress quickly to severe forms including miliary tuberculosis and tuberculosis of the meninges. Active disease should be excluded and preventive treatment offered when disease is absent, according to national guidance. A child may be infected and still have a normal chest radiograph, and a negative tuberculin test does not exclude infection in a child who is malnourished, very young, or living with HIV. Weigh the child, dose by weight, and record the weight you used.

Hepatitis B and hepatitis C are chronic viral infections that migration carries across borders and that produce disease decades later. Hepatitis B passes from mother to child at birth and also through sexual contact and blood, and the birth dose of vaccine is the single most effective way to prevent lifelong infection. Screening usually begins with hepatitis B surface antigen, followed by other markers when the result is positive. Chronic infection requires assessment of liver function and viral activity, and treatment with an antiviral such as tenofovir, commonly 300 milligrams by mouth once a day, is offered according to current guidance. Hepatitis C passes mainly through blood and is now curable in most people with direct-acting antiviral medicines taken by mouth for 8 to 12 weeks. The obstacle is rarely biology; it is cost, availability, and the pathway into care, and the nurse often makes that pathway real.

HIV testing must be voluntary, confidential, and linked to treatment, and stigma is a clinical problem rather than a social footnote. A person who fears that a positive result will reach family, employer, or immigration authorities may refuse testing, delay treatment, and disappear from care. Use opt-out testing where it is permitted, explain what is being tested and why, and state plainly who will see the result. Same-day initiation of antiretroviral therapy is now standard in many programs, typically with a regimen containing dolutegravir 50 milligrams, tenofovir, and lamivudine, all by mouth once a day, and that regimen must be checked against current national guidance and against the patient's kidney function, pregnancy status, and other medicines. Partner notification, where it exists, requires consent and attention to the real risk of violence. Your manner in that room may determine whether the patient returns at all.

Beyond the first test, the work is retention. A migrant living with HIV may lose access to medicines when status, address, or country changes, and an interruption risks viral rebound, resistance, and onward transmission. Ask at every visit whether the patient has enough medicine to reach the next visit, and count the days together if necessary. Children and adolescents who acquired HIV at birth may arrive with no records and no understanding of their own diagnosis, and disclosure must be planned with the family rather than improvised. Prevention, including condoms, pre-exposure prophylaxis where available, and treatment as prevention, belongs in the same conversation. Confidentiality practices should be designed so that one misplaced paper cannot expose a patient to harm.

Mental health is part of tropical medicine for a simple reason: the body keeps the record of what the mind has survived. People who flee war, persecution, or torture carry post-traumatic stress disorder, depression, anxiety, prolonged grief, and somatic symptoms that present first as headache, chest pain, fatigue, dizziness, or unexplained pain. Survivors of torture may have specific sequelae, including chronic pain, hearing loss, sexual dysfunction, and scars whose pattern tells a story. Ask about trauma only after basic trust exists, using an interpreter who is trained and not drawn from the patient's own small community, and never press for details that are not needed for care. Treat what you find with psychological therapy, sleep support, and antidepressant medication when indicated, checking every choice and dose against current guidance and against interactions with tuberculosis and HIV medicines. Somatic complaints are real and should be investigated rather than dismissed as stress, and a normal workup is no reason to stop attending to distress.

Children carry their own version of this burden. A child who has witnessed violence, lost a parent, or spent years in a camp may present with bedwetting, school failure, aggression, withdrawal, or physical complaints, and the family may describe the child as difficult rather than distressed. Adolescents may be navigating the ordinary turbulence of that age and the extraordinary weight of migration at the same time, and they may also be the family interpreter. Screening for mental health problems belongs in routine care for this population rather than in a specialty referral that never happens. Assess safety, including suicidal thoughts and any risk of violence at home, and involve the family where that is safe and exclude them where it is not.

Female genital mutilation causes lifelong physical and psychological harm and belongs to tropical medicine because it is encountered in women and girls who migrate from regions where it is practiced. It includes several forms, from cutting of the clitoral hood to removal of tissue and narrowing of the vaginal opening. Immediate complications include severe pain, hemorrhage, infection, urinary retention, and injury to nearby structures, and these may present in an emergency department years after the event. Long-term consequences include chronic pain, painful intercourse, difficulty passing urine, recurrent urinary infection, menstrual obstruction, and psychological distress. Childbirth is where the harm becomes most visible, because scar tissue can obstruct labor and lead to prolonged labor, tearing, hemorrhage, and obstetric fistula. Care must be nonjudgmental, private, and led by what the woman wants, and deinfibulation and reconstructive surgery are options to discuss without pressure. In many destination countries the practice is illegal, including when performed abroad, and safeguarding duties must be explained honestly rather than kept from the patient.

Maternal health in migrant populations is shaped by late presentation and missing information. A woman may arrive in the third trimester with no antenatal records, uncertain dates, and a history of female genital mutilation, anemia, or untreated infection. She may fear that maternity care will cost money she does not have or will expose her to immigration authorities. The first visit should establish gestational age, screen for anemia, HIV, syphilis, hepatitis B, and malaria where transmission is possible, and check vaccination status, especially for tetanus and influenza. Blood pressure, urine protein, and blood glucose deserve the same attention as in any pregnancy, and danger signs must be explained in words the woman can repeat back. Postpartum care should include contraception, mental health, and the newborn's screening and vaccination, because the baby of a migrant mother can fall out of the system at exactly the moment when follow-up matters most.

Where malaria is transmitted, pregnancy changes the risk in both directions. Infection in pregnancy causes maternal anemia, miscarriage, stillbirth, prematurity, and low birth weight, and it is often asymptomatic. Prevention and treatment follow national policy, and in many settings intermittent preventive treatment is given with sulfadoxine and pyrimethamine, while treatment of confirmed disease uses an artemisinin-based combination therapy chosen for the trimester. These regimens are revised, differ between countries, and must be verified against the current national guideline before they are prescribed. Weigh the woman at each visit, because doses depend on weight, and record the weight you used. Congenital syphilis and hepatitis B are both preventable with timely screening and treatment, which is why the first antenatal visit is worth protecting in a busy clinic.

Language is a clinical instrument, and when it fails the diagnosis fails with it. Never use a child as an interpreter for a parent's symptoms, for a mental health assessment, or for a discussion of sexual health, because it reverses the family hierarchy and places the child in a position that can harm everyone. Never use a bystander from the same community for sensitive information, because confidentiality may be lost and gossip can follow a patient for years. Use a professional interpreter, in person or by telephone, and brief that interpreter on the purpose of the encounter. Speak directly to the patient rather than to the interpreter, in short sentences, without jargon, and use teach-back to confirm understanding. Translate written material only when the translation is competent, because a machine translation of a medicine instruction can mislead a patient into harm.

Cultural and religious considerations are best approached as questions rather than as lists. Ask how the patient understands the illness, who makes decisions in the family, what foods are eaten or avoided, whether fasting is observed, and whether prayer or traditional medicine is part of the plan. Modesty may determine who can be examined and by whom, and gender concordance may be essential before a woman accepts a pelvic examination. Some patients take herbal preparations or see a traditional healer, and the useful response is curiosity rather than prohibition, because a patient who feels judged will stop telling you. Dietary advice during Ramadan or another fast must be adapted, particularly for diabetes and for medicines taken with food. Where a treatment plan conflicts with a belief, negotiate the parts that are flexible and be honest about the parts that are not.

The right to care and the legal barriers set in front of it are in direct tension, and the nurse stands in the middle of that tension. In many countries undocumented people are entitled to emergency care, to care for certain infectious diseases, and to maternity care, while other services are restricted or chargeable. Fear of being reported to immigration authorities keeps people away even when care is legally available, so state clearly what the service does and does not share, and keep health and immigration functions separate. Cost is a barrier even when care is permitted, and a bill of uncertain size can frighten a patient more than a diagnosis. Advocacy is therefore part of the nursing role here, whether it means finding a clinic that will see an undocumented patient, arranging a payment plan, or writing a letter that describes medical need without describing legal status. Know the local rules, ask about the patient's concerns directly, and never promise what the system will not deliver.

Continuity of care across borders is one of the hardest logistics problems in medicine, because neither infection nor chronic disease respects a frontier. Tuberculosis treatment is the clearest example, since an interrupted course can lead to relapse and to drug resistance that is far harder and more expensive to treat. The World Health Organization has long supported mechanisms for transferring tuberculosis care between countries, and the nurse should use them rather than start blind. Antiretroviral therapy for HIV must not lapse during resettlement or deportation, and a supply covering the journey plus a written summary of the regimen is the minimum. Give the patient a copy of the record in a language they can at least partly read, and tell them to carry it with the medicines.

The logistics of the clinic determine whether any of this happens. Appointment systems assume a fixed address and a working telephone number, and many migrant patients have neither, so walk-in hours, text reminders, and one stable point of contact matter more than an elegant booking system. Transport cost and childcare can decide whether a woman returns for follow-up, so fit several needs into one visit where you can. Vaccines and some medicines require a functioning cold chain, which fails when electricity is intermittent, so temperature monitoring and a plan for power failure are clinical duties. Laboratory specimens must be tracked, because a result that never reaches the patient is a test that was never done. Keep the record short, legible, and complete enough that the next clinician, in the next country, can act on it.

Monitoring is where good intentions are usually lost. A screening program counts how many people were tested, but it should count how many were treated and how many completed treatment. If a patient begins treatment for latent tuberculosis infection, the plan should include a defined follow-up schedule, a review for hepatitis and neuropathy, and clear instructions about what to do if jaundice or numbness appears. If a patient begins antiretroviral therapy, viral load and kidney function should be monitored according to guidance, and adherence should be discussed without accusation. Recall systems should use contact information that actually works, including a trusted friend or community organization, but only with the patient's permission. Outcomes should be recorded, counted, and reviewed, because programs improve when they look honestly at who was lost.

Adverse effects deserve explicit attention, because a patient may be taking several medicines with overlapping toxicity. Isoniazid can cause hepatitis and peripheral neuropathy, and pyridoxine reduces the neuropathy, while rifampicin induces liver enzymes and can reduce the effectiveness of other drugs, including some antiretrovirals and hormonal contraceptives. Tenofovir can affect kidney function and bone density, and dolutegravir interacts with some antituberculosis medicines, so combinations must be checked for interactions before they are prescribed. Antidepressants and antipsychotics require attention to dose, to interactions, and to the possibility of increased suicidal thinking early in treatment. Vaccines cause local reactions and occasionally systemic ones, which should be recorded and reported through the national system for adverse events following immunization. Every one of these medicines is dosed against the current guideline, the current weight, and current kidney and liver function, because a dose copied from an old chart is not a dose.

The nursing role in migrant and refugee health can be described as five continuous tasks. The first is engagement, which means making the first contact safe enough that the person returns. The second is assessment, which means taking the migration history as carefully as the medical history and letting it direct the examination. The third is action, which means vaccinating, screening, treating, and referring according to the guidance in force. The fourth is navigation, which means helping the patient through a system that was not designed with them in mind. The fifth is advocacy, which means changing the parts of the system that keep people out. None of the five is optional, and the last is what makes the first four sustainable.

One exposure is easily missed: the return visit. A migrant who has resettled may travel back to the country of origin to see family, and may stay for weeks in a rural area with intense transmission and no pretravel advice. Children born abroad may accompany them and meet malaria, typhoid, hepatitis A, and other infections for the first time with no acquired immunity at all. These travelers carry higher risk than ordinary tourists because they stay longer, live in family homes rather than hotels, and often seek no pretravel care. Ask every migrant patient about planned travel, and treat that travel as a clinical event with its own assessment, vaccination, and prevention plan.

Tuberculosis in mobile populations also raises the problem of drug resistance. A patient may have started treatment in one country and arrive in another having received partial or interrupted therapy, which is one of the settings in which resistance is selected. Take a precise treatment history, including each drug, the dose if known, the start date, and the reason for any interruption, and obtain whatever records exist. Never add a single new drug to a failing regimen. If resistance is suspected, refer for proper testing and expert management rather than improvising.

Malnutrition travels with displacement and interacts with infection in both directions. A child who is undernourished is more vulnerable to infection and more likely to have a severe course, and repeated infection worsens nutritional status in a cycle that is hard to break. Anemia may be caused by iron deficiency, by hookworm or schistosomiasis, by malaria, by chronic disease, or by several of these at once, so treating anemia without finding the cause is often ineffective. Growth should be plotted on the correct chart and the child weighed at every opportunity. Skin problems matter too, because scabies, fungal infection, impetigo, and infected insect bites are frequent in crowded housing and their treatment is simple when it is offered. Noninfectious problems such as dental disease, poor vision, hearing loss, high blood pressure, and diabetes are often untreated and should not be crowded out by the search for exotic infection.

Consent deserves attention in a population that may have learned that officials cannot be trusted. Consent must be informed, voluntary, and specific to what is proposed, and it must be genuine rather than a signature on a form in a language the patient cannot read. Explain what will be tested, what the result means, who will see it, and what happens next, then confirm understanding. Capacity should be assumed unless there is a reason to question it, and a language barrier is not such a reason. When a patient refuses a test, record the refusal and offer again at a later visit rather than imposing it. Coercion in the name of public health damages the trust on which all future care depends.

It is worth naming the wider context. Displacement worldwide is at a level not seen in recent decades, driven by conflict, persecution, economic collapse, and increasingly by climate-related disaster, and most displaced people are hosted by countries that are themselves low- and middle-income. The burden therefore falls hardest on the systems with the least capacity, while a nurse practicing in a high-income country may meet the same problems with far more resources. The ethical standard does not change with the setting: care should be offered on the basis of need, delivered with consent, and documented with the patient's interests first.

This work is also heavy for the nurse. The stories are difficult, the systems are frustrating, and the moral distress of being unable to provide what a patient needs is real. Supervision, peer support, clear boundaries, and attention to one's own health are conditions of sustainable practice rather than luxuries. Recognizing exhaustion in yourself and in colleagues is part of professional responsibility, and it protects patients as well as staff. A nurse who is depleted cannot extend the patience that this population requires.

Before leaving this chapter, take the framing that governs every dose in this book. Regimens described here reflect current guidance at the time of writing, guidance is revised periodically, and guidance differs between countries, so the current national guideline and a reliable drug reference must be checked before prescribing. The World Health Organization, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control all publish migrant health guidance that is updated as evidence changes. Doses are calculated from the patient's current weight, adjusted for kidney and liver function, and verified by a second qualified person where policy requires it. When a dose is uncertain, the correct action is to delay it and check, not to guess and proceed.

Finally, return to the patient in front of you. The most sophisticated screening program in the world fails if the person does not come back, and the reason a person comes back is usually a relationship rather than a protocol. That relationship is built in ordinary ways: by listening, by being on time, by remembering the name, by explaining the plan, and by being honest when you do not know. It is built when the nurse says what will happen to the information and then does exactly that. Continuity of care across borders begins with continuity of care within a single room.

To summarize, migrant and refugee health requires the nurse to place every patient on a migration timeline of origin, transit, destination, and ongoing movement, and to know what each phase makes likely. The healthy migrant effect makes early arrivals look healthier than they are, and screening programs reveal only what they are designed and resourced to treat. The distinction between testing for infection and testing for disease prevents both false reassurance and unnecessary alarm. Tuberculosis, hepatitis B, hepatitis C, and HIV each require a defined pathway from test to treatment, with attention to latent infection, to stigma, and to continuity across borders. Mental health, the consequences of female genital mutilation, maternal care, language, culture, and the legal barriers to care are clinical subjects rather than background. The logistics of follow-up, the monitoring of adherence, and the recognition of adverse effects decide whether the rest succeeds. The discipline that holds it all together is simple: treat the person, not the label.
