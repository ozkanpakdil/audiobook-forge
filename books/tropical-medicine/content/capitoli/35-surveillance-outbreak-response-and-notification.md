# Chapter 35 — Surveillance, Outbreak Response, and Notification

Surveillance is the nervous system of public health, and its purpose is not to produce reports but to produce action. It is the continuous and systematic collection, analysis, and interpretation of health data, tied from the start to the dissemination of what those data mean to the people who can act on them. A system that measures disease and never triggers a response is an expensive diary, and a response that begins without measurement is guesswork. The World Health Organization describes surveillance as covering both the early detection of outbreaks and the monitoring of trends over time, so that a program can know whether it is winning or losing. For the advanced practice nurse, surveillance is not an administrative duty that sits outside clinical work, because it is what happens when the observation made at the bedside is carried onward fast enough to protect someone you will never meet.

Surveillance answers several different questions with the same machinery. It detects outbreaks early, when the number of cases is still small enough that containment is possible. It tracks the burden of endemic disease so that ministries, donors, and clinics can place scarce staff and medicines where they are needed. It measures whether an intervention is working, which is why a vaccination campaign or an insecticide-treated bed net distribution must be followed by surveillance rather than by a closing ceremony. Programs for HIV, for tuberculosis, and for malaria each keep their own reporting streams, and those streams are strongest when they feed one district analysis instead of competing for the same nurse's time. When surveillance is weak, every one of these functions fails at once, and the failure is usually invisible until an outbreak has already escaped the district.

There are two broad ways of gathering this information, and mature systems run both at the same time. Indicator-based surveillance collects structured data on a defined list of diseases, reported at fixed intervals from fixed sites against agreed case definitions, so that counts can be compared from week to week and from place to place. Event-based surveillance is looser and faster, gathering unstructured signals from clinicians, laboratory staff, community members, journalists, teachers, and informal networks, and then verifying each signal before deciding whether it deserves investigation. An unusual cluster of deaths in a village, a rumor raised on a radio phone-in program, a pharmacist who notices that one antibiotic has suddenly sold out, and a spike in school absenteeism are all events in this sense. The two systems are complementary, because indicator-based reporting is reliable but slow and rigid, while event-based monitoring is fast and sensitive but noisy. A signal from either path is not yet an outbreak, and it becomes one only after someone verifies it.

Before any case can be counted, it has to be defined, and the case definition is the foundation on which everything else rests. A case definition is a standard set of clinical, epidemiological, and laboratory criteria that decides who counts and who does not, and it usually has tiers such as suspected, probable, and confirmed. Without a shared definition, two clinics reporting the same disease to the same district will count different patients, and the resulting line on the graph will be meaningless. A definition is a working tool rather than a statement of biological truth, and during an outbreak it is deliberately made sensitive so that few cases are missed, even at the cost of including some patients who turn out to have something else. The World Health Organization, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control all publish standard case definitions, and they do not always agree, which is why the national definition is the one to use. Because the definition changes as the outbreak evolves, every revision must be communicated immediately to the people doing the counting.

Surveillance systems are usually described by how the data arrive, and the four common types have very different strengths. Passive surveillance waits for health workers to report what they see, which is inexpensive and covers the whole country but depends on the diligence and available time of people who are already overloaded. Active surveillance sends someone out to look, visiting clinics and communities for cases that would otherwise go unreported, which is far more complete but costly and therefore reserved for outbreaks, elimination programs, and high-consequence diseases. Sentinel surveillance follows a chosen set of sites that report in greater detail than the rest of the system, which is how influenza and antimicrobial resistance are monitored without burdening every clinic. Syndromic surveillance counts patients by their presenting syndrome rather than by a confirmed diagnosis, so it can detect a rise in fever with rash or in acute watery diarrhea before any laboratory result exists, making it the fastest approach and the least specific. Most national systems combine all four, accepting that each has a characteristic failure mode that the others partly cover.

Much of Africa works within a framework called integrated disease surveillance and response, developed by the World Health Organization together with its regional office for Africa and used as the organizing logic of district health reporting in dozens of countries. The idea is integration, so that a single district team collects and analyzes data on a prioritized list of diseases rather than each vertical program demanding its own forms. The framework places responsibility at the district level, where data are analyzed and acted on locally, and it reaches into the community through trained community health workers who can recognize and report a small number of high-priority events. It also builds response into the same system, so that an alert is linked to a defined action rather than to another report. When it works, the district officer knows within a week what is happening at the periphery, and the periphery knows that someone is reading what it sends. When it fails, the forms still arrive at the capital and nobody at the district level has ever seen them.

The constraints that decide whether surveillance succeeds are almost never analytic, and they are completeness and timeliness. Completeness means the proportion of expected reports that actually arrive, and a district that receives 60 per cent of its weekly reports cannot distinguish a real decline in cases from a collapse in reporting. Timeliness means the interval between a case occurring and the information reaching a person who can act, and a confirmed case reported three weeks late has already had time to generate a hundred more. A useful discipline is the reporting of zeros, because a clinic that sends a report saying it saw no cases is giving information, while a clinic that sends nothing is giving none. Denominators are equally essential and equally unglamorous, since a count of cases without the population it came from cannot produce a rate. Feedback is the fuel of the whole arrangement, because health workers who never hear what became of their reports stop sending them. Supervision visits check that registers are complete and that case definitions are applied, and data audits compare what was written in the register with what reached the district office. Training must be repeated rather than delivered once, because staff rotate, and a new nurse who has never been taught the local case definitions will report either nothing or everything.

Numbers become decisions through thresholds. A threshold is a level of disease at which the system commits to an investigation, and it is built by comparing the current count with what would be expected at that place and in that season. The expected level is usually derived from several years of historical data and expressed as a baseline or a rolling average, and the alert threshold is set low enough to catch a rise early while the epidemic threshold is set at a level that clearly signals an outbreak. A single case of a disease that has been eliminated, such as polio or Guinea worm disease, is itself an outbreak and needs no threshold at all. A cluster of deaths, an unusual age group, an unusual place, or an unusual season can breach a threshold even when the total number is small. The trap is to treat the threshold as a mechanical line, when in practice it is a judgment that depends on the quality of the baseline and on how alarming the disease is.

When a threshold is crossed, the investigation proceeds through a sequence refined over decades, and the first step is to verify that the diagnosis is real. Verification means confirming that the cases exist, that the laboratory result is credible, that the same patient has not been counted twice, and that a change in testing practice, a new clinician, or a clerical error has not manufactured a cluster. It also means asking whether the event is genuinely unusual for that place and season, or whether it is the expected background that is finally being counted properly. Once the signal survives, the team defines the case for this particular event, writes the definition down, and makes sure everyone uses the same one. A definition that is too narrow will miss cases and hide the true extent of the outbreak, while one that is too broad will dilute the investigation with unrelated illness. Early in an outbreak the working definition is often clinical, because laboratory confirmation is slow and may be available in only one place. The definition will be revised, and each revision changes the shape of the epidemic curve, which is why the date of every revision is recorded.

Next comes the search for cases, which in practice means active case finding rather than waiting for patients to arrive. The team visits health facilities and reviews registers, goes into the community with community health workers, screens the contacts of every known patient, and follows up rumors that have not yet reached a clinic. Each person identified is entered on a line list, a single table in which every row is one patient with a name, an age, a sex, a place, a date of symptom onset, a date of report, a laboratory result, and an outcome. The line list is the most valuable object in an outbreak investigation, because nearly every later analysis is a rearrangement of its columns. It also protects patients, because it makes duplicate entries visible and makes it possible to find someone lost between facilities. Keeping it complete, legible, and current is unglamorous work, and the nurse who does it is doing some of the most consequential work in the response.

From the line list the team describes the outbreak by time, by place, and by person, and these are three views of one event rather than three separate descriptions. Description by place asks where cases occurred, mapped by village, neighborhood, school, workplace, or water source, and clustering in space often points directly at a shared exposure. Description by person asks who was affected, in terms of age, sex, occupation, pregnancy, vaccination status, and travel. It should also ask who was not affected, because the people who escaped a cluster often hold the clue to what protected them. A cluster confined to one age group suggests a shared exposure, a cluster confined to one water source suggests a common vehicle, and cases scattered along a road suggest a moving source or a mobile population. The pattern that emerges from these descriptions usually tells the team where to look next.

The description by time is drawn as an epidemic curve, a histogram of cases plotted against their date of onset, and learning to read it is one of the most useful skills in field epidemiology. The width of each column represents one interval, usually chosen as a fraction of the incubation period so that the curve has enough resolution to show its shape. The classic shapes have clear meanings: a single sharp peak suggests a point source exposure such as one contaminated meal, a plateau suggests a continuous common source such as a water supply that stays contaminated, and a series of progressively taller peaks suggests person-to-person transmission with each generation of cases larger than the one before it. The first case shown may be the true index case or simply the first one detected, and the gap between successive peaks can be used to estimate the incubation period and the serial interval. The curve also shows whether the outbreak is growing, stable, or shrinking, which tells the team whether control measures have begun to work, although that effect is always delayed by the incubation period. A curve can mislead badly if cases are plotted by the date they were reported rather than by the date their symptoms began, because a sudden improvement in laboratory capacity then looks exactly like a sudden increase in disease.

With the outbreak described, the team generates hypotheses about its source and its mode of transmission, and then tests them rather than assuming them. Hypotheses come from interviews, from visits to homes and markets, from food and water histories, and from the local knowledge that only people who live there can supply. A useful hypothesis is stated in a form that can be wrong, so that data can support it or discard it. The team asks what the cases have in common, what the people who remained well did differently, and whether the timing fits a single exposure or continuing transmission. It also asks whether the pattern fits a known disease, because the incubation period and the usual route of spread constrain the possibilities long before any laboratory result arrives. A hypothesis that is never tested can harden into an official story, and an official story that is wrong damages the response far more than an admitted uncertainty.

Testing a hypothesis usually means an analytic study, and the choice of design follows the situation. A cohort study follows a defined group, such as everyone who attended a funeral or ate at a wedding, and compares the attack rate among those exposed with the attack rate among those not exposed. A case-control study is used when the population is too large or too diffuse to enumerate, and it compares people with the disease against unaffected people from the same community, looking backward for differences in exposure. Either design produces a measure of association that tells the team how much more likely illness was among the exposed, with an indication of how much confidence to place in the result. The team must also consider bias, because people who are ill remember exposures differently from people who are well, and because the way cases were found may itself distort the comparison. A hypothesis that survives guides control, and a hypothesis that fails is discarded, which narrows the search rather than wasting it.

Control does not wait for the investigation to finish, because people are becoming ill while the analysis is underway. Measures follow from the mode of transmission, and they are often started on a reasonable hypothesis and refined later. For a waterborne outbreak the measures are disinfection of the supply, provision of safe water, and separation of sewage from drinking sources. For a foodborne outbreak they are removal of the implicated food, inspection of the premises, and education of the people who handle it. For a vector-borne outbreak they are vector control, including insecticide spraying and the elimination of breeding sites. For an outbreak spread by respiratory droplets or direct contact they are isolation, hand hygiene, personal protective equipment, and where relevant vaccination. Broader measures such as reactive vaccination campaigns, mass drug administration, or the closing of schools and markets carry their own costs and must be weighed against the harm of the disease and the harm of disruption. Every measure needs a defined owner, a start rule, a stop rule, and a way of measuring whether it worked.

Outbreaks are managed through an incident management system, a command structure that assigns clear responsibilities so that decisions are made once and communicated to everyone. The incident manager leads, and beneath that person sit a technical lead or epidemiologist, a laboratory coordinator, a case management lead, a logistics officer, an infection prevention and control lead, a risk communication lead, and a data manager. Case management is where the advanced practice nurse is most obviously essential, because the clinical care of patients during an outbreak is nursing work as much as medical work. Nurses also staff triage and screening points, collect specimens correctly, maintain the line list and the case notes, administer treatments and vaccines, monitor patients for deterioration, and supervise the infection prevention practices that keep a health facility from amplifying the outbreak. Community liaison is another nursing strength, because the nurse is often the person the community already knows and trusts. In a large response these roles are formal and full time, while in a small district the same nurse may hold three of them at once. That is why the nurse who has written the notification, kept the line list, and knows the stock levels is not peripheral to the response but central to it.

Case management during an outbreak rests on a small number of high-yield interventions, and the nurse should know them before the outbreak arrives. In cholera, the intervention that saves lives is rapid rehydration, given by mouth as oral rehydration salts wherever the patient can drink and intravenously as Ringer lactate solution when they cannot, with the volume judged by the degree of dehydration and repeated as needed. In measles, vitamin A is given by mouth on two consecutive days because it reduces mortality in children, at a dose of 200,000 international units for a child aged 12 months or older, 100,000 international units for an infant aged 6 to 11 months, and 50,000 international units for a child younger than 6 months. In meningococcal meningitis, a single dose of ceftriaxone given intramuscularly or intravenously is standard treatment and is often sufficient in a mass campaign setting. In an outbreak of viral hemorrhagic fever, care is largely supportive, with careful fluid and electrolyte management and strict attention to infection prevention. All of these regimens are revised from time to time, guidance differs between countries, and the current national guideline and product insert must always be checked before a drug is prescribed or administered. A nurse who has memorized an old dose and cannot find the current reference is more dangerous in an outbreak than a nurse who knows where to look.

Logistics is the part of outbreak response that decides whether any of the clinical intentions can be carried out, and it is the part most often underestimated. The team needs vehicles that run, fuel, drivers, communication equipment, and a way of moving specimens and supplies to places that may be far from any paved road. It needs a cold chain that keeps vaccines and certain medicines within their required temperature range from the national store to the last village, with temperature monitoring and a contingency plan for power failure. It needs a stock management system that knows what is on hand, what has been consumed, and what must be ordered, because a stockout of rehydration salts or antibiotics during an outbreak is a clinical disaster with an administrative cause. It needs enough trained staff and a plan for resting and rotating them, since exhausted responders make errors and can carry infection home. It needs money that can be released quickly, because slow financing is a reliable way to lose an outbreak. A nurse who understands the supply chain, and who reports consumption honestly and early, is doing outbreak response even when no patient is in front of her.

Whenever an outbreak response delivers medicines or vaccines to large numbers of people, it must also watch for harm, because the same intervention that saves lives can injure them. Adverse events following immunization, drug reactions, and errors in administration must be recognized, managed, and reported through the national pharmacovigilance system. The nurse is usually the first to see an adverse event, because she gives the injection or the tablet and because a family returns to her when something goes wrong. Reporting must be prompt and detailed, and it must avoid the assumption that the event was caused by the product, because the purpose of the report is to determine causation rather than to presume it. A program that conceals its adverse events loses the community's confidence the moment the truth emerges, and the damage spreads to every future campaign in that place. Honest surveillance of harm is not an obstacle to outbreak control, and it is what makes outbreak control sustainable.

The response itself must be monitored while it runs, so that it can be corrected before the outbreak ends. A daily situation report gives the team a common picture: new cases, new deaths, cases by location, patients under treatment, specimens pending, beds available, and supplies remaining. Indicators are chosen in advance so that the team is not inventing its measures in the middle of the emergency, and they usually cover speed, coverage, quality, and equity. Monitoring asks whether the interventions are reaching the people who need them, whether the most affected groups are being missed, and whether the response is creating new risks such as crowding in a treatment center or unsafe injections. Regular review meetings turn those numbers into decisions about redeploying staff, reallocating supplies, and revising the plan. The nurse's daily documentation is the raw material of this monitoring, which is why accurate records at the bedside are an instrument of policy and not merely a chore.

Because an outbreak in one country is a risk to every other, the world has a binding legal framework for notification. The International Health Regulations, revised in 2005 and binding on all member states of the World Health Organization, require countries to detect, assess, report, and respond to events that may constitute a public health emergency of international concern. Four questions decide whether an event must be notified: is the public health impact serious, is the event unusual or unexpected, is there a significant risk of international spread, and is there a significant risk of restrictions on travel or trade. Each country designates a national focal point that must report a qualifying event to the World Health Organization within 24 hours of assessment, after which the organization may convene an emergency committee and issue temporary recommendations. The regulations also require every country to maintain core capacities for surveillance and response, which is the part most often underfunded and the part that matters most in the long run. Reporting is not a courtesy to be offered when convenient, and it is a legal obligation that exists because the alternative is a fire nobody knows about until it has crossed a border.

Most countries also maintain their own notifiable disease lists, which are usually broader than the international obligation and are set by national law or regulation. The list varies from country to country, which is one reason a nurse moving between systems must learn the local list rather than assuming a previous one applies. The duty to notify usually falls on the clinician who first suspects the disease, and in practice that clinician is very often the nurse, which places notification squarely within nursing practice. Notification must be timely, which means knowing the required interval, often 24 hours for the most urgent conditions and one week for others, and knowing the route, which may be a paper form, a telephone call, a text message, or an entry in an electronic system. The report should contain the minimum needed to act: who is ill, where, when the symptoms began, what is suspected, and how the diagnosis was made or is being pursued. When in doubt, the nurse reports rather than waits, because the cost of a false alarm is a conversation and the cost of silence can be an epidemic.

Behind every confirmed case is a laboratory, and laboratory networks are the tiered backbone of surveillance. A typical network has a peripheral level that collects specimens and performs simple tests, a district or regional level with greater capacity, and a national reference laboratory that performs confirmatory and specialized testing such as culture, PCR, typing, and genomic sequencing. Specimens move between these levels along a defined transport chain, with triple packaging, cold chain maintenance, and documentation at every step, because a specimen that arrives degraded or leaking is worse than no specimen at all. Quality assurance matters as much as capacity, and laboratories that take part in external quality assessment produce results that can be compared across sites and over time. During an outbreak the network must absorb a sudden surge in demand, which is why the ability to prioritize specimens and to add capacity quickly is planned in advance. The laboratory is often the rate-limiting step in an investigation, and a nurse who collects the right specimen, at the right time, from the right patient, and sends it with the right form, does more for the response than a nurse who collects many specimens badly.

Risk communication is not public relations and it is not the last item on the response agenda, because an outbreak is as much a social event as a biological one. Good risk communication is fast, honest, consistent, and addressed to the specific fears people actually hold, in the language they speak, through messengers they already trust. It acknowledges what is not yet known, since a message that pretends to certainty and then changes is remembered as a lie, while a message that says the evidence is still developing keeps its credibility. Trust is destroyed in predictable ways: by denying an outbreak that everyone can see, by disclosing information late or only after it leaks, by giving different answers to different audiences, by promising supplies that never arrive, and by treating a community as a problem rather than a partner. Stigmatization is especially corrosive, because it drives the sick into hiding and makes every case harder to find. Community engagement is the practical form that trust takes, and it begins before the outbreak does, when a health committee, trained community health workers, and a working relationship with the local clinic can move an alert from a village to a district office in hours. During an outbreak, engagement means listening as much as broadcasting, holding meetings with leaders, and designing interventions around how people actually live rather than banning the practices they rely on.

Outbreaks end in a small number of ways, and understanding those ways helps a team know whether it is finished. An outbreak fueled by susceptible people ends when the pool of susceptibles is exhausted, either because people have been infected and are now immune or because they have been vaccinated. An outbreak driven by a common source ends when the source is removed, which is why finding the contaminated water point or the implicated food matters so much. An outbreak sustained by transmission from person to person or by a vector ends when the effective contact rate falls below the level needed to keep it going, through isolation, behavior change, vector control, or immunity. Seasonality can also end an outbreak, because the mosquito population or the environmental conditions that drive transmission change, and this ending is real but temporary. Some outbreaks simply burn out for reasons that are never fully explained, which is unsatisfying but common. Surveillance must continue after the last case, usually for a period equal to twice the maximum incubation period, before the outbreak can be declared over, because the last case detected is rarely the last case that will occur.

The period after an outbreak is where the next one is prevented or guaranteed. An after-action review brings the response team together to examine what worked, what failed, and why, and to write the lessons down while memory is still fresh. The surveillance system is strengthened by the experience if the new capacity is institutionalized rather than allowed to decay when emergency funding ends. Stockpiles are replenished, laboratory capacity is maintained, training is repeated, and the relationships built with the community during the response are kept alive rather than abandoned. The people who responded also need attention, because fatigue, fear, grief, and moral distress accumulate during an outbreak and are rarely acknowledged afterward. A health system that treats the end of an outbreak as a return to normal has learned nothing from it, and it will meet the next outbreak with exactly the same gaps.

To summarize, surveillance exists to turn observation into action, and it fails when reporting is slow, incomplete, or disconnected from a response. Indicator-based and event-based systems serve a common purpose, case definitions make counting possible, and passive, active, sentinel, and syndromic approaches each compensate for the weaknesses of the others. The integrated disease surveillance and response framework shows how a district can analyze its own data and act on them, but completeness, timeliness, and feedback remain the constraints that decide whether the system is real. Thresholds convert numbers into decisions, and when one is crossed the investigation moves through verification, case definition, case finding, description by time, place, and person, hypothesis, testing, and control, with the epidemic curve as the clearest picture of what is happening. The duty to notify is legal as well as ethical, laboratory networks make confirmation possible, and risk communication and community trust decide whether any of the technical work will be accepted. Outbreaks end through immunity, removal of the source, interruption of transmission, or the change of season, and they end safely only when surveillance continues well past the last case. Your part in all of this is concrete: recognize the signal, define it, report it promptly, document it accurately, care for the patient in front of you, and keep the community with you rather than against you.
