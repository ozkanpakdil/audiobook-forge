# Chapter 37 — Critical Care in Resource-Limited Settings

Critical illness where resources are few follows a different logic from critical illness in a wealthy hospital. Without a ventilator, an arterial line or a laboratory that runs at night, the clinician substitutes frequent observation and disciplined reasoning for the missing machines. Critical here means the patient can die within hours unless someone recognizes the direction of travel and acts. This chapter follows the guidance of the World Health Organization, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control, and it treats every dose as current guidance rather than settled truth.

The physiology that kills is consistent across malaria, pneumonia, diarrhea, sepsis and hemorrhage. Circulation fails, so oxygen stops reaching the tissues; breathing fails, so oxygen never enters the blood; the airway closes; the blood sugar falls. Each has a bedside sign that costs nothing and an intervention a nurse can begin before a physician arrives. Recognizing the pattern matters more than naming the organism, because the cause decides the definitive drug while the physiology decides whether the patient survives to receive it.

Triage is the first clinical act and the one that decides who lives, so it begins at the gate rather than at the registration desk. The simplest system asks four questions: can the patient walk, is the patient breathing fast, is the patient conscious, and is there severe bleeding. Anyone who cannot walk, who breathes rapidly, who is confused or who is bleeding heavily goes to the resuscitation area at once, while a patient with fever and a rash or a travel history waits briefly. Triage is a repeated judgment rather than a single stamp, and the nurse who performs it must be able to move a patient and start oxygen without waiting for permission.

Deterioration announces itself before it becomes arrest, and the signs need no equipment at all. In the hours before death, patients almost always show one of a small set of changes: a rising respiratory rate, a falling level of consciousness, a falling urine output, cold clammy skin, or a new inability to drink or sit upright. The respiratory rate is the earliest and most neglected sign, and it is measured by watching the chest for one full minute with a watch. A rate above 20 breaths per minute in an adult, above 30 in an older child or above 40 in a calm young child should stop the clinician and demand reassessment.

The vital signs that matter most are mental status, respiratory rate, systolic blood pressure, heart rate, temperature, oxygen saturation and urine output. Mental status is checked with a repeatable scale such as the Glasgow Coma Scale, or more practically by asking the patient's name, the place, the date and a simple command. Urine output is the most honest measure of perfusion in a patient who cannot speak, and a fall below 0.5 milliliters per kilogram per hour in an adult warns that the circulation is failing. An early warning system assigns points for values that deviate from normal, sums them and triggers a defined response at a defined threshold. It works only when the response is decided in advance and everyone knows it, because an alarm with no agreed action merely creates anxiety.

Sepsis is life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated response to infection, and septic shock is the point at which the circulation can no longer deliver enough oxygen even after fluids. The infection triggers inflammatory signals that dilate blood vessels, make the capillary walls leaky and disturb the heart and the clotting system, so blood pressure falls, tissues are underperfused and the patient becomes confused, oliguric and acidotic. The signs are fever or an abnormally low temperature, a fast heart rate, fast breathing, low blood pressure, delayed capillary refill, mottled skin, confusion and reduced urine output. In an older patient or a patient with HIV, fever may be absent, and the only sign may be sudden confusion or a fall.

Fluid resuscitation begins with a balanced crystalloid such as Ringer's lactate or normal saline, given as a bolus of 10 to 20 milliliters per kilogram over 30 minutes and repeated while the patient stays hypotensive and shows no sign of fluid overload. The World Health Organization suggests an initial 30 milliliters per kilogram for children in shock, given in divided boluses with reassessment after each, but guidance differs between countries and the national guideline must be checked before the first milliliter is given. The narrow line between too little and too much is the central difficulty, because too little leaves the patient in shock and too much produces pulmonary edema, a patient who drowns in the resuscitation meant to save them. Reassess after every bolus by listening to the lung bases and watching the respiratory rate, the jugular venous pressure where visible, the liver size in a child and the oxygen saturation. Stop the bolus if new crackles, a rising respiratory rate or a falling saturation appear, and in children with severe anemia, severe acute malnutrition or heart disease, give fluid more slowly and in smaller amounts.

When fluids alone do not restore the blood pressure, a vasopressor is started and the flow of fluid is slowed. Norepinephrine is the first choice, infused intravenously at 0.05 to 0.5 micrograms per kilogram per minute and adjusted to a mean arterial pressure of at least 65 millimeters of mercury in an adult. It must run through a secure intravenous line, and where a central line is impossible, a well-sited peripheral cannula in a large vein is used and checked often for leakage, because the drug damages tissue when it escapes the vein. Injection of a vasopressor into muscle or under the skin is never acceptable. If norepinephrine is unavailable, epinephrine or dopamine is used according to the national guideline, and the dose must be verified by a second clinician and written down, because vasopressor arithmetic is a common and lethal source of error.

Antibiotics are the definitive treatment for septic shock and should be given within the first hour of recognition, after blood cultures are drawn where a laboratory can process them. Drawing cultures first is ideal but must not delay the antibiotic by more than a few minutes, because in sepsis the delay costs lives. A common empirical choice for a severely ill adult is ceftriaxone 2 grams intravenously every 24 hours, with local resistance and the national guideline deciding whether a second agent is needed. In children the dose is usually 50 to 80 milligrams per kilogram intravenously once daily, and in meningitis the dose is higher and must be confirmed against current guidance. Where the source is probably the abdomen or the gut, metronidazole is commonly added at 500 milligrams intravenously every eight hours in an adult. Every one of these figures is current guidance rather than settled truth, because regimens are revised and differ between countries and age groups, so check the current guideline and verify the dose independently before the drug is given.

Severe malaria is a different fluid problem from sepsis, and treating it like sepsis can kill the patient. The parasite Plasmodium falciparum causes infected red cells to stick to the lining of small vessels, obstructing the capillaries of the brain, the gut, the kidney and the lung, so the patient is underperfused even when the circulating volume is adequate. These patients are often not depleted of volume, and a rapid bolus can precipitate pulmonary edema and brain swelling, which is why the recommendation is cautious, measured replacement rather than aggressive resuscitation. Give maintenance fluid, replace measured losses, correct hypoglycemia and severe anemia, and avoid the large boluses that sepsis guidance endorses. The specific treatment is artesunate, given intravenously at 2.4 milligrams per kilogram at the start, again at 12 hours and again at 24 hours, then once every 24 hours until the patient can take oral therapy. Where artesunate is unavailable, quinine is given as a loading dose of 20 milligrams per kilogram intravenously over four hours, then 10 milligrams per kilogram every eight hours, and the infusion must be rate-controlled because rapid quinine causes dangerous hypotension.

Severe malaria also brings complications that must be anticipated rather than discovered. Hypoglycemia is common, particularly in children and pregnant women, and it recurs, so glucose is checked at least every four hours and after any change in consciousness. Severe anemia from destroyed red cells needs a transfusion where blood is safe and available, and the decision is made on the hemoglobin and the clinical state together. Convulsions are treated with a benzodiazepine, the airway is protected and the patient is nursed on their side, because aspiration is a common and preventable death. Acute kidney injury is monitored through urine output and, where possible, creatinine, and the patient who stops passing urine needs dialysis or a clear referral plan. Every one of these patients should also be reviewed for a bacterial infection, because malaria and sepsis frequently coexist and a positive malaria test does not end the search.

Severe dengue presents the opposite problem, and understanding why changes the fluid plan. The dengue virus infects and activates the cells lining the capillaries and triggers an increase in vascular permeability, so plasma leaks out of the vessels into the chest, the abdomen and the tissues. The fluid has not been lost from the body but has left the vessels, so the patient may be swollen and heavy while the circulation collapses from a falling intravascular volume. The critical phase begins around the time the fever falls, usually between the third and the seventh day, and it lasts about 24 to 48 hours, which is the window in which shock occurs. Warning signs include persistent vomiting, severe abdominal pain, a tender liver, bleeding gums, restlessness, cold extremities, a rising or falling hematocrit and a narrowing pulse pressure. The fluid of choice is a balanced crystalloid at 5 to 10 milliliters per kilogram per hour during the critical phase, adjusted hour by hour against the hematocrit, the urine output and the vital signs. Reassess constantly, because the leaky vessels will return the fluid to the circulation as the critical phase ends, when the infusion must be reduced to avoid overload.

Hypoglycemia is a constant threat in every critically ill tropical patient and it kills quietly. It arises from malaria, severe malnutrition, sepsis, liver failure, alcohol, quinine therapy, and the fasting that follows admission to a busy hospital where nobody has prescribed food. The classic signs of sweating, tremor and hunger may be absent, and the patient may simply become drowsy, agitated, convulsing or comatose, so every change in consciousness demands a glucose measurement before any other explanation. Where no glucometer exists, the safest practice is to treat on suspicion, because dextrose given unnecessarily is far less harmful than hypoglycemia missed. An adult is given 50 milliliters of 50 per cent dextrose intravenously, followed by an infusion of 10 per cent dextrose at a rate that keeps the glucose above 60 milligrams per deciliter, and a child is given 2 to 5 milliliters per kilogram of 10 per cent dextrose intravenously slowly. Where intravenous access is impossible, glucagon may be given intramuscularly according to the guideline, but this is temporary and access should continue to be sought.

Severe acute malnutrition deserves its own paragraph, because the usual resuscitation rules are wrong in these children. Their hearts are small and underpowered, their cells are short of potassium and magnesium, and a standard fluid bolus can overwhelm the circulation and cause heart failure. Fluid is therefore given conservatively, blood glucose is watched obsessively, and the temperature is kept up, because a low temperature in a malnourished child signals sepsis and a step toward death. This is also where the dose of every drug must be checked against the child's weight and the malnutrition guideline, since the standard pediatric dose may be too high. The nurse who weighs the child accurately, records the weight in the chart and refuses to let a dose be calculated from a guessed weight is doing important clinical work.

The airway is the first priority, and most of the work can be done with hands, a position and a piece of tubing. A drowsy patient should be placed in the recovery position and suctioned when secretions or vomit are audible, because the tongue and the fluid are the two commonest causes of obstruction. The head tilt and chin lift opens the airway, the jaw thrust is used where a neck injury is suspected, and an oropharyngeal airway may be inserted in the unconscious patient who has no gag reflex. Where breathing is inadequate but the patient has a pulse, bag-valve-mask ventilation with a good seal and a slow steady squeeze can support the patient for a long time if a second person helps and the mask is held correctly. A supraglottic airway may be placed where trained staff and equipment exist, but it does not replace the ability to correct the simple mechanical causes of obstruction.

Oxygen is a drug with a dose, and it should be given for a reason rather than as a reflex. The patient with an oxygen saturation below 90 per cent, the patient with severe respiratory distress and the patient in shock should receive oxygen, started at 1 to 2 liters per minute by nasal prongs and increased according to the response, with a target saturation usually between 94 and 98 per cent. In a patient with chronic lung disease the target is lower, around 88 to 92 per cent, because too much oxygen can suppress the drive to breathe. Position matters as much as the gas, because sitting the breathless patient upright reduces the work of breathing, and the patient with a one-sided lung problem may lie with the healthy lung down according to tolerance. Non-invasive support includes continuous positive airway pressure, delivered with a tight-fitting mask and a simple flow generator, which helps the patient with fluid in the lungs or a collapsing airway, and where a ventilator does not exist the nurse creates the best conditions for the patient's own breathing.

The logistics of oxygen are unglamorous and decisive. Cylinders run out at night, concentrators fail when the power fails, tubing is reused until it cracks, and the patient who needs the most oxygen is often attached to the emptiest cylinder. Count the cylinders at the start of every shift, write the remaining pressure on the chart, and know how long the current flow rate will last before it becomes a crisis. Where concentrators are the main source, ensure a backup cylinder and a working plan for the generator, because a concentrator without electricity is a piece of furniture. Split the flow between two patients only where the tubing and the guideline allow it, and never divide a single source so far that neither patient receives an adequate dose. Train whoever is on duty at night to change a cylinder, because these tasks do not appear in clinical guidelines and they decide outcomes more often than many therapies do.

Monitoring in this setting is a rhythm rather than a machine, and the rhythm is set by how sick the patient is. A patient in shock is observed every 15 minutes until stable and then every hour, and the observation includes consciousness, respiratory rate, heart rate, blood pressure, temperature, urine output, capillary refill and the intravenous site. Every drug that is given needs a corresponding watch, because each has a characteristic harm. Artesunate can cause a delayed destruction of red cells days after the patient improves, so a falling hemoglobin after apparent recovery is a known and reportable event. Quinine causes hypoglycemia and can prolong the heart's electrical recovery, so glucose and the heart rhythm are watched, and norepinephrine given through a peripheral vein can damage tissue if it leaks, so the limb is inspected every hour. The point of observation is not the record but the decision it triggers, and a nurse who writes a number without acting on it has produced paperwork rather than care.

Every intervention in this chapter has a harm attached to it, and the nurse is the person most likely to see the harm first. Fluid overload produces a rising respiratory rate, new crackles at the lung bases, a swollen abdomen and a falling oxygen saturation, and the correct response is to stop the fluid rather than merely to note the findings. Quinine must never be pushed in as a rapid injection, because rapid administration causes hypotension and arrhythmia. Norepinephrine causes tissue injury when it escapes from a vein, so the site is inspected hourly and changed at the first sign of blanching or pain. Blood transfusion carries the risk of a reaction, and the patient's temperature, urine color and breathing are watched during and after every unit. Opioids can suppress breathing, so the observation attached to them is the respiratory rate rather than the pain score alone.

Some patients cannot be treated where they are, and recognizing that early is a clinical skill rather than a failure. The patient who needs a ventilator, dialysis, surgery, blood transfusion, safe anesthesia or a diagnostic test that this facility does not have must be moved, and the decision should be made while the patient is still stable enough to travel. The patient who is deteriorating despite adequate fluid, antibiotics and oxygen is also a referral, and so is the patient whose cause remains unclear after the basic assessment. The decision belongs to the team, but the nurse is often the first to notice the pattern and should say so plainly and early. Delay is the commonest error, because a patient who could have traveled at dawn is too unstable to travel by evening.

Stabilization before transport means attending to the things that will kill the patient on the road. Secure the airway, give oxygen, secure at least one reliable intravenous line and tape it well, start antibiotics and antimalarials that are due, correct hypoglycemia, control convulsions, splint fractures, cover wounds and keep the patient warm. Insert a urinary catheter where the transfer is long, so that the urine output can be observed on arrival, and record the last measurement before departure. Send the drugs the patient will need during the journey, including the next dose of antibiotic or antimalarial, because the receiving hospital may not have the same medicines. Confirm that the vehicle, the driver, the fuel, the escort and the receiving facility are all arranged before the patient leaves, and confirm that the receiving facility has accepted the patient by name, because a transfer without acceptance is a journey to a closed door.

Communication before transfer is as important as the oxygen cylinder. Speak directly to a named clinician at the receiving facility, describe the patient in a few structured sentences, state what is needed and obtain agreement to accept. Where the telephone network is unreliable, use the radio or the messenger system the district has agreed, and confirm the arrival. Document who was spoken to, when, and what was agreed, because the record protects the patient and the team. The escort should be a person who can give oxygen, ventilate the patient if breathing stops and recognize deterioration, so a skilled nurse or clinical officer travels with the patient rather than a driver alone. Give the family the destination, the reason and the expected cost, and where transfer is impossible, say so honestly and turn the plan toward the best available care in place, including palliation.

The documentation that travels with the patient is often the only continuity the receiving team will have. Send a concise structured summary: the presenting complaint, the history that matters, the examination findings with the trends, the working diagnosis, the treatments given with times and doses, the results of every test performed, the current observations, the weight, any allergies and the contact details of the referring clinician. Include the trends rather than only the latest value, because a blood pressure of 90 that fell from 120 tells a different story from one that rose from 70. Include what has not been done and why, and include the family name and the language they speak, so that the receiving team can communicate. Sign it, date it, time it and keep a copy, because the record is a legal document and a clinical instrument at the same time.

In many of these facilities the nurse is the most skilled person present, and this chapter should say so without embarrassment. The physician may be miles away, the clinical officer may be covering three wards, and the laboratory may be closed, so the nurse assesses, decides, starts treatment and calls for help in the same breath. That reality requires clear standing orders, agreed protocols and a defined scope of practice, so that acting quickly is authorized rather than improvised. It also requires training in the skills that matter most: airway opening, bag-valve-mask ventilation, intravenous cannulation, fluid calculation, glucose measurement, and the recognition of shock. Task-sharing is not a lowering of standards but a redistribution of them, and it works when the training is real, the supervision is available and the protocols are written down.

Scarcity forces choices that wealthy hospitals avoid, and the choices must be made openly rather than by default. When there is one functioning ventilator, one unit of blood or one oxygen cylinder, the decision about who receives it should follow a stated rule such as the greatest chance of survival within the resources available, applied to all patients alike, rather than the influence of the family or the seniority of the person asking. The ethical discomfort of rationing is real, because the nurse who must choose between two patients carries a moral weight that no protocol removes, and the team should debrief afterward not to relitigate the decision but to name that distress. Stewardship and the denial of treatment are not the same thing, because withholding an antibiotic that will not help is good practice, while withholding one that will help because of cost alone deserves to be named as what it is. Where the resource is absent, the ethical obligation shifts to advocacy, because the shortage itself is a political fact that can be changed.

Documentation is the thread that holds care together when shifts change, staff rotate and the patient moves. Write in a way that a stranger can understand, using the date and time for every entry, writing legibly, avoiding shortened words that another nurse may misread, and recording the clinical reason for each action rather than only the action itself. Record the observations as numbers with units, the drugs with dose, route and time, the fluids with volume and rate, and the response to each intervention. Record the conversations with the family and the decisions they made, because consent and refusal are clinical facts. Record what you were told at handover and by whom, and record what you told the next person, because a gap in the chain of information is where patients are lost. The record is also a legal document and, in an outbreak, a surveillance instrument, so a diagnosis written plainly and a case reported promptly can protect a whole community.

Infection control is critical care, because the patient who survives the shock can die of the infection acquired in the resuscitation room. Hand hygiene before and after every contact, clean gloves for body fluids, sterile technique for every intravenous line, and disinfection of shared equipment between patients are the same acts that prevent transmission of everything from malaria to viral hemorrhagic fever. Where protective equipment is scarce, use it for the tasks that generate the most contamination, and never reuse a needle or a glove as a substitute for washing hands. Sharps are disposed of in a rigid container at the point of use, and the container is replaced before it is full, because the needlestick injury happens when a nurse tries to force one more needle inside. Waste from a critically ill patient is infectious until proven otherwise, so it follows the facility waste route to the incinerator and not to the open pit.

The equipment that exists must be kept working, and this is a nursing responsibility rather than a technical footnote. Check the oxygen cylinders, the pulse oximeter and its batteries, the suction machine, the blood pressure cuff and the thermometer at the start of every shift, and report a broken item the same day rather than the same week. Keep a small resuscitation trolley stocked with the drugs, the airway equipment, the intravenous fluids, the dextrose and the gloves, with a written list and a named person responsible for restocking. Where a solar panel, a generator or a battery bank provides power, know how many hours it will last and what it can run. Surges from an outbreak, a flood or a road crash are predictable in shape even when they are unpredictable in timing, so decide in advance which space becomes the resuscitation area, where the extra beds go and who is called in first.

Critical care in a resource-limited setting still has room for comfort, and comfort is not the opposite of treatment. Keep the patient warm, relieve pain, moisten the mouth, position for breathing, treat fever, and speak to the patient even when they seem unresponsive, because hearing is often preserved. Explain to the family what is happening in plain language, in their own language, and allow them to stay and to help with feeding and washing where it is safe. Where death is inevitable, say so clearly and gently, and make the last hours free of avoidable distress, because a death without pain and without abandonment is an outcome that can be delivered everywhere. Prepare the family for what they will see, including the tubing and the monitors that make a dying patient look frightening, and give them permission to touch the patient. The referral does not end when the vehicle leaves, so telephone the receiving facility to confirm arrival, record the answer, and follow up the survivors, because continuity is a clinical intervention that costs almost nothing.

To summarize, critical care where resources are limited is the practice of substituting observation, repetition and judgment for the machines that are absent. Triage is the first act and the most consequential, and the signs that matter most are mental status, respiratory rate, blood pressure, temperature, urine output and oxygen saturation. Sepsis demands early antibiotics, careful fluid and a vasopressor when fluids fail, while severe malaria calls for cautious fluid and artesunate, and severe dengue calls for small, frequently adjusted volumes because the fluid has left the vessels. Hypoglycemia is a constant threat and should be treated on suspicion, the airway can be supported without a ventilator through positioning, oxygen, suction and bag-valve-mask ventilation, and the patient who cannot be treated here must be stabilized, communicated about and documented before transport. The nurse is frequently the most skilled person present, so standing orders, real training and a clear scope of practice matter, and every regimen here is current guidance rather than settled truth. Check the current guideline and verify every dose before any drug, fluid or oxygen is given.
