# Chapter 5 — Travel and Migration History: The Structured Exposure History

In tropical medicine the history is not the preliminary to the diagnosis. It decides which diagnosis is even possible. A patient can be examined from head to foot, and the examination will offer fever, perhaps a rash, perhaps an enlarged spleen, and none of that will distinguish dengue from scrub typhus, leptospirosis, acute HIV infection, or malaria. The exposure history is what separates them, because it tells you which organisms the patient could have met and when. Where the laboratory is a microscope and a refrigerator that works most days, the history carries even more of the weight.

Take the history in a fixed sequence, because the same order every time is the only reliable way to avoid leaving out the decisive question. Begin with the itinerary rather than the symptoms, since the itinerary is neutral ground and the patient can answer it without feeling accused. Ask the patient to walk you from departure to return, then close the gaps place by place, asking for towns, districts, islands, and villages rather than countries alone. Only then move to behaviors, then food and water, then animals and insects, then blood, needles, and sex, and finally to prevention. Save the intimate questions for the end and ask them in the same matter of fact voice you used for the flight schedule.

Destination is where geographic risk mapping begins, and the map is a clinical instrument. A country name is too blunt, because Brazil contains both a dengue epidemic in a coastal city and a rural Amazon basin where malaria and cutaneous leishmaniasis are ordinary events. Ask for specific places, then place them on the maps you carry, the malaria transmission maps, the distribution of dengue and chikungunya, the belt of schistosomiasis, and the range of Chagas disease. Then ask what the patient did there, because the same district carries different risks for a rice farmer and a conference attendee. The map tells you what is possible and the activity tells you what is probable.

Dates matter more than most clinicians expect, so write them down exactly. Record the day of departure, the day of return, and the day of onset, then calculate the interval between return and onset, because duration shapes the differential. Season determines which vectors were active and which water sources were contaminated, so ask about the rainy or dry season, about flooding, and about sleeping under a roof or outdoors. Altitude belongs here too, because the malaria line sits at roughly 1500 to 2000 meters and above it the risk falls away, while altitude illness becomes a diagnosis in its own right. Ask about headache, breathlessness, or confusion at height.

The return date anchors the whole differential, because every infection has an incubation window and the window tells you what the patient could not have acquired. The short group, measured in days, includes dengue, chikungunya, influenza, cholera, shigellosis, and the rickettsial diseases. The middle group, measured in one to three weeks, includes malaria, typhoid fever, leptospirosis, hepatitis A and E, acute HIV infection, brucellosis, Q fever, and yaws, the busiest window in a returning traveler clinic. The long group, measured in months, includes tuberculosis, leishmaniasis, filariasis, rabies, schistosomiasis, amoebic liver abscess, hepatitis B, Chagas disease, and echinococcosis. A fever that begins eight months after a trip cannot be acute dengue, and a fever that begins three days after a trip is not tuberculosis. Say the interval aloud and let it order the differential before anyone orders a test.

Two refinements of the incubation idea keep you out of trouble. The first is that the relevant interval is often measured from the last possible exposure rather than from the return date, so ask when the patient was last in a rural area, a freshwater lake, a market, or a household with animals. The second is the difference between the incubation period and the time a test becomes positive, because a patient with malaria can have a negative blood film on the first day of fever and a positive one on the third. When the interval does not fit the diagnosis you are considering, do not force it. Reconsider the exposure history and let the timing reopen the question.

Rural or urban is the next fork in the road, and accommodation is its practical form. The mosquito that transmits malaria, Anopheles, bites between dusk and dawn and breeds in rural and periurban water, so unscreened housing near standing water and the absence of an insecticide-treated bed net raise the risk. The mosquito that transmits dengue, chikungunya, Zika, and yellow fever, Aedes aegypti, bites during the day and breeds in domestic containers, so that risk follows the patient into the center of a city. Ask where the patient slept and whether there was air conditioning, screens, a net, a fan, a thatched roof, or a tent. Ask about running water, a flush toilet, a latrine, or open defecation, because that answer moves the enteric and helminth diseases up or down the list.

Food and water questions should name specific foods and practices, because general questions produce general answers. Ask about tap water, ice, unboiled water, bottled water, and whether the seal was intact, and about salads washed in tap water. Ask about raw or undercooked freshwater fish, which carries the liver flukes Clonorchis and Opisthorchis, raw crab, which carries Paragonimus, and water plants such as watercress, which carry Fasciola. Ask about unpasteurized milk and soft cheese, which carry Brucella, and undercooked pork, which carries the tapeworm Taenia solium, and about raw meat and bushmeat in general. Ask about street food, market food, and food from a wedding or a funeral, because shared meals explain shared illness.

Animal contact is a short set of questions with a long reach. Ask about dogs, cats, monkeys, bats, rodents, cattle, goats, sheep, and wild animals, and ask about the circumstances, a bite, a scratch, a lick on broken skin, a slaughter, a butchering, or a visit to a cave. A dog bite abroad raises rabies and tetanus, unpasteurized milk raises brucellosis, contact with rodents and their urine raises leptospirosis, contact with sheep and goats around a birth raises Q fever, and a cave full of bird or bat droppings raises histoplasmosis. Ask whether the patient cleaned a barn or a chicken coop, and whether they waded in water that animals could have contaminated. Ask about bites the patient dismissed, because a small bite from a bat may not be noticed and still requires attention.

Insect avoidance is where you learn how seriously the patient took the risk, and where you must not moralize. Ask what repellent was used, how often, whether a bed net was used and whether it was treated with insecticide, whether the net had holes, and whether the room was screened or air conditioned. Ask about bites, because a patient who remembers being bitten repeatedly is giving you useful information, while a patient who noticed no bites gives almost nothing, since many vectors bite painlessly or at night. Ask separately about day biting and night biting, which tells you whether to think about Aedes or Anopheles, and ask about the sandfly, the tsetse fly, the triatomine bug, the blackfly, and ticks. Ask whether the patient walked in tall grass, sat on logs, slept on the ground, or kept livestock. Ask whether malaria chemoprophylaxis was taken at all, and whether the decision not to take it was the patient's, a clinician's, or an accident of a late appointment.

Then move to the exposures that travel through blood and skin, and ask them plainly. Ask about new sexual partners during travel or after return, about condom use, and about partners whose infection status is unknown, because HIV and AIDS, syphilis, hepatitis B, and hepatitis C all belong in the differential of a fever after travel. Ask about tattoos, piercings, scarification, traditional circumcision, cupping, and any procedure that broke the skin, and ask where it was done and with what instruments. Ask about injections for any reason, including vitamins, pain relief, and cosmetic treatments, and about dental work, dialysis, surgery, and blood transfusion, because the safety of the blood supply and the sterility of needles vary between countries and between clinics within a country. Ask about needle sharing and drug use without a lecture.

Prevention is the last section of the itinerary and often the most revealing. Ask which vaccines the patient received before travel, how many doses, and when the last dose was given, and ask to see the yellow fever certificate and the vaccination card if the patient carries them. Then ask about malaria chemoprophylaxis by name, because brand names and generic names are remembered differently, and by schedule, because the schedule is where adherence fails. Doxycycline is usually taken as 100 milligrams once daily, mefloquine as 250 milligrams once weekly, and atovaquone with proguanil as one tablet once daily, each with its own rules about when to start, when to stop, and whether to take it with food. Ask whether the patient started before travel, continued after leaving the area, missed days, or stopped early, and whether any tablets remain in the bottle, because the bottle is a more honest witness than memory. Ask what happened when they took it, and treat the answer as clinical data rather than as a confession.

Before you act on any of this, remember how the guidance is made. The World Health Organization, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control all publish recommendations for chemoprophylaxis and vaccination, and those recommendations are revised as resistance patterns, drug availability, and safety data change. Countries adapt the guidance to their own epidemiology, so national programs differ. Doses and schedules quoted in any course, including this one, describe what guidance said at the time of writing and not what it says today. Check the current guideline, and the current national guideline for the place where you are prescribing, before you write the prescription. The same discipline applies to the treatment decisions that this history will later support.

The single most important category in travel medicine is the traveler who is visiting friends and relatives, and it deserves its own paragraph because it is so often missed. These travelers are usually members of a diaspora returning to a country of origin or to their parents' country, and the chart often records only the words visiting family, which hides everything that matters. They stay longer than tourists, stay in private homes, travel to rural districts, drink the local water, eat the local food, and sleep without nets when the household has none. They frequently travel with children who may never have had a consultation before departure, and those children are the least likely to have received chemoprophylaxis or routine vaccines. They often delay seeking care, because they do not think of themselves as travelers, and they are less likely to have taken chemoprophylaxis at all. They may also have partial immunity that makes malaria present atypically, so when you hear visiting family, raise your suspicion rather than lowering it.

The migrant and refugee history follows a different structure, because it is a history of movement over years rather than of one trip. Ask about the country of origin, the specific region and language, the route of transit and every country crossed, the duration of each stage, the camps, the settlements, and the time since arrival. The stage of migration changes the differential, because the first weeks bring acute infections and untreated conditions from the journey, the first months bring diseases with long incubation periods and the consequences of crowding and poor sanitation, and the years afterward bring latent tuberculosis, chronic hepatitis, schistosomiasis, and Chagas disease. Ask about the conditions of the journey, including detention, camps with shared water and latrines, boats, and crowded apartments. Remember the healthy migrant effect, the tendency for people well enough to travel to look healthier than the population they left, so a normal examination early after arrival proves very little.

There is a difference between what a chart asks and what a person will tell you, and closing that gap is a clinical skill. An intake form asks for country of origin and produces a word, and the word is then treated as though it were a history. A patient may not disclose migration status, may fear consequences for themselves or for family members, may have been instructed not to discuss the journey, or may simply not consider the past relevant to a present fever. Trust is built by explaining why the question is asked, by asking it in privacy, by using a professional interpreter rather than a family member or a child, and by making clear that the answer will be used for care. Ask about the past in the patient's own chronology and let them decide how much detail to give. If a person declines to answer, document the refusal without judgment and make it easy to answer at the next visit.

Adherence questions fail when they sound like an accusation and succeed when they sound like a request for information about a difficult task. Do not ask whether the patient took the tablets as prescribed, but ask which tablets were taken yesterday and which were missed in the last two weeks. Ask what got in the way, and be prepared for the real answers, including cost, a side effect the patient did not want to mention, vomiting, forgetfulness, a broken routine, a belief that the tablets were no longer needed, advice from a relative, or a pregnancy that changed the calculation. Ask whether the patient stopped early because the trip ended, because the tablets ran out, or because someone said the medicine was dangerous, and ask about traditional remedies and antibiotics bought without a prescription. Then say clearly that the answer changes the interpretation of every test you are about to order, because this is not a moral question but a pharmacological one.

Certain exposures are almost never volunteered, and you must ask for them by name. Sexual contacts, needle use, tattoos, piercings, scarification, traditional healing practices that break the skin, injections from unlicensed practitioners, and terminations of pregnancy are all in this category. So are less obvious ones, such as drinking water from a stream, swimming in a lake where schistosomiasis is transmitted, walking barefoot in soil where hookworm is common, handling a dead animal, and nursing a sick relative at home without protection. Patients omit these things because they are ashamed, because they think the detail is trivial, because no one asked before, because the memory is painful, or because they do not know that a freshwater lake is a route of infection. Ask in a way that normalizes the answer, and explain the link to the illness in one sentence, because a patient who understands why you are asking will answer more truthfully.

Prophylaxis has adverse effects, and knowing them serves two purposes, because it explains why adherence failed and it tells you what to watch for in a patient who did take the drug. Mefloquine can cause vivid dreams, anxiety, insomnia, dizziness, and rarely neuropsychiatric symptoms severe enough to stop the drug, and those effects explain many of the quiet discontinuations you will uncover. Doxycycline commonly causes nausea, heartburn, and sensitivity of the skin to sunlight, and it can irritate the esophagus if it is swallowed with too little water or taken while lying down, so the counseling detail matters as much as the dose. Atovaquone with proguanil is generally well tolerated but can cause abdominal discomfort, and it is taken with food so that it is absorbed. Ask what the patient actually experienced rather than what they were warned about, and write the answer in the record. In a patient who took the drug correctly and still became ill, the exposure history rather than the adherence becomes the focus.

Document the exposure history so that the next clinician can use it without repeating the interview. Use a consistent sequence in the note, the dates, the places, the accommodation, the food and water, the animals, the insects, the blood and skin exposures, the sexual history, and the prevention taken. Write the negatives as deliberately as the positives, because the absence of freshwater contact or of a dog bite changes the differential, and a negative that was never recorded will be repeated as an unknown. Record the exact interval between the last exposure and the onset of illness, and note who provided the information, whether the patient, a relative, or a document. Many patients carry vaccination cards, prophylaxis boxes, and laboratory reports in another language, so ask to copy these documents and reconcile them with the account, noting any discrepancy rather than choosing one version. Avoid vague phrases such as unwell after travel, because the next clinician must be able to act on what you write.

The emergency department is where tropical diagnoses are most often missed, and the misses follow a small number of patterns. The first is the failure to ask about travel at all, or the failure to ask in a way that produces the truth, so the patient who says no because they live locally is never asked where they were born or when they last visited family. The second is anchoring, the moment a plausible common diagnosis such as influenza, a urinary infection, or gastroenteritis is accepted and the exposure history is never taken. The third is the single negative malaria test, treated as a final answer when the blood film needs to be repeated over 48 to 72 hours. The fourth is discharge before the laboratory result returns, and the fifth is the absence of triage criteria that would move a febrile returning traveler into a rapid assessment pathway.

There are traps on the other side as well, and they are just as costly. The clinician who has just read about an exotic disease may pursue it while the common local illness goes untreated. The patient who took chemoprophylaxis may be assumed not to have malaria, although prophylaxis reduces risk without abolishing it, and the patient with partial immunity may present with a mild illness that is nevertheless severe malaria. The patient with a fever and a rash may be seated next to others before anyone considers measles, dengue, or a viral hemorrhagic fever, when infection control should have begun at triage. The person whose chart lists a chronic condition may have their fever attributed to that condition when it deserves its own assessment. The antidote is the same in every case, which is to ask the structured questions early, write the answers down, and repeat the assessment when the picture does not fit.

Once the history is on the table, the examination becomes efficient because you know what to look for. Undress the patient enough to see the skin, and look for an eschar, a rash, a burrow, a nodule, a migrating swelling, a wound, or a scar the patient did not mention, and look at the palms, the soles, the scalp, and the genital area, which are the places examinations miss. Examine the eyes for conjunctival suffusion, jaundice, and pallor, feel the neck, the axillae, and the groin for lymph nodes, and palpate the abdomen for the spleen and the liver, which are the two organs that change the differential in a febrile patient. Assess mental status, respiratory rate, hydration, capillary refill, and urine output, because these decide how quickly the patient must be treated regardless of the eventual diagnosis. Examine the nervous system when the history suggests trypanosomiasis, leprosy, or a neurological complication, and examine the joints when it suggests chikungunya or brucellosis. Let the history choose the examination rather than performing the same routine on every patient.

From the history you should be able to build a short chain that runs from exposure to organism to test to treatment. A patient who waded in fresh water where Schistosoma haematobium is transmitted and who now has blood in the urine needs urine filtration, and a patient who swam in a lake where Schistosoma mansoni is transmitted and who now has bloody diarrhea needs stool examination in addition. A patient who walked barefoot in soil where hookworm is common and who now has iron deficiency anemia needs stool examination rather than iron alone, because treating the anemia without treating the worm is only a temporary fix. A patient who handled a sick animal and now has an undulant fever needs blood cultures and serology, and a prolonged combination treatment is the eventual decision. A patient with a painless ulcer and contact with a person who had a similar ulcer should have yaws and syphilis considered together. The chain is what converts an interesting history into a clinical plan.

Adapt the history to the person in front of you, because the same questions carry different weight in different bodies. In pregnancy, ask about the trimester, the antenatal care received, and the timing of travel relative to conception, because malaria in pregnancy, dengue in pregnancy, Zika, and congenital Chagas disease have their own windows and consequences, and because many drugs are avoided or adjusted. In children, ask who traveled with the child, what the child ate and drank, whether the child was breastfed throughout, what vaccinations were given, and whether the child played in soil or water, and remember that a child born abroad may have been exposed to infections the parents never considered. In the immunocompromised patient, ask about the degree and duration of immunosuppression and any planned transplantation or chemotherapy, because the exposure history must be completed before suppression begins. In older adults, ask about medicines and chronic illnesses, because the risk of severe disease and of drug interaction both rise.

The exposure history is not a single event. Take it early, write it down, and return to it as the illness declares itself, because a detail that seemed irrelevant on the first day can become the whole answer on the fourth. Patients remember things between visits, and family members who were not present at the first interview often supply the crucial fact about a lake, a dog, a funeral, or a course of tablets. When the working diagnosis is not fitting, the productive move is usually to repeat the exposure history rather than to order a broader panel of tests. Ask about illness in the household or the travel group, since a shared exposure can explain two cases at once, and document each revision with its date.

The nursing role in all of this is larger than it may appear. In many clinics the nurse takes the history, and in many settings the nurse runs the consultation before travel and the one after return, teaches the patient what to record, administers the vaccines, and explains the tablets. That position carries the responsibility to ask the structured questions well, to make privacy and interpretation possible, to write the record so that it survives the shift change, and to speak the exposure findings to the team in a way that changes the plan. It also carries the responsibility to notice when a patient is deteriorating between rounds, because a history that predicts severe disease helps only if someone is watching closely enough to act. The nurse is often the only person who asks about cost, about a tablet that was stopped, and about the child who was not vaccinated.

There is an unglamorous logistics layer that decides whether any of this works in practice. The vaccination card exists because the record of doses must travel with the patient, and the cold chain fails where power is unreliable, which is why a vaccine given abroad may not have produced protection. The prophylaxis bottle exists because the tablet must be taken on a schedule nobody is watching, which is why the bottle is better evidence than the memory. The clinic record exists because the next clinician needs the itinerary, and the record fails when there is no form to hold the itinerary and no time to fill it in. The interpreter exists because the history is conducted in language, and the consultation fails when the interpreter is a child or a neighbor with their own agenda. Designing these small systems is as much a part of tropical nursing as recognizing an eschar.

Monitoring after the history is taken is where nursing attention converts assessment into safety. If malaria is possible, the blood film must be repeated over 48 to 72 hours while the patient is observed, and the nurse is the person who notices that the second and third samples were never sent. If a patient is started on treatment before the diagnosis is confirmed, the nurse watches for the response, for the rash that suggests a drug reaction, for the diarrhea that follows broad spectrum antibiotics, and for the deterioration that means the working diagnosis is wrong. If a patient is discharged, the nurse ensures that the instructions name the symptom that should bring the patient back, the fever that persists, the bleeding, the drowsiness, the reduced urine output, or the breathlessness. Document the monitoring plan and the threshold for escalation, because a plan that is not written depends on a particular person being on shift.

To summarize, the structured exposure history is the diagnostic instrument of tropical medicine, and everything else supports it. Take it in the same sequence every time, covering destination with enough geographic detail to place the patient on a risk map, exact dates and duration and season and altitude, rural or urban location and type of accommodation, food and water, animal contact, insect avoidance, sexual and blood borne exposures, and the vaccinations and chemoprophylaxis actually taken. Use the interval between the last exposure and the onset of illness to order the differential, and let the incubation window tell you which diagnoses are possible before you test for any of them. Notice the traveler who is visiting friends and relatives, and take the migrant and refugee history as a history of years rather than days, asking in privacy with a professional interpreter. Ask about adherence and about adverse effects without accusation, document the positives and the negatives so that the next clinician can act on them, and include the dose verification discipline that current guidance demands. Watch for the emergency department traps, and remember that the history belongs to the patient and to the public health system at the same time.
