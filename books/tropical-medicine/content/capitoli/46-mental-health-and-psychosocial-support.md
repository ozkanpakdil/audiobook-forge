# Chapter 46 — Mental Health and Psychosocial Support

In most of the places this course is meant for, specialist mental health care is not merely stretched, it is largely absent. The World Health Organization estimates that in low and middle income countries, between 76 and 85 per cent of people with a severe mental disorder receive no treatment at all in the course of a year. Many of those countries spend less than 2 per cent of their health budgets on mental health, and in some of them a single psychiatrist serves a population of 1 million or more. The nearest psychiatric bed may lie 2 days of travel away, and the nearest counselor may not exist. Understand what this means for your practice: for most of the people you will ever care for, you are the only mental health worker they will ever meet.

The World Health Organization has answered this gap with a program built to be delivered by people like you rather than by specialists, and its name organizes this chapter. It is called the mental health gap action program of the World Health Organization, and its companion volume, the intervention guide, converts what specialists know into step by step instructions for nonspecialist clinicians. It covers depression, psychosis, epilepsy, dementia, substance use, self-harm and suicide, and the psychological problems of children. Its central principle is task sharing: care moves from the psychiatrist to the nurse, and from the nurse to the community health worker, through protocols tested for exactly that purpose. When you manage a disturbed mind with a nurse's license and a thin formulary, you are not improvising; you are following a tested protocol, and that changes both your confidence and your duty of care. The regimens inside it are revised as evidence grows and guidance differs between countries, so check the current edition before you prescribe anything you hear here.

Emergency medicine worked out long ago that survival after cardiac arrest depends less on the brilliance of the hospital than on a chain of survival: early recognition, early resuscitation by whoever is standing there, early defibrillation, and only then transfer to advanced care, each link failing if the one before it fails. Mental health care in a remote setting obeys the same logic, and chain of survival thinking will organize everything you do. The first link is recognition, because a depression nobody names is a depression nobody treats. The second is early action with what exists, a conversation, a tablet, a family conference, started today rather than after a referral that may never come. The third is keeping the person safe and connected while time does its work, and the fourth is follow-up, the long, unglamorous carrying of the case. Your hospital training pulls you toward the heroic end of the chain, the transfer and the specialist, but the outcomes are decided earlier, in the village, by you.

Depression is the condition you will see most, and it hides. The textbook picture of low mood, loss of pleasure, broken sleep, poor appetite, fatigue, guilt, and slowed thinking is real, but in many cultures the doorway complaint is physical: heaviness in the body, burning in the chest, generalized aches, headache, or the phrase you will hear across three continents, thinking too much. Two questions screen for it as well as longer instruments in most hands: over the past weeks, has your mood been low, and are you still able to enjoy anything. Ask them while you take the blood pressure, and ask them of everyone with a chronic disease, because depression travels with tuberculosis, HIV, diabetes, and the social wreckage of displacement. A person can be sad about an illness and also have an illness of sadness, and the second is treatable.

Before the management, the mechanism, because it explains every step that follows. Depression behaves like a self-reinforcing cycle: bereavement, poverty, illness, or violence drains the capacity for pleasure and action, the person withdraws, withdrawal removes the activities that once gave reward and connection, and the empty life deepens the mood, which deepens the withdrawal. Meanwhile the stress response stays switched on, with the poor sleep and constant vigilance that implies. This is why moralizing fails, and why treatment works on the cycle rather than on character. The interventions ahead all do one of two things: they put action back before the mood is ready, or they rebuild the ability to solve the problems that keep the state low. Medication shifts the biology just enough for the psychology to take hold.

Do not diagnose depression in a vacuum, because several conditions wear its costume. Anemia, hypothyroidism, chronic malaria, advanced HIV infection, and malnutrition all flatten a person, and so do drugs: mefloquine given for malaria can produce depression, anxiety, and nightmares, efavirenz given in antiretroviral therapy does the same in a minority, and steroids, alcohol, and cannabis belong on the list. Check the hemoglobin if you can, look for fever and parasites, review every medicine the person takes, and ask the alcohol questions before you settle on the label. Ten minutes of exclusion at the start prevents 3 months of treating the wrong thing.

For mild and moderate depression the first line treatment is not a drug, and this is the most liberating fact in the chapter: the psychological interventions that work can be delivered by a trained nonspecialist, which in your setting means you. The intervention guide names problem-solving treatment, behavioral activation, and interpersonal therapy, individually or in groups, over roughly 6 to 8 sessions. These are structured skills, learned in days and refined with supervision, not mysterious arts, and the evidence for them in low and middle income settings is strong. Group interpersonal therapy has been delivered by trained lay workers to mothers in rural Africa with results that rival specialist care. Say it plainly: the best depression treatment available to your community is a conversation you can be trained to have.

Behavioral activation is the easiest to learn, so take it as the model. You help the person list the small actions that once gave pleasure or mastery, walking to the market, tending a goat, greeting a neighbor, and then schedule them back into the week before motivation returns, because action leads mood rather than the reverse. The plan starts absurdly small, one walk, one shared meal, and grows each week. Attendance matters more than brilliance, and a missed session is resumed, never punished. Mechanically you are re-coupling the person to the sources of reward the illness cut off, and the mood follows over weeks. If the person cannot reach you, teach the same structure to a community health worker and supervise it by telephone or radio.

Problem-solving treatment does the same work from the other direction. The person brings the 2 or 3 problems that grind them down, debt, a hostile in-law, an unmanageable illness, and you teach a fixed sequence: define the problem concretely, list every possible response without judging any, choose one, plan it, carry it out, and review the result the next week. The sequence matters because rumination, the circular helpless thinking that maintains depression, is replaced by defined steps, and agency returns with each step that ends in anything at all. It travels well across cultures, because people everywhere know how to make a list and make a choice. A typical course is 6 sessions and needs no materials the clinic does not already own.

Add medication when depression is moderate to severe, when there is hopelessness or suicidal thinking, when functioning has collapsed, or when sessions cannot be delivered. The 2 drugs most likely to be on your shelf, both named in the intervention guide, are fluoxetine and amitriptyline. Fluoxetine usually starts at 20 milligrams by mouth once daily, in the morning because it can disturb sleep, and is later raised to 40 or 60 milligrams in some people. Amitriptyline starts low, 25 to 50 milligrams by mouth at night, using its sedative effect to restore sleep, and climbs over 1 to 2 weeks toward a usual range of 75 to 150 milligrams at night. In older adults, start at one half of these doses and climb more slowly. Confirm every figure against the current national guideline and the current edition of the guide before prescribing, because regimens are revised and formulations differ.

Tell the person 3 things before the first tablet, because the telling itself improves adherence. Benefit takes time: 2 to 4 weeks before improvement, and a fair trial is 6 to 8 weeks at an adequate dose. The first days can bring nausea, jitteriness, headache, or insomnia with fluoxetine, and drowsiness, dry mouth, constipation, and dizziness on standing with amitriptyline; most of these fade. And stopping must be discussed before starting, because treatment continues for at least 6 to 9 months after the person feels well, longer after repeated episodes, and abrupt cessation causes a withdrawal that families mistake for relapse. Amitriptyline is dangerous in overdose, acting on the heart, so for any suicidal person dispense a limited quantity and let the family hold the bottle. Review the other drugs the person takes, because fluoxetine adds its serotonergic effect to medicines such as tramadol. Write the start date, the dose, and the review date in the register the day you prescribe.

Anxiety in most of the world speaks through the body before it speaks through the mind, and if you are unprepared you will chase it with the wrong tools. Palpitations, chest tightness, dizziness, breathlessness, burning in the epigastrium, numbness of the hands, heat rising in the head, and the conviction of physical disease: these are the ordinary presentations, and the mechanism is the threat response, the fight or flight system firing without a threat, with all the adrenaline of a stampede and nowhere to run. The symptoms are real physiology, misattributed. Your first task is exclusion, and it is quick: anemia, malaria, hyperthyroidism, heart failure, asthma, hypoglycemia, and alcohol withdrawal all masquerade as anxiety, and each can be suspected in a remote clinic. Once the physical causes are set aside, name the condition, explain the adrenaline, and say plainly that the racing heart will not injure them. That explanation is not a preamble to treatment; in anxiety it often is the treatment.

Then teach the skills. Slow breathing, about 6 breaths a minute with a longer exhale, reliably douses the threat response; teach it with your hands, count with the person, and set it as homework 3 times a day regardless of symptoms. Add progressive muscle relaxation, problem-solving treatment when the worries circle a solvable problem, and a graded return to the situations the person has begun to avoid, since avoidance feeds anxiety the way withdrawal feeds depression. For a panic attack in front of you, stay unhurried, breathe with the person, ground them in what can be seen and touched, and let the attack finish, which it will, in minutes. Benzodiazepines such as diazepam have a narrow role, the first days of overwhelming distress, alcohol withdrawal, or catastrophe, in short tapering courses of roughly 2 weeks, because dependence arrives quickly where refill decisions are made by whoever is present.

Psychosis arrives loudly in a village, and the volume matters, because the crowd that gathers can turn an emergency into a catastrophe. You will meet agitation, fear, disorganized speech, answering voices or unseen presences, fixed false beliefs, refusal of food, and sometimes a person roped by exhausted relatives. Before anything else, calm the situation, and understand that this step precedes medication the way clearing the airway precedes ventilation. Speak slowly and simply, let a single person speak at a time, and move the person if possible to a quiet, dim corner, offering water and removing anything that could become a weapon. Bring in the relative the person trusts most, who may persuade where you cannot. Many agitated people settle with this alone, and every patient you calm with your voice is one saved from a struggle, an injury, or a forced injection given in front of the entire village.

Before you diagnose acute psychosis, exclude the brain insults that mimic it, because in the tropics an acute psychiatric presentation is often a medical emergency in costume. Cerebral malaria, hypoglycemia, meningitis, sepsis, urinary infection in an older person, head injury, alcohol withdrawal, intoxication with cannabis, solvents, or khat, and drugs such as mefloquine and steroids can all produce agitation, hallucination, and disorganization. Check the temperature, the glucose, the pupils, the neck, the hydration, and any fever in the preceding days, and look for a seizure that was missed. A person acutely confused for the first time, with fever or fluctuating awareness, is delirious until proven otherwise, and the cause is usually found with your hands, a thermometer, and a rapid test. Treating the malaria is then the treatment of the psychosis, while haloperidol given by reflex, before the examination, can mask the very signs you need.

When medication is needed after de-escalation, the drug most likely to be on your shelf, and the one the intervention guide builds around, is haloperidol. For severe agitation, 5 milligrams intramuscularly, repeated if needed after 30 to 60 minutes until the person is calm, is the usual course, starting lower in small or older people; by mouth, 2 to 5 milligrams 2 or 3 times a day is a common range. Alternatives, where they exist, are chlorpromazine, 25 to 50 milligrams by mouth twice a day, and risperidone, 2 milligrams twice a day, often better tolerated, especially in the old. If the person can swallow, the oral route is always preferable to an injection, in dignity and in safety alike. Pair the first haloperidol doses, as many protocols direct, with an anticholinergic to prevent dystonia, and keep that pairing when you order supplies. As with every dose in this chapter, verify against the current guideline before prescribing.

The adverse effects of antipsychotics are not footnotes; they are the reason families abandon treatment. Acute dystonia, a sudden spasm of the neck, jaw, or eyes within days of the first doses, terrifies relatives, who read it as the drug poisoning the person; it is reversed with biperiden, 2.5 to 5 milligrams intramuscularly, repeatable. Akathisia is subtler and more dangerous, an inner restlessness that looks exactly like the agitation you were treating, so giving more drug makes the person worse while you believe you are helping; the correct move is a lower dose. Parkinsonism, the slowed, stiff, shuffling state, arrives over weeks, and tardive dyskinesia, the writhing movements of face and tongue, over months and years, and neither is quickly reversible. Haloperidol also prolongs a segment of the heart's electrical cycle, the interval seen on the electrocardiogram, and in heat it impairs sweating and temperature control, so in the hot season insist on water and shade for every patient on antipsychotics. Chlorpromazine adds sedation, low blood pressure on standing, and marked sun sensitivity; risperidone adds weight gain and, in the old, falls. Examine for movement effects at every visit, keep the dose at the lowest that works, and record what you find, because that examination is the safety net.

Long term care of psychosis is family care, because the family, not you, provides the daily treatment. Teach them that the person has an illness of the brain, not a curse, not a contagion, and that it usually responds to medication taken daily and for a long time. Ask them to learn the early signature of relapse, poor sleep, withdrawal, suspiciousness, because early action at home can prevent the crisis entirely. Never stop an antipsychotic abruptly, because rebound psychosis punishes the person for the health system's failure, and when you rotate posts, write the story of the case in the register so the next nurse continues rather than restarts.

Suicide hides best of all the risks, and the core skill is to ask about it directly, in plain words, because the fear that asking plants the idea has been tested repeatedly and is wrong. Ask: have you felt so low that you wished you were dead. Have you thought of ending your life. Have you thought about how you would do it. Do you have the means at home, tablets, a pesticide, a rope, a weapon. Have you tried before, and what has stopped you so far. The answers place the person on a ladder, from passive wishes, to active thoughts, to a plan, to preparation, and each rung raises the urgency of your response. Do not promise secrecy you cannot keep, and do not flinch; your steadiness is part of the treatment.

What you do with the answers is mostly practical, not medical, and it is where a nurse outperforms an entire specialist system. Remove the means, and in a rural community the decisive one is the pesticide, because organophosphate self-poisoning fills district hospitals and kills more surely than almost any method available at home, so work with the family to lock away chemicals, tablets, and tools. Dispense sub-lethal quantities, days of amitriptyline rather than months, held by a relative. For a person with a plan, arrange continuous company, a family roster day and night if needed, and review the situation yourself every few days. For passive wishes alone, a scheduled review within the week, plus treatment of the underlying depression, is usually the right intensity. Say directly that these precautions are how you keep the person alive while the treatment starts working, and remember that alcohol dissolves restraint, so a low mood plus a drinking habit multiplies the risk. Where the community will listen, village level means restriction, locked pesticide stores and controlled sales, saves lives year after year, and it is legitimate nursing advocacy. Self-harm in adolescents, cutting or burning without intent to die, gets the same direct questions, because the behaviors overlap more than they differ, and the underlying distress is the treatable thing; balance the young person's confidentiality against their safety, share what must be shared with a caregiver, and say exactly what you are sharing and why.

Substance use belongs here because it is the most common mental health problem in most of your communities, and because a nurse can do more about it in 5 minutes than a specialist ever will. Screen routinely: how many days in the past month did you drink, and how much on a typical day. The brief intervention, tested by the World Health Organization across cultures, has a fixed shape: give feedback on what the screening showed, place responsibility with the person, give clear advice, offer a menu of options, listen with empathy, and end by strengthening their confidence in their own ability to change. A single conversation in that shape measurably reduces drinking at 6 months, a claim most drugs in this book cannot make. For khat in the Horn of Africa, cannabis everywhere, and solvents among street youth, the same structure holds in a different language, and harm reduction beats a lecture that changes nothing.

Dependence is a different matter, because stopping suddenly is where drinking kills. Untreated alcohol withdrawal escalates from tremor and sweating to seizures and then to delirium, with confusion, fever, and terror, and the last stage carries real mortality, so a dependent drinker who must stop needs cover. The usual agent is diazepam, a common regimen being 10 milligrams by mouth 4 times on the first day, tapering over 5 to 7 days and adjusted to sedation, and before any sugar or feeding, give thiamine, 100 milligrams by mouth or intramuscularly, because thiamine deficiency is why withdrawn drinkers lose their memory and collapse. Liver disease travels with alcohol, which matters because tuberculosis and HIV treatment also stress the liver, and the combination is the routine problem of district medicine rather than the exception. These regimens vary between programs, so check your current national protocol before managing a withdrawal at home.

Grief fills your clinic in communities carrying war, famine, epidemic, and displacement, and your first duty is to stop treating it as an illness. Normal grief comes in waves, triggered by reminders and mixed with moments of ease and even laughter, and it does not flatten a person permanently. Communities hold grief with ritual, the wake, the prayer, the mourning period, the shared meal, and one of the cruelest wounds of displacement and of epidemic control is that these rituals get severed, the burial no one could attend in the viral hemorrhagic fever outbreak, the mourning cut short in a camp. Where you can, protect and enable the ritual rather than replacing it with counseling, because the ritual is the therapy the community already owns, and your psychological vocabulary is a poor substitute. Grief becomes an illness worth treating when, after weeks, the waves stop and the flatness stays, unrelenting guilt, self-loathing, no relief at all, and thoughts of death, and that is depression, and it responds to the depression treatments of this chapter. The famous stages of grief are one observer's description, not a schedule, and a person who does not follow them is not failing.

Trauma is where untrained helpers do the most damage, because the instinct, to have the person tell the story, to relive it in detail, to ventilate, feels helpful and is often harmful. The tested framework for the first days and weeks is psychological first aid, described by the World Health Organization and embedded in the emergency guidelines of the Inter-Agency Standing Committee: promote safety, a place to sleep, food, protection from further harm; promote calm, your unhurried voice and clear information; promote connectedness, finding the family, the children, the neighbors; promote self-efficacy, asking what the person needs and letting them do it; and promote hope, with concrete plans rather than promises. Notice that none of this requires describing the event at all. Offer to listen, never force the telling, and never signal that the distress is a disorder in the making, because the great majority recover from terrible events with support and time and never need a diagnosis.

Now the trap, named plainly because whole programs have fallen into it: do not run single-session debriefing after an incident, the structured session in which everyone exposed recounts the event once, in sequence, in the days afterward. Trials have been run on this intervention; it does not prevent post-traumatic stress disorder, and in several studies it made outcomes worse, plausibly by forcing processing before the mind is ready and by labeling normal reactions pathological. It persists because it feels humane and gives an agency something visible to do. What works instead is watchful support: psychological first aid in the first days, problem-solving treatment for those still struggling after a few weeks, and, for the minority who, a month or 2 on, still carry nightmares, avoidance, and a body that startles at everything, a trauma-focused therapy from someone trained in it, or a referral if one exists. Keep a list of who was exposed, follow them up, and let time do most of the work under your supervision.

Epilepsy is not a mental illness, but in many communities you will be its only defender, and the two travel together in stigma. The seizure, and the confusion, fear, or aggression that follow, get read as madness or possession, children are barred from school, adults from marriage and work, and burns from falling into the cooking fire are a signature you will meet. Your tasks are concrete: teach first aid, do not restrain, force nothing into the mouth, roll the person to the side, protect the head, time the seizure, and stay until alertness returns; treat with anticonvulsants and keep the supply continuous, because interrupted treatment produces seizures; and think through the tropical causes, neurocysticercosis, cerebral malaria, birth injury, covered elsewhere in this course. Common drugs are carbamazepine, started at 200 milligrams by mouth twice a day and often increased, and phenobarbital, typically 60 to 180 milligrams at night, with the choice set by what your program stocks and by the current national guideline, which you should verify before prescribing. Ask about adherence at every visit, because half of seizure relapses in remote settings are supply failures, not drug failures.

Intellectual disability is the other condition routinely mistaken for madness, and it usually reaches you through an exhausted family, a child who does not speak, an adult labeled violent. Your first act is to look for pain and discomfort, because behavior is communication in a person who cannot explain: a bad tooth, an ear infection, constipation, an untreated fracture, abuse no one has asked about, and the behavior often changes when the pain is treated. Only then consider medication, and reluctantly, because antipsychotics given for the behavior of intellectual disability sedate without helping and carry exactly the long term harms described earlier. Give the family what they most need, which is a plan rather than a drug: how to communicate, how to structure the day, what the person can still learn, which school will take them. Speak to the person and not about them, and correct the village's labels when you have the standing to do it. None of this requires a specialist, and all of it is nursing.

Here is the hard sentence this chapter owes you: the referral you requested will almost never come. There is no bed, or no money for transport, or the family refuses, because the distant hospital is, in their experience, where people go to die. The psychosis, the epilepsy, the depression is yours, and the real question of remote mental health work is how a single nurse carries a chronic caseload without being crushed by it. The answer is not stamina; it is structure, and the rest of this chapter is that structure.

Begin with the register, because the case that is not written down is remembered until you are transferred and then lost. Every person under active mental health care gets a line: diagnosis, treatment, dose and start date, risk status, who watches them at home, and the next review date, and you sweep the register weekly for dates falling due. Follow-up is scheduled and brief, not an open door into chaos. Trained community health workers visit between your sessions, watch for relapse signatures, count tablets, and report back, extending the chain of survival beyond your physical reach. Groups multiply you, because one nurse can serve 20 people in a morning of group interpersonal therapy, and the members carry each other for the rest of the week, which in a village outweighs your clinic. Meet the traditional and faith healers the families actually consult, learn what they do, agree on who they send to you, and never mock them, because a war between you and them ends with the patient losing in the middle. And find supervision wherever it lives, by radio, telephone, or telemedicine, because a nurse who carries psychoses alone and unreviewed for a year is practicing without a net.

Documentation deserves its own paragraph, because in remote settings the chart is the continuity, and the continuity is the treatment. Write what you found, what you excluded, what you gave, and what you decided not to do and why. Record risk in full, the suicide questions asked, the answers, the plan, and the family instruction, because if a death follows, your note is the care. Record the movement examination after antipsychotics, the mood questions and their answers, the doses and the dates, so the next nurse continues rather than restarts. And write carefully in a small community, where registers are read by clerks, relatives, and the curious: keep it factual, spare the humiliating detail, and store the book in a locked box, because confidentiality broken once ends trust for the whole village.

Monitoring is the discipline that separates treatment from dispensing, and it needs rhythm rather than heroics. Every visit asks the same 4 things: sleep, appetite, function, and the 2 mood questions from earlier. A person started on an antidepressant is reviewed at about 2 weeks, mainly for adverse effects and early agitation, and at 4 to 6 weeks for benefit; if nothing has moved by 8 weeks at an adequate dose, reconsider the diagnosis, check adherence, address alcohol, and raise or switch rather than drift. A person on an antipsychotic is examined for movement effects at every visit, with the dose kept at the lowest that holds them settled. A person who has been suicidal is asked about suicide again at every visit until the risk has clearly passed, because risk moves. Weigh people when you can, ask the old about falls, and stop the treatments that are not working rather than accumulating them. The unglamorous habit that makes all of this happen is a fixed review day with the register in front of you.

Logistics is the least romantic paragraph here and the one that decides whether the rest survives reality. Psychotropic medicines need no cold chain, but they need forecasting, because in mental health a stockout is not an inconvenience: abrupt haloperidol interruption tips a person into relapse, abrupt diazepam interruption causes seizures, abrupt amitriptyline stoppage produces a withdrawal the family reads as relapse. Order against the register, against the actual caseload, not against a vague idea, and keep a buffer for the stable long term patients who depend on you alone. A sensible remote formulary mirrors the intervention guide: fluoxetine or amitriptyline, haloperidol with its biperiden, chlorpromazine or risperidone, diazepam under lock with a running count, thiamine, and an anticonvulsant or 2, carbamazepine or phenobarbital. Guard the diazepam and log it, because it is the drug most likely to walk away. Check expiry on a set day each quarter and dispose of outdated stock by the rules you apply to any medicine, because a clinic that hands out expired psychotropics forfeits the trust its tablets depend on. Where you can, stock the supplies that make psychological treatment real: a quiet room, a bench in the shade, and time on your schedule with a door that closes.

The last duty in this chapter is to yourself, and it is the point the syllabus insists on: how to carry the case alone without carrying it home. You will carry the woman who drank the pesticide while you were away, the man who hanged himself the week after a good visit, the child tied to a post by a family you had nothing to offer, and they will follow you to bed if you let them. What protects you is not detachment, it is ritual and structure: a deliberate end to the working day, a handover of worry to a colleague or a notebook, a walk, a run, a prayer, whatever reliably closes the door. Peer support, other clinicians who do this work, is the strongest single protection known in humanitarian practice, and you should build it before you need it, the way you build the drug buffer. Supervision is where the moral injury of impossible choices gets processed before it calcifies into cynicism. And watch yourself for the markers you now know so well, sleep, mood, alcohol, enjoyment; ask yourself the 2 questions as if you were the patient, because in a posting with no replacement nurse, your own untreated depression is a clinical risk to an entire district.

To summarize, mental health care in remote tropical settings is not a referral problem, it is a chain of survival problem in which you are the recognizer, the therapist, the prescriber, and the follow-up system at once. Recognize depression behind its somatic mask and treat it with behavioral activation and problem-solving treatment, before or alongside fluoxetine or amitriptyline, with the review dates kept. De-escalate psychosis with your voice and your arrangements before reaching for haloperidol, exclude the medical emergencies that mimic it, and guard against the movement effects that make families stop treatment. Ask about suicide directly, act on the answers by restricting the means and scheduling review, remember that alcohol and pesticides kill more than despair alone, offer psychological first aid and time, and refuse the single-session debriefing. Treat grief as a process the community already knows how to do, and defend epilepsy and intellectual disability against the label of madness. Build the structure that carries chronic cases alone: the register, the review day, community health workers, groups, and supervision, and write everything down so the care survives your rotation. Every regimen named here comes from the intervention guide of the mental health gap action program of the World Health Organization, and each is revised periodically and differs between countries, so verify the current guideline before you prescribe. And when the day ends, close the door deliberately, because the people you serve need you back tomorrow, and the only way to be back tomorrow, year after year, is to refuse to carry into the night what belongs to the daylight.
