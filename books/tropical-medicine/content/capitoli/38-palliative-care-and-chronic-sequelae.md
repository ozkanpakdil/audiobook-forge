# Chapter 38 — Palliative Care and Chronic Sequelae

Palliative care is the active, total care of a person whose illness can no longer be cured, and it is defined less by a place than by an intention. It begins with the relief of pain and other distressing symptoms, and it reaches into the psychological, social and spiritual suffering that serious illness creates in a patient and in the family around that patient. The World Health Organization describes it as an approach that improves the quality of life of patients and families facing life-threatening illness, and it insists that palliative care can be provided early, alongside treatments that still aim to modify the disease. That early framing matters, because the phrase is often heard as a synonym for the final days, for the withdrawal of care, for giving up. In tropical medicine that misunderstanding carries a heavy cost, since the conditions described throughout this book generate years of chronic suffering long before death arrives, and much of that suffering responds to simple medicines and attentive nursing. Palliative care is therefore not the opposite of cure. It is what continues when cure becomes uncertain, and it is often what makes an aggressive cure tolerable in the first place.

The need for this care in low-resource tropical settings is vast, and the supply of it is thin. The World Health Organization and other bodies have estimated that most of the people in the world who need palliative care live in low-income and middle-income countries, while the great majority of them never receive it in any formal sense. The diseases that create the need are the ones this book has followed from the beginning. Advanced cervical cancer and breast cancer present late where screening does not reach, Kaposi sarcoma accompanies advanced HIV infection, and tuberculosis leaves survivors with destroyed lungs and chronic breathlessness. Beyond those, the long aftermath of the neglected tropical diseases produces disability that lasts for decades, in limbs, in eyes, in nerves and in hearts. As antiretroviral therapy and tuberculosis treatment reach more people, the number of survivors living with chronic complications grows rather than falls, and the same clinics now carry diabetes, hypertension and cancer alongside infection. Palliative care, in other words, is not a specialty reserved for the wealthy or the dying. It is a general competence that tropical nursing already requires.

Where does this care actually happen? Mostly at home, in a health center, on a district hospital ward, and only rarely in a dedicated hospice or a room with a door that closes. The setting determines the method. There is often no oxygen, no infusion pump, no refrigerator for medicines, no night nurse and no ambulance. The family becomes the nursing team, and the advanced practice nurse becomes the teacher, the prescriber working under protocol, and the person who returns.

Care of this kind depends on a small list of medicines that must exist in oral form, and the most important of them is morphine. The World Health Organization includes morphine on its list of essential medicines and treats the relief of severe pain as a matter of human rights, yet in many countries the drug is legally permitted and practically absent. Global consumption of morphine remains concentrated in a small number of wealthy nations, while the countries with the greatest burden of cancer pain and AIDS-related pain consume almost none of it. That gap is not a matter of pharmacology. It is logistics, regulation, fear and neglect, and it is a gap that a nurse can learn to name and, in some places, to close.

Pain is the symptom patients fear most, and it is the one most reliably relieved when the right medicine is available. The analgesic ladder published by the World Health Organization remains the simplest guide, and a nurse should be able to recite its three steps aloud. For mild pain, begin with a non-opioid such as paracetamol, given in a dose of 500 to 1000 milligrams every four to six hours in an adult, to a maximum of 4 grams in 24 hours, together with attention to the cause of the pain. For moderate pain, or for pain that persists at the first step, add a weak opioid such as codeine, commonly 30 to 60 milligrams every four hours by mouth, or tramadol at 50 to 100 milligrams every six hours, while the non-opioid is continued. For severe pain, move to a strong opioid, which in almost every setting means morphine. Adjuvant medicines are added at any step when the pain has a particular character: an antidepressant such as amitriptyline for burning neuropathic pain, an anticonvulsant such as gabapentin or carbamazepine for shooting pain, a corticosteroid for pain from bone deposits, nerve compression or raised pressure inside the head, and radiotherapy where it exists for a painful bone metastasis. The ladder is not a staircase to be climbed timidly. When the pain is severe at the outset, the third step is where treatment starts, and there is no obligation to begin at the bottom.

Morphine is titrated to the patient rather than fixed by a printed table, and this is the single skill that most changes what a dying person experiences. In an adult who has not taken an opioid, a reasonable start is 5 to 10 milligrams of immediate-release oral morphine every four hours, with an additional dose of the same size offered for breakthrough pain. When two or more breakthrough doses are needed in a day, the regular four-hourly dose is increased by the total of the breakthrough doses, and the process repeats until the pain is controlled. A sustained-release preparation may then be substituted for convenience, but never for a patient whose pain has not yet been stabilized on the immediate-release form. The predictable adverse effects are constipation, nausea, drowsiness and, at higher doses, respiratory depression, and constipation in particular must be anticipated with a stimulant laxative and an osmotic agent rather than treated after it becomes an obstruction. Drowsiness usually settles within a few days and should not be mistaken for impending death. Respiratory depression is uncommon when the dose is titrated against pain, because pain itself opposes the depressant effect, and the genuine clinical danger is that a nurse frightened of the drug will under-dose a patient who is dying in pain. The same principles apply at the end of life, where the dose may need to rise sharply as the patient weakens, and where the appropriate ceiling is the relief of suffering rather than a number in a formulary.

The barriers that keep morphine from patients are mostly not clinical. A country may have signed the international conventions that govern narcotic drugs and still fail to make morphine available, because a national law requires a special licence that no district hospital holds, because the annual import quota is set far below the amount that patients need, because only physicians may prescribe and there is no physician within a day of travel, or because a pharmacist fears inspection more than a patient fears pain. Some ministries worry, with reason, about diversion and misuse, and that caution is not irrational; the answer is not unrestricted supply but a regulated system that counts what it dispenses and still allows a nurse working under standing orders to give the medicine to the person in front of them. Where oral morphine does not exist, an injectable form may be the only one available, and the nurse must then know the conversion, roughly one milligram of morphine by injection for every two to three milligrams by mouth, and must know that a subcutaneous injection lasts about four hours in the same way that the oral dose does. Barriers of distance, cost, cultural fear and family shame add to the legal ones, and a nurse who understands all of them is better placed to work around the ones that can be worked around. Regimens and controlled-medicine rules are revised periodically and differ between countries, so the current national guideline must be checked before prescribing. That verification step is not a formality; it is the difference between relieving pain and causing harm.

Breathlessness in advanced disease comes from many causes, and the nurse should separate those that can be reversed from those that cannot. A pleural effusion can be drained, a large airway obstruction may respond to a corticosteroid, anemia may be correctable, and in a tropical setting a new fever with breathlessness may be pneumonia, tuberculosis or heart failure rather than the underlying cancer alone. When the cause cannot be removed, the measures that help are simple: sitting the patient upright, opening a window, using a hand-held fan across the cheek, teaching breathing that slows the exhale, and small doses of morphine, often 2.5 to 5 milligrams by mouth every four hours, which reduce the sensation of breathlessness rather than the respiratory rate. Oxygen helps the patient who is short of oxygen and does little for the patient who is not, and it is frequently unavailable in any case. Nausea and vomiting in advanced illness arise from the gut, the brain, the medicines and the metabolic state, and the choice of antiemetic follows the cause: metoclopramide when the stomach empties slowly, haloperidol or a phenothiazine when a chemical trigger is suspected, dexamethasone when raised pressure inside the head or stretching of the liver capsule is responsible, and a serotonin-blocking drug such as ondansetron when the cause is chemotherapy or radiotherapy. Whichever is chosen, the nurse should give it by a route the patient can tolerate, which in the last days is often under the skin rather than by mouth, and should review its effect after 24 hours rather than leaving it unchallenged.

Other symptoms fill the days of a patient with advanced disease, and each has a practical nursing answer. Fever may be the disease itself, but in the tropics it is more often an untreated infection, and a fever in a person with advanced illness deserves the same malaria film, the same search for tuberculosis and the same attention to a urinary or wound source that any other patient receives, because treatment of the cause can restore weeks of comfort. When fever is not reversible, paracetamol and tepid sponging relieve it, and the nurse should not forget that the dying patient may be warm from the process of dying itself. A fungating wound that smells is one of the most isolating experiences in medicine, and it is controllable: gentle cleansing, a dressing that does not adhere, metronidazole by mouth or applied to the wound for the odor of anaerobic bacteria, and charcoal dressings where they exist. Agitation and confusion in the final weeks usually have a reversible cause, and the nurse should look for unrelieved pain, a full bladder, constipation, dehydration, a lack of oxygen, an unfamiliar room and a medicine that has been added, rather than assuming that delirium is simply part of dying. When agitation persists and distresses the patient, haloperidol or a benzodiazepine may be needed, and in the last hours a small dose can allow a family to sit with a calm relative instead of a terrified one. Mouth care, pressure area care and the treatment of constipation are never optional, because a dry mouth, a sacral ulcer and an unrelieved bowel obstruction are the failures that families remember.

Telling a family bad news is a skill that can be learned, and in tropical settings it is inseparable from culture. In many communities the family, not the patient, is the unit that receives the diagnosis, and relatives may ask the clinician to withhold the news from the person who is ill. That request deserves respect and negotiation rather than a lecture on autonomy. The nurse can begin by asking the family what they already understand and what they fear, then asking what the patient wishes to know, and then offering the truth in words that are simple and unhurried. Choose a private place, sit at the same level, turn off distractions, use the name of the illness rather than a euphemism, and then stop talking. Silence gives the family room to absorb what has been said, and the first question they ask is the one that matters most to them. An interpreter, if one is needed, should be briefed beforehand and should translate the meaning rather than the words, and a child in the family should not be pressed into that role. After the conversation, write down what was said and what was decided, because the family will remember it differently and the next clinician will need it.

Good communication leads naturally to a plan, and the plan should be made while the patient can still take part in it. Where does this person want to die, at home or in the hospital, and who will be there, and what will the family do when the breathing changes or the pain breaks through? In settings where there is no telephone number to call at night, the family needs written instructions, a supply of medicines and a named person to contact. The nurse should also discuss the limits of treatment honestly, which in a district hospital often means deciding that a patient will not be sent on a futile journey, and recording that decision so that the next shift does not undo it. In the last days of life, the signs of dying should be recognized rather than fought: the patient becomes drowsy, the swallow weakens, the urine output falls, the hands and feet cool, and breathing becomes irregular. At that point the appropriate nursing action is to stop unnecessary monitoring and medicines, continue pain relief and mouth care, position the patient for comfort, keep the family present if they wish, and explain each change as it comes. Anticipatory prescribing of morphine and of a medicine for agitation prevents the crisis call at two in the morning, and it is one of the most valuable things an advanced practice nurse can arrange.

Now the chronic sequelae, which this book has been describing all along, and which are the reason that palliative and rehabilitative care overlap so heavily in tropical medicine. Lymphedema of the leg from lymphatic filariasis, and the identical swelling of podoconiosis that follows years of walking barefoot on irritant clay soil, both progress if they are left alone and both can be halted and partly reversed by a regimen the patient can learn. The limb is washed every day with soap and clean water, kept short and clean at the toenails, and examined between the toes for the cracks and fungal infection that allow bacteria to enter. The leg is elevated whenever the patient sits or lies, exercised through a range of movements to pump the lymph, and then wrapped in layers of short-stretch bandages that create a graded pressure discouraging further fluid accumulation. Compression garments replace the bandages once the limb has shrunk, and shoes that fit the swollen foot must be found or made. The acute attacks of pain, redness and fever that punctuate the disease are treated promptly with an antibiotic and with rest, because each attack leaves the limb harder and larger, and antiseptic care of entry wounds prevents the next one. Where the health system supports them, self-care groups let patients teach one another and reduce the isolation that the disease imposes.

Hydrocele is the complication of lymphatic filariasis that men hide most carefully and that surgery cures most completely. The swelling of the scrotum can reach the size of a football, makes walking and work painful, and carries a shame that keeps men from seeking care even when a hospital is near. The nurse's first task is to name the condition without disgust, and to explain that the fluid is lymph, that it is not a sexually transmitted disease and that it does not by itself make a man infertile. Surgical repair by hydrocelectomy is a short operation with a good result, and campaigns in endemic districts have shown that demand appears as soon as the operation is offered locally and respectfully. Where lymphedema of the leg and hydrocele coexist in the same household, the whole family benefits from care delivered together. After surgery the nurse watches the wound, supports the scrotum, and teaches the patient what to expect in the first weeks, including the swelling that is normal and the redness that is not.

Blindness in the tropics is often preventable and almost never reversible once it has arrived. Onchocerciasis, caused by the worm Onchocerca volvulus and spread by the blackfly, produces the itching, skin damage and disease of the cornea and retina that earned it the name river blindness. Ivermectin, given as a single oral dose of about 150 micrograms per kilogram and repeated once a year in community-directed treatment, prevents new eye disease and relieves the itching, but it does not restore sight that has already been lost. Trachoma, caused by repeated infection with Chlamydia trachomatis, scars the inner eyelid until the lashes turn inward and scrape the cornea, and the surgery that corrects that trichiasis can save the remaining sight if it is done before the cornea is destroyed. Azithromycin at 20 milligrams per kilogram as a single dose reduces infection in a community, while facial cleanliness and access to clean water and latrines reduce transmission. For the person who is already blind, the nurse's work shifts to rehabilitation: teaching orientation and mobility, connecting the patient to a white cane or a community guide, screening the household for other affected members, and treating the depression and dependency that follow the loss of sight. A blind adult in a rural household is often also a person who cannot farm, and the social consequences of that are as clinical as the eye itself.

The brain that survives a severe infection does not always survive intact. Children who recover from cerebral malaria may be left with cognitive impairment, behavioral change, epilepsy, unsteadiness of gait, or a subtle loss of language and memory that is only noticed when school begins. Bacterial meningitis leaves hearing loss in a substantial proportion of survivors, and that hearing loss is frequently missed because no one tests for it before the child fails to learn to speak. The deficit may appear months after discharge, which is exactly when follow-up has usually ended, and the nurse should therefore schedule a developmental and hearing review rather than assuming that a child who walks out of the ward is cured. Where audiology exists, referral is straightforward; where it does not, simple behavioral testing, observation of responses to sound and attention to parental concern can identify the children who need help. Anticonvulsants, hearing aids, school support and family education are cheap compared with the lifetime cost of untreated disability, and a nurse who writes the follow-up appointment into the discharge plan is doing prevention as much as care.

Epilepsy is the most common neurological consequence of a tropical parasitic infection, and neurocysticercosis is its leading cause in many parts of Latin America, Asia and Africa. The larval cysts of the pork tapeworm Taenia solium lodge in the brain and cause seizures when they degenerate and provoke inflammation, and the seizures may continue for years after the cysts have calcified. Treatment has two arms that must move together: an anticonvulsant to stop the seizures, and, for viable cysts, a course of albendazole or praziquantel with a corticosteroid to control the inflammatory reaction that treatment itself provokes. The choice of anticonvulsant depends on what is available, and carbamazepine, phenobarbital and levetiracetam all have a place, each with its own burden of monitoring, sedation and interaction with antiretroviral medicines. Adherence is the real determinant of outcome, and the nurse should treat a missed prescription as a clinical event rather than a moral failing. Stigma around epilepsy is severe in many communities, and counseling the family and the school is as important as the tablets in the bottle.

Chagas disease produces a chronic disease of the heart muscle that can remain silent for decades and then present as heart failure, abnormal rhythm or sudden death. The parasite Trypanosoma cruzi damages the conduction system, the muscle of the heart and the apex of the left ventricle, so a patient may arrive with complete heart block, with a rapid ventricular rhythm, with a stroke from a clot inside the heart, or with the swollen legs and breathlessness of advanced heart failure. Antiparasitic treatment with benznidazole or nifurtimox is considered mainly in younger patients and in the earlier phases, and its role in established cardiac disease is limited and must be weighed against its toxicities. The heart failure itself is managed with the standard medicines: a diuretic for congestion, an angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor and a beta blocker when the blood pressure allows, spironolactone for advanced disease, amiodarone for abnormal rhythm, and anticoagulation when there is atrial fibrillation or a visible clot, though the bleeding risk in a rural setting must be part of that decision. A pacemaker transforms the life of a patient with a slow pulse and fainting, and a nurse who recognizes those signs should refer before the next collapse. At the end of the illness the same palliative principles apply, and the relief of breathlessness and pain becomes the priority.

Loss of a limb is a common end point in tropical practice, whether from a diabetic foot that necrosed, a snakebite that destroyed tissue, a mycetoma that invaded bone, an untreated infection in a person with leprosy, or a road injury on a highway with no ambulance. The chronic wounds of mycetoma deserve particular mention because they are so often tolerated for years. The painless swelling of the foot with sinuses that discharge grains is caused by a fungus or by a bacterium, the distinction determines the drug, and treatment may require itraconazole or ketoconazole for many months or years together with surgery. The nurse keeps the wound clean, controls odor, supports the limb, monitors liver function when the antifungal is prolonged, and helps the patient decide when amputation is the kinder option rather than a defeat. Amputation carries a grief of its own, and the pain does not always stop at the incision. Phantom limb pain and a stump neuroma are neuropathic, and they respond to the analgesic ladder with an adjuvant such as amitriptyline or gabapentin rather than to more opioid alone. Stump care, shaping and preparation for a prosthesis belong in the same nursing plan.

Rehabilitation is what turns survival into a life, and it is chronically under-provided in exactly the places where it is most needed. Physiotherapy restores strength and movement after a stroke, a meningitis, a burn or an amputation; occupational therapy adapts the tasks of daily living to a changed body; and speech therapy addresses the swallowing and communication problems that follow neurological injury. Assistive devices are the tools that make those gains usable: a wheelchair, crutches, a walking frame, a hearing aid, a white cane, a prosthesis, a pressure-relieving cushion, a raised toilet seat. The World Health Organization maintains a list of priority assistive products precisely because most people who need them cannot get them, and the shortage is as much about repair and maintenance as about the first fitting. A wheelchair that cannot be repaired on a dirt road becomes furniture within a year. The nurse's part is to identify the need during the admission rather than after discharge, to make the referral while the patient is still on the ward, to teach the family how to use and care for the device, and to improvise the cheap adaptations that make a home navigable: a ramp of packed earth, a rope beside the latrine, a chair with arms.

Disability in tropical medicine is best understood through the social model, which locates the problem not in the impaired body but in the barriers the world places around it. A person who cannot walk may still farm, trade or teach if the path is passable, the clinic is on the ground floor and the employer does not equate disability with incapacity. The Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities sets out the obligations that follow, and most countries have signed it, yet the gap between signature and practice is wide. Inclusion means accessible clinics with level entrances and a latrine a wheelchair can enter, information in formats a person with sensory loss can use, inclusive schooling for children with epilepsy or learning difficulty, and the deliberate presence of disabled people in the planning of programs that concern them. It also means noticing that the burden of disability from neglected tropical diseases is largely preventable, because lymphedema, blindness, amputation and epilepsy all begin with an infection that treatment can stop or slow. Prevention and inclusion are therefore two ends of one nursing responsibility, and neither can be left to a specialist service that a rural patient will never reach.

Stigma is a clinical variable, and it changes outcomes in every disease this book has covered. Leprosy is the classic example, a disease that became curable in the middle of the twentieth century and still causes people to be driven from their villages by a fear that outlived the bacillus. Lymphatic filariasis, HIV infection, tuberculosis, epilepsy and mental illness carry their own versions, and the mechanisms are recognizable: fear of contagion, horror at disfigurement, the belief that illness is a punishment or a curse, and the calculation that a sick family member damages the marriage prospects of everyone related to them. The consequences are delayed diagnosis, concealment, loss of work, isolation and depression, and that depression is treatable and frequently untreated. The nurse has specific tools: keep confidences absolutely, use language that names a person before a disease, explain transmission accurately and repeatedly, invite cured patients to speak to the community, involve traditional and religious leaders rather than opposing them, and screen for the despair that stigma produces. Stigma is not defeated by a poster. It is reduced by ordinary contact with a treated person, which is something a nurse can arrange in a single afternoon.

The nurse holds this whole field together, and the reason is continuity. In settings where the physician changes with each visit, the nurse is often the only person who has watched the same patient across months, who knows that the dose was increased last week, who remembers the name of the daughter who does the lifting, and who will still be working in the district next year. That continuity makes the nurse the natural author of the care plan, the person who writes the standing order that lets a colleague act at night, and the one who teaches the family what to do. It also makes the nurse a witness to indignity, and the response to indignity is practical: wash the patient, cover the wound, close the curtain, ask permission before touching, address the adult by name, and never discuss the diagnosis over the patient's head. Bereavement care does not end at the funeral. A follow-up visit or a telephone call to the family six weeks later identifies the relative who has slid into depression, the child who has stopped attending school and the household that has lost its income with the patient. Nothing about this work is optional in a well-run service, and nothing about it is easy in an understaffed one. Scarcity forces choices, and the nurse who makes them should record them plainly rather than carry them silently, because unshared moral distress is the shortest road to burnout.

To summarize, palliative care in tropical medicine is active total care for patients and families facing life-threatening illness, provided early and alongside curative treatment, and it belongs in homes and district wards rather than only in specialist units. Pain is controlled through the analgesic ladder of the World Health Organization, with oral morphine titrated to the individual and with the fear of the medicine recognized as a barrier in its own right, and the regulatory and supply obstacles that keep opioids from patients must be named and worked at locally. Symptoms, including breathlessness, nausea, fever, wounds, agitation, constipation and the dry mouth of the final days, each have a practical nursing answer, and honest negotiated communication allows a family to receive hard news without being abandoned by it. The chronic sequelae described throughout this book, lymphedema, hydrocele, blindness, neurological deficit, epilepsy, chagasic heart failure, amputation and the wounds of mycetoma, are managed with self-care teaching, rehabilitation, assistive devices and disability inclusion rather than with resignation. Stigma is a clinical variable that shrinks under accurate explanation and ordinary contact, and the nurse is the professional best placed to sustain continuity, dignity and bereavement care. Above all, every regimen and dose in this chapter reflects guidance that is revised periodically and that differs between countries, so the current national guideline must be checked before prescribing, and the nurse who verifies a dose before giving it is practicing the same careful attention that this chapter describes.
