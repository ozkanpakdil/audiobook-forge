# Chapter 40 — Global Health Ethics, Humanitarian Work, and Field Practice

The chapters before this one taught you what to do. This chapter asks a harder question, which is who you are while you do it. Tropical medicine is practiced in places where the health system is thin, where government may be absent or predatory, and where the people you treat have often been failed by institutions several times before they ever meet you. Ethics in that space is not a decorative set of rules appended to clinical work. It is the structure that decides who receives the last available bed, who is told the truth about a diagnosis, and who is quietly left out. The advanced practice nurse who works in humanitarian and field settings needs a working moral framework rather than a slogan. This chapter builds that framework and then follows it into the unglamorous reality of logistics, security, teamwork, exhaustion, and the discipline of leaving behind something that survives your departure.

Begin with the 4 classical principles, because they remain the shared vocabulary of clinical ethics and they are the terms in which your decisions will be defended or attacked. Beneficence is the obligation to act for the patient's good, to choose the intervention that offers real benefit rather than the one that merely looks busy. In a field clinic, beneficence is tested by limits, because you may be able to help 1 person greatly or 20 people modestly with the same 6 hours. Non-maleficence is the obligation to avoid harm, and it reaches further than most nurses expect. It covers the harm of a drug given without a clear indication, the harm of a needle reused because supplies ran out, and the harm of a promise made to a family that the program cannot keep. Autonomy is the obligation to respect the person's own decision, which in practice means consent that is informed, voluntary, and understood. Justice is the obligation to distribute benefit and burden fairly, and in global health it is almost always the principle under the greatest strain.

Beneficence and non-maleficence pull in opposite directions more often than textbooks admit. A child with severe malnutrition may benefit from a therapeutic feeding program that requires the family to walk 3 hours each way, and the harm of that journey to a household with other children is real. The nurse who weighs only the biological benefit has not finished the ethical analysis. You also have to ask who bears the cost of the good you are doing, and whether that cost was ever consented to. A useful habit is to state the benefit and the harm in the same sentence, aloud, before you decide. If the two cannot both be said plainly, the decision is not yet clear.

Autonomy requires more than a signature. Many of the people you serve have had little experience of a clinician asking what they want, and some have good reason to distrust the question. Consent depends on language, on literacy, on whether the patient is speaking for themselves or for a household, and on whether saying no is genuinely possible without losing access to care. In many settings the family is the decision-making unit, and the skilled nurse works with that reality without using it as an excuse to bypass the patient. Where a patient cannot consent, document who consented on their behalf and why. The test is whether the patient, hearing the story afterward, would recognize the decision as their own.

Justice is the principle that governs the queue, the allocation, and the boundaries of the program. It asks why a child in this district dies of a disease that is treatable 200 kilometers away, and it asks the harder question of what you, with the resources in front of you, owe to the person you cannot reach. Distributive justice in a field setting is not an abstraction, because it is the set of rules that determines who is enrolled, who is transferred, and who is sent home with symptomatic treatment because the diagnostic capacity has run out. Those rules must be written down, applied consistently, and open to challenge. A rule that cannot be explained to the people it excludes is not a rule but a preference.

Scarcity makes the principles concrete. Imagine 1 functioning oxygen concentrator and 2 patients who both need it, a young woman with pneumonia and an older man with advanced heart failure and a life expectancy measured in weeks. Beneficence points toward each of them. Justice forces a comparison. The defensible answer rests on likelihood of benefit, urgency, and the ethical priority of saving life over prolonging an expected death, and it should not rest on age, wealth, tribe, language, or the persuasion of the family. Write the criteria down before the crisis, because criteria invented during a crisis are usually criteria that protect the powerful. The nurse who participates in that allocation is doing clinical work, not administrative work, and should be supported rather than blamed.

The most corrosive effect of scarcity is not the rationing itself but the secrecy that grows around it. When staff cannot name the limits, they start to ration by avoidance, by not asking, and by not looking at the patient who cannot be helped. That pattern protects the clinician from grief and abandons the patient. Naming the constraint in a team meeting, writing the criteria where they can be seen, and rotating the hardest decisions among several people all reduce the damage. Moral distress is the name for the pain of knowing the right thing and being unable to do it, and it is an occupational hazard that a good team can share and a bad team will hide.

Now consider the 4 humanitarian principles, which came from the experience of relief work in war and have been carried into peacetime practice. Humanity means that human suffering is recognized and answered wherever it is found, without a prior judgment about whether the sufferer deserves it. Neutrality means that assistance does not take sides in hostilities or in a political or ideological dispute, and it does not mean that the nurse has no opinions, only that aid is not a weapon. Impartiality means that assistance is guided by need alone, with no priority given to nationality, race, religion, gender, or political affiliation. Independence means that humanitarian action is autonomous from the political, economic, military, or other objectives that any actor may hold in the area. Together they form a working doctrine, and each of them is easier to state than to live.

The tensions appear as soon as real actors arrive. Neutrality is tested when one party to a conflict controls the road you must use, or when an armed group demands a share of the medicines you brought. Impartiality is tested when the population you can reach is not the population most in need, because the most needy are behind a front line. Independence is tested when the money for your program comes from a government with a stake in the outcome. Humanity is tested when a casualty arrives from one side of the fight and someone in your team has a reason to feel nothing. The principles do not resolve these cases, but they make the case visible, which is the necessary first step.

Dual loyalty is the everyday version of that problem. It describes the situation in which the clinician owes an obligation to an institution, an employer, a state, or a commander, and a simultaneous obligation to the patient, and the two obligations point in different directions. A prison nurse is asked to certify fitness for an interrogation. A company nurse is asked to declare a worker fit for a task the worker cannot safely perform. A ministry nurse is asked to record a case as something other than what it is, because a certain diagnosis triggers reporting obligations and bad publicity. The nurse is caught in the middle, and the middle is where professional identity is either built or quietly surrendered.

There is no formula that dissolves dual loyalty, but there are habits that protect you. Know before you sign what your contract obliges you to report and to whom. Know which professional codes and licensing bodies hold jurisdiction over you and can be appealed to when the employer is not the final authority. Document clinical findings in your own record, in your own words, at the time, so that a later dispute has a factual base. Tell the patient what you are obliged to disclose before you disclose it. Find at least 1 colleague who can hear the situation without punishing you for naming it.

A serious nurse must also hold the critique of humanitarianism, because the movement's own literature raises it. Relief can create dependency when it substitutes for local systems rather than strengthening them. It can distort a local economy when international staff are paid salaries that a national health worker cannot match, drawing the best clinicians out of the public system and into the project. It can concentrate decision-making in distant capitals, so that a district's priorities are set by a funding cycle rather than by a district's patients. None of these failures makes relief illegitimate. They make it accountable, and an accountable program measures its footprint on the local system, not only its outputs.

The critique also concerns the image of the helper. The nurse who arrives believing that they are the solution, rather than a temporary and narrow contribution to work that locals have been doing all along, will make predictable mistakes. Parachute practice, in which a clinician works outside their competence because no one is watching, harms patients and discredits the profession. Practicing beyond your scope in a remote clinic is not courage, because it is a risk you have transferred to someone who did not consent to it. The dignified position is to name your limits out loud and to build the referral, the supervision, and the second opinion around them.

The argument for local capacity is therefore not a slogan but a design requirement. The people who will still be there in 10 years are the national staff, the community health workers, the district officers, and the families. A program that treats them as the delivery mechanism rather than the owners of the work has misunderstood its own purpose. Localization means sharing authority over budgets, protocols, and priorities, not only sharing the labor. It means paying national colleagues fairly for their skill rather than thanking them for their sacrifice. It also means accepting that a locally set priority may not be the one your organization finds most attractive, and supporting it anyway.

What a humanitarian nurse actually does is worth stating plainly, because the popular image is wrong. The work is logistics, security, teamwork, and cultural navigation at least as much as clinical care, and often more. You will count, order, transport, unpack, and protect supplies. You will sit in coordination meetings where the schedule of a mobile clinic depends on whether a road is passable and whether a militia has moved a checkpoint. You will negotiate with a village leader for a place to hold the clinic and with a driver about a route that avoids an ambush. The clinical hour is the visible part of a day that was mostly spent making that hour possible.

Logistics is clinical care in disguise. A vaccine that has lost its cold chain protects no one, so the temperature log matters as much as the injection technique. Quantification, which is the arithmetic of how much of each drug and supply a population will need over a stated period, prevents both stock-outs and expired stock. A stock-out of an antimalarial in the middle of a transmission season is an ethical failure with a supply chain, and it is often foreseeable. Keep a simple paper register of what goes out and what comes in, reconcile it weekly, and report shortages early, because early reporting is the only kind that can still change an order. The nurse who manages the store well has treated every patient in the queue.

Where clinical management enters the field, give the same attention to the dose that you would give at home. Severe dehydration is treated with oral rehydration salts, 1 sachet dissolved in 1 liter of clean water and given by mouth in small, frequent sips, with a plan to reassess the patient within 4 hours. Severe malaria in an adult may be treated with artesunate, 2.4 milligrams per kilogram intravenously at the start, again at 12 hours, and again at 24 hours, followed by a full course of an artemisinin-based combination therapy when the patient can swallow. A common field antibiotic regimen is ceftriaxone, 1 gram intravenously every 24 hours in an adult, with pediatric dosing calculated by weight, and simple analgesia such as acetaminophen, 1 gram by mouth every 6 hours, with a lower ceiling in liver disease. The World Health Organization and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention both publish current regimens, and you should read that guidance directly rather than rely on a remembered number. Regimens are revised, guidance differs between countries, and the current guideline must be checked before prescribing. Verify the drug, the concentration, the dose, the route, and the interval with a second person whenever the calculation is unusual.

Monitoring and adverse effects are the part of field practice that quietly decides outcomes. Every patient who receives fluid needs a plan for urine output, mental status, and respiratory rate, observed and recorded at stated intervals rather than estimated at the end of a shift. Every patient on a long course of doxycycline needs to know about photosensitivity so that a sunburn is not mistaken for a drug allergy, and anyone started on mefloquine should be warned about and asked about sleep disturbance, vivid dreams, anxiety, and mood change, because the neuropsychiatric effects are the reason that drug is avoided in some patients. Ivermectin is generally well tolerated, but the fever and itching that follow treatment of a heavy filarial or onchocercal burden are expected reactions that should be explained in advance so that they are not read as treatment failure. Write the adverse event down, report it through the channel that exists, and tell the next clinician, because a side effect that is never recorded cannot inform anyone else's prescribing.

Documentation and data belong in the same breath. A field program runs on paper registers, tally sheets, and clinic ledgers long before it runs on a database, and those sheets are the evidence for the next order, the next grant, and the next outbreak investigation. Record the patient identifier, the date, the diagnosis as you understand it, the treatment given, and the outcome, using the same definitions every day so that the numbers mean something. Data quality is a nursing responsibility and not an administrative imposition, because a duplicate entry undercounts coverage and an unchecked tally can hide a cluster of cases. Protect the register as carefully as the medicines, because a lost register is a lost patient history and, in some contexts, a danger to the people named in it.

Security awareness is a professional skill and not a personality trait. It begins with context analysis, which means understanding who controls the area, what recent incidents have occurred, and what the local rules of behavior are. It continues with acceptance, which is the patient and gradual building of consent for your presence among the community, the authorities, and the armed actors who are not the same people. Practical habits include a curfew that you actually observe, travel in a marked vehicle with a driver who knows the route, a communication plan with a check-in time and a missed check-in procedure, and a rule that no one moves alone. Know the difference between a checkpoint that is routine and one that is not, and know that the safest response is usually calm, slow, and unremarkable.

Security also means understanding the boundaries of your own role. You are not a combatant, and you should not be used as one, which means refusing to carry weapons, to transport fighters, or to let your vehicle or your radio be used for military purposes. Coordination between civilian and military actors is sometimes necessary for access and always delicate, because proximity to one armed actor costs you access to another. Accept the security advice of people who know the context, including national staff, even when it conflicts with the plan you brought. Report incidents factually and without embellishment, because exaggeration destroys the credibility the next team will depend on.

Teamwork in the field is cross-cultural by default, and cultural navigation is a core competency rather than a courtesy. Learn how decisions are actually made, who speaks first, how disagreement is expressed, and what a direct question may cost the person answering it. Work with interpreters as professionals by briefing them before the consultation, speaking in short units, addressing the patient rather than the interpreter, and never using a family member as an interpreter for a sensitive disclosure. Gender norms may determine who can examine whom, and ignoring that does not make you progressive, it makes you ineffective. When you are unsure, ask, and ask the person with the least power in the room as well as the most.

Working with national staff as colleagues rather than assistants is the single most important professional decision you will make. Your national colleagues know the language, the geography, the politics, and the clinical reality of the place, and they are the ones who will remain when you leave. Pay them equitably, include them in decisions that affect them, name them in reports, and support their progression into the roles you currently occupy. Do not create a two-tier team in which international staff hold authority and national staff hold risk, because in an evacuation that arrangement becomes lethal. Ask what they need, believe the answer, and be prepared to be told that your presence is itself the problem.

The psychological cost of this work deserves the same seriousness as the clinical content. Moral injury is the injury that comes from witnessing or participating in something that violates your own moral commitments, and it is different from exhaustion. You may be required to follow a policy you believe is wrong, to leave a patient you cannot help, or to choose between 2 people who both deserve the bed. The damage accumulates quietly, and it does not disappear when the contract ends. Noticing it early, in yourself and in your team, is a clinical skill that you should practice deliberately.

Burnout is the later stage, marked by detachment, cynicism, irritability, sleep disturbance, and a loss of the sense that the work means anything. What genuinely helps is unglamorous, and it includes protected sleep, days off that are actually off, a supervisor who listens without turning the conversation into an evaluation, peer support from people who have seen the same things, and a rotation out of the highest-exposure role before the breaking point. What helps less than organizations hope is a single debrief after a critical incident, a poster about resilience, or an expectation that staff will manage it alone. Ask directly about suicide risk, intrusive memories, and alcohol use, and know where to refer someone who needs more than a conversation.

Practical preparation is part of ethics, because an unprepared nurse becomes a burden and a risk. Build the skills your context demands, which may include obstetric emergency care, wound care, triage, basic fracture management, and the use of the ultrasound machine that will be the only imaging available. Confirm your vaccinations and check what the destination requires, since some countries require proof of yellow fever vaccination for entry and some require prophylaxis documentation for certain visas. Arrange medical evacuation insurance that actually covers the country you are going to and not merely the region, and know the policy number and the evacuation trigger. Clarify your registration and licensure status, because practicing under a license that does not cover the setting can leave you and your patient unprotected.

Preparation also covers the life you are leaving behind. Set up your finances, your medications, and a way for family to reach you, and write down the decisions you want made if something happens to you. Discuss the separation with the people who depend on you honestly, including its length and its uncertainty, rather than promising a date you cannot guarantee. Decide in advance how you will communicate and how often, because silence during a security incident is its own kind of harm to the people at home. Anxieties that are anticipated and planned for consume far less of your attention in the field than anxieties that ambush you there.

Plan the exit from the first week and not the last. Sustainability means that a service, a skill, or a system continues after your contract ends, and it has to be designed deliberately. Identify who will take over each task, write the protocols in the language and the format the successor can use, and train toward competence rather than attendance. Plan the supply transition early, and do not let a program end by simply running out of the drug that patients have come to depend on. Handing over authority is harder than handing over a task, and it is the test of whether the work was ever about the patients or only about the organization.

The professional duty to advocate is the last obligation and the one most easily dropped. You will see things that are not medical problems at all, such as a market with no latrines, a school fee that keeps a child out of care, or a road that closes in the rainy season and takes the referral with it. Your witness has value, and it has limits, because speaking out can endanger the people who remain. Advocate with the consent of those affected, with accurate documentation, and through channels that protect them, whether that is a professional association, a national health authority, or a humanitarian organization's policy office. Bearing witness is part of nursing, and it is done most safely as a collective rather than as an individual act of conscience.

Community engagement is the practical mechanism by which those principles reach the ground. A program that arrives without explanation, selects its beneficiaries by a list no one has seen, and leaves without a farewell will be understood as an extraction rather than a service. Engagement means meeting community leaders and women's groups before the first clinic day, explaining what the program can and cannot do, and asking what the community itself sees as the priority. It means managing rumors early, because a false belief about what a vaccine or a drug does spreads faster than any health education poster. It also means accepting that some communities will decline, and that a refusal honestly given is a legitimate answer rather than an obstacle to be worked around.

Monitoring, supervision, and quality review are part of the ethical obligation and not a bureaucratic layer above it. A nurse who supervises others should visit the peripheral site, watch the work, check the register against the stock, and give feedback that is specific and private. Clinical audits, simple case reviews, and a running log of complications and deaths turn individual experience into shared learning rather than repeated error. Adverse-event surveillance is the same discipline applied to medicines, and it depends on staff believing that reporting will not be punished. Where a death is reviewed openly and without blame, the next patient is safer, and where it is hidden, nothing is learned.

To summarize, global health ethics is the practice of applying beneficence, non-maleficence, autonomy, and justice when resources are short and institutions are weak. The humanitarian principles of humanity, neutrality, impartiality, and independence give you a shared language, and the tensions between them must be named rather than smoothed over. Dual loyalty is a daily reality, and it is survived by knowing your obligations, documenting clearly, and finding colleagues who will hear the truth. The field nurse works through logistics, security, teamwork, and cultural navigation as much as through clinical skill, and the dose, the cold chain, and the register are all ethical instruments. National colleagues are the owners of the work and the future of the system, so authority and pay must reflect that. Prepare your skills, your vaccinations, your insurance, your registration, and your family before you go, protect your mind and the minds of your team while you are there, and plan the exit so that the work outlives your presence. Finally, carry the duty to advocate carefully, accurately, and with the consent of the people you serve.
