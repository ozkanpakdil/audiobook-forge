# Appendix B — Incubation Periods and Clinical Decision Points

An incubation period is the time between the moment a pathogen enters the body and the moment the first symptom appears. In tropical medicine it is the most useful piece of arithmetic you have, because it turns a vague travel history into a probability statement. Fever 3 days after leaving a malarious region argues against malaria and toward dengue, chikungunya, influenza, cholera, shigellosis, or a rickettsial infection. Fever 12 days after leaving moves malaria, typhoid, leptospirosis, acute HIV infection, and the hepatitis viruses to the front. This appendix walks through the windows by length, then sets out the decision points where that arithmetic changes practice.

Two clocks run at once in every fever, and confusing them is the commonest error in this reasoning. The first measures days since exposure, which is what an incubation period describes; the second measures days of illness, which is what the chart records. When a patient arrives on the fifth day of fever, work backward to an exposure window before comparing that illness with any range. Incubation periods are ranges, not appointments, and they shift with inoculum size, route of entry, immune status, prior vaccination, and chemoprophylaxis. Treat every number here as a distribution with a long tail.

The first group is the very short, less than 7 days, and it dominates when illness begins soon after exposure. Dengue appears 4 to 10 days after the bite, usually 5 to 7, and chikungunya appears after 3 to 7 days, with a range of 1 to 12. Both are carried by Aedes mosquitoes, which bite in daylight, so ask about daytime biting in towns and cities. Dengue opens with abrupt fever, headache, eye pain, muscle and joint pain, and often a rash as the fever falls. Chikungunya is set apart by disabling joint pain that can persist for months, and neither disease has a rapid cure, so the value of this window lies in the monitoring it triggers.

Influenza appears 1 to 4 days after exposure, usually 2 days, and in the tropics it is easily mistaken for dengue. Cholera is faster, appearing a few hours to 5 days later, usually 1 to 2 days, with profuse painless watery diarrhea and rapid dehydration rather than fever. Shigellosis follows 1 to 3 days after exposure and causes fever, cramps, tenesmus, and bloody diarrhea. Rickettsial infections appear 2 to 14 days later, usually about 5 to 7 days, with fever, headache, and muscle aches, and the clue is an eschar at the site of a tick, mite, or flea bite. These illnesses can become critical before any laboratory result returns, so rehydration begins on the clinical picture.

What the short windows mean diagnostically is that they serve as an exclusion tool as much as a diagnostic one. A fever beginning within 7 days of exposure cannot be explained by the long-incubation diseases unless an older second exposure exists. The examination then turns to specific findings: the rash and falling platelet count of dengue, the dehydration of cholera, the blood in the stool of shigellosis, and the eschar of rickettsial disease. A single negative test is least reassuring here, because organism counts rise and fall across the first days and a specimen taken too early is often falsely negative. Repeat sampling and paired serology are the answers, so record every date and time precisely.

The second group covers 1 to 3 weeks, and it is the busiest window in tropical medicine. Malaria sits at its center, and the incubation period of Plasmodium falciparum, the species responsible for the most severe form of malaria, is 7 to 30 days, usually 10 to 15. Plasmodium vivax and Plasmodium ovale can relapse weeks or months after a bite, because dormant liver forms reactivate, which is why malaria can appear long after a patient leaves an endemic area. No clinical feature reliably separates malaria from the other causes of fever in this window. The World Health Organization therefore advises parasitological testing for every febrile patient with a compatible exposure, and repeat testing when the first result is negative.

Typhoid fever appears 6 to 30 days after exposure, usually 7 to 14, and it unfolds in steps. Fever climbs through the first week, abdominal pain and a faint rash follow in the second, and untreated cases may bleed or perforate the bowel in the third. Leptospirosis appears after 5 to 14 days, with a range of 2 to 30, and often begins with sudden fever, severe headache, calf muscle pain, and red eyes, progressing in severe cases to jaundice, kidney injury, and bleeding. Both diseases are diagnosed by tests that perform poorly early in the illness, so ask about flooded streets, rodent contact, freshwater swimming, and unpasteurized dairy. When leptospirosis is suspected with jaundice and kidney injury, treatment should not wait for confirmation.

Hepatitis A has an incubation period of 15 to 50 days, averaging about 28, and hepatitis E runs 15 to 60 days, averaging about 40. Both spread by the fecal-oral route and can cause jaundice, dark urine, and fatigue, and though both are usually self-limited, hepatitis E is dangerous in pregnancy. Acute HIV infection appears 10 to 28 days after exposure and often looks like a mononucleosis-like illness with fever, sore throat, rash, and enlarged lymph nodes, so test for HIV whenever the window and the syndrome fit. Brucellosis appears 5 days to 6 months later, usually 2 to 4 weeks, with undulant fever and joint pain after livestock contact or unpasteurized milk. Q fever appears after 9 to 39 days, and yaws appears after 9 to 90 days as a painless skin lesion.

The middle window is where most diagnostic confusion lives, because many diseases share the same 1 to 3 week interval and the same nonspecific fever. The answer is not to test everything at once but to let the exposure history and the pace of illness order the possibilities. A patient with a slow pulse and abdominal symptoms after travel differs from one with jaundice, red eyes, and muscle pain after a flood. Serology depends on the immune response rather than the organism, so testing too early produces false negatives. This is the group where a repeat sample 2 to 4 weeks later is most valuable.

The third group is measured in months, and it shifts the work from acute resuscitation to chronic assessment and public health action. Active pulmonary tuberculosis usually emerges within the first 2 years of infection, yet latent infection can reactivate decades later when immunity falls, so age, HIV infection, diabetes, and steroid use dominate the risk assessment. Cough for more than 2 weeks, fever, night sweats, and weight loss suggest tuberculosis, but their absence does not exclude it. Because transmission is airborne, the diagnosis triggers isolation and notification before it triggers treatment. Screening household contacts and offering preventive therapy belong to the same decision, and the regimen depends on current World Health Organization guidance.

Cutaneous leishmaniasis appears weeks to months after the bite of an infected sand fly, while visceral leishmaniasis emerges after 2 to 8 months with prolonged fever, weight loss, an enlarged spleen and liver, and suppression of the bone marrow. Lymphatic filariasis may produce no swelling for many months or years, so the parasite can be present long before the limb changes, and onchocerciasis takes 1 to 3 years before skin and eye changes appear. Once symptoms begin, rabies is almost universally fatal, so its incubation period of 1 to 3 months, sometimes longer, is a window for prophylaxis rather than a waiting game. Any bite from a suspect animal needs wound washing and immediate assessment for vaccination and, for high-risk exposures, immune globulin.

Schistosomiasis causes an acute illness 2 to 8 weeks after exposure to contaminated freshwater, with fever, cough, abdominal pain, and an enlarged liver and spleen, and the chronic form develops over months to years as eggs lodge in the bladder, bowel, or liver. Amoebic liver abscess usually appears weeks to months after infection, with fever and right upper quadrant pain, and it may occur without preceding diarrhea. Hepatitis B appears 45 to 180 days after exposure, averaging about 90 days, and may pass unnoticed into chronic infection. Chagas disease begins 1 to 2 weeks after the bite of an infected triatomine bug and then persists silently for decades before causing heart failure or digestive dilation. Echinococcosis develops over years, with cysts found incidentally or when they cause pain, so a migrant who has not traveled for 20 years can still carry these infections.

The fourth group is the very long, meaning conditions that can appear decades after exposure. Leprosy has an incubation period commonly given as 2 to 20 years, averaging about 5 to 8 years, and it begins insidiously with a skin patch that has lost sensation. Strongyloidiasis can persist for decades through autoinfection, in which larvae mature within the same host without leaving, and it may cause only intermittent symptoms until immunity is suppressed. Corticosteroids, cancer chemotherapy, or transplantation can then convert a quiet infection into disseminated strongyloidiasis with overwhelming sepsis, which is why screening before starting corticosteroids is a standard safety step for patients who have lived in endemic areas. A negative recent travel history is never a reason to stop asking where a patient was born and raised.

The diagnostic meaning of the long windows is that today's illness may be the first evidence of an infection acquired years ago, so the search widens rather than stops. Serology is strongest here, because the immune response has had time to develop, but a positive result may reflect past exposure rather than active disease. Imaging carries more weight: chest radiography for tuberculosis, ultrasound for liver abscess and echinococcosis, and echocardiography for Chagas heart disease. Public health follow-up becomes part of the clinical plan, because tuberculosis, leprosy, and hepatitis B require notification and contact investigation. The nurse often reconstructs the timeline from old documents and migration records, so write down the country, the years of residence, and the date of arrival.

The first decision point is when to treat empirically, before a diagnosis is confirmed. Empirical treatment is justified when the likely disease is dangerous and the available test is slow, insensitive, or absent, and when waiting carries more risk than the drug. Familiar examples include suspected severe malaria, cholera with severe dehydration, suspected bacterial meningitis, and suspected rickettsial infection with an eschar. Obtain the specimens you can obtain quickly, then start treatment without letting sampling delay the first dose. The agent must follow current World Health Organization guidance or the equivalent national guidance, and every dose must be verified against the patient's weight, kidney and liver function, allergies, and pregnancy status before it is given.

The second decision point is when to refer urgently, and in tropical medicine the answer is often sooner than expected. Severe malaria with impaired consciousness, convulsions, difficulty breathing, dark urine, or low blood glucose needs immediate parenteral treatment and monitoring. Dengue with warning signs needs admission even when the patient looks stable. Suspected rabies, suspected viral hemorrhagic fever, tetanus, meningococcal infection, snakebite with coagulopathy, and any patient in shock or respiratory failure also belong here. Referral is not a transfer of responsibility but a clinical intervention, so stabilize the patient, document what has been given, and speak directly to the receiving team before the journey begins.

The third decision point is when to isolate, which protects the patient and the health workers alike. Airborne precautions are required for suspected pulmonary tuberculosis, measles, and varicella, and for some viral hemorrhagic fevers. Contact precautions, with gowns, gloves, and careful hand hygiene, are required for cholera, shigellosis, and other enteric infections, and for infected wounds that shed organisms. Suspected viral hemorrhagic fever usually requires contact and droplet precautions together, and personal protective equipment must be put on and removed with a trained observer watching every step. Clean water, safe waste disposal, and hand hygiene stop most transmission in a ward, and suspected notifiable disease is reported early rather than after confirmation.

The fourth decision point is the timing of repeated malaria films, and it is the most contested arithmetic in tropical fever. A single negative blood film does not exclude malaria, because parasite density fluctuates and can fall below detection for hours at a time. Guidance from the World Health Organization and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention advises repeating thick and thin films every 12 to 24 hours for a total of 48 to 72 hours, usually three sets. A rapid diagnostic test can be used alongside microscopy, but it may remain positive for weeks after a treated infection, and a polymerase chain reaction test is more sensitive but is not available everywhere. The nursing contribution is decisive: label every specimen with the exact time, make sure the second and third films are actually drawn, and alert the clinician if the patient deteriorates before the series is complete.

The fifth decision point is the day of illness at which dengue turns critical. The critical phase begins as the fever falls, usually between day 3 and day 7 of illness and most often around day 4 to day 6, and it lasts about 24 to 48 hours. This is when plasma leakage raises the hematocrit, lowers the blood pressure, and, without careful fluid management, causes shock. The paradox is that the patient often feels better as the temperature drops, so watch for persistent vomiting, severe abdominal pain, bleeding, black stools, restlessness, and cold clammy skin. The nursing plan is serial monitoring of vital signs, urine output, hematocrit, and platelet count, with fluids adjusted to the leakage and verified for each patient against current World Health Organization guidance.

The logistics of this appendix matter as much as its biology, because a window is useful only if the specimen is collected and delivered correctly. Draw blood films for malaria before treatment begins and send them without delay, and draw blood cultures before antibiotics whenever that does not delay treatment. Serology usually needs paired samples, an acute specimen in the first week and a convalescent specimen 2 to 4 weeks later, and both must be stored so that the pair can be tested together. Cold chain failures, mislabeled tubes, and specimens left overnight in a hot vehicle commonly make a result uninterpretable, and the nurse who checks the label and the timing prevents most of them. Some diseases must be reported to a public health authority at the moment of suspicion, and when a result is delayed the clinical plan still moves forward.

Monitoring is where these windows become concrete nursing work. For any febrile illness in the short group, record temperature, pulse, blood pressure, respiratory rate, and level of consciousness at a defined interval, and record urine output, because urine is the most honest measure of perfusion. In dengue, track the hematocrit and the platelet count and watch the trend rather than a single value, since a rising hematocrit with a falling platelet count signals leakage. In severe malaria, check the blood glucose at least every 4 to 6 hours, because hypoglycemia is common and rapidly reversible, and watch for convulsions, anemia, and difficulty breathing. In cholera and other severe diarrheal disease, count the stools or weigh them, replace the losses, and reassess continuously, and write every observation down with its time.

Adverse effects deserve the same attention as the diseases, because treatment can harm a patient who is already fragile. Doxycycline causes nausea and photosensitivity and is generally avoided in pregnancy and in young children for most indications, though the balance changes in serious rickettsial disease. Isoniazid can injure the liver and cause peripheral neuropathy, which is why pyridoxine is often given alongside it, and rifampin turns urine orange and interacts with many other drugs, including antiretroviral therapy and hormonal contraception. Ethambutol can damage the optic nerve, so vision is assessed before treatment and monitored during it. Praziquantel and the drugs used against filarial infections can provoke fever, rash, and swelling as the parasites die, so every one of these effects is a reason to warn the patient and schedule follow-up.

The nursing role across all of these windows is one of construction, surveillance, and translation. The nurse builds the timeline by asking about travel, residence, freshwater contact, animal exposure, sexual history, injections, and blood transfusion, and by writing every date down. The nurse watches for the change that the window predicts, whether that is the fall in temperature that opens the dengue critical phase or the third negative film that lets malaria be set aside. The nurse translates the plan for the patient and the family, explaining why a second blood test is needed next week and why a medicine that turns urine orange is still the right one. The nurse also holds the safety net by verifying the dose before it is given, and often is the only person who recognizes that a former resident of an endemic area with eosinophilia and steroid exposure needs screening for strongyloidiasis.

The regimens described in this appendix are drawn from current guidance of the World Health Organization, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control, and that guidance is revised as resistance patterns and evidence change. A dose that is correct in one country may be wrong in another, and drug names, formulations, and pediatric schedules differ between national programs. Before you prescribe or administer anything described here, read the current guideline for your setting and confirm the dose, the route, the frequency, and the duration against the patient in front of you. Check the weight, kidney and liver function, allergies, and pregnancy status, and when two clinicians independently calculate the same dose, the chance of arithmetic error falls sharply. The responsibility for the dose you give rests with the person who gives it.

To summarize, the incubation period is a filter and not a verdict, because it tells you which diseases are possible in the time you have measured. Illness within 7 days of exposure points to dengue, chikungunya, influenza, cholera, shigellosis, and rickettsial disease, and it demands rapid assessment of hydration, bleeding, and shock. Illness at 1 to 3 weeks points to malaria, typhoid, leptospirosis, hepatitis A and E, acute HIV infection, brucellosis, Q fever, and yaws, and it requires that malaria be excluded with repeated films over 48 to 72 hours. Illness measured in months points to tuberculosis, leishmaniasis, filarial infection, rabies, schistosomiasis, amoebic liver abscess, hepatitis B, Chagas disease, and echinococcosis, and it shifts the work toward serology, imaging, notification, and contact investigation. Decades later, leprosy and strongyloidiasis can still appear, so the residence history matters as much as the travel history. Know the window, verify the dose against current guidance, monitor the trend rather than the single number, and let the timeline choose the next test.
