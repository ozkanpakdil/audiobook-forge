# Chapter 36 — Antimicrobial Stewardship and Resistance

Antimicrobial resistance is not a failure of a drug; it is evolution happening in front of you, because every dose of an antibiotic is also a selection experiment. When a bacterial population meets an antibiotic, most cells die, but a very small number may already carry a gene or a mutation that lets them survive, and those survivors then multiply without competition. The result is a population no longer susceptible to the drug that once killed it, and the change is heritable, so the daughter cells inherit the advantage. Resistance arises by spontaneous mutation in the chromosome, and it also arrives from outside when a bacterium takes up a small circular piece of DNA called a plasmid that carries a resistance gene. Because bacteria share these elements across species, resistance is a common pool that any organism can draw from. The nurse who prescribes, dispenses, or administers an antibiotic is therefore influencing the genetics of a whole microbial community.

Several mechanisms of transfer explain why resistance travels so fast. Conjugation passes a plasmid directly from one bacterium to another through a bridge, and it can move a resistance gene between organisms that are not closely related. Transduction carries genes inside a bacterial virus, and transformation takes up free DNA from the environment, so even a dying bacterium can donate its resistance to a living neighbor. Integrons, which are genetic cassettes that capture resistance genes one after another, allow one organism to accumulate resistance to several drug classes at once. Resistance then spreads through carriage as much as through illness, because a patient may carry a resistant organism in the gut or on the skin, feel entirely well, and still pass it on through the hands of a caregiver. In crowded hospitals and households, in food and untreated water, and along the routes of migration and trade, resistant organisms move steadily between people and continents.

Resistance usually carries a fitness cost, because the protective gene often makes the organism less efficient when the drug is absent, but a second mutation can compensate, and the resistant strain then persists even when the antibiotic is withdrawn. That is why resistance rarely reverses quickly on its own and why prevention is so much easier than reversal. The burden falls hardest where the infections are already most lethal, where the laboratory cannot tell you what you are treating, and where the next effective drug may be unavailable or unaffordable. In those settings the nurse often stands at the point where the decision is actually made, which is why stewardship is a nursing subject as much as a medical one.

The drivers of resistance are not mysterious, and most are visible in any busy clinic or market in a tropical country. The first and largest is access without a prescription, because antibiotics are sold openly by shopkeepers, market vendors, and unlicensed dispensers, and a patient with a fever can buy a capsule without ever seeing a clinician. In many places the purchase follows a neighbor's advice or the memory of what worked last time, and the drug chosen may be the wrong class, the wrong dose, or an old preparation kept in a hot room. This is not simply ignorance, because the alternative may be a long walk, a lost day of work, and a fee the family cannot pay, so buying a cheap antibiotic is a rational act within a constrained life. The danger is that a low, irregular exposure selects resistant organisms while failing to cure the patient.

Substandard and falsified medicines are a second driver, and they are a supply chain problem rather than a patient behavior. A substandard medicine was made by a legitimate manufacturer but contains less active ingredient than the label claims, or it degrades before its expiry because it was stored in heat and humidity, while a falsified medicine deliberately misrepresents its identity or its source. Either way the patient receives a dose too low to kill the organism, so a substandard course is functionally the same as a course stopped early, exposing the bacteria to a drug without finishing them off. The problem is worst where regulation is weak and where a genuine product and a counterfeit look and smell the same. Nurses can act on this by checking packaging, batch numbers, and expiry dates, by storing medicines correctly, and by reporting products that fail to work. When a patient does not respond to a correctly prescribed antibiotic, a counterfeit medicine belongs on the list of explanations alongside resistance and a wrong diagnosis.

Stopping a course early is the third driver, and it is the one most amenable to nursing action. Patients stop for reasons that make sense to them: the fever settles after 2 days, the pills cost money that is needed for food, the side effects are unpleasant, or the clinic is a full day's journey and the family cannot spare another trip. From the organism's point of view the reason does not matter, because the bacteria that survive an incomplete course are precisely the ones that were least susceptible, and they now multiply in a body that has been given a partial selection advantage. The classic teaching about finishing every course has been refined, because some infections are now treated for a shorter, precisely defined duration, but the principle is unchanged: the duration should be the one current guidance recommends for that diagnosis, and it should be completed. Ask directly about the pills left in the packet, because unfinished packets at home are both a sign of failure and a future source of unsupervised self-treatment.

Agriculture is the fourth driver, and it operates on a scale that dwarfs human medicine in several countries. Antibiotics are given to poultry, pigs, and cattle not only to treat illness but to promote growth and to offset crowded, stressful conditions, and they are also used in fish farming and sprayed on crops in some regions. A large share is excreted unchanged into soil and water, where it continues to select resistant organisms in the environment and in the gut flora of farm workers and their families. Resistant bacteria and resistance genes then move from farms into food, into rivers, and into the human population. This is why stewardship is described as a single health problem linking human medicine, veterinary medicine, and the environment, and why nurses should support policies that end the routine use of medically important antibiotics for growth promotion.

The fifth driver is the absence of diagnostics, and it most often forces a nurse to treat blindly. In a clinic without a microscope, a culture incubator, or a reliable rapid test, a child with a fever can be treated only by pattern recognition, and the temptation is to cover every possibility with an antibiotic. Malaria and bacterial illness look alike in the early hours, a viral respiratory infection and pneumonia may both begin with a cough and a fever, and a diarrheal illness is usually viral even when an antibiotic is handed over. When the only available test is a rapid diagnostic test for malaria, a negative result removes one diagnosis but does not say which of the remaining diagnoses is responsible. The result is empirical treatment on a large scale, and much of it selects resistance without benefiting the patient, so better diagnostics are a stewardship intervention in their own right.

The consequences are already visible in the organisms that cause the most common serious infections. The beta-lactam antibiotics, which include the penicillins and the cephalosporins, share a chemical ring that many bacteria destroy with enzymes called beta-lactamases. Some of these enzymes, the extended-spectrum beta-lactamases, break down not only the older penicillins but also the modern cephalosporins designed to resist them. They are usually carried on plasmids, so they spread easily, and they are found most often in Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae, two of the commonest causes of urinary tract and bloodstream infection. An infection with one of these organisms may resist almost every oral antibiotic a clinic stocks, turning a simple bladder infection into a problem needing an intravenous drug and a hospital bed. A patient with a previous resistant urinary infection or a recent hospital stay is at higher risk, and a culture before treatment is especially valuable in that patient.

Carbapenems are the broadest and often the last reliable class of beta-lactam antibiotics, and resistance to them is the most serious development in this field. Some organisms produce carbapenemase enzymes that destroy these drugs as well, and the genes for these enzymes are also mobile, which has let them spread among Klebsiella pneumoniae, Escherichia coli, Acinetobacter species, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa. An organism resistant to carbapenems is frequently resistant to nearly everything else, so treatment may depend on older, more toxic drugs such as colistin or on newer agents unavailable or unaffordable in many tropical settings. These infections cluster in intensive care units, in neonatal wards, and among patients with long hospital stays or invasive devices, and they carry a very high death rate. The nurse's contribution to control is unglamorous and decisive: hand hygiene, care of intravenous lines, removal of unnecessary catheters, and separation of affected patients. Because treatment options are so few, prevention is the main strategy rather than one strategy among several.

Typhoid fever, caused by the bacterium Salmonella enterica serovar Typhi, shows how resistance reshapes a familiar disease. The organism was once controlled easily with chloramphenicol, then with ampicillin and co-trimoxazole, and resistance to each followed widespread use in turn. Fluoroquinolones became the treatment of choice, and resistance to them is now common across South Asia and beyond, so a traveler with typhoid may need a third-generation cephalosporin such as ceftriaxone or an oral macrolide such as azithromycin instead of the older tablets. More recently, strains have appeared in Pakistan that produce extended-spectrum beta-lactamases and resist even ceftriaxone, which leaves few oral options and raises the prospect of a disease that once needed a single week of tablets requiring intravenous treatment. Because the diagnosis is often made clinically, and because blood culture is unavailable in many settings where the disease is common, much of this resistance is generated and transmitted without ever being documented.

Malaria provides the clearest example of how resistance can undo decades of progress, and it deserves close attention because it is measured in child deaths. Artemisinin-based combination therapy works because the artemisinin component kills the bulk of the parasites quickly while the partner drug clears the remainder, and resistance begins when parasites clear more slowly than expected. The first signs appeared in the Greater Mekong subregion, where mutations in the parasite, particularly in a gene called kelch13, allow a fraction of the parasites to survive the artemisinin exposure, and these parasites have since been detected in eastern Africa. Partial resistance to artemisinin alone is dangerous because it leaves more parasites for the partner drug to handle, and if the partner drug also fails, the combination fails as a whole. The nurse who performs a follow-up test on day three, records the result, and reports delayed clearance is protecting the drug for everyone else.

Mass treatment programs against trachoma and yaws have produced a different kind of resistance, and they show that even a well-run public health campaign must be watched. Trachoma, an infection of the eye caused by Chlamydia trachomatis, and yaws, a skin disease caused by Treponema pallidum subspecies pertenue, have both been targets of repeated mass administration of azithromycin to whole communities. This approach is effective and has brought both diseases close to elimination in several countries, but azithromycin is also a macrolide, and macrolides are used for pneumonia and other respiratory infections. Where the drug has been distributed repeatedly and widely, resistance has emerged in Streptococcus pneumoniae and in other organisms, and the same concern applies to the treponeme itself. The answer is not to abandon mass treatment, which saves sight and prevents disfigurement, but to monitor resistance and to use the campaigns alongside water, sanitation and hygiene measures that reduce the need to repeat them.

Stewardship is the set of practices that keep antimicrobials effective for the patients who need them, and it is a discipline of balance rather than of restriction. It asks four questions at once: does this patient have an infection that an antimicrobial will treat, is this the right drug for the likely organism, is the dose and duration right for this person, and can the treatment be narrowed or stopped sooner than planned. It is not the same as withholding treatment, and it is not the same as always prescribing the cheapest or narrowest option, because an ineffective antibiotic is not stewardship but harm. In a working system stewardship is shared among prescribers, pharmacists, laboratory staff, infection prevention teams, and nurses, and the nurse often notices that a drug has been running for days without a plan. Good stewardship improves outcomes, reduces adverse effects and cost, and slows resistance at the same time.

Local guidelines and formularies are the foundation, because a general recommendation from a distant authority cannot tell you which organisms are circulating in your own hospital. A formulary is a short, agreed list of the medicines a facility will stock and use, with the indication, dose, and duration for each, and it narrows choice in a way that improves consistency and simplifies procurement. An antibiogram is a yearly summary of which organisms were isolated in your laboratory and which drugs they were susceptible to, and it converts your own culture results into a guide for empirical treatment. Standard treatment guidelines built on that local data tell the clinician what to start before the culture returns. The World Health Organization publishes an essential medicines list and model treatment guidance that many countries adapt, and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control both publish stewardship frameworks, but adaptation to local resistance and local supply is what makes a guideline usable.

Better diagnostics are the intervention with the largest effect on antibiotic use, because they replace a guess with a result. Blood culture is the reference standard for invasive bacterial infection, and it is often the only way to know which drug will work, but it requires a specimen taken before the first dose, careful technique to avoid contamination, and a laboratory that can incubate and identify organisms reliably. Rapid diagnostic tests for malaria have already changed fever management by allowing a negative result to redirect the clinician away from antimalarials, and similar point-of-care tests are beginning to do the same for other infections. Markers of inflammation such as C-reactive protein can help distinguish a bacterial infection from a viral one in some settings, and their greatest value may be in giving the clinician confidence to stop an antibiotic rather than to start one. Molecular tests identify organisms and resistance genes directly from a specimen within hours, but they are costly and must be linked to a clinical decision or their information is wasted.

Right drug, right dose, and right duration is a single idea with three parts, and dose is where nursing practice has the most direct leverage. Underdosing is not a cautious choice but a resistance risk, because a concentration below the level needed to kill the organism suppresses the susceptible bacteria and leaves the less susceptible ones to grow. Many antibiotics are dosed by weight, and low body weight, malnutrition, and edema all change the volume into which a drug distributes, so a dose copied from an adult who weighs 80 kilograms may be wrong for the patient in front of you. A dose correct for a healthy kidney can become toxic when clearance falls, so a baseline creatinine measurement belongs before the first dose. Current guidance from the World Health Organization gives amoxicillin at 500 milligrams by mouth every eight hours for many respiratory infections, ceftriaxone at 1 to 2 grams intravenously once daily for severe infection, and gentamicin at 5 to 7 milligrams per kilogram once daily with monitoring of kidney function and hearing, but these are examples of what guidance looks like and not a substitute for it. Regimens are revised as evidence accumulates, they differ between countries and between programs, and the World Health Organization, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control do not always agree, so the current guideline must be checked before the first dose is given. Reading the dose aloud, confirming the weight, and having a second person verify a high-risk medicine prevent the opposite errors of overdose and underdose.

De-escalation is the practice of narrowing treatment once the organism and its susceptibilities are known, and it is the step most often forgotten because starting feels more urgent than refining. When the culture identifies an organism susceptible to a narrow drug, the broad agent should be replaced, both to reduce collateral damage to the patient's own flora and to preserve the broad agent for someone who will need it. The switch from intravenous to oral therapy is a related decision, and it can be made when the patient has been free of fever for a defined period, is eating and absorbing well, and has no infection that requires intravenous treatment such as infection of the heart valves, meningitis, or a deep abscess. This switch shortens hospital stay, lowers the risk of line infection, and frees a bed, and in many infections the oral drug achieves the same tissue concentrations if it is well absorbed. The nurse is usually the first to notice that the patient has met those criteria, because the nurse takes the temperature, watches the meals, and counts the days of the intravenous line.

Cultures before antibiotics is an easy rule to state and a difficult one to keep, because the sickest patient needs the drug immediately and the specimen takes time. The rule exists because a culture taken after the first dose may be sterile, and then the clinician is left with a resistant organism that has never been identified and a treatment that cannot be narrowed. The practical compromise is to draw the cultures and take the other specimens first, then give the antibiotic without delay, so the order is specimen, then drug, within the same few minutes. The nurse should know the correct volume of blood for each bottle, because too little blood is the commonest reason a culture is falsely negative, and should label the specimen at the bedside with the time, the site, and whether antibiotics have already been given. The antibiotic time-out then reviews every antibiotic at 48 to 72 hours, when the cultures are often available and the patient's response can be judged. It asks whether the patient still needs an antibiotic, whether the diagnosis is still right, whether the drug can be narrowed or given by mouth, and how many more days it should run, and a time-out that ends in stopping an antibiotic is a success rather than a failure. Nurses can initiate it, because they hold the information about the fever curve and the patient's tolerance.

Adherence counseling is where stewardship becomes a conversation, and it decides whether the plan you write is the plan the patient follows. The patient needs to know what the medicine is for, how many days it should be taken, and what to do if a dose is missed, in plain language and without a lecture. Teaching should include the expected side effects and those that should bring the patient back, because a patient who is not warned about nausea or a rash may simply stop the drug and say nothing. The teach-back method, in which the patient explains the plan in their own words, reveals misunderstanding far better than asking whether they have understood. Practical barriers should be named and solved: the cost of a full course, the distance to the clinic, the need to take a dose at work, the storage of a medicine that must stay cool, and any belief that the illness was caused by something else. Counseling before the first dose and repeated at every contact prevents the incomplete courses that breed resistance.

Monitoring is the other half of safe prescribing, because an antibiotic that is working can still injure the patient, and the injury is often detectable before it becomes serious. Kidney function should be watched in anyone receiving an aminoglycoside or a glycopeptide such as vancomycin, and hearing should be assessed in anyone receiving an aminoglycoside, because the damage from these drugs can be permanent. Liver enzymes matter for the antituberculosis drugs, and a patient on a prolonged course should be asked about nausea, dark urine, and yellowing of the eyes. Allergic reactions range from a mild rash to anaphylaxis, and the nurse should record the exact drug and the exact reaction rather than the vague label of an allergy, because an unverified allergy label pushes prescribers toward broader and more expensive drugs. Diarrhea during or after a course may signal infection with Clostridioides difficile, which requires specific treatment and isolation precautions. Prolonged courses also promote fungal overgrowth, so the mouth, the skin folds, and the genital area should be inspected in any patient who has received repeated antibiotics.

The logistical side of stewardship is unglamorous and decisive, because a guideline can only work if the right drug is on the shelf when it is needed. A facility needs a reliable system for forecasting how much of each antibiotic it will use, for ordering in time, and for keeping a buffer against delays, since a stockout forces substitution with whatever is available and substitution is a common route to inappropriate treatment. Antibiotics must be kept dry, out of direct sun, and within the temperature range on the label, with the cold chain protected where a product requires it. Expiry dates should be checked on receipt and on the shelf, and stock should be rotated so older supplies are used first, because an expired medicine that is given anyway is a substandard dose. When a shortage is unavoidable, the clinician should know in advance which alternative is acceptable and which is not. The nurse who manages the cupboard, records the consumption, and reports a shortage early is performing stewardship as surely as the prescriber who narrows a drug.

Infection prevention and control is stewardship by another name, because an infection that never occurs never needs an antibiotic. Hand hygiene at the five moments of patient contact remains the single most effective measure, and it requires not only knowledge but also working sinks, clean water, soap, and alcohol-based rub placed where the work happens. Clean equipment, sterile technique for intravenous lines and urinary catheters, prompt removal of those devices, and safe handling of waste and sharps all reduce the bacterial burden that antibiotics are asked to control. In outbreaks of resistant organisms, grouping affected patients together, dedicating equipment, and screening high-risk admissions can interrupt transmission when the environment itself is colonized. Vaccination reduces the infections that would otherwise be treated empirically, and environmental cleaning deserves particular attention, because resistant organisms survive on surfaces and a ward that looks clean is not necessarily disinfected. None of these measures is dramatic, and together they do more than any new drug likely will.

The hardest question in stewardship is the ethical one, and it must be answered honestly rather than avoided. In a setting where the next clinic is a day's journey away, where a laboratory result will never return, and where the patient may not be able to come back, withholding an antibiotic from a febrile child in order to protect a global resource is not obviously right. The principles collide: the duty to help this patient, the duty to avoid harm, the duty to use scarce resources fairly, and the duty to respect the patient's own judgment about their care. A stewardship program that ignores this reality becomes rationing dressed as science, and it loses the trust of the staff and the community on which it depends. The defensible answer is usually to treat the patient in front of you with the best available assessment, to document the reasoning, to use a narrow drug when the diagnosis supports it, and to arrange a follow-up that is actually possible, whether by a return visit, a community health worker, or a telephone contact. Where resources are genuinely limited, the fair question is not whether to treat but how to distribute the treatment that exists, and that question should be answered openly by the team rather than silently at the bedside.

The global response is organized around a plan adopted by the World Health Assembly in 2015, the Global Action Plan on Antimicrobial Resistance, which set five strategic objectives for every country. Those objectives are to improve awareness and understanding of resistance, to strengthen surveillance and research, to reduce the incidence of infection through sanitation and infection control, to optimize the use of antimicrobial medicines in human and animal health, and to ensure sustainable investment in new medicines, diagnostics, and vaccines. Countries were asked to write national action plans, and many have done so, though implementation depends on funding, laboratory capacity, and political attention that are unevenly distributed. The plan rests on the recognition that human health, animal health, and the environment form one system, so action in a hospital alone cannot succeed while agricultural use continues unchecked. New antibiotics will help but will not solve the problem alone, because developing one is expensive and slow and the commercial incentive is weak when the drug must be used sparingly. Vaccines against the organisms that drive resistance, including pneumococcus, typhoid, and Haemophilus influenzae type b, prevent the infections that lead to antibiotic use, and faster bedside tests would change decisions more than any single drug. Behavior change among prescribers and patients, supported by training, feedback, and public communication, remains the least expensive and most underused intervention, and the nurse is well placed to deliver it.

To summarize, resistance is evolution driven by use, and every antibiotic decision is a selection pressure that reaches beyond the patient in front of you. It emerges through mutation and through the exchange of mobile genetic elements, and it spreads through hands, food, water, devices, and the movement of people, so it belongs to the whole community and not only to the hospital. In tropical settings the drivers are access without a prescription, substandard and falsified medicines, courses stopped early, routine use in agriculture, and the absence of diagnostics that can tell a bacterial infection from a viral one. The consequences already include extended-spectrum beta-lactamase producing organisms, carbapenem resistance, resistant typhoid, partial resistance of malaria parasites to artemisinin, and macrolide resistance after mass treatment of trachoma and yaws. The interventions that work are local guidelines and formularies, better diagnostics, the right drug at the right dose for the right duration, de-escalation, and the early switch from intravenous to oral treatment. Your part is concrete: take the cultures before the first dose, run the antibiotic time-out, counsel for adherence, monitor for adverse effects, keep the supply chain honest, prevent infection, and speak plainly when scarcity forces a choice. Check current guidance from the World Health Organization, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, or the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control before you prescribe, because regimens change and they differ between countries, and hold the balance between protecting this patient and preserving the drugs for the next one.
