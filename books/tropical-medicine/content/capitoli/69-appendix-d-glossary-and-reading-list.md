# Appendix D — Glossary, Key Guidelines, and Reading List

This appendix is not a list to be scanned but a set of definitions to be heard aloud. Each term is given as a sentence, because a fragment cannot be repeated back to a colleague or reasoned with. These words appear again and again in the chapters before this one. A listener who has them settled will hear every later sentence more accurately. Listen once for shape, then return whenever a word in a guideline stops making sense. The reading list at the end describes each title by what it contains.

A vector is a living carrier that moves a pathogen from one host to another. A reservoir is the population or environment where a pathogen normally lives and multiplies. A host is any organism that harbors the pathogen. The definitive host is where the parasite reaches sexual maturity, and the intermediate host is where it passes through an immature stage. An accidental host is one the pathogen can infect but does not need in order to persist. A zoonosis is an infection that passes naturally from an animal to a human being. Spillover is the moment when a pathogen crosses from its usual animal reservoir into a person.

Incidence is the number of new cases in a defined population over a defined period. It shows how fast transmission is happening now. Prevalence is the total number of existing cases in a population at a given moment. It includes people who have been ill for a long time as well as those just infected. A disease can have a low incidence and a high prevalence when it is acquired slowly and cannot be cured. The opposite pattern occurs when a disease kills quickly or resolves quickly. Incidence answers the question of risk, and prevalence answers the question of burden.

Endemic means that a disease is constantly present at a predictable and expected level. It does not mean harmless, because malaria and tuberculosis are endemic across large regions and still kill enormous numbers. Epidemic means that cases have risen above what is expected for that place and season. An outbreak is the same event on a smaller or more localized scale. Pandemic means an epidemic that has spread across several countries or continents with sustained transmission in each. Hyperendemic and holoendemic describe how intense malaria transmission is in an area. A single case of an eliminated disease is an outbreak.

Control means reducing a disease burden to a manageable level, and it accepts that transmission may continue. Elimination means interrupting transmission in a defined geographic area, so that no new locally acquired cases occur there. Surveillance and the tools must be maintained, because the pathogen can return. Elimination as a public health problem is weaker than elimination of transmission, because it allows cases to continue below an agreed threshold. Eradication means the permanent global reduction of cases to zero, so that intervention can stop. It has been achieved only for smallpox. A nurse who uses these four words precisely will be understood by a program officer.

Surveillance is the ongoing, systematic collection, analysis, and use of health data, and it exists to drive action. Indicator-based surveillance counts cases of named conditions through clinics and laboratories. Event-based surveillance listens for rumors, unusual clusters, and signals that fit no form. A case definition is the standard set of clinical, laboratory, and epidemiological criteria that decides whether a person counts as a case. It usually provides categories of suspected, probable, and confirmed. The same patient can be a suspected case in one country and a confirmed case in another. Case definitions are revised during outbreaks, so a nurse working from a version from the previous week may classify patients incorrectly.

Sensitivity is the proportion of people with a disease whom a test correctly identifies as having it. Specificity is the proportion of people without the disease whom a test correctly identifies as not having it. A highly sensitive test rarely misses a case but often raises a false alarm. A highly specific test rarely raises a false alarm but often misses a case. Predictive value depends on how common the disease is in the population being tested. The same test therefore performs differently in a referral hospital and in a village screening program. This is why a negative rapid malaria test may be repeated after 2 to 3 days when the story is convincing.

The incubation period is the time from infection to the first symptom or sign of illness, and it shapes quarantine and contact tracing. The prepatent period is the time from infection to the point at which the pathogen can be detected. The two periods are not the same, which explains why a test can be negative while a person is already infected. Parasitemia is the amount of parasite present in the blood, and it changes a malaria decision at the bedside. Viremia is the amount of virus present in the blood, and it governs the chance that a mosquito will be infected from that patient. Neither measurement is fixed, because both rise and fall with the days of the illness.

Chemoprophylaxis is the use of a medicine before or during exposure to prevent disease rather than to treat it. It is most familiar in malaria, where it suppresses the parasite instead of sterilizing the infection. Suppression holds the parasite below the level that causes symptoms, so illness can follow after the drug is stopped. This is why a course is sometimes finished after departure, and the schedule belongs to current national guidance. Chemoprophylaxis differs from presumptive treatment, which is a full course given on the suspicion of infection. It also differs from terminal prophylaxis, which clears dormant liver forms at the end of exposure.

Mass drug administration is the treatment of an entire at-risk population at intervals, without testing each person first. It is the engine of programs against lymphatic filariasis, onchocerciasis, soil-transmitted helminths, schistosomiasis, and trachoma. The medicines are safe enough at population scale, and repeated rounds reduce the reservoir until transmission falls. Coverage, meaning the proportion of eligible people who swallow the medicine, decides whether the program succeeds. Mass drug administration also requires community consent and a system for recording adverse effects. Directly observed therapy means that a named person watches the patient swallow each dose, and the observer may be a health worker, a trained community member, or a family member. It was developed for tuberculosis, where irregular treatment breeds resistance.

Task-shifting is the deliberate redistribution of clinical tasks from more highly trained workers to workers with shorter training. It is supported by protocols, supervision, and a clear scope of practice. It allows a clinic with a single physician and 12 nurses to function, and it is why an advanced practice nurse may prescribe in a district hospital. Task-shifting is safe when the protocols are current, the supervision is real, the referral path is agreed, and the law authorizes the worker. It is unsafe when it is used as a substitute for staffing. The nurse who accepts a shifted task also accepts the duty to escalate when a case falls outside the protocol.

The World Health Organization is the agency of the United Nations responsible for international public health. Its role is to synthesize evidence across countries and issue guidance that a ministry of health can adapt. Its malaria guidelines, its tuberculosis guidance, and its model list of essential medicines are the documents a program officer hands to a new nurse. The organization also maintains international standards for surveillance and outbreak reporting. Its guidance carries grades that describe how strong each recommendation is and how confident the evidence is. Because it speaks to a global audience, its advice is often broader than the national guidance that follows it.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention is the national public health agency of the United States, best known in tropical medicine for its travel health guidance and its surveillance reports. Its travel medicine handbook, known as the Yellow Book, gathers pretravel advice, country risks, and vaccine and chemoprophylaxis recommendations. Its weekly report on morbidity and mortality is one of the fastest ways to read about an outbreak while it is still unfolding. The European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control monitors communicable disease across the European Union. Its value lies in its surveillance bulletins and its short, dated rapid risk assessments. Those assessments assume European laboratory capacity, so they must be read alongside a national protocol.

Beneath the international agencies sit the national programs, and it is the national program that a nurse must obey. It has adapted the global evidence to the local drug supply, the local laboratory, and the local law. Ministries of health issue their own treatment guidelines, essential medicine lists, and reporting forms, and these carry legal weight in a clinic. The adaptation matters because drug availability, resistance patterns, and diagnostic capacity differ. This is what the nurse must hear clearly before prescribing. Treatment regimens are revised as evidence accumulates, national recommendations differ from one country to another, and they change from year to year. The current national guideline and the product label must be checked immediately before any medicine is prescribed or administered.

A guideline is only as good as its date and its method. The first two things to look for are when it was published and how its evidence was graded. A document that states its search strategy, its conflicts of interest, and the strength of each recommendation deserves more trust than a page of assertions with no author. When a new guideline appears, read the summary of changes first, because that is where the revisions affecting practice are concentrated. In a setting with intermittent electricity and expensive data, keep one authoritative copy per clinic, dated on its cover. Refresh it through the district health office or the national program, and give one named person that task, because an unowned document becomes an old document.

Logistics are unglamorous and decisive, and the shelves a nurse uses are shaped by weight, batteries, and weather. A textbook in the clinic and a printed protocol in a plastic sleeve are consulted more often than a perfect file on a laptop. The danger is the well-thumbed outdated printout, which is trusted precisely because it is familiar. Every printed page should carry its date and be replaced as a unit rather than page by page. Expired medicines, cold chain failures, and stock-outs also belong to logistics. The nurse who checks the vaccine carrier temperature is doing clinical work. When a drug is unavailable, substitution is not casual, because the alternatives differ in dose, safety, and monitoring.

Monitoring after a medicine is started is where attention converts into safety, and the parameters depend on the drug. Some medicines require a blood count, some require liver or kidney tests, and some require a glucose measurement. A baseline value taken before the first dose turns a later abnormality into a comparison instead of a puzzle. The practical instrument is a flow sheet that records the date, the dose given, the observation, and the person who gave it. Monitoring also includes the patient's report of sleep, appetite, vision, hearing, skin, and mood, because some harm is felt before it is measured. The nurse who asks the same short set of questions at every visit will detect toxicity early.

An adverse effect is a harmful and unintended response to a medicine at a normal dose, and reporting it is a professional duty. The four things worth writing down are what was given, when it was given, what else the patient was taking, and what happened next. National pharmacovigilance centers collect these reports, and the World Health Organization coordinates them internationally. That is how a rare harm becomes visible when it would be lost in one clinic. Serious reactions and reactions to a newly introduced medicine deserve a report even when the link is uncertain, because suspicion is the raw material of signal detection. Causality is assessed later and formally, so the nurse should never withhold a report for fear of being wrong.

The nursing role in all of this is to be the last check between a recommendation and a patient. Weight is recorded in kilograms, the dose is calculated in milligrams per kilogram, and a second person verifies the decimal point. Look-alike names, confused units, and a tired night shift are the conditions in which a tenfold error happens. The second check exists because competence is not the same as infallibility. Beyond the arithmetic, the nurse translates the guideline into the household's language, explains why the full course matters, arranges the follow-up, and recognizes the deterioration that means referral now. The nurse also holds the ethical duty to say aloud when a protocol cannot be followed safely.

A reading list is useful only if each title is described by what it contains. Manson's Tropical Diseases is the large reference that covers the field in depth and serves mechanism, differential diagnosis, and the unusual presentation. Hunter's Tropical Medicine and Emerging Infectious Diseases is its closest companion, strong on clinical management and on the infections of returning travelers. The Oxford Handbook of Tropical Medicine is the compact field handbook that answers the common question quickly. Markell and Voge's Medical Parasitology teaches life cycles and laboratory identification, and Diagnostic Medical Parasitology by Garcia is the bench reference for the microscope. Where There Is No Doctor remains the plain-language guide for practice far from a hospital, and Travel Medicine covers the pretravel consultation.

The journals divide into those that publish primary research, those that publish reviews, and those that publish surveillance. The American Journal of Tropical Medicine and Hygiene and the Transactions of the Royal Society of Tropical Medicine and Hygiene are the two general journals of the field. The Lancet Infectious Diseases and Clinical Infectious Diseases publish the trials and reviews that change practice. The open access journal of the Public Library of Science devoted to neglected tropical diseases is rich in program and field research. Emerging Infectious Diseases and the weekly report on morbidity and mortality are the fastest routes to outbreak information. The Bulletin of the World Health Organization serves policy evidence, and the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews appraises treatment questions. Reference documents age at different speeds, so an anatomy lesson may outlive a dose.

To summarize, this appendix is a small vocabulary and a small library rather than a set of rules. The definitions of vector, reservoir, host, and zoonosis explain how an infection travels, while incidence, prevalence, endemic, epidemic, and pandemic explain how much of it there is. Control, elimination, and eradication describe what a program tries to achieve, and surveillance and case definitions describe how a program knows what is happening. Chemoprophylaxis, mass drug administration, directly observed therapy, and task-shifting are the arrangements by which prevention and treatment are delivered at scale. The guideline bodies supply the source, the national program supplies the authority, and the reading list supplies the depth. None of the three replaces the habit of checking the current document before a medicine is given.
