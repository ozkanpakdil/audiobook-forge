# Chapter 33 — Vector Control, Water, Sanitation and Hygiene

Most infections in this book reach a person through the environment rather than the clinic, and the measures in this chapter close the door before the pathogen arrives. A mosquito that never bites, a latrine that is used, and a water jar that stays covered decide how much disease a community carries. The World Health Organization attributes much of the global burden of communicable disease to unsafe water, poor sanitation, poor hygiene, and vector exposure, and that burden falls hardest on children under 5 years of age. Vector control and water, sanitation and hygiene are therefore the foundation of tropical medicine rather than its background, and the advanced practice nurse usually designs, delivers, and repairs them.

Integrated vector management is a concept rather than a product. It means choosing a combination of methods that fits the local vector, house, disease, and budget, and then measuring whether the combination works. No single tool controls every mosquito, and a program that relies on one tool fails when resistance, behavior, or a change in housing defeats it. The World Health Organization frames integrated vector management around evidence, collaboration across sectors, community engagement, and the rational use of insecticides so that their effectiveness is preserved. The practical question for the nurse is which intervention will reach this vector in this village, and who will keep it working next year.

Vectors differ in ways that make tools interchangeable only on paper. The Anopheles mosquitoes that carry malaria bite mostly at night and rest on walls afterward, so nets and indoor spraying reach them. The Aedes mosquitoes that carry dengue, chikungunya, Zika, and yellow fever bite by day and breed in household containers, so nets and wall spraying largely miss them. Culex mosquitoes breed in polluted water, sandflies in moist organic debris, blackflies in fast rivers, and tsetse flies and triatomine bugs in niches of their own. A plan that ignores these differences wastes money and misses the vector it was meant to stop.

Insecticide-treated bed nets work through two barriers at once. The mesh keeps a mosquito away from a sleeping body, and the insecticide bound into the fibers irritates, knocks down, or kills a mosquito that lands on it, because the pyrethroid acts on the mosquito nervous system through contact. When coverage is high, nets also reduce the number of older infectious mosquitoes, so even people who are not under a net gain some protection, and mass campaigns that give nets free of charge reach a coverage that retail sales never achieve. The limitations are practical and predictable. A net is hot, so a household may not hang it in the cool season and then forget it when the rains return, and a net tears, is used for fishing, or is left behind when a family moves. The insecticide washes out with repeated laundering unless the net is a long-lasting product, and even those remain effective for only about 3 years of use and 20 washes. Nets do not protect people who are awake before bedtime, they do not protect anyone outdoors, and they do not touch a day-biting Aedes mosquito, so a net on the shelf protects no one and use matters as much as ownership.

Because ownership and use are not the same thing, monitoring must look at both. A coverage survey asks whether a household has a net and how many people slept under one the previous night, and the ratio of use to ownership tells a program whether its nets are working. During a home visit the nurse can check whether the net is hung, tucked in, or torn beyond a finger width, and whether the family knows it must be used every night. Teaching a household to hang and repair a net, and explaining why a treated net should not be laundered in the river, are small acts with large consequences. Reporting the reasons people give for not using nets is as valuable as reporting the number distributed.

Indoor residual spraying works on a different principle. A trained sprayer applies a residual insecticide to the interior walls, eaves, and ceilings of a dwelling, and the deposit remains active for months. A mosquito that rests on the treated surface after feeding picks up a lethal dose through its legs and body, so the spray kills the vector after it has fed but before it can transmit the next infection. For this to work, the insecticide must suit the local vector and be applied at the correct concentration. The walls must be sprayable, coverage of dwellings must be high, and the timing must place the residual effect across the transmission season. Those conditions fail in ways that are usually logistical or social. Walls of mud, dung, or unfinished plaster absorb the insecticide and may be replastered soon after spraying, removing the deposit. Householders must cover food and water and accept strangers walking through their rooms, which depends on trust built long before the first sprayer arrives. Programs must train and supervise sprayers, supply compressors and nozzles, and keep insecticide within its expiry date, because stopping abruptly after several seasons can let transmission rebound. Sprayers need gloves, coveralls, eye protection, and washing facilities, and programs using organophosphate or carbamate compounds should monitor exposure, since those classes can cause headache, sweating, blurred vision, and weakness. Insecticide containers must never be reused for drinking water, and every serious exposure should be documented and reported, because the pattern of reports protects the next community.

Larval source management attacks the mosquito before it can fly. Environmental management changes the land or the container so water no longer stands where larvae develop, by draining ditches, filling borrow pits, clearing irrigation channels, and covering domestic containers. Larviciding adds a product that kills the aquatic stages, such as the bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis israelensis, the growth regulator diflubenzuron, or the organophosphate temephos, and it may also mean introducing larvivorous fish into wells. These methods work best where breeding sites are few, fixed, and findable, because every site must be identified, treated, and revisited on a schedule that matches the local mosquito. In a landscape of countless transient puddles, larviciding is expensive and rarely keeps pace. Space spraying is the emergency tool, and its name describes its limit. Thermal fogging or ultra-low-volume spraying puts a fine cloud of insecticide into the air, and the droplets kill adult mosquitoes flying at the moment of application. The effect lasts hours to a few days, not weeks, because the cloud disperses and leaves almost no deposit. Programs use it during a dengue outbreak to cut the adult population quickly, and it requires access to houses and yards, favorable wind, working equipment, and timing for the hours when the vector is active. Repeated application is costly and increases insecticide pressure, so space spraying supplements source reduction rather than replacing it.

Insecticide resistance is the slow emergency that decides whether these chemical tools will still exist in 20 years. Resistance appears when a population survives exposure often enough that the survivors reproduce, and it accelerates when the same insecticide classes are used in agriculture and public health at once. A mosquito may resist by changing the target site in its nervous system so the insecticide no longer binds, or by producing enzymes that break the molecule down before it acts. Programs monitor resistance with bioassays and molecular tests, then respond by rotating insecticide classes, by using mosaics or mixtures, and by leaning harder on nets, screening, environmental management, and behavior. For the nurse, resistance appears as an intervention that used to work and no longer does, and reporting that observation is the first step in the response.

The Aedes problem breaks the assumptions of malaria control. Aedes aegypti is a small, dark mosquito with white markings that bites during the day and breeds in the clean water people keep around their homes. Tires, buckets, flower pots, discarded bottles, roof gutters, and storage jars all hold enough water for a brood. A bed net offers nothing to a person sitting in the yard at noon, and indoor spraying does little to a mosquito that rests in a shaded corner of the compound. Because these mosquitoes live close to people and fly short distances, the containers themselves are the target. Source reduction, which means emptying, scrubbing, covering, or discarding every container that can hold water, matters most, and it must be repeated weekly because the aquatic stages develop in about 1 week in warm weather.

Source reduction exposes a paradox that field workers learn early. Households without a reliable piped supply must store water, and every storage container is a potential breeding site, so improving the water supply is itself a dengue intervention. When supply is intermittent, families store more water for longer and the risk rises. Covering storage jars with tight lids, screening the openings, and treating stored water with a larvicide reduce the risk without depriving a family of water. Community clean-up campaigns, in which neighbors and students walk the same route every week, remove the containers individual families overlook, and the nurse who organizes them is practicing vector control in its most durable form.

The sandflies that transmit leishmaniasis require a different approach because their bites are easy to miss. The sandflies of the genus Phlebotomus in the Old World and Lutzomyia in the New World are tiny, silent, hairy insects that bite at night, often indoors, and breed in moist soil, cracks in walls and floors, rubbish, and animal sheds. They do not breed in open standing water, so larviciding ponds is irrelevant to them. Fine-mesh nets work, because the mesh must be small enough to exclude an insect that can pass through a standard mosquito net, and treating those nets with insecticide adds a chemical barrier. Indoor residual spraying of houses and animal shelters reduces the resting population, and clearing organic debris and moving animal shelters away from sleeping quarters lowers the sites around the home. House screening is the quiet intervention that keeps giving, because screens on windows and doors, closed eaves, ceilings, and insecticide-treated curtains reduce the number of mosquitoes that enter a dwelling and work against both night-biting Anopheles and day-biting Aedes. Unlike a spray, screening does not need renewal every season, and unlike a net, it protects everyone in the house at every hour. The obstacles are cost, maintenance, and design, because a torn screen or a gap under the door restores the entrance, and screens can be difficult to fit on traditional housing. The nurse can identify the entry points during a home visit, suggest the cheapest effective changes, and check later that they were made.

Repellents protect an individual rather than a community, and they are most useful for the traveler, the outdoor worker, and anyone who must be outside during biting hours. Diethyltoluamide is effective at concentrations of about 20 to 50 per cent, and higher concentrations do not repel better but do last longer. Picaridin and oil of lemon eucalyptus are common alternatives. A repellent is applied to exposed skin and clothing, reapplied according to the product and the sweating and washing of the user, washed off when protection is no longer needed, and applied after sunscreen rather than before it. It should not be applied to the hands of a small child, to broken skin, or to an infant younger than 2 months. Clothing and bed nets can be treated with permethrin, which kills rather than repels, while coils and vaporizing mats reduce biting in a room but do not replace nets or screening.

For the clinician, personal protection is part of professional practice. Long sleeves and trousers, permethrin-treated clothing, avoidance of outdoor activity during peak biting hours, and chemoprophylaxis where the itinerary and the parasite justify it reduce the chance that a nurse becomes a patient. The World Health Organization, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control publish guidance on these measures, and their recommendations differ because they weigh local epidemiology, cost, and program capacity differently. Guidance is revised as evidence accumulates, so every dose, concentration, and interval in this chapter is a summary of what current guidance says rather than a settled fact. The current national guideline and the product label must always be checked before a substance is prescribed, mixed, or applied.

Water, sanitation and hygiene is the second half of this chapter, and its target is the fecal-oral route. Waterborne and water-washed infections include cholera, typhoid fever, other bacterial diarrheas, hepatitis A, hepatitis E, giardiasis, cryptosporidiosis, and the soil-transmitted helminths, and inadequate sanitation also sustains schistosomiasis and trachoma. The route runs from feces, through fingers, fluids, flies, and fields, to a new mouth, and every link can be broken. The interventions concern not only the water a person drinks but the hands that prepare food, the flies that visit both the latrine and the plate, and the soil that receives the excreta. A program that treats drinking water and leaves the hands unwashed has broken one link and left four intact.

Thinking of a safe water chain helps the nurse find where contamination happens. Water is drawn from a source, carried home, stored, treated or not, and finally poured or dipped out for use, and each step can undo the one before it. A source may be protected and clean, and the water may still be contaminated by a dirty container, by a cup that was dipped back into the jar, or by hands that touched the water while drawing it. Storage in a covered container with a narrow opening and a long-handled ladle preserves quality far better than an open bucket with a shared cup. The nurse who checks the chain from source to cup, rather than only the source, will find the point of failure. No single household method is perfect, so the practical rule is to stack barriers in a way that fits the household. Settling or filtering removes the particles that would consume chlorine, chlorination or boiling inactivates the organisms that remain, and safe storage protects whatever quality was achieved. A family that understands this sequence can choose the cheapest combination that works in its circumstances instead of relying on one method that fails, and the nurse who asks the household to explain the sequence back learns whether the message landed.

Boiling is the most reliable household method, because heat kills bacteria, viruses, protozoan cysts, and helminth eggs alike. Bringing water to a rolling boil for 1 minute is enough at ordinary altitudes, and about 3 minutes is recommended at high altitude, where water boils at a lower temperature. The costs are real, because fuel must be bought or gathered, boiling consumes time and contributes to indoor air pollution, and the water must then be stored in a clean, covered container. Boiled water can be recontaminated within minutes by a dirty cup, so a household that pours it back into the same open jar has gained less than it thinks. Teaching a family to boil, to cool the water covered, and to keep a separate ladle is a short conversation with a durable effect. Chlorination is cheaper and easier to scale than boiling, but it has boundaries. Chlorine reliably kills bacteria and viruses and is less effective against the protozoan Cryptosporidium, so it should not be the only barrier where that organism circulates. The World Health Organization recommends that drinking water retain a free chlorine residual of at least 0.2 to 0.5 milligrams per liter at the point of delivery, and in emergencies a residual of 0.5 milligrams per liter or more is often targeted, with a contact time of about 30 minutes. Turbidity consumes chlorine, so cloudy water should be settled or filtered first, and the dose must be adjusted to the volume and quality of the water treated. Household products include sodium hypochlorite solution and chlorine tablets, and each has its own concentration, so the label must be read rather than assumed. Where the residual can be measured, a simple test kit lets the nurse verify that the dose was adequate, and where the taste of chlorine discourages use, that objection must be addressed honestly.

Filtration removes the larger pathogens physically, and its performance depends on the pore size of the barrier. Ceramic candle filters, biosand filters, and membrane filters remove protozoan cysts, helminth eggs, and much of the bacteria, and simple cloth filtration removes the copepods that carry Guinea worm larvae. Most household filters do not reliably remove viruses, which are small enough to pass through pores that catch a cyst, so a filter is best combined with chlorination or boiling, and it demands maintenance, because the element must be scrubbed, inspected for cracks, and eventually replaced. Solar disinfection uses sunlight instead of fuel, and it works when clear plastic bottles filled with water lie in full sun for about 6 hours, or for a full day when the sky is overcast, so that heat and ultraviolet light inactivate most organisms. It requires clear water and bottles that transmit light, and the treated water must still be stored covered and drawn with a clean utensil, so the method is easily abandoned when the household does not understand why the bottle must lie in the sun.

Sanitation is the link that most programs underestimate, and it is described as a ladder. At the bottom is open defecation, above it an unimproved latrine without a slab or with an open pit, above that a shared facility used by several households, then a basic household latrine that is not shared, and at the top safely managed sanitation in which excreta is contained, emptied, treated, and disposed of without contact with people. A simple pit latrine with a slab and a lid interrupts the route from feces to mouth, and a ventilated improved pit latrine adds a pipe that reduces flies and odor and therefore increases use. Siting matters, because a shallow pit in sandy soil over a high water table can contaminate the groundwater that neighbors drink, and the best latrine on paper is worthless if the household will not use it, so design and location must be decided with the family. Maintenance is what turns a latrine from a construction project into sanitation. A full pit must be emptied, covered, and replaced, and the process requires equipment, trained workers, protective clothing, and a plan for the sludge, because dumping it in a stream reintroduces the pathogen to the community. Shared latrines fail when no one is responsible for cleaning them, when the door does not lock, when there is no light at night, or when the distance discourages children and older people. Handwashing water and soap or ash must be present at the latrine, or the handwashing step is simply skipped, and menstrual hygiene needs, anal cleansing materials, and the needs of people with disabilities belong in the design rather than being added later. Where a committee is responsible for repairs and a small fund exists for spare parts, facilities last, and where responsibility is vague, they do not.

Community-led total sanitation addresses the demand side by putting the decision in the community's hands. Facilitators help a village map where people defecate, estimate how much feces reaches the environment, and trace the routes by which it reaches food and water, and the community then decides for itself to end open defecation. The method relies on collective action and social pressure rather than on a subsidy for each household latrine, and villages that reach the goal are recognized as open defecation free. Recognition is only the beginning, because facilities fall into disrepair and families slip back into old habits, so the status must be verified and revisited rather than declared once. The nurse who supports the process and returns a year later to see whether latrines are still used and still clean is doing the part of the work that determines whether the achievement survives.

The health gains from sanitation are easiest to see in the soil-transmitted helminths and in schistosomiasis. Ascaris lumbricoides, Trichuris trichiura, and the hookworms spread through eggs or larvae that reach soil contaminated with human feces, and a latrine that keeps feces out of the yard removes the source. Where transmission is intense, treatment reduces the worm burden while sanitation reduces the force of reinfection, so the two are complementary rather than alternative. The World Health Organization recommends periodic preventive treatment, for example albendazole 400 milligrams as a single dose, or mebendazole 500 milligrams as a single dose, or mebendazole 100 milligrams twice a day for 3 days, with the choice and schedule set by the national program and the age of the child. The exact product, dose, and interval must be confirmed against the current national guideline before it is prescribed, because recommendations are revised and they differ between countries.

Even a well-built latrine changes nothing if the last few steps of behavior do not follow. The feces of infants and young children are often considered harmless, yet they carry the same pathogens as adult feces and must go into the latrine, and anal cleansing materials must be put into the pit rather than left on the ground. Hands must be washed after defecation and after cleaning a child, not only before eating. Where a latrine is dark, distant, or frightening to a child, the household returns to the bush, so lighting, a safe path, and a child-sized opening are practical health measures. The nurse who observes how a household actually behaves, rather than how the survey says it behaves, learns where the intervention is failing. Handwashing with soap is the single most cost-effective hygiene intervention in this chapter, and it depends on critical moments rather than on frequency alone. Hands should be washed after defecation, after cleaning a child who has defecated, after handling animal waste, before preparing or handling food, before eating, and before feeding a child or administering medicine. Soap and running water are ideal, and where soap is scarce, ash can serve as an abrasive that lifts contamination, though it must be kept dry and used with water. A tippy tap, a jerrycan with a small hole and a string, makes handwashing possible with a very small volume of water and is cheap to build. Alcohol-based hand rub is useful in a clinic but does not remove all pathogens, including Cryptosporidium, and the nurse who places water and soap beside the latrine and the kitchen has done more than the nurse who gives a lecture about germs.

The evidence behind handwashing reaches well beyond diarrheal disease. Consistent handwashing at the critical moments reduces diarrheal illness, respiratory infection, and the transmission of trachoma, and it improves outcomes in maternal and newborn care, where clean hands at delivery protect both mother and infant. In a clinic, hand hygiene before and after every patient contact protects the next patient as much as the current one, and it is the cheapest infection control measure available. The constraint in most communities is not knowledge but access, because a household may know that washing matters and still have no water at the latrine and no soap that is not reserved for laundry. Measuring whether handwashing stations exist, whether they hold water, and whether soap or ash is present tells a program far more than asking people whether they wash their hands.

Footwear is an unglamorous intervention that prevents one of the most common infections in tropical practice. Hookworm larvae, including those of Necator americanus and Ancylostoma duodenale, develop in moist soil and penetrate the skin of a bare foot that steps on contaminated ground. The same route is used by Strongyloides stercoralis, and the result can be chronic anemia in a person who never notices an acute illness, because the worms feed on blood in the small intestine over years. Wearing shoes or sandals interrupts this step, and it is especially important for children who walk to school, for farmers, and for workers in plantations, mines, and construction sites. Shoes alone are not enough where the environment is heavily contaminated, so footwear belongs in the same package as latrines, handwashing, and periodic deworming treatment. The nurse who can explain the route from soil to skin can persuade a family to buy the cheapest pair of shoes it will ever own.

Guinea worm disease ties water treatment, environmental change, and community behavior together in a single story, and it is close to becoming the second human disease ever eradicated. The parasite Dracunculus medinensis develops in people and releases larvae into water when an infected person enters a pond to relieve the burning pain of the emerging worm. The larvae are swallowed by tiny water fleas of the genus Cyclops, and a person who drinks unfiltered water containing those fleas becomes the next host. The intervention is astonishingly simple, because drinking water can be filtered through a fine cloth or treated so that the fleas are killed. Because the same ponds are shared, the cloth filter works only if everyone uses it, which is why the campaign joined the filter to case containment, surveillance, and community commitment. The nurse who teaches the filter, distributes it, and checks that it is used is practicing the blend of technology and trust that has brought the disease to the edge of elimination.

When a person does develop Guinea worm disease, the nursing care is slow, gentle, and decisive for the community. The worm emerges through a blister, usually on a leg or foot, and it is extracted by winding a few centimeters each day around a small stick, over days to weeks, without pulling hard enough to break it, because a ruptured worm causes intense inflammation and secondary infection. The wound is kept clean and covered, tetanus vaccination status is checked, and antibiotics are used only when a bacterial infection is present. The essential public health action is to keep the patient out of every source of drinking water until the worm has finished emerging, because a single immersion can release thousands of larvae and restart transmission in a village. That rule is hard for a patient in pain, so the program provides water at the bedside, a shaded place to rest, and food while the nurse explains why the restriction protects the neighbors. Cases are reported and investigated quickly, because the final stage of eradication depends on finding every case and every contamination event.

The nurse in this chapter is a health educator, and education that changes behavior is a craft rather than a lecture. Adults learn best when the subject answers a problem they already have, so the conversation about nets begins with the child who had fever last month rather than with the life cycle of the parasite. Demonstration and teach-back work better than explanation, because the nurse can show how to hang a net or cover a jar, ask the household to do it while watching, and then ask the household to explain it back in its own words. Messages should be delivered in the language the family uses, at the literacy level it has, and with pictures where reading is limited. One message at a time, repeated on later visits, is remembered, while a list of ten instructions at the end of a clinic visit is not.

The nurse is also a community organizer, and this is where the work becomes political in the ordinary sense of the word. Durable programs are built with the people who will maintain them, including village committees that set a small fee for repairs, community health workers who visit households and report failures, schools that run container clean-ups, women's groups that manage water points, and religious leaders whose support legitimizes a new practice. Participatory planning, in which the community identifies its own priorities and its own solutions, produces an ownership that a vertical campaign cannot buy. The nurse brings technical knowledge into that process without taking it over, connects the community to the district supply system, and makes sure the voices of women, older people, and people with disabilities are heard when a site or a schedule is chosen. A program the community helped design is a program the community will repair.

Logistics decide whether a good intervention exists on the day it is needed, and they are as much a nursing responsibility as any dressing change. Nets must be forecast, procured, stored dry, and delivered before the transmission season, insecticide must arrive in the correct formulation with a valid expiry date, and chlorine loses strength over time and in heat and light, so it must be stored cool and dark and used before it degrades. Latrine slabs, pipes, taps, filter elements, sprayer nozzles, and spare parts must be available locally, because a broken hand pump that waits 6 months for a part is a household that returns to the river. Distribution over the last kilometer is the hardest part, particularly where roads are seasonal and settlements are scattered, and the nurse who helps plan a route or a community pickup point prevents the stock-out that undoes a year of preparation. Recording what arrived, what was used, and what remains is what allows the next order to be correct.

Monitoring closes the loop between intention and effect, and it uses a small number of measures well rather than a large number badly. For vector control, programs track net ownership and use, the proportion of houses sprayed, and entomological indicators such as the number of adult mosquitoes resting in houses or the proportion of containers holding larvae. For water, they test the free chlorine residual and the presence of indicator bacteria at the point of use rather than only at the source, because contamination often happens in the home. For sanitation, they measure the proportion of households with a latrine and the proportion of those latrines that are actually used and clean, and for hygiene they record whether a handwashing station with water and soap or ash is present. Data quality, timeliness, and completeness matter more than the sophistication of the analysis, because a figure collected late or entered twice cannot guide a decision. Feeding the results back to the community in a form people can understand is what turns monitoring into accountability.

Adverse effects and safety reporting belong to the same monitoring system, and the nurse is usually the first to see them. Insecticides can cause skin irritation, eye injury, headache, and nausea, and in severe cases the cholinergic syndrome of organophosphate poisoning, so anyone with symptoms after mixing or applying a product needs to stop, wash, and be assessed. Chlorine solutions irritate the skin, eyes, and airways, and they must never be mixed with ammonia or with acid, because the resulting gas can be lethal. Alcohol-based hand rub has been contaminated with methanol in some supply chains, causing outbreaks of poisoning, so the source and the label must be verified rather than trusted. Burns from cooking fires and from carrying boiling water, and injuries from broken filters, are common household harms that a program should anticipate. Every serious event should be documented and reported through the national system, because a safety system for medicines and pesticides is built from reports that clinicians take the time to make.

The most important lesson in this chapter is that programs rarely fail on biology and usually fail on acceptance and maintenance. The mosquito is well understood, the chlorine kills the organism, and the latrine design works, so what fails is the last meter between the technology and the household. A net becomes a fishing net because the family was never asked whether it wanted one, a latrine becomes a store room because it was built in the wrong place, a hand pump stands idle because no one collected money for the spare part, and a spray campaign is refused because the sprayers arrived without warning the year before. Trust, convenience, cost, gender, and habit decide more outcomes than efficacy data ever will. This is why the nurse, present in the household across time and able to hear the objection behind a refusal, is the person best placed to keep a program alive. The hardware is purchased once, while the software, which is the conversation, the follow-up, and the repair, must be renewed every season.

Because so much of this chapter involves substances that are measured and applied, one rule deserves to be stated plainly before the chapter closes. The concentrations, doses, and intervals described here, from the chlorine residual that protects stored drinking water to the albendazole or mebendazole dose used in deworming and the insecticide load applied to a wall, are summaries of current guidance rather than fixed truths. The World Health Organization, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and national programs revise their recommendations as resistance patterns, product formulations, and evidence change, and they do not always agree with one another. The current national guideline, the product label, and the local protocol must therefore be checked every time a substance is mixed, applied, or prescribed, and any discrepancy between a remembered figure and the current document is a reason to stop and verify.

To summarize, vector control and water, sanitation and hygiene are the preventive foundation of tropical medicine, and they work by interrupting the environmental steps that carry a pathogen from one person to the next. Integrated vector management combines nets, indoor residual spraying, larval source management, screening, and personal protection in a mix that fits the local vector, and it treats insecticides as a resource whose effectiveness must be preserved through resistance monitoring and rotation. The Aedes mosquito, which bites by day and breeds in household containers, requires source reduction rather than nets, while the sandfly, the blackfly, the tsetse fly, and the triatomine bug each demand their own approach. Safe water is achieved through boiling, chlorination, filtration, solar disinfection, and safe storage stacked together, while sanitation depends on latrines that are used, emptied, and maintained, and hygiene depends on handwashing at the critical moments and on footwear that keeps hookworm larvae out of bare skin. Guinea worm shows how a simple cloth filter, joined to case containment and community trust, can bring a disease to the edge of eradication. Programs succeed or fail on acceptance and maintenance rather than on biology, and the nurse who educates, organizes, monitors, and repairs is the person who turns these tools into protection.
