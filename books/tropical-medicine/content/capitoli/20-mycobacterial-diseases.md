# Chapter 20 — Mycobacterial Diseases: Tuberculosis, Drug Resistance, and Leprosy

The mycobacteria are rod-shaped bacteria with a cell wall so rich in long-chain fatty acids that ordinary dyes cannot penetrate it, and only heat or prolonged staining forces them in. That property gives them the name acid-fast bacilli and explains much of their behavior. The waxy wall makes them slow, so one division may take 18 to 24 hours and a culture can take weeks to turn positive, and it also lets them survive drying, dust, and the acid interior of the stomach. This chapter deals with the two mycobacteria that matter most in tropical practice, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, the cause of tuberculosis, and Mycobacterium leprae, the cause of leprosy. They share a genus and a habit of striking the poor and the crowded, but they differ sharply in how they spread, how they are treated, and what the nurse must do about them.

Tuberculosis spreads almost entirely by air, and that single fact organizes everything you do to prevent it. When a person with pulmonary disease coughs, sings, or speaks forcefully, the airway secretions break into fine droplets, the water evaporates within moments, and tiny dried particles called droplet nuclei hang suspended in the air for hours. Those particles are small enough to bypass the nose and reach the alveoli, where macrophages take up the bacilli that are supposed to destroy them. Transmission therefore depends on sharing air, not on touching surfaces or sharing food or using the same toilet, and the risk rises with closeness, with time spent together, and with poor air renewal. A patient with a positive sputum smear, a cavity on the chest radiograph, and a heavy cough is far more contagious than one with minimal disease, while young children are usually not contagious because their lesions are small and their coughs generate few infectious particles.

Infection is not the same as disease, and the difference is the most important concept in tuberculosis care. When droplet nuclei reach the alveoli, most people mount an effective cell-mediated response that walls the bacilli inside granulomas, and the infection becomes latent, meaning the organism is alive but contained, the person has no symptoms, and nothing is transmitted. Only about 5 to 10 per cent of infected people ever develop active disease, most of them within the first 2 years or much later if immunity weakens, and the tuberculin skin test and the interferon gamma release assay detect this immune memory rather than illness. Anything that weakens cell-mediated immunity raises the risk of reactivation, and advanced HIV is the strongest factor, which is why tuberculosis is a leading cause of death among people living with HIV. Malnutrition, diabetes, silicosis from mining dust, kidney failure, alcohol use disorder, and treatment with corticosteroids or the biologic drugs that block tumor necrosis factor all add risk. Crowding and poor ventilation in prisons, mines, refugee camps, and slums increase the chance of being infected in the first place, acting at the level of transmission rather than reactivation, and because these factors often stack in one person, the nurse should weigh them together rather than as a checklist of single causes.

The natural history of untreated tuberculosis runs in two directions, and the patient in front of you may be at either end. Primary infection is usually silent or passes as a mild cold, but in a minority it causes pneumonia, pleural effusion, or, in infants and the immunosuppressed, widespread bloodborne spread. After that, the immune system holds the bacilli in check and the person enters the latent state with a lifelong risk of reactivation, which typically produces the chronic upper lobe illness and can appear decades after the original exposure. Without treatment, roughly half of those with active pulmonary disease die within 5 years, and the rest oscillate between illness and partial recovery while continuing to transmit. Effective treatment changes all of this within about 2 weeks for infectiousness and within 6 months for cure in most drug-susceptible cases.

Pulmonary tuberculosis is the commonest form, and its presentation is a chronic illness unfolding over weeks. The patient reports a cough lasting more than 2 weeks, at first dry and later productive of sputum that may be streaked with blood. Night sweats that soak the bedding, low-grade fever, loss of appetite, and weight loss are the constitutional features that, taken together, should raise suspicion at once in an endemic setting. Chest pain, breathlessness, and hoarseness appear as the disease destroys lung tissue, and the chest radiograph typically shows patchy consolidation or cavitation in the upper lobes with hilar or mediastinal node enlargement, though any pattern is possible. Because early disease produces few findings, the history of a cough that will not go away is often more informative than the examination.

Extrapulmonary tuberculosis accounts for roughly one fifth of cases in immunocompetent adults and a much larger share in people with advanced HIV, and it can affect almost any organ. Tuberculous lymphadenitis is the commonest form in many settings, usually painless enlargement of the cervical nodes that may later fluctuate and drain. Pleural effusion causes fever, chest pain, and breathlessness, with fluid that is an exudate low in glucose and rich in lymphocytes. Tuberculous meningitis presents with headache, fever, neck stiffness, confusion, and cranial nerve palsies, and it is one of the most lethal forms because delay costs lives. Miliary tuberculosis is bloodborne dissemination that seeds the lungs with tiny nodules and can cause a prolonged fever of unknown origin with enlargement of the liver and spleen, while skeletal disease, called Pott disease when it affects the spine, destroys vertebral bodies and can compress the spinal cord. The nurse should remember that a patient with fever and no localizing signs may have tuberculosis in a place that is not the lung.

Diagnosis begins with sputum, and how the specimen is collected determines whether the result can be trusted. Two or three samples should be obtained on separate days, at least one of them early in the morning, and the patient must produce material from deep in the chest rather than saliva. Where sputum cannot be produced, induction with inhaled hypertonic saline or collection of gastric aspirate in a child may be needed. Microscopy after Ziehl-Neelsen staining reveals acid-fast bacilli, and in a symptomatic patient in an endemic area that finding is strong evidence of disease. Its limitations matter just as much, because microscopy detects only about half of all cases, needs roughly 10,000 bacilli in each milliliter of sputum to turn positive, and cannot distinguish Mycobacterium tuberculosis from other mycobacteria or reveal drug resistance. A negative smear therefore never excludes tuberculosis.

Culture remains the reference standard because it detects far fewer organisms and because the grown organism can be tested against the drugs that will be used. A liquid culture system may turn positive within 10 to 14 days, while solid media take 4 to 8 weeks, and the laboratory then identifies the species and performs susceptibility testing. Molecular testing has changed the pace of diagnosis by detecting the organism's genetic material directly in the specimen within a few hours, and modern cartridge-based tests also detect the common mutations that confer resistance to rifampicin. Because rifampicin resistance is a reliable marker for multidrug-resistant tuberculosis in most settings, that result is both a diagnosis and a warning, and it should trigger a full resistance workup and referral to a program that manages resistant disease. Molecular tests are highly specific but do not replace culture, which still provides the full susceptibility profile and the isolate. The nurse's practical contribution is to get the specimen to the laboratory promptly, to state on the request that tuberculosis is suspected, and to discard nothing until the result returns.

The tuberculin skin test and the interferon gamma release assay both measure immune memory rather than the organism itself, and neither can separate latent infection from active disease. The skin test injects a purified protein derivative into the forearm and is read 48 to 72 hours later by the diameter of the hardened area, with the threshold for a positive result adjusted for the person's risk category. Its weaknesses are considerable, because it cross-reacts with BCG vaccination, it is falsely negative when cellular immunity is impaired by illness, malnutrition, measles, or advanced HIV, and it requires a second visit to be read. The interferon gamma release assay measures the release of interferon gamma by the patient's lymphocytes when they meet mycobacterial antigens that are not present in BCG, so it is not confused by prior vaccination, and it needs only a single blood draw. It is less available and more expensive in low-resource settings, however, and it shares the problem of being unreliable in advanced immunosuppression. In a seriously ill patient with a negative skin test, the test result should never override the clinical picture, because these tests guide preventive treatment rather than rule out active disease.

Diagnosing tuberculosis in children and in people with advanced HIV is a genuine challenge, and the nurse is often the person who assembles the evidence. Children have few bacilli in their secretions, they swallow sputum rather than expectorating it, and they cannot always cooperate with a good specimen, so pediatric diagnosis is usually clinical. It rests on a household contact with an infectious adult, symptoms lasting more than 2 weeks, a radiograph showing hilar adenopathy or miliary change, and evidence of infection on an immune test. In advanced HIV the inflammatory response is blunted, so the radiograph may look normal or show lower zone infiltrates, pleural effusion, or miliary shadowing instead of the typical cavity, while the smear and the immune tests are frequently negative. The disease is more likely to be disseminated, so blood cultures and sampling of any enlarged node or abnormal fluid may be needed, and the patient may present with fever and weight loss alone. Every person newly diagnosed with HIV should be assessed for tuberculosis, and every person with tuberculosis should be offered HIV testing, because the two epidemics feed each other. Low suspicion is the main reason these diagnoses are missed, so a patient with unexplained fever, night sweats, and weight loss deserves a sputum molecular test and a chest radiograph even when the examination is unremarkable.

The standard regimen for drug-susceptible pulmonary tuberculosis is a 6 month course in two phases, and its design follows from the biology of the organism. The intensive phase lasts 2 months and uses four drugs, isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol, because a large bacterial population must be killed quickly before resistance can emerge. The continuation phase lasts 4 months and uses two drugs, isoniazid and rifampicin, to eliminate the slow-growing persisters that survive the first phase. Doses are weight-based and given by mouth once daily, and current guidance from the World Health Organization and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention gives isoniazid at 5 milligrams per kilogram, up to 300 milligrams, rifampicin at 10 milligrams per kilogram, up to 600 milligrams, pyrazinamide at 25 milligrams per kilogram, and ethambutol at 15 to 20 milligrams per kilogram. Fixed-dose combination tablets that contain several drugs together reduce the pill burden and the risk of a dosing error, and weight bands printed on the packaging are the safest way to prescribe for adults.

Duration is adjusted for the site and severity of disease, and this is where local guidance matters most. Standard pulmonary disease is treated for 6 months when the response is good, but when the radiograph shows cavitation and the sputum culture is still positive after 2 months, the continuation phase is extended to 7 months, making 9 months in total. Tuberculous meningitis, bone and joint disease, and disseminated disease are usually treated for 9 to 12 months, and meningitis and pericarditis also receive corticosteroids to reduce inflammation and its complications. Pregnancy is not a contraindication to treatment, streptomycin is the injectable that must be avoided because of fetal harm, and breastfeeding should continue while the infant receives preventive treatment and follow-up. Before you prescribe any regimen, verify it against current guidance, because regimens are revised as evidence accumulates, they differ between countries and programs, and the World Health Organization, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control do not always agree. The current guideline, not memory and not an old chart, must be checked before the first dose is given.

Directly observed therapy is the practice of watching the patient swallow every dose, and its rationale is simpler than it first appears. Tuberculosis is treated over months, patients feel well long before the organism is gone, and the temptation to stop is powerful when the pills cause nausea, the clinic is far away, and the family needs income. Irregular intake is the perfect way to select resistant mutants, because the surviving bacilli are exposed to one or two drugs at a time and the resistant ones multiply. Watching every dose is one way to guarantee adherence, but the principle that matters is that a reliable, trained observer, who may be a nurse, a community health worker, a pharmacist, or a family member, supports the patient through the whole course, and video-observed therapy now serves patients who have a phone. Support must be paired with counseling, reminders, and food assistance where malnutrition is part of the problem, because adherence is a social behavior and not merely a personal virtue.

Monitoring during treatment tells you whether the plan is working and detects the two great threats, treatment failure and drug toxicity. Sputum should be examined at the end of the intensive phase, usually at 2 months, and again at the end of treatment, with culture and susceptibility testing if it is still positive. Weight should be recorded at every visit, because a falling weight may signal treatment failure, poor absorption, or a second diagnosis, while a rising weight is one of the most reassuring signs. Symptoms should be reviewed directly rather than assumed to have resolved, with specific questions about cough, fever, night sweats, and appetite. Every visit is also an opportunity to ask about the adverse effects patients do not volunteer, including numbness in the feet, changes in vision, ringing in the ears, and nausea. In a patient with HIV, the recovering immune response can cause a paradoxical worsening called immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome, which is managed by treating the underlying infection and, in severe cases, with corticosteroids. Because rifampicin powerfully induces the liver enzymes that clear many other drugs, antiretroviral regimens must be adjusted with expert advice, and this interaction is a common cause of avoidable treatment failure.

The adverse effects of the antituberculosis drugs are predictable, and the nurse is usually the person who sees them first. Isoniazid causes peripheral neuropathy, prevented in patients at risk by pyridoxine at 10 to 25 milligrams by mouth daily, and it can also cause hepatitis, so it must be stopped if liver enzymes rise sharply or jaundice appears. Rifampicin harms the liver, turns urine, sweat, and tears an orange color that patients must be warned about, and reduces the effectiveness of many drugs, including hormonal contraceptives and some antiretroviral agents. Pyrazinamide is the drug most likely to injure the liver and it also raises uric acid, which can produce joint pain and, rarely, gout. Ethambutol causes optic neuritis with loss of red-green color vision and reduced acuity, so patients should be warned to report any change in vision at once and should have visual testing wherever it is available. The injectable agents, streptomycin, kanamycin, amikacin, and capreomycin, damage the eighth cranial nerve and the kidney, so hearing and kidney function must be tested before and during treatment. Bedaquiline prolongs the interval between the Q wave and the T wave on the electrocardiogram and can trigger a dangerous ventricular rhythm, while linezolid suppresses the bone marrow and can cause peripheral and optic neuropathy. Clofazimine discolors the skin and the whites of the eyes, and cycloserine and ethionamide cause psychiatric and neurological effects including confusion, depression, and seizures.

Baseline testing before treatment begins makes every later comparison possible. Liver enzymes should be measured before starting isoniazid, rifampicin, and pyrazinamide, and repeated when symptoms or risk factors suggest injury, since routine monthly testing is not always available and does not replace clinical vigilance. Kidney function and hearing should be documented before any injectable agent is used, and vision should be tested with acuity and color plates before ethambutol and at intervals afterward. A pregnancy test and a review of all other medicines, including traditional remedies and supplements, belongs in the same visit, and alcohol use should be addressed honestly because it multiplies the risk of hepatitis and undermines adherence. When a patient reports jaundice, dark urine, right upper abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, or a rash, the drugs should be held and the patient assessed the same day.

Multidrug-resistant tuberculosis is defined by resistance to at least isoniazid and rifampicin, the two most powerful drugs in the standard regimen, and it arises mostly from irregular treatment, from transmission of an already resistant strain, or from both. Extensively drug-resistant tuberculosis adds resistance to a fluoroquinolone and to at least one of the other second-line agents, and it leaves very few options, with mortality that remains high even where treatment is available. Resistance is not a laboratory curiosity, because a patient with resistant disease who continues to receive the standard regimen will fail treatment, remain infectious, and can pass the resistant strain to others. Any patient who remains sputum positive at the end of the intensive phase, or who deteriorates after an initial response, therefore requires susceptibility testing and expert review. Molecular tests that identify rifampicin resistance are the fastest way to raise the alarm, and the nurse's role is to recognize the failing patient, obtain the specimen, and make sure the referral actually happens.

Treating resistant tuberculosis requires a regimen built for the individual patient and supervised by an expert, and the details change as new evidence arrives. For many years the standard approach was a longer regimen of 18 to 20 months using at least five effective drugs, including a later-generation fluoroquinolone such as levofloxacin or moxifloxacin, together with agents chosen from bedaquiline, linezolid, clofazimine, cycloserine or terizidone, ethionamide, and others, guided by susceptibility testing. A shorter regimen of 9 to 12 months was developed for selected patients with rifampicin-resistant disease who had not previously received second-line drugs, and it cured many people at lower cost. More recently, the World Health Organization has recommended a 6 month, all-oral regimen combining bedaquiline, pretomanid, linezolid, and moxifloxacin for many patients with multidrug-resistant or extensively drug-resistant disease, which shortens treatment dramatically and removes the injectable drugs. Because guidance in this area is revised frequently and differs between national programs, no regimen should be prescribed from memory, and the current national guideline must be checked for every patient. Directly observed therapy, nutritional support, and management of adverse effects matter as much here as the choice of drugs, and cure rates fall when social support is absent.

Preventing the airborne spread of tuberculosis in a clinic without a negative-pressure room is possible with attention to air, distance, time, and organization. The first measure is to identify the infectious patient at the door by asking everyone with a cough how long it has lasted, and to move anyone with a cough of more than 2 weeks to the front of the queue so that waiting time, the main determinant of exposure, is reduced. The second is to separate them from other patients while they wait, ideally outdoors or in a well-ventilated area, and to give every coughing patient a surgical mask and teach them to cover the mouth and nose with a tissue or an elbow. The third is ventilation, the most powerful engineering control available in a simple building, so open windows on opposite sides of a room to create cross-ventilation, use ceiling or standing fans to keep the air moving, and place an exhaust fan to pull air away from the waiting area. Sunlight destroys bacilli, so an outdoor waiting area or a room with open, sunlit windows is genuinely protective. Where the structure allows, upper-room ultraviolet germicidal irradiation can inactivate airborne bacilli above the heads of the people in the room without rebuilding anything.

Staff who care for infectious patients need a fit-tested particulate respirator rather than a surgical mask, and the fit test must be repeated whenever the model changes or the wearer's face changes shape. Sputum collection is the moment of greatest risk, so it should be performed outdoors or in a dedicated, well-ventilated space that other patients do not use, never in an open waiting room or a shared toilet. Hand hygiene, waste disposal, and cleaning matter for general safety but do not prevent airborne transmission, and the nurse should understand this so that effort goes to the measures that work. Health workers should be screened for tuberculosis at the start of employment and periodically afterward, and those with HIV should be offered preventive treatment. When a colleague develops a cough that lasts, the same suspicion you apply to patients must be applied to them.

BCG is a live vaccine derived from a weakened strain of Mycobacterium bovis, and what it does and does not do must be stated plainly. Given to a newborn, it protects against the most severe forms of childhood tuberculosis, disseminated disease and tuberculous meningitis, with good but not complete effectiveness. It does not reliably prevent pulmonary tuberculosis in adults, it does not prevent infection, and therefore it does not stop transmission, so a person vaccinated as a child may still develop tuberculosis and a scar on the arm is not evidence of protection. Vaccination is a reason for a positive tuberculin skin test but not for a positive interferon gamma release assay, which is why the blood test is preferred in people who have been vaccinated. In high-burden countries the vaccine is given at birth, including to infants born to mothers with HIV who are themselves well, while it is avoided in infants with symptomatic HIV because the live organism can disseminate, and in low-burden countries it is reserved for selected high-risk people.

Leprosy, also called Hansen disease, is caused by Mycobacterium leprae, an organism that has never been grown in a laboratory flask and grows only in the mouse footpad and the nine-banded armadillo. Its doubling time is about 12 to 14 days, the slowest of any known human pathogen, which explains why the incubation period is measured in years, commonly 2 to 5 years and sometimes much longer. The bacillus prefers cool areas of the body, which is why the skin, the superficial nerves, the upper airway, the eyes, and the testes are most affected. It spreads mainly through droplets from the nose and mouth during prolonged, close contact with an untreated person who has the multibacillary form, and it is not acquired by a handshake, a shared meal, or a brief encounter. Most people exposed to it never develop disease, because the cellular immune response of a healthy adult usually contains it, and this fact is central to counseling patients and their families.

The clinical spectrum of leprosy runs from tuberculoid disease at one end to lepromatous disease at the other, and where a patient falls on that spectrum determines the lesions, the bacillary load, and the risks. In tuberculoid, or paucibacillary, disease the immune response is strong, so there are few lesions, they are asymmetrical, sharply defined, and dry, sensation within them is lost, and a nearby nerve trunk is thickened and tender. The skin test is positive, slit-skin smears contain few or no bacilli, and the patient is not significantly infectious. In lepromatous, or multibacillary, disease the immune response fails, so lesions are numerous, symmetrical, and diffuse, the face thickens into the fold called leonine facies, the eyebrows and eyelashes fall out, the nasal bridge collapses, and the testes shrink. Smears are teeming with bacilli and the patient is infectious before treatment, while the borderline forms between the two poles are immunologically unstable and most likely to shift and to develop reactions. Recognizing where a patient sits on this spectrum guides the treatment duration and the intensity of surveillance for nerve damage.

The cardinal sign of leprosy is a skin lesion with loss of sensation, and the finding of a thickened nerve is its companion. The earliest lesion is often a single hypopigmented or reddish patch with a dry, hairless surface and reduced or absent sensation to light touch, pinprick, and temperature. The nerves most often thickened are the ulnar nerve above the elbow, the median nerve at the wrist, the common peroneal nerve at the knee, the posterior tibial nerve behind the ankle, the greater auricular nerve in the neck, and the facial nerve. Sensory testing with a wisp of cotton and a pin, and voluntary muscle testing of the small muscles of the hand and foot, should be performed at every visit and the results written down so that change can be detected. Slit-skin smears from several sites give the bacterial index and are used to classify disease and monitor response, while skin biopsy shows the granulomas and the bacilli, and molecular testing by PCR can identify the organism in difficult cases. Any patient with a numb patch or a thickened nerve in an endemic area should be evaluated for leprosy, because the diagnosis is still made on the skin and the nerve rather than on a blood test.

Reactions are the immunological complications of leprosy, and they are the main cause of permanent nerve damage, so they must be recognized as emergencies. A type 1 reaction, also called a reversal reaction, occurs when cellular immunity suddenly strengthens, most often in borderline disease and particularly after treatment has started, and existing lesions become red, swollen, and painful while the nerves become tender and enlarged with new weakness or sensory loss that can progress within days. A type 2 reaction, also called erythema nodosum leprosum, is an immune complex phenomenon seen in multibacillary disease, and it produces crops of painful red nodules, fever, joint pains, enlarged lymph nodes, inflammation of the eye, and inflammation of the testis. Both reactions threaten the eye, the hand, and the foot, and both can be triggered by treatment, pregnancy, infection, or stress. Type 1 reactions are treated with prednisolone, commonly starting at 40 to 60 milligrams by mouth daily and reduced slowly over 12 weeks or more. Type 2 reactions are treated with thalidomide, clofazimine, or corticosteroids, and thalidomide must never be given to a woman who could become pregnant because it causes severe birth defects, so pregnancy must be excluded first and reliable contraception used.

Antibacterial treatment of leprosy uses a fixed combination of drugs, because monotherapy rapidly selects resistance and the organism is naturally slow to die. Current World Health Organization guidance gives all patients rifampicin at 600 milligrams by mouth once a month, supervised, together with dapsone at 100 milligrams by mouth daily. Multibacillary disease adds clofazimine at 50 milligrams by mouth daily and 300 milligrams once a month, and it is treated for 12 months, while paucibacillary disease uses only rifampicin and dapsone and is treated for 6 months. Children receive weight-based doses, with rifampicin at 10 to 15 milligrams per kilogram, dapsone at 1 to 2 milligrams per kilogram, and clofazimine at 1 milligram per kilogram daily and 6 milligrams per kilogram monthly. A single-lesion, paucibacillary case can be treated with one supervised dose of rifampicin at 600 milligrams, ofloxacin at 400 milligrams, and minocycline at 100 milligrams. Some programs continue treatment longer for multibacillary disease, and rifampicin-resistant leprosy is managed with second-line drugs such as ofloxacin, minocycline, and clarithromycin under expert guidance.

These drugs have adverse effects you can anticipate and explain. Dapsone commonly causes mild anemia and can cause severe hemolytic anemia in people with glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency, so this deficiency should be considered before treatment wherever testing is available. Dapsone can also cause methemoglobinemia, which presents as cyanosis with breathlessness, and a rare hypersensitivity illness with fever, rash, jaundice, and enlarged lymph nodes that requires immediate withdrawal. Rifampicin can injure the liver, colors secretions orange, and reduces the effectiveness of hormonal contraceptives, so alternative contraception must be advised, while clofazimine causes a harmless reddish-brown discoloration of the skin and conjunctivae that may be deeply distressing if it is not explained in advance. Corticosteroids used for reactions bring their own risks of high blood glucose, high blood pressure, weight gain, and infection, so patients on a long taper need monitoring and support.

Finding cases and following their contacts is how leprosy transmission is interrupted, and the nurse is usually at the center of it. Every patient should be asked about household members and others in close contact, and those contacts should be examined and taught the early signs of disease. The World Health Organization recommends a single dose of rifampicin at 600 milligrams for eligible household and social contacts of a person with leprosy as post-exposure prophylaxis, since this reduces the risk that a contact will develop the disease. Contacts are also advised how to recognize a numb or discolored patch and to return for evaluation, because prophylaxis does not remove the need for surveillance. Contact tracing is equally important in tuberculosis, where a systematic household investigation finds both active cases and infected people who need preventive treatment, which uses isoniazid daily for 6 to 36 months or weekly rifapentine with isoniazid for 12 weeks, offered only after active disease has been excluded.

Disability in leprosy is preventable, and much of the prevention is nursing care delivered early and carefully. Nerve damage begins insidiously and passes through a phase in which it is still reversible, so sensory testing and voluntary muscle testing at every visit, recorded on a simple chart, are what allow you to catch it in time. Once sensation is lost, the hand, the foot, and the eye are at risk from ordinary injury, burns, and pressure, so the patient must learn daily self-care, including inspection of the feet, soaking, protective footwear with a firm sole and no rough seams, eye care with lubricants, and exercises and splinting that preserve movement in clawed fingers and the dropped foot. Stigma may do more lasting harm than the bacillus, because leprosy has been feared for centuries and patients hide their lesions, delay treatment, lose work, and are sometimes rejected by their own families, and tuberculosis carries its own stigma, often bound up with HIV. Naming the disease accurately, explaining that treated leprosy is not transmitted by touch, and correcting the myths a patient has absorbed do more good than any poster. Confidentiality, respectful language, and protection of a patient's information are professional duties with concrete consequences, because a diagnosis made public can cost a person their livelihood, and mental health support should be offered rather than assumed unnecessary. The nurse who treats a patient as a person rather than a case is practicing the most effective public health intervention available for these diseases.

To summarize, tuberculosis is an airborne infection in which most people who are infected never become ill, and the task of the clinician is to tell the contained infection from the active disease, because only the active disease is contagious and only it needs the full four-drug regimen. The standard treatment is 6 months of isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol followed by isoniazid and rifampicin, adjusted for the site of disease and supervised throughout, with pyridoxine to prevent peripheral neuropathy and careful surveillance for liver injury, visual change, and hearing loss. Resistance, whether to isoniazid and rifampicin alone or to the second-line drugs as well, changes the regimen entirely and must be detected by molecular testing and susceptibility testing rather than assumed. Airborne precautions in a simple clinic rest on finding the coughing patient at the door, separating them, moving the air, and using sunlight and ultraviolet light, rather than on a room most clinics will never have. BCG protects infants against the most severe forms of tuberculosis but does not prevent infection or transmission, so vaccination never replaces the other measures. Leprosy is a slowly progressive disease of the skin and nerves that is diagnosed by a numb patch and a thickened nerve, is treated with a fixed combination of rifampicin, dapsone, and clofazimine, and threatens the patient mainly through reactions and nerve damage that nursing surveillance can catch. Because all of these regimens are revised as evidence accumulates, differ between countries and programs, and change with the age, weight, pregnancy status, and other medicines of the patient, the current guideline must be checked before every prescription, and the nurse who verifies the dose, watches the patient take it, examines the skin and the nerves, and treats the person with dignity prevents more disability than any single drug.
