# Chapter 6 — The Febrile Returning Traveler

Fever is the most frequent reason a returning traveler seeks medical care, and it is the reason that most often conceals real danger. A traveler comes back from a trip with a temperature, a story, and a set of assumptions, and the clinician has one urgent task before every other: to decide whether this is malaria, and specifically whether it is falciparum malaria, because that is the diagnosis that can kill a healthy adult within a day or 2. The parasite Plasmodium falciparum, the species responsible for the most severe form of malaria, is the organism to exclude first, not because it is always the most likely diagnosis but because it is the most lethal and the most time-sensitive. Nearly every other febrile illness can wait an hour while that question is answered. This chapter walks through the first encounter, the triage decisions, the differential organized by incubation period, the physical findings that shift probability, the actions that belong to the first hour, and the nursing work that holds the process together. It follows the framework set out by the World Health Organization and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and it treats every regimen it mentions as a moving target rather than a settled fact.

The word returning does important work in that title, because the returning traveler is not the same patient as the person who became ill in a village with no laboratory and no pharmacy. The returning traveler usually arrives in a setting where blood films, blood cultures, serology, imaging, and intensive care are available, and that changes the calculus in both directions. It means you can afford to wait for a diagnosis in a stable patient, and it means you have no excuse for failing to obtain one. It also means the differential is broader, because a patient who has been in 3 countries in 3 weeks may have carried 3 different exposures home in the same body. The clinician who treats a returning traveler must therefore hold 2 ideas at once: the possibility of a tropical infection that is rare locally, and the possibility of an ordinary infection that is common everywhere. Influenza, urinary infection, pneumonia, and infectious mononucleosis occur in travelers too, and the exotic diagnosis is not automatically the right one.

Triage begins before the patient is fully undressed, and it begins with a decision about how sick this person is right now. You are looking for the patient who cannot wait, the one whose mental status has changed, whose breathing is fast, whose hands are cold, whose skin is mottled, or whose urine output has fallen. The first minute should establish whether the airway is open, whether the patient is breathing comfortably, and whether there is a palpable pulse and adequate circulation. If any of those are in doubt, the patient moves to resuscitation and the history waits. If the patient is stable and talking, you have bought yourself a few minutes to ask where they have been, when they returned, and whether they took any medication to prevent malaria.

Mental status is the single most important bedside observation in a febrile returning traveler because it converts a routine fever into a neurological emergency. A patient who is confused, drowsy, agitated, or difficult to rouse may have cerebral malaria, bacterial meningitis, encephalitis, typhoid encephalopathy, a metabolic derangement such as hypoglycemia, or sepsis with poor cerebral perfusion. Ask the patient's companion whether this is different from the patient's normal self, because a quiet person may look quiet rather than confused. Document a formal level of consciousness rather than the word alert, so the next clinician can compare. A change in mental status in the context of fever from the tropics is a red flag until proven otherwise.

The respiratory rate is the vital sign that is most often recorded inaccurately and most often ignored, and in this population it is a powerful signal. A rate above the expected range for the patient's age suggests pneumonia, pulmonary edema, severe anemia, acidosis, or the beginning of acute respiratory distress. Count it for a full 60 seconds with the patient at rest and without announcing what you are doing, because breathing changes when it is watched. A rising respiratory rate in a patient who looked stable an hour ago is often the first sign of deterioration, and it frequently appears before the blood pressure falls. Treat a rising rate as an urgent finding, not as anxiety.

Capillary refill, skin temperature, and the character of the pulse tell you about perfusion in a way that a single blood pressure reading cannot. Press the fingertip or the sternum for 5 seconds and count until color returns, remembering that the finding is unreliable in a cold room and in the elderly. Cold peripheries with a normal blood pressure suggest compensated shock, and compensated shock can decompensate quickly in a patient with severe malaria or dengue. Mottling of the knees, a thready radial pulse, and a narrow pulse pressure all belong to the same story. When you find them, you have found a patient who needs intravenous access and fluid now.

The skin and the urine output complete the picture, and both are easy to overlook in a busy triage area. Look at the whole skin surface, including the palms, soles, trunk, and any area the patient cannot easily see, and ask whether the rash is new. Look for petechiae, purpura, bruising, bleeding gums, epistaxis, blood in the stool, and any puncture or bite mark. Urine output is the most honest measure of end-organ perfusion available at the bedside, so ask when the patient last passed urine, how much, and what color, and catheterize or measure if the patient is unwell. A patient who has not passed urine for 12 hours is a patient in trouble, whatever the blood pressure says.

The red flags that demand immediate action can be spoken as a short litany, and every nurse who works with returning travelers should be able to recite it. Impaired consciousness, a seizure, a respiratory rate above the expected range, oxygen saturation below the expected range, systolic blood pressure below the expected range, capillary refill greater than 3 seconds, cold and mottled skin, urine output below the expected volume, any bleeding, jaundice with abdominal pain, severe anemia, and a blood glucose below the expected range all require action within minutes. Add to that list a patient who looks worse than the numbers suggest, because experienced clinicians recognize severe illness before the monitor does. Any one of these findings should trigger a call to the responsible clinician and a move to a monitored area. None of them should wait for the laboratory result that will explain them.

When a red flag is present, the response is a sequence rather than a single act. Establish intravenous access, draw blood for the tests that will matter, measure the glucose immediately, and begin fluid resuscitation if the circulation is compromised. Give oxygen if the saturation is low, position the patient to protect the airway if consciousness is impaired, and treat a seizure with the locally approved anticonvulsant. Start empirical treatment for the most dangerous plausible diagnosis rather than waiting for confirmation, because in severe malaria and in bacterial sepsis the delay itself harms the patient. The nurse who begins this sequence is not exceeding the scope of practice; the nurse who begins it is the reason the patient survives.

The differential diagnosis of fever in a returning traveler is most usefully organized by incubation period, because the interval between exposure and illness eliminates whole categories of disease. Ask when the patient was away and when the fever began, and count the days carefully, because a fever that started 4 days after return has a different differential from one that started 6 months after return. The exposure history from the previous chapter supplies the other half of the equation, since the place, the season, the insects, the water, the food, and the sexual contacts all modify probability. Incubation period and exposure together narrow the field far more than either does alone. Write the interval down, because memory drifts and the arithmetic matters.

Illnesses with an incubation of days, roughly up to 10 days, include dengue, chikungunya, influenza, and a group of acute viral and bacterial infections that arrive fast. Dengue is the classic example, with fever, severe headache, pain behind the eyes, muscle and joint pain, nausea, and sometimes a rash, and it is important to remember that the fever of dengue typically breaks just as the dangerous phase begins. Chikungunya produces fever with disabling joint pain, often in the small joints of the hands and feet, and it rarely causes the plasma leakage of dengue. Influenza is common in travelers and easy to forget, and it can be severe in the elderly, in pregnancy, and in those with chronic disease. A fever that begins within 2 days of return, before any tropical incubation could have elapsed, is usually not a tropical disease at all.

The same short window contains enteric fever, meaning typhoid and paratyphoid fever, which usually appears 7 to 14 days after exposure but can appear as early as 3 days. Rickettsial disease, including scrub typhus and the spotted fever group, commonly appears 6 to 20 days after a bite, so it straddles the boundary between the day group and the week group. Measles and meningococcal disease belong in this window as well, and both are public health events that require notification and infection control measures. Zika virus usually produces a mild illness with rash, conjunctivitis, and joint pain, and its importance lies in congenital infection rather than in the acute fever itself. When the interval is short, think in terms of these fast-moving infections and do not anchor on the most exotic name in the travel history.

A fever with an incubation of days also raises the possibility of a viral hemorrhagic fever, and although these diseases are rare, the consequences of missing one are severe. Lassa fever, Ebola disease, Marburg disease, and Crimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever can all present initially as an undifferentiated fever, and the early symptoms overlap with malaria and with typhoid. The clues that should raise suspicion include a history of travel to an area where such transmission occurs, contact with a person who had a similar illness, work in a health facility or with animals, and the appearance of bleeding, a rash, or unexplained deterioration. If suspicion is real, do not wait for the diagnosis to act on infection control, because the safety of the staff and of the other patients depends on early precautions. Notify the responsible public health authority at the moment of suspicion, not after confirmation.

Illnesses with an incubation of weeks include malaria, which remains the diagnosis to exclude first for reasons of both frequency and lethality. Falciparum malaria usually appears 7 to 30 days after infection, but it can appear within a week or be delayed for months, especially in a patient who took incomplete or ineffective prevention. Plasmodium vivax and Plasmodium ovale can present many months after exposure because they leave dormant liver forms that reactivate, and this late presentation is a common reason for a missed diagnosis. Plasmodium malariae can cause fever years after exposure, and Plasmodium knowlesi, found in Southeast Asia, can progress rapidly. Any fever in a patient with a compatible travel history is malaria until the films say otherwise.

The week group also contains leptospirosis, hepatitis, and acute HIV infection, each of which has a characteristic pattern that helps. Leptospirosis appears 5 to 14 days after contact with water or soil contaminated by rodent urine, and it produces fever, severe headache, muscle pain in the calves, and red eyes from conjunctival suffusion. Hepatitis A and hepatitis E appear 2 to 6 weeks after exposure and produce fever, nausea, malaise, dark urine, and jaundice, with the fever often fading as the jaundice deepens. Acute HIV infection appears 2 to 4 weeks after exposure with fever, sore throat, rash, lymphadenopathy, and a flu-like illness that is dismissed as a flu. None of these three is rare in a returning traveler, and each has a specific test that should be sent when the story fits.

Brucellosis and the rickettsial infections complete the week group, and both reward a careful exposure history. Brucellosis follows the consumption of unpasteurized milk or cheese or contact with livestock, appears 1 to 4 weeks later, and produces fever that is often drenching and nocturnal, with sweats, weakness, and joint pain. The rickettsial diseases follow a bite from a tick, a mite, or a flea, and they produce fever with headache, muscle pain, and often a rash or an eschar. Acute schistosomiasis, also called Katayama syndrome, appears 2 to 8 weeks after freshwater contact with fever, cough, abdominal pain, and eosinophilia. These are diagnoses that depend on the clinician asking the right question, because the patient will not volunteer the answer.

Illnesses with an incubation of months form the last group, and they behave differently because they are usually chronic rather than acute. Tuberculosis appears weeks to years after exposure, and it should be considered in any traveler with fever, night sweats, weight loss, and cough, especially if they have lived or worked in a high-burden setting or have HIV. Visceral leishmaniasis produces months of fever, splenomegaly, pancytopenia, and wasting, and it is often mistaken for a hematological malignancy. Lymphatic filariasis and other filarial infections can produce fever with lymphangitis and swelling, though fever is less prominent than the swelling itself. Amebic liver abscess appears weeks to months after exposure with fever and right upper quadrant pain, and it is frequently misdiagnosed as a bacterial abscess or a malignancy.

The chronic group also includes schistosomiasis, which may produce intermittent fever with abdominal or urinary symptoms months after freshwater exposure, and subacute infective endocarditis, which is not tropical at all but is easily missed in a patient with fever and a heart murmur. Chronic brucellosis, chronic Q fever, and tuberculosis of the abdomen or the spine all belong to the same long list. A fever that has lasted more than 2 weeks without an explanation deserves a systematic second evaluation, with repeated cultures, imaging, and a deliberate return to the exposure history. The most common error in this group is to stop after the first negative test. The second most common error is to give a course of antibiotics that blunts the fever without producing a diagnosis.

Physical findings shift probability in ways that are worth learning by heart, because they can be found in a 5 minute examination and can change the entire plan. An eschar, which is a black crusted lesion at the site of a bite, is one of the most useful findings in tropical medicine. It is characteristic of scrub typhus, of African tick bite fever, and of several other rickettsial infections, and it is often hidden in the axilla, the groin, the belt line, or under the breast. Search for it deliberately, because a patient with fever, headache, and an eschar should receive doxycycline immediately rather than after the serology returns. The presence of an eschar converts a broad differential into a narrow one.

Conjunctival suffusion, which is redness of the eyes without discharge, is a signature finding of leptospirosis and is also seen in some viral illnesses. It differs from the watery discharge of a common cold and from the crusting of bacterial conjunctivitis, and it is often accompanied by severe muscle pain in the calves and by a headache that does not respond to ordinary analgesia. A patient with fever, red eyes, calf pain, and a history of freshwater contact or rodent exposure should be treated for leptospirosis while the test is pending. The finding is subtle and it is missed when the eyes are not examined in daylight. Ask the patient to look up and down while you retract the lids, and record what you see.

Splenomegaly is found by palpation and percussion, and it is a clue to a specific set of diseases. An enlarged spleen in a febrile traveler suggests malaria, enteric fever, visceral leishmaniasis, brucellosis, infective endocarditis, leptospirosis, or a hematological condition. Palpate from the right lower quadrant toward the left costal margin, and use percussion to confirm what your hand cannot feel, because a soft spleen in a muscular patient is easily missed. Remember that a tender enlarged spleen can rupture, so avoid vigorous deep palpation in a patient with suspected enteric fever or infectious mononucleosis. Document the finding in terms of how many centimeters below the costal margin, so that others can track it.

Relative bradycardia, sometimes called the Faget sign, means a pulse rate that is slower than expected for the degree of fever, and it is a classic clue in enteric fever, brucellosis, leptospirosis, yellow fever, and some rickettsial infections. It is a pattern rather than a diagnosis, and it should prompt you to consider those illnesses rather than to exclude them. The finding is easiest to appreciate when you record the temperature and the pulse together and compare them with the expected relationship. Do not let a normal pulse reassure you in a patient with a high fever and a compatible history. The bedside observation of a slow pulse with a high fever is worth more than many laboratory results.

Jaundice in a febrile returning traveler has a short but important differential that includes hepatitis A and hepatitis E, leptospirosis, severe malaria, yellow fever, and biliary sepsis. Jaundice with anemia and hemoglobinuria suggests severe falciparum malaria, and jaundice with renal failure and hemorrhage suggests leptospirosis in its severe form. Jaundice with a tender enlarged liver and a high fever suggests ascending cholangitis, which is a surgical emergency rather than a tropical one. Jaundice with a slow pulse and bleeding in a patient returning from an area where yellow fever occurs is a diagnosis that must be notified. In every case, jaundice in a febrile patient is a marker of severity and a reason to move the patient to a monitored setting.

Lymphadenopathy is a finding that should be described precisely, because its location and character carry meaning. Tender enlarged nodes in the neck and the axillae occur in acute HIV infection, in dengue, in chikungunya, and in many viral illnesses. Firm matted nodes, especially in the neck with a discharging sinus, occur in tuberculosis, and the nodes may need aspiration rather than excision. Generalized lymphadenopathy with fever and weight loss is a reason to consider HIV, tuberculosis, and lymphoma. A single inflamed node with a bite history in a patient returning from an area where plague or tularemia occurs is an urgent finding that requires prompt treatment. Always ask how long the nodes have been present and whether they are painful.

The rash deserves its own systematic approach, because the timing, the distribution, and the character together narrow the differential more than any single feature. A blanching maculopapular rash on the trunk suggests typhoid, dengue, or a viral exanthem, while petechiae and purpura suggest a bleeding disorder, meningococcal disease, or severe dengue. A rash that starts on the face and spreads downward suggests measles, and a rash with a slapped cheek appearance suggests parvovirus. An eschar with a surrounding rash suggests a rickettsial infection, and a rash on the palms and soles suggests secondary syphilis or a rickettsial disease. Ask whether the rash appeared before or after the fever, because that sequence matters. Photograph or describe the rash in the record so that the next examiner can compare.

What must happen in the first hour is a list of actions rather than a single decision, and it should be rehearsed until it is automatic. Measure the vital signs including the temperature, the respiratory rate, the blood pressure, the oxygen saturation, and the level of consciousness. Measure the blood glucose, because hypoglycemia is common in severe malaria, in sepsis, and in children, and because it is immediately reversible. Obtain intravenous access and draw blood cultures from 2 separate sites before antibiotics are given. Send a thick and thin blood film for malaria, a complete blood count, a renal profile, liver enzymes, a coagulation screen, and lactate, and if the patient could be pregnant, send a pregnancy test. Give fluid and antibiotics as indicated by the clinical picture rather than waiting for results.

The malaria film is the single most important early test, and it should be ordered and repeated in a way that accounts for its limitations. A single negative film does not exclude malaria, because parasite density fluctuates and may be below the detection threshold early in the illness. Repeat the film every 12 to 24 hours for at least 3 sets, and in a patient who has taken prophylaxis or partial treatment, expect the density to be low and tell the laboratory to look carefully. Ask for a thick film for detection and a thin film for species identification and parasite density, because the density guides management. If the first films are negative and the patient is severely ill with a compatible history, treat for severe malaria anyway while continuing to investigate.

Rapid diagnostic tests for malaria detect parasite antigens and provide a result within minutes, but the nurse must understand what they cannot do. The most common test detects a protein produced by Plasmodium falciparum, and it can give a false negative when the parasite does not produce that protein, a situation seen in some regions. The test also remains positive for weeks after successful treatment, so a positive result in a patient who was treated recently does not prove current infection. Use the rapid test to speed up the decision in an emergency, but always send microscopy as well. A negative rapid test in a seriously ill patient with a compatible exposure must not close the question.

Empirical treatment before the diagnosis is certain is sometimes the right choice, and the decision rests on the severity of the illness and the danger of the disease being treated. In a patient with fever and an eschar, or with a compatible exposure and a severe headache, give doxycycline immediately because rickettsial disease responds quickly and delays are costly. In a patient with impaired consciousness and a travel history that includes an area where malaria occurs, treat as severe malaria without waiting for the film. In a patient with suspected bacterial meningitis, begin antibiotics before the lumbar puncture if the patient is unstable or if imaging must come first. The principle is to treat the disease that will kill the patient fastest, then to refine the diagnosis as the results arrive.

Severe malaria is treated with intravenous artesunate, and the current guidance is a dose of 2.4 milligrams per kilogram given intravenously at the start of treatment and again 12 hours and 24 hours later, then once every 24 hours until the patient can take oral treatment. Children receive the same weight-based dose, and the dose should be calculated on the actual weight rather than estimated. If artesunate is unavailable, alternative parenteral treatments include artemether given intramuscularly and quinine given intravenously, but these are less preferred. Rectal artesunate can be given as a pre-referral treatment when a patient with severe malaria cannot reach definitive care quickly. In every case, the nurse should verify the weight, the dose, and the route against the current guideline before the drug is given.

Rickettsial disease is treated with doxycycline, and the usual adult dose is 100 milligrams given by mouth or intravenously every 12 hours. Children are treated with 2.2 milligrams per kilogram by mouth or intravenously every 12 hours, and current guidance favors treating children rather than withholding the drug, because the risk of the infection exceeds the risk of the short course. Pregnant patients are a more difficult group, and the decision should follow the local guideline and the severity of the illness. Treatment is usually continued for 5 to 7 days and until the patient has been afebrile for at least 48 hours. The clinical response is often rapid, and a patient who improves within 48 hours of the first dose probably has the diagnosis right.

Enteric fever and suspected bacterial meningitis are treated with a third-generation cephalosporin, most often ceftriaxone, and the dose depends on the syndrome rather than on the fever alone. For enteric fever, the usual adult dose is 1 to 2 grams given intravenously every 24 hours, and for meningitis it is 2 grams given intravenously every 12 hours, with dose adjustment for children by weight. Antibiotic resistance has removed fluoroquinolones from use in many parts of the world, so the choice must follow the local resistance pattern and the travel destination. Blood cultures should be drawn before the first dose whenever this can be done without delaying treatment. Do not treat a fever with an antibiotic simply because the patient is anxious, because an unnecessary course obscures the diagnosis and drives resistance.

Every regimen in this chapter is a snapshot of guidance that changes, and the listener must hear that clearly. Doses and regimens are revised as resistance emerges, as new drugs are licensed, and as trials report new results, and guidance differs between countries and between agencies. Before prescribing or administering any drug named here, check the current guideline from the World Health Organization, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, or the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control, and check the local formulary as well. Confirm the dose against the patient's weight, renal function, pregnancy status, and allergies rather than relying on memory or on this text. When 2 clinicians disagree about a dose, the correct response is to open the guideline, not to split the difference.

Adverse effects deserve anticipation rather than reaction, because the drugs used here are powerful and the patients are often unstable. Intravenous artesunate can cause delayed hemolysis, with falling hemoglobin and rising bilirubin appearing 1 to 3 weeks after treatment, so arrange a blood count at 1 week and again at 2 and 4 weeks. Quinine can cause hypoglycemia and can prolong the interval between the Q and T waves on the electrocardiogram, so monitor the glucose closely and watch the cardiac rhythm. Doxycycline causes nausea, heartburn, and photosensitivity, and it should be taken with a full glass of water while sitting upright to prevent irritation of the esophagus. Ceftriaxone can cause rash, diarrhea, and biliary sludge, and any new rash during an infusion should be treated as a possible hypersensitivity reaction until proven otherwise.

Monitoring in the hours after admission is where most of the preventable harm is avoided, and it is nursing work from beginning to end. Record the vital signs at a frequency that matches the severity of the illness, which may mean every 15 minutes in a patient with severe malaria and every 4 hours in a stable patient with a mild febrile illness. Measure the blood glucose every 4 to 6 hours in any patient with severe malaria, impaired consciousness, or poor oral intake, and more often if the patient is receiving quinine. Record the fluid balance hourly in a patient receiving intravenous fluids, and record the urine output as a volume rather than as a word. Track the temperature trend, because a fever that is not responding to treatment is information, and a fever that has resolved followed by a return is also information.

Neurological monitoring belongs in every serious febrile illness, because the brain is the organ that tells you the treatment is failing. Check the level of consciousness at every set of observations, and check the pupils, the limb strength, and the ability to speak clearly. Watch for a seizure, for a new focal deficit, for abnormal posturing, and for a headache that is worsening despite analgesia. In a patient with cerebral malaria, the return of consciousness usually follows the fall in parasite density, and its absence after 48 hours should prompt a search for another cause. Document each neurological observation so that a subtle decline becomes visible across shifts. The nurse who notices that the patient answered questions yesterday and does not today has made a diagnosis before the laboratory has.

The nurse's role between rounds is not a passive one, and it is often the difference between a good outcome and a catastrophe. Deterioration rarely announces itself as a single dramatic event; it announces itself as a rising respiratory rate, a fall in urine output, a change in the character of the pulse, a new confusion, or a cooler periphery. Because the nurse is the person who is actually present, the nurse is the person most likely to see these changes first. Escalate on the basis of a trend rather than waiting for a threshold, because thresholds are crossed only at the end of a process. A brief, structured handover that names the trend and the concern is more useful than a complete recitation of numbers.

Pregnancy changes both the differential and the management, and it requires a lower threshold for admission and for investigation. Malaria in pregnancy is more severe, more likely to cause hypoglycemia and pulmonary edema, and more likely to cause fetal loss, premature delivery, and low birth weight. Current guidance supports the use of intravenous artesunate in all trimesters for severe malaria, and oral artemisinin-based combination therapy for uncomplicated disease, with the specific choice made in consultation with the current guideline. Doxycycline and tetracyclines are generally avoided in pregnancy, so an alternative must be chosen when a rickettsial infection is suspected, and the decision should involve a clinician experienced in the field. Document the pregnancy test result before giving any drug, because the result changes which drug is safe.

Children deteriorate faster than adults and compensate longer before they decompensate, so the thresholds for concern are different. A child with fever from the tropics should be weighed accurately, because both fluid and drug doses depend on the weight and estimated weights are often wrong. Hypoglycemia is common in children with severe malaria, seizures are common, and severe anemia develops quickly. Dehydration and shock present differently in children, and the signs include a prolonged capillary refill, cool peripheries, a weak fast pulse, and reduced skin turgor. Monitor the glucose, the level of consciousness, and the urine output closely, and involve the caregiver in watching the child between observations. A child who is playing again after fluids is telling you the resuscitation worked.

Immunocompromised patients and those without a spleen need a broader differential and a lower threshold for empirical treatment. A patient with advanced HIV may present with tuberculosis, with a fungal infection, with a bacterial sepsis, or with one of the opportunistic infections, and the fever may be blunted by the underlying immune state. A patient who has had a splenectomy or who has sickle cell disease is at high risk from encapsulated organisms and from malaria, and a fever in such a patient is an emergency by itself. Patients taking corticosteroids, chemotherapy, or biologic drugs may be febrile from an infection that would otherwise be mild, including strongyloidiasis with hyperinfection. Ask about immunosuppressive drugs and about the spleen, because neither question appears on a standard travel form.

Migrants and travelers who are visiting friends and relatives deserve specific attention, because they often fall through the gaps in both prevention and care. They may travel for months, they may stay in rural areas rather than in hotels, they may not seek pre-travel advice, and they may delay care after return because they assume they have immunity or because they fear the cost. They may have partial immunity to malaria, which means they can carry parasites with few symptoms while remaining capable of transmitting the infection to mosquitoes in the destination country. They may also be reluctant to disclose the full travel history, so the history must be taken with respect, without judgment, and with an explanation of why it matters. A fever in this group deserves the same urgency as a fever in a tourist, and sometimes more.

Logistics decide whether good clinical thinking becomes good care, and the nurse manages most of the logistics. Find out when the laboratory can process a blood film, whether the thick film is read on site, and how long the result takes, because the answer shapes the timing of treatment. Arrange transport for specimens that must travel, protect them from heat and delay, and label them with the time of collection. Keep the cold chain intact for tests that require it, and do not leave a specimen on a bench while waiting for a form to be completed. Confirm that the laboratory has received the specimen and that a result is expected, because a specimen that is never processed is worse than no specimen at all.

Infection control and notification are part of the first hour when a dangerous transmissible infection is possible. If a viral hemorrhagic fever is suspected, place the patient in a single room, use the personal protective equipment that the local protocol requires, restrict visitors and staff, and use dedicated equipment where it exists. Notify the responsible public health authority immediately, because the notification pathway is what brings the reference laboratory and the specialist advice. Handle sharps and waste according to the protocol, and clean spills with the approved disinfectant. Document the precautions that were used and the time they began, because the record protects both the patient and the staff. Never wait for a laboratory confirmation before beginning these measures.

Communication with the patient and the family is clinical work, not an afterthought, and it determines whether the patient returns for follow-up. Explain in plain language why the malaria films are being repeated, why a negative result does not end the investigation, and why admission is sometimes advised even when the patient feels able to go home. Give written and spoken instructions about the warning signs that should bring the patient back immediately, including confusion, bleeding, difficulty breathing, inability to drink, and a fall in urine output. Provide the follow-up appointment and the contact number before discharge, and confirm that the patient has understood. Where language is a barrier, use an interpreter rather than a family member for the clinical conversation.

Discharge advice must also address the unfinished business of the returning traveler, because the diagnosis may not be complete at the moment of discharge. A patient discharged with an undiagnosed fever needs a clear plan for repeat testing, a named person to contact, and an appointment within a defined interval. A patient treated for malaria needs a blood count after treatment to detect delayed hemolysis, and a patient treated for enteric fever needs to know that relapse can occur. A patient with a chronic exposure risk needs the next travel consultation arranged before the next trip rather than after it. Record the plan in the discharge summary in terms that the next clinician can act on, and make sure the patient has a copy.

Referral thresholds should be agreed before the shift becomes busy, because decisions made in advance are clearer than decisions made in a crisis. A patient who needs a level of care that your facility cannot provide should be transferred early, and the transfer should be arranged while the patient is still stable enough to travel. Discuss the case with the receiving clinician by telephone, and send a written summary with the patient that includes the exposure history, the vital sign trends, the treatment given, and the tests pending. Never send a patient with unexplained confusion, shock, or a bleeding tendency without a clinician accompanying the transfer or an agreed monitoring plan. The most dangerous moment in the care of a severely ill traveler is the gap between 2 facilities.

Quality improvement belongs in this chapter because the same errors recur in every setting. The most common failures are a single negative malaria film that is treated as final, a rickettsial infection that is missed because the skin was never fully examined, an enteric fever that is diagnosed too late because no blood culture was drawn, and a deteriorating patient whose trend was not escalated. Review the cases in which the diagnosis was delayed, and ask whether the delay came from knowledge, from process, or from staffing. Feed the answer back into the triage protocol, the admission checklist, and the handover format. A unit that reviews its own delays is a unit that will recognize the next dangerous traveler faster.

To summarize, fever in a returning traveler is a time-sensitive problem in which malaria, and especially falciparum malaria, must be excluded first because it is the diagnosis most likely to kill quickly. Triage begins with mental status, respiratory rate, capillary refill, skin, bleeding, and urine output, and the red flags among those findings demand immediate action rather than observation. Organize the differential by incubation period, with dengue, chikungunya, typhoid, and rickettsial disease in the first days, malaria, leptospirosis, hepatitis, acute HIV infection, and brucellosis in the following weeks, and tuberculosis, leishmaniasis, filariasis, amebic liver abscess, and schistosomiasis in the following months. Let the physical findings, including the eschar, conjunctival suffusion, splenomegaly, relative bradycardia, jaundice, and lymphadenopathy, refine that probability. In the first hour, measure the vital signs and the glucose, obtain cultures before antibiotics, send and repeat the malaria films, and treat the most dangerous diagnosis when the evidence supports it. Verify every dose against the current guideline from the World Health Organization, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, or the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control before you give it, because regimens change and guidance differs between countries. Anticipate the adverse effects of the drugs you administer, monitor the trends rather than the thresholds, and remember that the nurse who watches the patient between rounds is frequently the first person to know that the illness is turning. Carry the logistics, the infection control, the notification, the communication, and the follow-up with the same care you give the clinical assessment, because in this disease the details are the treatment.
