# Chapter 50 — Protocol-Based Practice

Imagine the hour that decides a great many remote cases. It is 2 in the morning, the generator has been silent for 3 hours, a small child has been convulsing for 4 minutes, and the nearest physician is 200 kilometers away and asleep. In that room, the difference between a good outcome and a preventable death is rarely a machine. It is a remembered sequence of steps, performed in the right order, at the right moment. A protocol is that sequence written down, argued over, tested on thousands of patients, and carried into the room ahead of you. It exists because memory under fear is unreliable, and because the clinician who learned the lesson at the bedside of another child is not standing beside you.

Understand first what a protocol actually is, because the word has been made to sound dull. A protocol is a decision path. It states who enters it, what must be assessed, how the findings are sorted into categories, what action belongs to each category, when to reassess, and when to hand the patient onward. It is written as conditions and consequences: if the breathing rate is above a threshold and the chest is indrawing, then this classification, then this treatment. That structure is what allows a nurse with 2 years of training to reach decisions that specialists would recognize as sound. The protocol does not think for you; it arranges your thinking so that nothing essential is forgotten under pressure.

There is a humbler way to describe the same thing. A protocol is what years of other people's mistakes look like when someone finally writes them down. Every threshold in it marks a patient who deteriorated just below that line. Every instruction to give a drug before referral marks a death on a road. Every warning about a rare adverse effect marks a person who was harmed so that the next person would not be. This is why protocol-based practice and humility belong in the same sentence. You are not following a bureaucrat's opinion; you are following a compressed record of harm, and that record deserves the respect you would give to a senior colleague's hardest lesson.

The protocols available to you come in a few great families, and knowing which family you are holding tells you what it can and cannot do. The first family is syndromic primary care, built for nurses and community health workers who must act without a laboratory. The second is the specialist field manual, written for humanitarian and surgical contexts where the disease is severe and the resource is absent. The third is the treatment guideline, which tells you what drug, at what dose, for how long, and what to watch for. Most of the pages in your clinic belong to one of these 3 families, and each answers a different question.

The best known of the syndromic family is the integrated management of childhood illness, developed by the World Health Organization and now taught in more than 100 countries. It takes a child under 5 years of age and walks the clinician through cough and difficult breathing, diarrhea, fever, ear problems, feeding, growth, anemia, and immunization status, then sorts the child into a small number of urgency categories. A red category means treat now and refer, a yellow category means treat here and review soon, and a green category means counsel the family and send the child home with a plan. Its genius is that it converts a dozen possible diagnoses into a few actions that a nurse can complete in 15 minutes with a timer, a tape, and a scale. Its regimens are revised as evidence accumulates and they differ between countries, so the current national adaptation must be checked before you prescribe from memory.

Its adult counterpart, the integrated management of adult illness, extends the same logic to adolescents and adults. It covers the chronic and the life-threatening together: tuberculosis, human immunodeficiency virus infection, diabetes, hypertension, mental disorders, and palliative needs, arranged so that a single visit can address more than one problem. The design acknowledges a fact that hospital training often obscures, which is that a person rarely arrives with one disease. A man with human immunodeficiency virus infection and a cough is not 2 separate consultations; he is one person who must be assessed, classified, and treated in one coherent plan. Reading this family well means learning to hold several pathways open at once without dropping any of them.

Within the same syndromic family sits the mental health gap action program of the World Health Organization, which translates what psychiatrists know into protocols a nonspecialist can deliver. It covers depression, psychosis, epilepsy, dementia, substance use, self-harm, and the psychological problems of children, and its central method is task sharing. The same principle governs the others: the specialist writes the path, and the nurse, and behind the nurse the community health worker, walks it. When you manage a convulsion, a severe dehydration, or a first episode of psychosis with a protocol in your hand, you are not pretending to be a specialist. You are delivering the part of specialist care that can be safely standardized, and knowing exactly where the standardized part ends is the skill.

The second family, the specialist field manual, is exemplified by the clinical guidelines of Doctors Without Borders. These grew out of surgical and obstetric practice in war and displacement, and they go where the primary care protocols stop: the damage control of a gunshot wound, the anesthesia and pain relief available without a specialist anesthetist, the management of obstructed labor and postpartum hemorrhage, the care of a burns patient, and the rationing decisions of a mass casualty event. Their obstetric and surgical guidance is unusually explicit about the moment when a procedure exceeds what the facility can safely do, which makes it a model of honest limits. Because these manuals assume austere conditions, their recommendations often travel further than their authors intended, and they too are revised, so verify the current edition before you use them.

The third family is the treatment guidance of the World Health Organization, including its hospital care manuals, its disease specific treatment guidelines, and the essential medicines concept that tells a health system which drugs must always exist. Where the syndromic family says who to treat and roughly how, this family says exactly what to give, at what dose, by which route, for how many days, and what to monitor. It also names the drugs that must not be used together and the populations in which an ordinary dose becomes dangerous. Treat these documents as the reference shelf behind your protocols rather than as bedside reading for a crisis, because nobody can search a long guideline while a patient is bleeding.

Above all of these sits the national guideline of the country where you work, and its authority is greater than any international document. Ministries of health adapt global guidance to local disease patterns, drug availability, laboratory capacity, and law, and a national protocol that contradicts a global one has almost always done so for a reason you cannot see from your clinic. When the 2 conflict, follow the national document and record the conflict in your register so that the difference is visible. Where no national adaptation exists, the international protocol is your default, and your documentation should say which document you followed and when you consulted it. The principle is simple: name your source, and make the source recoverable by whoever reviews the case later.

Now learn to read a protocol as a map rather than a recipe. A recipe tells you that 3 ingredients in a fixed order produce a cake, and it fails if any ingredient is missing. A map tells you where you are, where the exits are, and what lies between them, and it still works when the road is flooded. Read every new protocol 3 times before you need it. Read it first for the story, so you know the shape of the illness it addresses. Read it second for the branch points, the questions and thresholds that send a patient down one path instead of another. Read it third for the actions, the doses, the reassessments, and the moment of referral. That third reading is the one people skip, and it is the one that decides whether the drug is given in the first hour or the fourth.

Pay particular attention to the branch points, because that is where the protocol is really making its judgments. The thresholds are deliberately crude. A breathing rate cutoff, a temperature, a capillary refill time, a mid-upper arm circumference: each was chosen to catch nearly everyone with the dangerous condition, and the price of that choice is that some people without it are treated as though they had it. This is a deliberate trade, and understanding it will save you from 2 opposite errors. The first error is to distrust the protocol because a particular child did not have pneumonia. The second is to treat the number and forget the child in front of you. The cutoff protects a population; your examination protects the person.

What a protocol cannot see, it cannot weigh. It cannot see that a mother has walked 3 hours and will not return for review, so a yellow category that assumes a follow-up visit is really a red one. It cannot see that the family cannot afford the second drug, that the nearest road is impassable in the rains, that a traditional healer has already given an unknown preparation, or that the patient is the only wage earner for 9 people. It cannot see fear, or grief, or the fact that the person has decided to refuse. These are not irrelevant details to be cleared away before the real medicine begins. They are the context in which the protocol either works or fails.

This is what it means to follow a protocol faithfully while keeping the patient, not the paper, at the center. The protocol sets a floor beneath your care, never a ceiling above it. It tells you the minimum that a person with this presentation must receive, and then it releases you to give more: more explanation, more examination, more time, more attention to the family, more thought about what else could be wrong. A clinician who does only what the page requires has practiced safely but thinly. A clinician who uses the page as the beginning of the assessment, and then looks up at the patient, has practiced well. The paper serves the person; the reverse is a corruption of the whole method.

Watch for the failure mode that looks like discipline but is not. It is possible to complete every step of a protocol correctly and still harm the patient, because the steps were performed as ritual rather than as thought. The register is filled in, the drug is given, the box is marked, and nobody notices that the child is now limp, or that the woman's bleeding has not slowed. The corrective is built into almost every good protocol and is almost always the step that gets dropped: reassessment. Check the response, repeat the observations, and compare the patient with the patient of 30 minutes ago. If a protocol ever seems to forbid reassessment, you have misread it. Reassessment is not a departure from the protocol; it is the protocol's way of noticing that the patient has not read it.

There are legitimate reasons to deviate, and you should be able to name them, because a nurse who cannot articulate a reason will either deviate silently or follow blindly. Deviation is justified when the patient has a known allergy or a previous severe reaction to a named drug. It is justified when a contraindication exists that the protocol's broad category does not capture, such as pregnancy, severe kidney failure, or a dangerously slow heart rate. It is justified when the patient is already taking a medicine that interacts with the one proposed. It is justified when the required drug or device simply does not exist in your clinic and a safer alternative does. It is justified when the presentation falls outside the algorithm, as it often does. And it is justified when you hold better information than the algorithm had, such as a laboratory result, a recent consultation, or a clear statement from the patient.

Deviations fall into 3 rough groups, and naming the group helps you decide what to do afterward. The first group is the patient who does not fit: the protocol is right in general and wrong for this person. The second group is the place that does not fit: the protocol assumes a drug, a laboratory, a distance, or a specialist that your setting does not have, so the same instruction cannot be carried out here. The third group is the protocol that is simply out of date or simply mistaken, which happens to every document eventually, because medicine moves and paper does not. The first 2 groups are yours to manage with documentation. The third group obliges you to report the problem upward, in writing, to whoever owns the document.

Just as important is knowing what is not a legitimate reason. Convenience is not a reason. Fatigue is not a reason. A vague memory of how it was done at the last posting is not a reason. Disagreement with the guideline in principle, without a specific clinical argument about this patient, is not a reason. Pressure from a relative, from an administrator, or from your own wish to be seen as decisive is not a reason. And there is one absolute boundary: never deviate from a step that exists to prevent immediate death unless you have already put a substitute in its place. Airway protection, breathing support, circulation, glucose for the person with low blood sugar, temperature control in the small child, and control of hemorrhage are the irreversible steps, and the protocol's insistence on them is the least negotiable thing it contains.

When you do deviate, document it so that it is defensible and so that it teaches. Write the deviation in the patient's record at the time it happens, not the next morning. State the time, the finding that triggered your decision, the exact step you departed from, the reason in clinical terms, what you did instead, the patient's response, whom you informed, and when the patient will be reviewed. Write it in sentences a colleague can follow, because a note that only you can decode is not documentation, it is memory. If the clinic keeps a paper register, record the departure there as well, and if there is an incident or quality form, complete it. The purpose is not to build a defense against blame. It is to make your reasoning visible to the next clinician, to your supervisor, and to your future self.

Defensibility and learning are 2 sides of the same note. A contemporaneous, specific, honest record lets a reviewer see that you knew the rule, understood why it existed, and had a clinical reason to set it aside. Without that record, the same decision looks like negligence, because the reviewer can see only the outcome and the absence of explanation. With it, the decision becomes reviewable evidence about whether the protocol itself needs to change. This is the quiet contract of protocol-based practice: you accept the discipline of the path, and in exchange the system accepts the obligation to learn from every time the path proved wrong. Hold up your end in writing, and insist, politely and persistently, that the system holds up its end too.

That learning happens only if someone reads the deviations together. A single departure may be a single patient; 5 departures in the same direction are a message about the protocol. So build the habit of reviewing deviations monthly, alongside the register, looking for clusters. If the same drug is routinely unavailable, the supply chain is the problem. If the same threshold is routinely overridden at night, the threshold may be wrong for your setting, or the training may be thin. If the same step is skipped by every clinician, the step is probably impractical and should be redesigned. A protocol that is deviated from constantly and never revised is not being followed; it is being quietly abandoned, one shift at a time.

Now take up the hardest case, which is the protocol that is wrong for the local reality. This is not rare. A fluid protocol may be perfect for a well-nourished child and dangerous in a population where severe acute malnutrition is common, because the heart and the circulation respond differently. A fever protocol may assume a laboratory that can exclude malaria, when your only tool is a rapid diagnostic test and your clinical judgment. A referral instruction may assume a hospital 30 minutes away when yours is 2 days away and closed during the rains. A drug that is standard elsewhere may be unavailable, unaffordable, or illegal where you work. When the mismatch is real, the protocol is not merely inconvenient; it is a hazard, and following it blindly converts a safety tool into a source of harm.

The response to that situation is adaptation, never abandonment. Abandoning a protocol returns you to the era of individual memory, which is exactly what the protocol was built to replace. Adapting means changing the smallest possible element to fit the local reality while preserving the intent of the whole. Begin by describing the mismatch precisely and with evidence: how many patients, which step, what happens when you follow it, and what happens when you do not. Then consult the people who own the document or the national program responsible for it, because they may already have an adaptation for your context, and because an unconsulted rewrite carries no authority. Propose the smallest change that resolves the problem, test it on a defined number of patients, ask the staff what it does to their work, and set a date to review it.

Adaptation must be written down, dated, and versioned, or it becomes folklore. Keep one master copy of the local protocol, mark it with the date it takes effect and the name of the person who authorized it, and give every clinical area the same version. Remove or clearly mark the superseded pages, because 2 versions in circulation is worse than one imperfect version, and a night shift will always find the wrong one. Record what you changed and why, in a short preface of 2 or 3 sentences, so that a new nurse arriving 6 months from now does not think the change was an accident. Then train the change explicitly, since a protocol that lives only in a folder has not been adopted at all.

Building a local protocol from nothing is a skill worth practicing, and the first decision is which problem to write about. Choose by frequency and by harm, not by interest. The condition that recurs every week and occasionally kills someone is the right candidate; a dramatic disease you saw once is not. In many remote settings the short list looks like convulsions in a child, severe dehydration, postpartum hemorrhage, anaphylaxis, snakebite, and injury from a needlestick or another sharp instrument. Pick one, write it on a single page, and only then consider a second. A clinic with 3 excellent one-page protocols that are actually used is safer than a clinic with 30 that nobody can find.

What goes on that page is a matter of discipline, and the elements are always the same. First the trigger, stated so plainly that a tired nurse recognizes it in 10 seconds. Then the immediate actions in strict order, with the ones that cannot wait placed first. Then the doses, written for weight bands rather than requiring arithmetic at the bedside. Then the branch that says what to do when the preferred drug or device is missing. Then the point at which the patient must be referred, and the point at which transfer is futile. Then what must be documented, and finally the date of the next review. If a page cannot carry those 7 things legibly, the page is trying to do too much and should be split.

Dosing deserves its own moment, because the arithmetic is where protocol-based practice most often breaks. Weight-based dosing is safe in principle and fragile in practice, since the dose per kilogram must be multiplied by a weight that somebody has to measure, remember, and calculate correctly while a child is fitting. Reduce that fragility deliberately. Use a length-based tape or a printed band chart, weigh in kilograms rather than in local units that require conversion, and keep the dose tables for your stock formulations on the same page as the protocol. For drugs with a narrow margin, require a second person to check the calculation aloud and sign it. And make one rule absolute: when the dose you calculate looks surprising, stop and recalculate before you draw it up.

Logistics is unglamorous and decisive, so give it real attention. A protocol that exists only in your head is not a protocol, and a protocol that exists only on a laptop is unavailable at 2 in the morning. Print it, laminate it if you can, and place it where the work happens: on the wall above the examination couch, in the night desk, in the outreach bag, and in the transport kit. If your patients and staff read another language, translate the local version properly, with a speaker of that language checking the clinical terms, because a mistranslated threshold is a hazard. Pictures help where literacy is uneven, hand-drawn marks on a wall chart are acceptable if they carry the intended meaning, and a copy kept on your phone is useful only if the phone is charged and the file is on the device rather than in an inbox.

Then marry the protocol to the shelf, because a protocol and a store room that disagree will produce failure. Before the season in which a condition peaks, check that every drug, fluid, and device named in the protocol is physically present, in date, and in a quantity that matches the expected caseload. If the first line drug is out of stock, the protocol should already say what happens next, and if it does not, that is a gap to fix in writing, not a gap to fill with improvisation at the bedside. Keep the protocol's drug list and the clinic's order list aligned, so that ordering reflects what you are actually committed to doing. A protocol whose drugs are constantly absent is not being tested by reality; it is being defeated by logistics, and the logistics is part of the clinical work.

Monitoring is what turns a stack of paper into a system, and it has 2 faces. The first is process monitoring: are the steps being done? You measure this from the register, by counting how many patients with the target presentation received the right assessment, the right drug, the right referral, and the right review date. The second is outcome monitoring: are patients doing better? This matters more, and it is harder, because outcomes arrive slowly, patients disappear, and a rural clinic may see only a handful of a given case in a year. Use both, and be honest about the small numbers. A perfect compliance rate attached to a rising death rate is a signal that the protocol is being followed into the wrong place.

Adverse effects deserve a monitoring plan of their own, and it must cover the drug and the protocol alike. Learn the adverse effects that the protocol itself warns about, such as the low blood sugar, the low blood pressure, the allergic reaction, the kidney injury, and the drug that prolongs the electrical activity of the heart, and teach the team the specific signs that require the drug to stop. Then watch for the harms that protocols generate as systems: fluid given too fast to a malnourished child, a diagnosis missed because the algorithm closed the question too early, a referral that exhausted a family without changing the outcome, an antibiotic given because the page said so. When an adverse event occurs, document it fully, report it through the national system for drug safety if one exists, and keep the suspect drug and its packaging so that the report can be investigated.

Training is what keeps a protocol alive between revisions, and it works best when it is active. Reading a protocol aloud to staff changes very little; walking a team through a simulated case changes a great deal. Drill the low-frequency, high-consequence events, the convulsion, the postpartum hemorrhage, the anaphylaxis, and the needlestick, until the sequence is automatic, and rotate the roles so that everyone has played the person who must decide. For community health workers, use the simplified version of the protocol, taught the same way, because the task shared downward must arrive with the same clarity it had at the top. And build supervision in from the beginning, since a protocol without a supervisor is a protocol without a witness.

Here the nursing role becomes unmistakable, and it is larger than the word implementation suggests. The nurse is the custodian of protocol-based practice in a remote setting, because the nurse is the person present at 2 in the morning, the person who notices that the same step fails every week, the person who trains the health worker, and the person who writes the note that makes a deviation understandable. Under task sharing you have the authority to act, and with that authority comes the accountability to act within the agreed path and to report when the path is wrong. Owning the protocol means knowing it well enough to defend it, to teach it, to adapt it with evidence, and to say aloud when it has failed a patient. That is not administrative work added to clinical work; it is clinical work.

Do not let the protocol displace consent, because a decision path is not a substitute for a conversation. The person in front of you is entitled to know what you think is wrong, what you propose to do, what the alternatives are, and what might go wrong, in words they can use. A protocol can standardize the treatment but never the explanation, and where a patient refuses a step, that refusal is a clinical fact to be documented, explored, and respected within the limits of the law. The same applies to a family in a pediatric case: the paper authorizes the drug, but the parent authorizes the care. Consent taken seriously also improves adherence, because a person who understands the plan is far more likely to complete it.

There will be times when the protocol cannot be completed, and how you handle that says a great deal about your practice. A drug runs out, a laboratory closes, a vehicle fails, the referral hospital has no bed, and the document in your hand assumes a world that no longer exists. When resources are short, ration openly using clinical criteria that you can state and defend, and tell the patient and the family the truth about what is available and what is not. Do not hide behind the protocol as though the paper made the decision. Rationing is a human judgment made under constraint, and it belongs in the record with the reasoning attached, both for the patient's sake and for the system's learning.

Handover is where protocols earn much of their value, because they give 2 shifts a shared language. A structured handover names the patient, the working problem, what has been done, what is pending, what the protocol predicts next, and the specific trigger that should bring the clinician back to the bedside. The same structure travels with the patient to the receiving facility, where a single page that shows what was given and when is worth more than a long story told from memory. Write the handover as though the next clinician knows nothing about your shift, because at 3 in the morning that is exactly true. A protocol plus a clear handover is how care survives the change of the person holding it.

Fatigue deserves honest mention, since protocols exist partly for the tired brain. Cognitive performance degrades with sleep loss, hunger, heat, and the accumulated weight of a long shift, and the degradation is worst in exactly the tasks protocols protect: arithmetic, sequencing, and remembering the rare possibility. This is why the first hour of a night shift and the last hour of a long day are the moments to reach for the page rather than for memory. It is also why checklists, weight bands, and pre-drawn doses are not insults to your competence but aids for a human nervous system under load. Using them well is a sign of professional maturity, not a confession of weakness.

Guard against the opposite problem, which is too many protocols. A clinic can accumulate a binder of documents nobody reads, each one authoritative and none of them used, and the effect is worse than having none, because the presence of the binder creates the illusion of a system. Curate ruthlessly. Keep the protocols that match the conditions you actually see, archive the rest in a clearly marked place, and put the 5 or 6 documents that save lives where they will be seen. The one-page protocol that a night nurse can find and follow beats the flawless 40 page guideline that stays in the office. A small, living set that is revised beats a large, dead one that is merely admired.

In the end, protocol-based practice is not the opposite of clinical judgment; it is the training ground for it. Following a well-built path teaches you the natural history of a condition, the points where it turns, and the interventions that change it, and after enough patients you begin to recognize the pattern before you reach for the page. That recognition is what allows an experienced nurse to notice the patient who is not following the expected course and to say so early. The protocol builds the pattern; the judgment detects the exception. Neither replaces the other, and a clinician who has only one of them is incomplete.

What the method teaches, if you let it, is a particular kind of humility. The protocol is the visible trace of people who were wrong, of patients who paid for the lesson, and of a profession that decided to remember rather than to repeat. Standing inside that inheritance, you are less alone than the empty clinic at night suggests, and you are also less free to invent. You follow the path because it carries the experience of many hands, you step off it when the patient requires it, and you write down why, so that the next version of the path is a little better than the one you inherited.

To summarize, protocols exist in remote practice to carry collective experience into a room where no senior clinician is standing beside you, and they should be read as decision paths rather than as recipes. Know the families: the integrated management of childhood illness and the integrated management of adult illness for syndromic care, the mental health gap action program of the World Health Organization for mental disorders, the clinical guidelines of Doctors Without Borders for surgical and obstetric care, and the treatment publications of the World Health Organization for drugs and doses, all of them subordinate to the national guideline of the country where you work. Follow them faithfully while keeping the patient at the center, reassess as the protocol itself demands, and deviate only for a clinical reason you can name, such as a contraindication, an interaction, a missing resource, or a presentation outside the algorithm. Document every deviation at the time it happens, monitor both the process and the outcome, watch for the adverse effects of the drug and of the protocol, align your logistics with the page before the season arrives, and adapt rather than abandon a protocol that does not fit local reality. These regimens and thresholds are revised as evidence grows and differ between countries, so name your source and verify the current edition before you prescribe. And carry the humility the method teaches, because the protocol is what years of other people's mistakes look like when they are finally written down.
