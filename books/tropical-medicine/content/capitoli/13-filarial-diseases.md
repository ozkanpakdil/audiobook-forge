# Chapter 13 — Filarial Diseases

Filarial diseases are the infections caused by a family of long, thread-like worms called filariae, and they occupy a distinctive place in tropical medicine because they disable people slowly rather than kill them quickly. Three of them dominate the clinical picture, each with its own insect carrier and its own pattern of damage. Lymphatic filariasis, caused by Wuchereria bancrofti and by Brugia malayi and Brugia timori, is carried by mosquitoes and obstructs the lymphatics, producing swollen limbs and genitalia. Onchocerciasis, caused by Onchocerca volvulus, is carried by blackflies and damages the skin and the eye. Loiasis, caused by Loa loa, is carried by deerflies and produces transient swellings that wander across the body. This chapter follows the guidance of the World Health Organization, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control, and it treats every regimen described as current guidance rather than settled truth, because recommendations are revised and countries differ, so the guideline in force must be checked before anything is prescribed.

The biology of these worms explains almost everything about how they present and how they are treated. An infected insect deposits larvae onto the skin while it feeds, and those larvae enter through the puncture and mature over many months into adult worms that live in the tissues for years. The adults mate, and the female releases very large numbers of microscopic offspring called microfilariae, which circulate in the blood or migrate through the skin according to the species. The insect picks up microfilariae at its next blood meal, and inside that insect the parasite develops through larval stages before it can infect another person. This long cycle means that one exposure does not produce immediate disease, that one course of treatment rarely erases an established infection, and that control depends on interrupting transmission across a whole community rather than on treating one patient.

Most of these worms carry an inner partner that has become essential to them, a bacterium of the genus Wolbachia that lives inside the cells of the worm. It is not a harmless passenger but a requirement for the development, fertility and longevity of the worm, and when it is removed the worm sickens and the infection is slowly curtailed. That discovery turned the antibiotic doxycycline from a curiosity into a real instrument in filarial medicine, particularly for lymphatic filariasis and onchocerciasis. It also explains part of what happens when worms are killed by treatment, because dying parasites release bacterial products that provoke fever, itching and inflammation. Once you understand that the reaction to treatment is partly a reaction to the death of an internal bacterium, its timing and character become predictable and therefore manageable.

Lymphatic filariasis is the most widespread of the three and the one most likely to walk into your clinic, because it is chronic, visible and common. The World Health Organization estimates that approximately 120 million people are infected and that many hundreds of millions live in areas where transmission continues, with the greatest burden in South Asia, in sub-Saharan Africa and in parts of the Pacific. The parasite is transmitted by several kinds of mosquito, and the dominant vector differs by setting. Culex quinquefasciatus breeds in polluted water in dense cities, Anopheles species carry the infection across rural Africa, and Aedes species transmit it on some Pacific islands. Knowing the local vector is therefore not an academic exercise but the basis for deciding whether bed nets, drainage or another measure will reduce transmission in a particular village.

The microfilariae of Wuchereria bancrofti appear in the peripheral blood in a rhythmic pattern, and this periodicity is the detail that most often defeats a diagnosis. In most endemic settings they are most numerous at night, roughly between 10 in the evening and 2 in the morning, and during the day they retreat into the deeper vessels of the lungs. A blood sample taken at midday may therefore be entirely negative in a heavily infected patient, and a clinician who does not know the rhythm will wrongly conclude that the person is free of the parasite. Blood for microscopy must therefore be collected during the period of peak density, and that requirement has to be built into clinic staffing, because somebody must be present late in the evening.

The clinical course of lymphatic filariasis runs through 3 broad phases, and one patient may pass through all of them over decades. In the first phase the infection is silent, with microfilariae in the blood and adult worms in the lymphatics but no symptoms at all. In the second phase acute inflammatory attacks appear, and in the third the lymphatic vessels are permanently damaged and a limb or the genitalia become swollen and hard. What drives it is a combination of the intensity and duration of infection, the number of adult worms, the immune response of the host and the frequency of bacterial infection superimposed on damaged lymphatics. That last factor is the one you can influence most directly, which is why so much of the nursing care in this chapter concerns skin, hygiene and infection rather than drugs alone.

The acute attack, called adenolymphangitis, is the event that brings most patients to care, and it is important to recognize what it usually is. The patient develops sudden fever, chills and a painful, red, warm streak running up the limb, with tender enlarged lymph nodes along the way. The attack may last several days and then subside, only to return weeks or months later, and each recurrence leaves the lymphatic system a little more damaged. Although the attack is often blamed on the worms, in most patients in endemic areas the immediate cause is bacterial infection of skin and lymphatic tissue already injured by the parasite. Damaged channels cannot clear bacteria efficiently, so organisms that would ordinarily be trivial, entering through a cracked heel or a small fissure between the toes, find a hospitable environment and spread. This is why the management of an acute attack is largely the management of bacterial cellulitis, and why preventing that infection from recurring is central to preserving the limb.

Chronic lymphedema develops when lymphatic drainage is so impaired that protein-rich fluid accumulates in the tissues and cannot be cleared. The earliest stage is soft and pitting, so that pressing the skin with a finger leaves a depression, and at this point the swelling may still reduce with elevation. As months and years pass, the tissue becomes firm and then hard, the skin thickens, and the surface develops a rough, warty texture. In advanced disease the limb becomes enormously enlarged and heavy, a state often called elephantiasis, and the normal contours are lost. In men the most common chronic manifestation is hydrocele, a collection of fluid around the testis that can grow to the size of a melon and cause great discomfort, difficulty walking and embarrassment.

Diagnosis of lymphatic filariasis is made by demonstrating the parasite, and the method depends on the setting and the resources available. Microscopy of blood for microfilariae remains the classic test and must be timed to the period of peak density. For Wuchereria bancrofti, an antigen test on a finger-prick blood sample has become the main tool in public health programs because it is rapid, does not depend on the time of day and detects adult worms rather than microfilariae. PCR is also used and can identify the species when the result will guide a programmatic decision. Ultrasound can sometimes show adult worms moving inside dilated vessels, a memorable image but rarely necessary for care, and antibody tests indicate exposure rather than active infection. In practice, choose the test that answers the question you actually have, and in a community survey the antigen test usually answers it best.

The central strategy for controlling lymphatic filariasis is repeated community-wide treatment, which the World Health Organization calls mass drug administration. Rather than seeking out infected individuals, the program offers treatment to every eligible person in an endemic district once a year, usually for 4 to 6 consecutive years, reducing the reservoir of microfilariae and eventually interrupting transmission. The drugs used are diethylcarbamazine, ivermectin and albendazole, given in 2 drug or 3 drug combinations depending on the region and on whether onchocerciasis is also present. Where onchocerciasis is not present, the usual combination is diethylcarbamazine at 6 milligrams per kilogram of body weight together with albendazole at 400 milligrams. Where onchocerciasis is present, ivermectin at 150 to 200 micrograms per kilogram replaces diethylcarbamazine, because diethylcarbamazine can provoke severe reactions in people carrying the eye worm. A 3 drug regimen combining all of these has more recently been introduced in selected settings, and because guidance is revised and programmatic choices are country-specific, the current national protocol must be verified before a campaign is planned.

The nurse is usually the person who makes a mass drug administration campaign work, and the skills required are logistical as much as clinical. Before the campaign begins, districts must be mapped so that every eligible community is identified, community health workers must be trained, and the purpose of treatment must be explained in language that residents trust. Treatment is often delivered house to house or at a fixed point, and the dose must be adjusted for weight and age rather than estimated by eye, which means that scales and height boards belong on the supply list. Registers and tally sheets recording how many people were treated and how many refused are the evidence on which the program reports its coverage, and incomplete records can waste a season of work. The nurse also supervises the team, answers the fears of people who are wary of tablets, and ensures correct storage even though these drugs tolerate heat better than vaccines do. Above all, the nurse protects the credibility of the program by taking every complaint seriously and documenting it, because trust is the currency of every campaign and it is spent quickly.

Adverse reactions to treatment are common and must be expected rather than treated as failure. When a person carrying microfilariae receives one of these drugs, the rapid death of the parasites releases antigens and bacterial products that provoke a reaction known as the Mazzotti reaction. It typically begins within a day of treatment and includes fever, headache, itching, rash, muscle aches, joint pain, dizziness and sometimes tender swelling of lymph nodes. Management is supportive, with rest, fluids, an antipyretic such as acetaminophen and, when itching is severe, an antihistamine, and most reactions settle within a few days. What matters most is that the community was warned in advance that some people feel unwell afterward, because an unanticipated reaction frightens people away from the next round.

Eligibility rules protect the people whom treatment could harm, and every campaign team must apply them without cutting corners. Pregnant women are excluded and offered treatment after delivery, and women who might be pregnant are handled according to the national protocol, which varies. Very young children are excluded, and the age or height threshold is set by the program and must be confirmed rather than remembered. People who are acutely ill, and those with a serious chronic illness that is not controlled, are usually deferred until they are stable. In areas where onchocerciasis or loiasis is present the choice of drug matters enormously, and where loiasis is highly endemic the use of ivermectin requires particular caution, as described later. The nurse should also ask about other medicines the person takes, because interactions and cumulative effects are possible, and should record the reason whenever anyone is excluded, since that record allows the next round to reach them.

Caring for a lymphedematous limb is one of the most valuable things a nurse can teach, and it is done with ordinary materials and consistent habits rather than expensive technology. The foundation is skin hygiene: wash the limb every day with soap and clean water, dry it carefully including between the toes, and keep the skin soft with an emollient so that it does not crack. Elevate the limb whenever the person rests, ideally above the level of the heart, so that gravity assists what the lymphatics can no longer do. Encourage gentle movement and exercise, because muscle activity helps move fluid, and teach the person to avoid prolonged standing or sitting with the limb hanging down. Protect the limb from injury, because every break in the skin is a doorway for bacteria, so a person with lymphedema should wear shoes rather than walk barefoot and should use gloves for heavy work. Treat even small wounds promptly with cleaning and an antiseptic, and never dismiss a minor abrasion as unimportant in a limb whose defenses are already compromised.

When an acute attack occurs, the priority is to treat the bacterial infection and to relieve the patient while the episode runs its course. Current guidance supports an antibiotic active against streptococci and staphylococci, and a commonly used regimen is cephalexin at 500 milligrams by mouth 4 times a day for 7 to 10 days, with alternatives chosen according to local resistance patterns and the patient's allergies. Rest, elevation of the limb and simple analgesia reduce pain and swelling, and the patient should drink enough fluid. An antifungal cream applied between the toes is valuable when fungal infection is present there, because that is a common portal of entry and treating it reduces the frequency of attacks. Any patient with systemic signs such as high fever, spreading redness, severe pain or a rapidly worsening limb needs urgent assessment, because a deeper soft tissue infection can destroy tissue and threaten the limb. After the attack resolves, the work of prevention begins again, and the nurse should review hygiene, footwear and skin care before discharge rather than assume those lessons were retained.

Hydrocele is the complication that most often requires surgical care, and it deserves a direct conversation because embarrassment keeps many men away from treatment. Fluid collects in the sac around the testis, the scrotum enlarges progressively, and the weight and bulk interfere with walking, work, sexual function and sleep. Surgery to remove the sac, usually under local or regional anesthesia, is curative and is one of the most satisfying operations in tropical surgery because the change in the patient's life is immediate. Postoperative nursing care includes wound inspection, control of swelling, pain management and clear instructions about when to return, because infection of the wound is the main complication. It is also important to explain that surgery corrects the hydrocele but does not remove the adult worms, so the person may still need treatment and should still take part in community programs. Where surgery is not available, hygiene and supportive care remain essential, and the nurse should be honest about what can and cannot be done rather than leave the patient to guess.

Onchocerciasis, also called river blindness, is caused by Onchocerca volvulus and is transmitted by blackflies of the genus Simulium. The blackfly breeds in fast-flowing, well-oxygenated rivers and streams, which is why the disease clusters along river valleys and why the blindness it causes came to be called river blindness. The female blackfly bites during the day, and her blood meals both acquire and transmit the larvae, and because the flies can travel considerable distances, control has historically required attacking the vector as well as treating people. Adult worms gather in fibrous nodules beneath the skin, where they can live for more than a decade and continue to release microfilariae. Those microfilariae migrate through the skin and the eye, and it is they, rather than the adults, that cause the itching, the skin disease and the blindness. The intensity and duration of exposure determine the burden, so people who live and work near breeding rivers for years accumulate the greatest damage.

The clinical features of onchocerciasis unfold over years and are easy to underestimate at any single visit. The earliest symptom is usually intense itching, which may be the only complaint for a long time and is often dismissed as an allergy or a habit. The skin then changes, with rashes, patches of altered pigmentation, thickening, dryness and a loss of elasticity that makes it look prematurely aged. In some patients the lymph nodes in the groin enlarge and the skin of the lower abdomen hangs in folds, a change known as hanging groin. Nodules containing adult worms can be felt over bony prominences such as the pelvis, the ribs and the skull, and their number gives a rough indication of the parasite burden. Eye involvement is the feared complication, since microfilariae in the cornea cause punctate keratitis, in the anterior chamber they cause a painful uveitis, and in the retina and the optic nerve they cause damage that ends in irreversible blindness. Because the eye disease is progressive yet often painless early, the eye examination deserves to be a routine part of the assessment rather than an afterthought.

The diagnosis of onchocerciasis rests on finding the parasite, and treatment is built on a drug that kills microfilariae and thereby interrupts the damage. Small pieces of skin are snipped from areas without nodules and examined for microfilariae, and the yield improves when several snips are taken from different sites. The treatment of choice is ivermectin at 150 micrograms per kilogram of body weight, repeated at intervals set by the program, usually once or twice a year for many years, because it does not reliably kill adult worms and therefore does not cure the infection in one course. Community-directed treatment with ivermectin means that the community itself distributes the medicine, chooses its distributors and keeps its own records, and this approach has proved more durable than a program run from outside. Doxycycline has been used as an adjunct because of its effect on Wolbachia, and its role continues to be evaluated, so the current recommendation should be checked rather than assumed. Vector control, including larvicides applied to rivers and the distribution of insecticide-treated materials, has been an essential companion to treatment.

The elimination of onchocerciasis from the Americas is one of the genuine achievements of modern public health, and it carries lessons that apply elsewhere. Transmission was limited to a few foci in 6 countries, and a sustained program of community-directed treatment combined with vector control drove the infection down until transmission was interrupted in each focus. The initiative required decades of consistent political and financial commitment, careful surveillance to prove that transmission had truly stopped, and the participation of the affected communities themselves. In Africa the challenge is far larger, because the affected areas are vast, the vectors are widespread, and the disease overlaps with lymphatic filariasis and loiasis in ways that complicate the choice of medicines. The American experience nevertheless shows that even a disease measured in decades of disability can be ended when treatment, vector control and community engagement are sustained together.

Loiasis is caused by Loa loa and is transmitted by deerflies of the genus Chrysops that breed in the canopy of the tropical forest. Adult worms migrate through the subcutaneous tissues, moving several centimeters in a day, and can live for many years. The most characteristic sign is the Calabar swelling, a transient, painless or mildly painful swelling on a limb or around a joint that lasts a few days and then fades, only to reappear elsewhere weeks or months later. These swellings are the visible response to the passage of an adult worm and are often mistaken for arthritis, an allergy or a sprain. Another classic presentation is the eye worm, when a migrating adult is seen moving across the conjunctiva, a sight that alarms the patient but usually leaves the eye unharmed. Many infected people carry large numbers of microfilariae in the blood, and it is this burden that creates the greatest danger when treatment is considered.

The serious problem of ivermectin in areas where loiasis is co-endemic is one of the most important safety lessons in filarial medicine. When a person with a very high level of microfilariae in the blood receives ivermectin, the rapid death of those parasites can produce a severe inflammatory reaction in the brain, leading to encephalopathy, coma and sometimes death. The risk rises sharply when the microfilarial count exceeds a threshold of roughly 30,000 per milliliter of blood, and it is highest in people who have lived for years in the forested areas where the worm is transmitted. This is why campaigns in co-endemic regions cannot simply treat everyone with ivermectin, and why programs have developed strategies to identify people at high risk before treatment. One approach is to test for microfilariae and exclude anyone with a high count from ivermectin, offering another drug or another program instead. Another is to use a lower dose in a supervised setting, or to test at the time of treatment and withhold the drug if the count is too high. Whatever the approach, the nurse must understand that the decision to give or withhold ivermectin in these areas is a safety decision and not a matter of convenience.

Treatment of loiasis itself is more difficult than treatment of the other filarial infections, because the drugs available are less satisfactory. Diethylcarbamazine kills microfilariae and some adult worms, and a common regimen is 8 to 10 milligrams per kilogram per day in divided doses for 21 days, but the reaction to treatment can be severe and the drug does not always eliminate the adults. Albendazole at 400 milligrams twice a day for 21 days is used as an alternative or in combination, and it is generally better tolerated, though also not fully reliable. When the microfilarial count is extremely high, a procedure to remove microfilariae from the blood, similar in principle to apheresis, may be used before drug treatment to reduce the burden and the risk of reaction. The regimens described here are current guidance rather than fixed rules, so the national protocol and the latest World Health Organization recommendations must be verified before treatment begins.

Because these 3 infections overlap geographically, the practical work of control is a work of mapping and coordination. A district may carry lymphatic filariasis and onchocerciasis together, in which case the treatment that is safe for one is chosen for both, which is why ivermectin replaces diethylcarbamazine in those areas. If loiasis is also present the plan becomes more complex, because the same ivermectin that is safe for the first 2 diseases may be dangerous for a person with a heavy load of Loa loa. Programs therefore begin with surveys that establish which infections are present and how intensively each is transmitted, and the results determine the drug regimen, the frequency of treatment and the eligibility rules. The nurse contributes to that mapping by recording what is seen in the clinic, helping with community surveys and reporting unexpected reactions promptly. Coordination between programs also avoids the waste of parallel campaigns that reach the same village with different tablets on different days, and it makes better use of the community's patience and time.

Adverse event monitoring is a nursing responsibility that protects both patients and programs, and it deserves more attention than it usually receives. Every team member should know the difference between an expected mild reaction and a serious event, and should know exactly how to report each one. An expected reaction, such as fever, itching or a rash in the days after treatment, is recorded and managed supportively. A serious event, such as difficulty breathing, loss of consciousness, a seizure, severe eye pain or a rapidly spreading skin infection, requires immediate referral rather than reassurance. The report should record the drug given, the dose, the time, the symptoms, the timing of their onset and the outcome, because those details are what let a program weigh benefits and risks honestly. A program that hides its adverse events loses the trust of the community and eventually its legitimacy, while a program that reports them openly can adjust its practice and keep that trust.

Long-term disability care is the part of filarial medicine that stretches over years, and it belongs as much to nursing as to any specialty. A person with elephantiasis of the leg or a large hydrocele faces stigma, loss of work, reduced marriage prospects and, frequently, depression and social isolation. The nurse should ask directly about these consequences, because patients rarely volunteer them, and should treat them as clinical problems deserving attention rather than as unfortunate background. Self-care groups, in which people with lymphedema meet to learn hygiene and to support one another, reduce the frequency of acute attacks and improve mood and function. Teaching a person to care for a leg day after day is a slow intervention, and its benefit is measured in prevented attacks and preserved independence rather than in a laboratory value. The goal of this care is not a cured leg but a life that remains workable, and that goal is worth stating plainly to the patient.

Prevention of filarial infection rests on reducing contact with the insects that transmit it, and none of the measures is exotic. Insecticide-treated bed nets reduce exposure to the mosquitoes that carry lymphatic filariasis in many settings, particularly where the vector bites at night. Repellents, long clothing and screens help against daytime biters such as the blackfly and the deerfly, and these matter most for people working near rivers or in the forest. Community-wide chemotherapy reduces the reservoir of infection so that even a biting insect is less likely to pick up the parasite, which is why treatment and vector control work better together than either does alone. Because the ecology of each vector differs, the right combination must be chosen for the local setting, and the nurse should know which vector dominates the area where the patients live.

To summarize, filarial diseases are chronic infections of the lymphatics, the skin and the eye, caused by worms transmitted by mosquitoes, blackflies and deerflies, and they cause disability that accumulates over years. Lymphatic filariasis produces acute adenolymphangitis, progressive lymphedema and hydrocele, and its control depends on repeated community-wide treatment with diethylcarbamazine, ivermectin and albendazole, on meticulous skin and limb care, and on prompt treatment of bacterial infection. Onchocerciasis produces itching, dermatitis, nodules and river blindness, and it is treated with ivermectin distributed through community-directed programs that eliminated the infection from the Americas. Loiasis produces Calabar swellings and the eye worm, and it demands special caution because ivermectin can cause dangerous encephalopathy in people with heavy microfilarial loads. The nurse stands at the center of this work, in the logistics of campaigns, in the monitoring of adverse events, in the teaching of daily self-care and in the long support of people living with disability. Because regimens are revised, because guidance differs between countries and because the current guideline must always be checked before prescribing, the practitioner who verifies the protocol each time is the one who practices safely.
