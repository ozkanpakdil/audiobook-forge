# Chapter 56 — Telemedicine

In a remote clinic, the most powerful diagnostic instrument you own may already be in your pocket, and it is the one you use least deliberately. The mobile telephone that carries your messages and your photographs can also carry a consultation, a second opinion, and a plan for a patient who cannot travel. Telemedicine is not a new specialty so much as a new route for an old habit, which is asking a colleague for help. What has changed is that the colleague can now be hundreds of kilometers away and still be useful within the hour. The World Health Organization treats telemedicine as part of health system strengthening rather than as an exotic extra, and its consolidated telemedicine implementation guide describes how such services can be built where specialists are scarce. In this chapter we will treat the telephone, the camera, and the message as clinical tools, with the same discipline you apply to a scalpel or a syringe.

Telemedicine means the practice of medicine at a distance through information and communication technology, and it takes three practical forms in field work. The first is synchronous consultation, in which you and a remote clinician speak or see one another at the same time by telephone or video call. The second is store-and-forward consultation, in which you assemble a case, a set of images, and a question, send them, and wait for a considered answer. The third is remote monitoring, in which measurements travel from the patient or the clinic to a clinician who watches a trend over time. Most real services use all three, choosing between them by the urgency of the problem and the quality of the connection. The distinction matters because each form has its own etiquette, its own failure modes, and its own standard of documentation.

Telemedicine cannot examine the patient for you, and it should never be sold as a substitute for your hands. A remote clinician cannot feel the abdomen, hear the lung through a stethoscope placed on a struggling child, smell a wound, or watch a mother walk into the room and know that she is worried. Compression of images loses the fine detail of texture and color that a bedside look supplies, and a slow connection hides the pauses, the hesitations, and the family's unspoken question. Telemedicine also cannot conjure drugs, oxygen, blood, or a surgical team that does not exist. Its real product is not remote care but better local care, because it sharpens the judgment of the person who is actually present. If you remember that, you will use it well and you will not lean on it beyond its strength.

What telemedicine does best is change a decision at a moment when the decision is still cheap to change. It can turn a vague worry into a ranked list of possibilities, confirm a diagnosis you suspected, talk you out of a treatment that would have harmed the patient, or tell you that the case must be evacuated tonight rather than tomorrow. In obstetric emergencies it can put a specialist's voice in the room while you give a loading dose of magnesium sulfate for eclampsia or misoprostol under the tongue for postpartum hemorrhage. In pediatrics it can help you decide whether a child with a fever and a fast breathing rate needs referral or can be managed at the clinic. In trauma it can guide the focused assessment with sonography in trauma and the decision to transfer. In each of these the outcome changes not because the specialist does something but because you do something sooner and more accurately.

The mechanism is simple and worth saying aloud, which is that distance removes expertise, and expertise is often the scarce resource rather than the drug. When the only nurse within a day's journey must decide alone, a fifteen minute conversation with someone who has seen the condition a hundred times compresses years of experience into the moment. Reports from low resource settings show the clearest benefit where a time critical decision, a dangerous drug, or a referral threshold is involved. The evidence is weaker for chronic and slowly evolving problems, where the value lies in continuity rather than urgency. The World Health Organization guideline on digital interventions for health system strengthening is candid about this uneven evidence, and you should read its recommendations rather than assume that every digital tool improves care. Your task is to notice which of your own recurring problems telemedicine genuinely solves.

The core skill of telemedicine is not the technology but the structured case summary, and it is a skill you can learn and teach. A good summary is short enough to be read in two minutes and complete enough that the reader does not need to ask a basic question. It carries the relevant history, the findings, your working concern, and one specific question that can be answered. Everything else is background, and background is what makes a remote consultant stop reading. Think of it as the spoken handover you would give an arriving colleague, ordered so that the most important sentence comes first. The discipline of writing it will also improve your bedside thinking, because a case that cannot be summarized is usually a case that has not yet been understood.

The relevant history begins with the reason for contact and the timeline, because a remote clinician cannot see how sick the patient looks. State the age, the sex, whether the patient is pregnant or breastfeeding, and the weight in kilograms if the patient is a child. Give the duration of the illness, the symptoms in the order they appeared, and any treatment already given, including traditional remedies and medicines bought without a prescription. Include the exposure history that matters in your setting, such as travel, contact with animals, recent water contact, or a tick bite. Note the allergies, the chronic conditions, and the drugs the patient takes every day. Finish with the social facts that shape the plan, such as the distance to the hospital and whether the family can pay for transport.

Then give the findings, and be exact about how you obtained them. A temperature of 38.5 degrees Celsius, a pulse of 120 beats a minute, a respiratory rate of 32 breaths a minute, and a blood pressure of 90 over 60 mean much more when the reader knows that you measured them yourself rather than accepting the record of the night shift. Say what you examined and what you found, in plain anatomical order, and say clearly what you could not examine. Describe a rash by its distribution and its character rather than by a diagnosis, and describe a wound by its size in centimeters, its depth, its discharge, and its smell. Mention the tests you have already done and their results, with the units written in full words. A remote clinician can reason with uncertainty but cannot reason with silence.

Your working concern is a short statement of what you think is happening and how worried you are, and it should be honest rather than impressive. Say what you have already excluded and why, so that the consultant does not repeat work you have done. Then ask one specific question, because a summary that ends in four questions usually receives an answer to none of them. The best questions offer the consultant a decision to make, with the options your clinic can actually execute and the constraints you face. For example, ask whether the child can be treated at the clinic with a supervised first dose and review in 24 hours, or must travel now. Add the practical facts the answer depends on, such as whether you have the drug, whether the road is open, and whether a vehicle is available. A consultant who knows your constraints gives you an answer you can use.

When the consultation happens by telephone rather than in writing, the order of the summary matters even more, because the listener cannot skim. Open with one sentence that names the problem and its severity, such as a two year old child with a fever for 4 days who is now drowsy and breathing fast. Then give the history in a few sentences, the findings that confirm your concern, the treatment already given, and your question. Pause after the headline and let the listener catch up, because a rush of numbers is easier to lose than to follow. If you are interrupted, answer the interruption first and then return to your structure rather than abandoning it. Write the summary down before you call, even if you never read it aloud, because writing is what forces the order.

A photograph can carry information that words cannot, and a bad photograph carries a falsehood. Before you take the image, decide what question the image is meant to answer, because one photograph answers one question well and three questions badly. A lesion needs a close view for texture and a wider view for distribution, taken in the same light and at the same distance so that the two can be compared. A wound needs a view that includes a recognizable scale and a view that shows its relation to the joint or the limb. An eye needs a view of the whole face and a view of the eye itself, with the eyelids held open if the patient can tolerate it. If you cannot say what the image is for, do not send it, because it will only invite a guess.

Lighting is the most common reason a useful image becomes useless. Use daylight when you can, but move the patient into the shade of a wall or a roof, because direct sun bleaches color and casts the shadows that hide depth. Never use a flash close to the skin, because the glare removes exactly the surface detail you were trying to show. Avoid mixing daylight with the yellow light of a lamp, and avoid colored curtains, painted walls, and bright clothing behind the patient, because the camera adjusts its color balance to whatever dominates the frame. If you must work at night, use two light sources from different directions so that the shadows fill in. The test of lighting is simple, in that if you cannot see the texture of the skin with your own eyes, the camera will not see it either.

Scale is what turns a photograph into a measurement, and its absence is what makes a consultant hedge. Place a ruler beside the lesion, or a coin of known size where no ruler is available, and keep it in the same plane as the skin rather than closer to the lens. Photograph a wound with a tape measure along its length and another across its width, and say the numbers in the message so that the reader does not have to guess the units. For a rash, a distant view with a full limb or the whole trunk in the frame gives the reader the size of the body as a reference. Never crop the scale out afterward when you adjust the image. A measurement spoken in the text and shown in the image is worth more than either one alone.

The background and the angle decide whether the patient is shown with dignity and whether the reader can interpret the image. Use a plain sheet, a clean wall, or a neutral cloth, and remove anything that identifies the patient or the household, such as documents, photographs, or a name on a door. Take the image from directly above for a flat lesion, and from the side with a light held low for anything raised, because side lighting reveals height and depth. For the mouth, the eye, or the genital area, ask a colleague to help and explain to the patient exactly what will be photographed and why. Offer a screen or a cloth so that only the necessary area is exposed, and stop if the patient withdraws consent. A photograph taken without care for modesty will not be read carefully, and it should not be.

Consent for a clinical photograph is a conversation, not a signature collected in a hurry. Explain that the image will leave the clinic, who will see it, how it will be stored, and how long it will be kept, in words that the patient or the family can repeat back. Say plainly that the patient may refuse, that refusal will not change the care, and that the patient may ask for the image to be deleted later. For a child, ask the parent or the guardian, and for an adolescent, ask the young person as well as the adult, because their dignity is their own. Record the consent in the chart with the date and the name of the person who gave it. If the patient cannot consent and no one can consent for them, treat the image as unusable, however interesting it looks.

A video call is the closest thing to a bedside visit that distance allows, but it lives or dies on the connection. Test the link before the patient is in the room, and choose the spot with the strongest signal, which may be a particular corner of the clinic or the top of a low hill outside. Ask the remote clinician at the start whether the image and the sound are clear, and agree on a fallback so that if the video fails you can continue by voice without losing the consultation. Have the chart, the drugs, the weighing scale, and the measuring tape within reach before you connect. Prepare the patient and the family for what will happen, including the camera, the voices from the speaker, and the possibility that they will be asked to show a part of the body. A prepared call takes half the time of an improvised one.

Even a good video call compresses the image and flattens the examination, so you must supply with your hands what the camera cannot. Ask the patient to walk across the room and back, to lift both arms, to squeeze your fingers, and to breathe deeply while you count, so that the remote clinician can see effort and symmetry. Hold the camera steady on the chest while you listen with the stethoscope and describe what you hear, and move slowly enough that the image is not a blur. For a skin lesion, hold the camera still and let the lesion fill a quarter of the frame rather than chasing focus. Never interpret a movement you did not see as normal, because a frozen frame hides a tremor and a small screen hides a limp. The camera shows what you point it at, and nothing more.

Store-and-forward consultation is often the better tool where the network is unreliable, because the case travels when the connection allows rather than when the patient is present. You assemble the summary, the images, the laboratory results, and the question into one package, label each item clearly, and send it. The remote clinician reads it later, compares it with previous cases, and replies with a considered answer, sometimes with references and a plan in stages. Label every image with the patient identifier you use locally, the body site, and the date, and keep the original files in the chart. Say in the message what you will do if no answer arrives within the time you can wait. The disadvantage is the delay, and the discipline is to set an escalation rule before you send rather than after the answer fails to come.

The examination a remote clinician can talk you through is broader than most nurses expect, and it begins with vital signs measured properly. Count the respiratory rate for a full minute while the child is calm, because that number is the most sensitive sign of pneumonia and the one most often estimated rather than counted. Measure the blood pressure with the correct cuff size, and record the oxygen saturation if you have a pulse oximeter, remembering that a cold hand and dark nail polish both mislead the reading. Weigh the patient in kilograms rather than estimating, because every pediatric dose depends on it. Measure the middle upper arm circumference in a child, and the height of the fundus in a pregnant woman. A number you have measured yourself converts a remote opinion into a local decision.

Beyond the numbers, a specialist can guide you through a structured examination step by step. She can tell you where to place your hands for the liver edge, how to percuss the chest, how to test the neck for stiffness, and how to check the reflexes and the power in each limb. She can talk you through the obstetric examination, the position of the baby, the fetal heart rate, and the signs of imminent delivery. Where you have a point-of-care ultrasound device, she can watch the images as you acquire them and correct your probe position in real time, which is one of the most powerful uses of a video link. She can also guide a lumbar puncture, a wound exploration, or the reduction of a dislocated shoulder by voice alone. The rule is that she directs the sequence while you own the hands, the findings, and the decision to stop.

Patient data sent across a network is patient data exposed, and the obligation to protect it does not change because the clinic is remote. Use the platform that your ministry of health or your organization has approved, and use it for the purpose for which it was approved. Remove identifying details from images wherever the diagnosis does not require them, and never send a face, a name, a national identity number, or a household photograph with a clinical image. Lock every device that stores patient data with a code, keep the screen away from the waiting area, and be careful with a telephone that is shared between staff. Encrypt the device if the operating system offers it, and delete images from the personal gallery once they are filed in the chart. When a platform has no privacy policy that you can read and understand, treat it as unsuitable for clinical use.

The platforms available to you will range from an official national system to an application on your own telephone, and each carries a different risk. A national or organizational telehealth system usually offers a secure channel, a record of the consultation, and a route to the specialist on duty, and it should be your first choice where it exists. Where it does not, a professional messaging application used within a defined group, with a written rule about what may be sent and what must not, can work well enough in practice. Very slow connections may force you toward voice calls and text messages, which carry less data and often succeed when images fail. Whatever the platform, agree in advance who is on the other end, what hours they cover, and what to do outside those hours. The tool matters less than the agreement around it.

Working with a remote specialist is a relationship, and it improves with the same care you give any colleague. Learn the specialist's preferences, because some want a structured written summary and others want to be called and asked questions. Respect the time you are asking for by preparing fully and by not sending a case you could have resolved with the protocol on your shelf, and know the boundaries of your clinic before you call, including the drugs in stock, the oxygen, the blood, the transport, and the staff on shift. Tell the specialist what you have already tried and what happened, because a recommendation repeated is a recommendation wasted. Ask for the reasoning behind the advice and not only the advice, so that the next similar case does not need a consultation at all. Say when you disagree, and say why, because a specialist who never hears your doubts cannot correct them. Thank the person in writing for the cases that went well, because goodwill is a clinical resource.

The closed loop is what separates a consultation from a conversation, and it is the part most often left unfinished. Write the recommendation down as it is given, then read it back to the specialist in your own words and ask whether you have understood it correctly. If the advice changes an order, write the change in the chart with the time and the name of the person who gave it. Carry out the plan and record what actually happened, because the effect of the advice is part of the case. Then close the loop by telling the specialist the outcome, whether the patient improved, deteriorated, or died, and whether the advice was possible to follow. That feedback is how a remote service learns, and it is also how you earn the credibility that makes the next call easier.

Medication advice given at a distance deserves the same caution as any prescription, and more, because the adviser has not seen the patient. Confirm the weight in kilograms, the allergies, the pregnancy status, the kidney and liver function if you know it, and every other drug the patient takes, because interactions are the commonest avoidable harm in remote prescribing. Ask whether the recommended drug is actually in your stock, whether it can be stored correctly, and whether the patient can take it by mouth or needs another route. Dose examples that may come up include oral rehydration salts, one sachet dissolved in 1 liter of clean water and given in small frequent sips, zinc at 20 milligrams once a day for 10 to 14 days in a child older than 6 months, and artemether with lumefantrine dosed by weight band over 3 days. The World Health Organization's guidelines for malaria, its guidance on the management of childhood diarrhea, and its recommendations for the prevention and treatment of postpartum hemorrhage are the sources for those figures, and its consolidated telemedicine implementation guide frames how advice should be given and recorded at a distance. Any regimen you receive remotely is a starting point for verification rather than a settled instruction, because guidance is revised as evidence accumulates, national recommendations differ, and you must check the current version of the relevant guideline before you prescribe or act.

The first failure mode of telemedicine is false reassurance, and it is more dangerous than a wrong diagnosis. A remote clinician who is told a story that sounds mild may answer mildly, and the anxiety that prompted the call is relieved without the patient having been examined. A normal looking photograph of a rash on the first day does not exclude a serious illness that will announce itself tomorrow, and a single normal set of vital signs does not exclude deterioration within hours. Reassurance is a treatment, and like any treatment it has indications, a dose, and adverse effects. When you receive reassurance, ask what would change the advice, and write down the specific signs that should bring the patient back. If you remain worried after a reassuring answer, say so, and ask for the plan that assumes you are right.

The photograph that misleads is the second failure mode, and it has recognizable causes. Color balance flatters or dulls a rash, so that a red lesion may look pink or brown without a reference in the frame. A single view hides distribution, so that a lesion which is one of many looks solitary. An image taken after treatment shows the effect of the treatment rather than the disease, which is why the date of first appearance and the first photograph both matter. Poor light or poor focus makes a severe lesion look trivial and a trivial one look serious. Send the clinical facts alongside the image, and ask the remote clinician to reason from the whole case rather than from the picture alone. When the image and the story disagree, believe the patient in front of you.

The third failure mode is over-reliance, in which the telephone quietly replaces the examination and the protocol. A consultation is not a substitute for looking at the patient again, and a diagnosis given at a distance is a hypothesis that your own findings must confirm or refute. Set an escalation rule before the consultation, so that a defined set of signs leads to referral or evacuation whatever the remote advice says. Do not let a queue of waiting patients shorten the summary you send, because a rushed summary produces a rushed answer. Do not ask the same specialist the same question every week without writing down the answer, because repetition is a sign that the knowledge is not being kept. Never let a remote opinion override your own eyes when they disagree, because the patient is in your room and not in the consultant's.

The record of the encounter belongs in the patient's chart, because distance does not dissolve responsibility. Write the date and the time, the name and role of the person consulted, the means of contact, and the reason for the consultation in one or two sentences. Record what you sent, including the images and the results, and where the originals are stored. Record the advice you received as close to its words as you can, and then record what you actually did, which is the part that proves the standard of care. Note the follow-up plan and the signs that should bring the patient back, and note who will review the patient and when. If the consultation failed because the line dropped or no one answered, record that too, because an unreachable system is a clinical fact.

The nurse who is present remains the responsible clinician, and the remote consultant advises rather than takes over. That principle appears in the World Health Organization's guidance on telemedicine and is echoed in the professional standards of many nursing boards, though the details vary between countries and you must check the rules that govern your own license before you rely on them. Practicing across a border raises questions of registration, liability, and the legal status of an electronic record, and those questions are answered by your employer and your regulator rather than by the application on your telephone. Consent to a remote consultation is part of consent to care, so the patient should know that a distant clinician is being involved. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention has published practical guidance on telehealth that many programs adapt, and it is worth reading for the operational detail rather than the clinical content alone. Delegation does not transfer accountability for the decision you act on, and no advice protects you if you act against your own clinical judgment without question.

Telemedicine is learned, and the learning is mostly repetition with feedback. Practice the structured summary aloud with your colleagues, and review the cases you sent six months ago to see which summaries actually worked. Build a small library of your own teaching images, taken with consent and stored lawfully, so that the next nurse can see what a healing wound or a resolving rash looks like at each stage. Teach the health workers in the villages to collect the vital signs and the photographs to the same standard, because the quality of a consultation is set at the point of collection. Run a drill for the night when the network fails, with a written protocol for the telephone call that replaces the video. Audit whether the recommendations you received were carried out, because that number measures whether the service is real, and notice who is being left out, since the patient without a telephone, the woman who needs permission to travel, the family that speaks only a minority language, and the person with a hearing or vision impairment are all at risk from a service designed around a device. Offer the clinic telephone for the call, arrange an interpreter before the consultation rather than during it, and keep a written alternative such as a scheduled radio call or a paper referral carried by a health worker. A service that reaches only the patients who are easiest to reach is not a service, it is a convenience.

To summarize, the telephone in your pocket is a clinical instrument, and the discipline that makes it useful is the structured case summary built from the relevant history, the findings you measured yourself, your working concern, and one answerable question. The image that teaches is lit by daylight, scaled by a known object, framed with dignity, and taken with consent, while the image that misleads is unbalanced in color, solitary in view, or taken after treatment has already changed the lesion. Video calls and store-and-forward messages each have their place, and both depend on a secure platform, an agreed contact, and a closed loop in which the recommendation is read back, carried out, and reported to the person who gave it. Medication advice at a distance is verified against the current national guideline before it is acted on, because regimens are revised and countries differ. The failure modes are false reassurance, the misleading photograph, and over-reliance on a voice that cannot see the patient, and each is countered by your own examination and your own escalation rule. Everything that happens at a distance is written in the patient's chart, with who advised, what was advised, and what you did. Distance does not dissolve responsibility, and the nurse in the room remains the clinician who owns the decision.
