# Chapter 48 — Dental, Eye, and Ear, Nose and Throat Basics

Teeth, eyes, ears, noses and throats fill more clinic days than any textbook admits, and the word basics in this title is a trap. These small structures crowd the face around the airway and the brain, and their diseases can blind, choke, or descend into the deep spaces of the neck. The woman who has chewed on one side for two years comes to you because you are the only person who will look. The discipline is constant: understand the mechanism before the maneuver, do only what you can do safely, and refer what deserves a specialist while the patient can still travel.

Dental pain is one of the commonest reasons an adult walks hours to a rural facility. Caries, or tooth decay, begins when bacteria in plaque ferment sugar and produce acid that dissolves the enamel; once it is breached the decay spreads toward the pulp, the living core of nerve and blood vessel. Inflammation of that pulp, called pulpitis, is the throbbing pain that keeps people awake, and when the pulp dies the infection escapes at the root tip into the bone, forming the periapical abscess. Fluoride strengthens enamel and the frequency of sugar drives the acid attacks, so the most useful dental act in a village is teaching families to brush twice a day with a fluoride toothpaste.

To examine the painful tooth you need light, a dry field, and time, in that order. Have the patient point with one finger, then inspect each surface: the biting surface, the two neighboring sides, the cheek side, and the tongue side. Tap each tooth with a metal handle, because tenderness to percussion means the inflammation has reached the ligament that suspends the tooth in bone. Press the gum over each root, looking for a tender bulge, the gumboil, which drains a chronic abscess, and rock the crown between two instruments to test mobility. Finish with the questions that localize the pain: triggered by cold and gone in seconds, spontaneous and worse at night, or sharp only on biting?

The answers sort pulpitis into its two forms, and the distinction decides what you can offer. Pain only with cold, sweet, or biting, fading within seconds and pointing to an obvious cavity, is likely reversible pulpitis: the pulp is inflamed but alive, and cleaning the decay and sealing the tooth relieves it. Pain that arrives unprovoked, wakes the patient at night, and is hard to localize is likely irreversible pulpitis: the pulp is dying, and no dressing from outside will quiet it for long. A periapical abscess adds percussion tenderness, a tooth that feels high on closing, a gumboil, facial swelling, and sometimes fever. Without radiographs you make this distinction at the chair and record which tooth and what you found.

Dental triage comes down to one question: has the infection left the tooth? Caries with mild reversible pulpitis or a painless gumboil can wait for the visiting dentist for days. Mark these findings as urgent: facial swelling crossing the midline or below the jaw line, fever above 38 degrees Celsius, trismus, meaning the mouth opens less than two fingerbreadths, difficulty swallowing, drooling, or a muffled voice. Swelling in the floor of the mouth spreading from a lower molar is Ludwig angina until proved otherwise, an airway emergency: keep the patient sitting upright and refer now. Acute necrotizing ulcerative gingivitis presents with sudden painful bleeding gums, craters punched out between the teeth, a foul metallic taste, and breath that fills the room, and it responds to metronidazole, 400 milligrams by mouth three times a day in an adult, plus cleaning, fluids, and food. When the same destruction invades the cheek of a malnourished child, it is noma, a gangrene that can perforate the face within days; give penicillin and metronidazole, feed the child, and refer.

A temporary filling is one of the small procedures that genuinely saves discomfort, and it suits a clean cavity with reversible pulpitis, a lost filling without deep decay, or a tooth awaiting the dentist. Clean out debris and soft decay with a spoon shaped excavator, rinse with clean water, and dry the cavity with gauze, because moisture is the reason most dressings fall out. Press in a zinc oxide temporary restorative material and adapt it to the walls with the flat end of an instrument. Have the patient bite on a strip of gauze, because a filling that sits high hurts within hours, and the patient feels it first, so trim it. Send the patient away with one instruction: nothing hard on that tooth, and return if the pain worsens rather than settles.

Dental anesthesia makes all of this possible. For upper teeth the jawbone is thin, so a supraperiosteal infiltration works: inject one to two milliliters of 2 per cent lidocaine with epinephrine into the gum beside the root tip, aspirating first. For lower molars the bone is dense, and you need an inferior alveolar nerve block: with the mouth wide open, insert the needle into the hollow just behind the last lower molar at the height of the chewing surface, advance one to two centimeters until gentle bone contact, then withdraw, aspirate, and deposit about 1.5 milliliters slowly. A 1.8 milliliter cartridge of 2 per cent lidocaine contains 36 milligrams, and the ceiling is about 7 milligrams per kilogram with epinephrine and 4.5 without it, so count your cartridges. Do not inject into visibly infected tissue, because acidic pus inactivates the anesthetic and the needle carries infection deeper. Watch for tingling lips and a metallic taste, the first warnings of lidocaine toxicity.

Extraction is the definitive treatment for most teeth you will meet, and the safe rule is to extract only teeth loose enough for the forceps to do most of the work. Teeth firmly fixed in healthy bone, upper molars whose roots sit near the maxillary sinus, and any tooth in a hot, swollen field belong to a dentist, because a fractured root or a hole into the sinus turns a simple extraction into a complicated one. Wait until the lip or tongue is truly numb before you begin. Luxate the tooth with an elevator worked between root and socket wall, then grasp the crown as far down the root as possible and deliver with slow rocking rather than a straight pull. Inspect the tooth for broken roots, leave the clot undisturbed, and have the patient bite on folded gauze for 15 to 20 minutes before checking the socket again.

Aftercare is a short list the patient can repeat back to you. Bite on the gauze for half an hour, with no rinsing, no straws, and no spitting for the first 24 hours, because suction can pull the clot out. Eat soft, cool food on the other side, expect saliva streaked with blood for a day, and return for frank bleeding. From the day after, rinse gently with warm salt water, half a teaspoon of salt in a cup of boiled and cooled water, three times a day after meals. Give paracetamol, 15 milligrams per kilogram up to 1 gram every six hours by mouth, adding ibuprofen, 10 milligrams per kilogram every eight hours, when the stomach and kidneys are sound. Tell the patient when to return: uncontrolled bleeding, swelling that increases after the second day, fever, difficulty opening the mouth, or pain that worsens after three days.

The commonest complication after extraction is not infection but dry socket, and recognizing it spares the patient pointless antibiotics. The story is a return two to four days after extraction, often of a lower molar, with severe throbbing pain, a bad taste, and an odor, but no fever, no swelling, and a socket whose white bone lies exposed where the clot should be. The mechanism is loss of the clot with a bare, inflamed bone surface, and the risk rises after difficult extractions, in smokers, and in patients who rinsed too early. Treatment is mechanical: irrigate the socket with warm saline and pack it with ribbon gauze carrying a eugenol dressing, changed every one to two days until the bone is covered. Antibiotics are not indicated unless there is spreading swelling, fever, or trismus.

Antibiotics have a narrow place in dental care, and prescribing them for every toothache is how a clinic manufactures resistance. They do not relieve the pressure pain of pulpitis or cure an abscess pocket, which is why drainage is the treatment and the antibiotic is only the escort. Give them when there is fever or spreading cellulitis, when the patient is malnourished, diabetic, or immunosuppressed, and when extraction must wait: amoxicillin, 500 milligrams by mouth three times a day for three to five days in an adult, or 40 to 50 milligrams per kilogram per day in a child, with metronidazole, 400 milligrams three times a day, added for anaerobic spread. For penicillin allergy, clindamycin is the classic alternative. The World Health Organization and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention both teach that the antibiotic is an adjunct to removing the cause, never a substitute for it. Regimens are revised regularly and guidance differs between countries, so check the current national protocol before you prescribe.

Keep a register of extractions, the reasons, and the complications, because a clinic that can say it removed 400 teeth last year can argue for a visiting dentist and a fluoride program in a way a clinic with no data cannot. Everything that enters a mouth is sterilized between patients, and a pressure cooker at full steam for 20 minutes is a legitimate field method where an autoclave is absent. Ask about oral pain in every antenatal and well child visit, and teach the link between sugary drinks and night pain. Remember that recurrent oral thrush in an adult raises the question of human immunodeficiency virus infection or uncontrolled diabetes, and deserves testing.

Turn now to the eye, and begin every eye encounter by measuring what the patient can see, because vision is the vital sign of the eye and the number that decides urgency. Test each eye separately with the other covered, using a chart at 6 meters, a reduced chart at 3 meters, an illiterate E chart, or the ability to count fingers, see hand movements, or perceive light. Write the number down, because a red eye with normal vision is almost never an emergency, while a red eye with reduced vision always is. After vision comes the flash examination with a torch: the pupil, the pattern of redness, the clarity of the cornea, and the red glow from the depths of the eye. Fluorescein strips, moistened with clean water and swept across the lower lid, stain raw epithelium green.

The foreign body in the eye is the commonest ocular procedure a remote clinic performs, and the history sets the risk. Grinding or hammering metal throws fragments that can penetrate the globe with little external evidence, so that history obliges you to exclude a penetrating injury first. Evert the upper lid by folding it over a probe while the patient looks down, because the speck that moves with every blink often hides there. After a drop of topical anesthetic, irrigate with clean water, since many specks float away, then lift what remains with a moistened cotton swab; a rust ring can be teased off with a flat sterile needle. If the eye may be penetrated, shown by an irregular or teardrop pupil, blood in the front chamber, or a visible tear, place a rigid shield resting on the orbital bones, give nothing by mouth, check tetanus cover, start systemic antibiotics, and refer urgently. Never press on a globe that may be open, and never give topical anesthetic for home use, because the eye feels fine while an ulcer eats.

A corneal abrasion, a scratch through the surface epithelium, presents with sudden pain, foreign body sensation, tearing, and photophobia, and fluorescein shows the raw patch glowing green. Management rests on two facts: the epithelium usually heals within 24 to 72 hours, and the raw surface is open to bacteria, above all Pseudomonas, which can destroy an eye in a day or two. Once no foreign body remains under the lid, give antibiotic ointment, chloramphenicol 1 per cent three times a day or tetracycline twice a day for three to five days, and control pain with oral paracetamol and ibuprofen. Review at 48 hours: worsening pain, a cloudy cornea, or a white level of cells in the front chamber means an infected ulcer, not an abrasion. The old custom of a tight eye patch has been settled against: patching does not speed healing, it hides the eye that most needs watching, and a contact lens wearer, whose particular danger is a Pseudomonas ulcer, must never be patched.

Conjunctivitis, inflammation of the membrane over the white of the eye and the inner lids, is usually a nuisance, but sorting bacterial from viral decides what you do and what you teach. Bacterial infection gives a thick discharge that glues the lashes shut overnight and often crosses to the other eye; it settles with lid hygiene and a topical antibiotic such as tetracycline ointment twice a day for five to seven days. Viral infection gives a watery discharge, grittiness, a tender gland in front of the ear, and often a cold; no antibiotic shortens it, and your treatment is hygiene education: hand washing, separate towels, and keeping the child home. Purulent discharge in a newborn is gonococcal until proved otherwise and can melt the cornea within days, so it is an emergency. Severe pain, reduced vision, or a cloudy cornea is never simple conjunctivitis, and the label mild must follow the findings.

One form of conjunctivitis stands above all others in the tropics, because trachoma, caused by the bacterium Chlamydia trachomatis, is the commonest infectious cause of blindness on earth. Repeated childhood infections scar the inner surface of the upper lid, and years later the scar contracts and rolls the lashes inward, so that every blink drags hair across the cornea; that stage, trichiasis, is what finally blinds, and it is prevented by nothing more exotic than a washed face. Look for foamy follicles like grains of rice on the inner upper lid in children, and in adults for inturned lashes the patient plucks out with tweezers, which puts the patient on a surgical list. The World Health Organization recommends community treatment with a single dose of azithromycin by mouth, 20 milligrams per kilogram, erythromycin for 14 days in pregnancy, surgery for trichiasis by outreach teams, and sustained face washing and sanitation. The surgery takes minutes and saves the eye, so your job is case finding.

Learn the red flags of the painful eye by heart, because each is a sight threatening emergency wearing the mask of a routine red eye. Severe pain with a hard globe, a half dilated fixed pupil, halos around lights, nausea, and vision loss in an older patient is acute angle closure glaucoma: the drainage angle has shut and the pressure has climbed, so refer immediately. Deep aching with photophobia, a small sluggish pupil, and a ring of redness around the cornea, called ciliary flush, suggests anterior uveitis, often linked to tuberculosis, leprosy, or an arthritis syndrome. A cloudy cornea with a white or yellow level of cells in the front chamber, called a hypopyon, indicates a bacterial or fungal ulcer, and the same sign after surgery or trauma means endophthalmitis, which counts in hours. The rule is simple: a red eye with vision loss, ciliary flush, a cloudy cornea, or a hypopyon goes to the eye unit today.

The flash examination of the pupil is your neurology and your pressure gauge at once. Shine a torch into one eye and watch both pupils, which should be round, equal, and briskly reactive. A pupil fixed in the middle position, in a red eye with a hard globe, belongs to angle closure. A small, irregular pupil with ciliary flush belongs to uveitis, and a teardrop pupil drawn to one corner means a ruptured globe. For pressure sense, rest two fingers gently on the closed upper lids and compare the two eyes, because an eye in angle closure feels like a stone, and an eye that may be open must never be pressed. Check the red reflex last: a clear red glow means the media are clear, while a white reflex in a child, called leukocoria, means cataract or a tumor, and the child must reach a surgeon.

Vitamin A deficiency is the leading cause of preventable childhood blindness, and it lives where diet is poor and measles, diarrhea, and severe acute malnutrition are common. The vitamin maintains the epithelia of the body, including the conjunctiva and cornea, and it is the raw material of the retinal pigment; deficiency first steals night vision, then dries the conjunctiva, then softens and perforates the cornea, a stage called keratomalacia that blinds. Your screening signs are the child who stumbles after dusk, a foamy gray patch on the white of the eye, called a Bitot spot, and any cornea that looks dry or hazy in a child with measles or malnutrition. Treatment is high dose vitamin A by mouth on the World Health Organization schedule: 200,000 international units on the day of diagnosis and the next day, plus a third dose two to four weeks later, for a child over 12 months, with one half that dose between 6 and 11 months and one quarter under 6 months. In pregnancy high doses can harm the fetus, so the doses for women of reproductive age are lower and weekly; verify the exact regimen in the current World Health Organization guidance.

Cataract, the clouded lens, is the leading cause of blindness worldwide, and most of it is cured by an operation that takes about 20 minutes, which makes your referral pad a tool for restoring sight. The mechanism is lens protein losing its transparency under age, ultraviolet light, diabetes, trauma, or congenital infection; the story is painless progressive blurring with glare, and the finding is a dim red reflex or a white pupil. The rule that separates it from urgent causes is that cataract is painless and quiet: slow loss over months is cataract, while a cloudy eye with pain or rapid loss is something else. What the distant service can do is what you cannot: a small incision, removal of the lens, and implantation of a plastic lens, delivered at a base hospital or by a surgical team that sets up for a week in a district center. Your part needs no surgeon: record the acuity of each eye, keep a list of candidates with name, age, and village, counsel that the operation works, arrange transport, and review the eye on the first day, because new pain and worsening vision then is endophthalmitis.

Eye injuries follow a few patterns. The chemical burn leads the list, because alkali from cement, lime, or bleach penetrates deeper than acid and keeps dissolving tissue from within; treatment is immediate, copious irrigation with clean water for at least 20 to 30 minutes, prying the lids open while the water runs across the eye. Blunt trauma gives its spectrum: blood layered in the front chamber, called hyphema, needs rest, an upright position, a shield, and daily review, with extra vigilance in sickle cell disease. A sunken eye with double vision on looking up is an orbital floor fracture, referred in days, while a tense eye bulging forward with falling vision after trauma is a retrobulbar hemorrhage, one of the few minute counted emergencies in this field. The suspected open globe is handled like a cast: no drops, no ointment, no pressure, a rigid shield on the orbital bones, systemic antibiotics, tetanus prophylaxis, nothing by mouth, and urgent referral with the vision you measured written on the slip.

The ear examination rewards slow discipline. Choose the largest speculum the canal accepts, brace your hand against the head, and pull the pinna upward and backward in adults and downward and backward in small children. Inspect the canal skin, then the eardrum: its color, its position, its translucency, and the light reflex. A normal drum is pearly gray and moves when you puff air against it, while a bulging red drum that does not move is acute otitis media, and a clean hole with hearing loss defines chronic perforation. Screen hearing with a whispered voice, and if you carry a 512 hertz tuning fork, compare bone and air conduction and note which side the sound favors when the fork rests on the skull. Those tests tell you, without any audiometer, whether the loss sits in the middle ear or in the nerve.

Acute otitis media begins with a blocked eustachian tube, usually after a viral cold, so fluid pools in the middle ear and bacteria grow in it; the pressure on the drum causes the pain, and the drum bulges, reddens, and loses its light reflex. In a small child the signs may be only night crying, ear tugging, and fever, which is why the integrated management of childhood illness approach tells you to look at the drum in every unexplained fever. Treatment is analgesia first, because most of what the child needs is relief for two days while the tube drains: paracetamol, 15 milligrams per kilogram every six hours by mouth, and ibuprofen, 10 milligrams per kilogram every eight hours with food. Antibiotics belong to the bulging, painful drum in a child under two years, in a toxic child, or when pain persists past 48 hours; the World Health Organization pocket book of hospital care for children gives amoxicillin at 40 to 50 milligrams per kilogram per day for five days, while several national guidelines use twice that dose for ten days, so check the current guideline rather than memory. Treat the complications as emergencies: a boggy, tender swelling behind the ear is mastoiditis, and a lopsided smile is a facial nerve palsy.

Otitis media with a perforated drum announces itself by discharge: the drum bursts under pressure, the pain eases suddenly, and pus wells from the canal. The treatment then is dry mopping: twist a strip of clean absorbent cloth into the canal, remove it, and repeat until it comes out dry, three or more times a day, because drops swimming on top of pus never reach the middle ear. Then give topical quinolone drops, such as ciprofloxacin 0.3 per cent, four drops twice a day for one to two weeks; quinolones are chosen because they do not deafen, whereas aminoglycoside drops, the gentamicin and neomycin family, can deafen through an open drum and must never be used. Discharge through a drum for more than two weeks defines chronic suppurative otitis media; such a child needs the same treatment, a hearing check, and a place on the list for an ear surgeon. The finding that ends patience is a perforation at the margin with a pearly mass or a long foul smell, which suggests cholesteatoma, an expanding skin cyst that erodes bone and needs surgery.

Otitis externa is inflammation of the ear canal skin rather than the middle ear, and the tragus test separates them: pressing the tragus or pulling the pinna causes sharp pain in otitis externa and little in a simple middle ear infection. The canal is swollen, its skin red and moist, and itching is often as prominent as pain. In the tropics the fungal form is common, with wet debris that looks like soggy newspaper, often caused by Aspergillus species, and it follows water, sweating, and cotton buds. Treatment is aural toilet and dryness: clean the canal with dry wicks, then apply acetic acid 2 per cent drops or quinolone drops for the bacterial form and antifungal drops for the fungal one, according to what you see. Forbid cotton buds, explain that the canal cleans itself from the inside out, and keep the ear dry. The severe end is necrotizing otitis externa: an older diabetic with deep unrelenting pain, granulation tissue on the canal floor, or a sagging face has infection penetrating bone and needs intravenous antibiotics and referral.

Foreign bodies in the ear and nose are a pediatric classic, and the technique is chosen by the object, not by habit. A living insect in the ear is the one emergency of the set, because the drumming of wings against the drum is agony; drown it first with lidocaine or clean oil, then extract the immobilized body with forceps. Irrigate a smooth object with warm water only if the eardrum is intact, never irrigate a bean, which swells and wedges, and treat a button battery as a burn that must come out within hours. In the nose, look for a one sided, foul discharge and a child who was playing with seeds or foam. Have the child occlude the clear nostril and blow hard, or make a seal over the open mouth, occlude the clear nostril, and give a short puff, which drives the object out without any instrument. An object you can see may be grasped with forceps or hooked with a right angle hook, always passed behind it and pulled forward, never pushed back toward the throat. If two attempts fail, stop and refer rather than traumatize the child.

Epistaxis is controlled almost always by pressure and posture, and the technique deserves to be taught to every staff member. Sit the patient up, leaning slightly forward over a basin, pinch the soft cartilage of the nose tip, not the bony bridge, firmly for a full 10 to 15 minutes without letting go to peek. Tell the patient to spit blood out rather than swallow it, because swallowed blood causes nausea and vomiting, which restart the bleed. More than 90 per cent of nosebleeds come from a tangle of vessels at the front of the septum, so pressure on the tip is pressure on the source. If bleeding continues after two rounds of pressure, inspect the septum with a light and a speculum, and either touch a visible vessel with a silver nitrate stick or pack the nose with petroleum jelly ribbon gauze, layered from the floor upward, removed at 24 to 48 hours. Blood trickling steadily down the throat and failure of a good anterior pack mean a posterior bleed, which needs hospital with the airway watched. Always ask which anticoagulant or traditional medicine the patient takes, and whether malaria or dengue has lowered the platelets, because the nosebleed is sometimes the first sign of the disease you must actually treat.

Sore throat is nearly always viral, and the discipline is to find the minority that needs penicillin, because in the tropics that minority carries lifelong consequences. Bacterial infection with Streptococcus pyogenes, the group A streptococcus, is suggested by fever, tonsils swollen with white exudate, tender lumps at the front of the neck, and no cough, whereas viral sore throat arrives with a runny nose, hoarse voice, and mouth ulcers. The reason to treat is the aftermath, because untreated streptococcal pharyngitis can trigger acute rheumatic fever, which scars the heart valves for life, and it can inflame the kidneys. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention recommend penicillin V by mouth, 500 milligrams twice a day for ten days for adults and 250 milligrams twice a day for small children, or a single intramuscular dose of benzathine benzylpenicillin, 1.2 million units, with a cephalosporin or azithromycin as the alternative in penicillin allergy. Insist on the full ten days even when the throat is quiet by day two, because the course is for the immune system, not the symptoms. These are the doses of current guidance at the time of writing; regimens are revised, national protocols differ, and you must verify the current guideline before you prescribe.

The sore throat that is more than a sore throat announces itself through the airway, and four swellings must be recognized on sight. A peritonsillar abscess, or quinsy, shows one tonsil bulging toward the middle with the uvula pushed aside, a muffled, hot potato voice, and difficulty opening the mouth; it needs drainage and intravenous antibiotics, which means referral unless you are trained and equipped for aspiration. A retropharyngeal abscess, usually in a child under five, shows a neck held stiffly still, refusal to swallow, drooling, and sometimes a bulge of the back wall of the throat; computed tomography in hospital maps it, but the diagnosis is made at the door, and it needs drainage under anesthesia. Epiglottitis, now rare where the Haemophilus influenzae type b vaccine reaches children but still seen where it does not, is the fastest of all: a child sitting still, leaning forward, drooling, with a muffled voice and labored breathing. For that child the rule is absolute: do not examine the throat, do not lay the child down, sit the child up, give oxygen if you have it, and escort to hospital. Diphtheria completes the set: a gray membrane over the tonsils that bleeds when scraped, and a swollen bull neck; the priority is antitoxin early, while it can still neutralize the toxin, and urgent referral with respiratory precautions.

The neck abscess must never be missed, because it is the deep infection that hides behind an ordinary fever and a stiff neck. A patient with fever, tenderness and fullness on one side, torticollis, a swelling that pushes the tonsil or the throat wall inward, or the danger signs of drooling, hoarseness, or stridor has a deep space infection until proved otherwise, and the treatment is intravenous antibiotics and drainage in hospital, never incision in your procedure room. The urgency is anatomy: the fascial spaces of the neck run down into the chest, so an untreated infection can descend into the mediastinum, clot the internal jugular vein, and erode the great vessels. The jugular clot is the syndrome called Lemierre, in which Fusobacterium necrophorum, a normal mouth bacterium, seeds septic clots to the lungs of a young adult days after a sore throat. Look inside the mouth of every fever with a stiff neck, feel both sides for warmth and lost contours, and remember that a deep abscess in its first days is hard and tender rather than soft.

Across these three systems run the same small procedures that save discomfort, and the same cautions about the drugs they use. Lidocaine toxicity announces itself with tingling lips, a metallic taste, and ringing ears before it progresses to seizures, so count your cartridges and aspirate always. Metronidazole causes nausea and reacts severely with alcohol, quinolone drops sting, the ibuprofen family strains the kidneys of the dehydrated patient, and high dose vitamin A can raise the pressure inside an infant's head for a day, with vomiting and a bulging fontanelle. Monitoring is the quiet half of care: check the extraction socket before discharge and again at half an hour, review the corneal abrasion and the otitis media at 48 hours, the nasal pack at 24 to 48 hours, the hyphema daily, and the eye operated for cataract on the first day. Put every review date in a register with the patient's village, because a patient who cannot be found cannot be reviewed, and the defaulters list is a clinical tool.

Documentation carries the same weight as the procedure itself. Record which tooth, which eye, which ear, the vision or hearing before treatment, the anesthetic and cartridges, the complications, the drugs and doses, the instructions, and the follow up date; your note is the patient's next consultation, and in referral it is the letter that travels. Logistics decides whether any of it happens, so build three trays deliberately: the dental tray with the light, mirrors, excavators, elevators, forceps, cartridges and syringe, gauze and suture; the eye tray with fluorescein strips, topical anesthetic, antibiotic ointment, a torch, a shield, and an acuity chart; and the ear, nose and throat tray with an otoscope and spare batteries, alligator forceps, a right angle hook, ribbon gauze, silver nitrate sticks, and a 512 hertz tuning fork. Everything that enters a mouth, an ear, or a nose is sterilized between patients, single use needles stay single use, and expiry dates are checked monthly. If a dental or surgical eye team passes through twice a year, your lists, your register, and your ready trays are half of their productivity.

The discipline of referring what deserves a specialist is a skill, not a defeat, and it starts with knowing your own hands. Write down before you need it what you can do confidently, what you can do with coaching, and what you will never attempt, and let that list change only with training, not with the pressure of a bad night. Photographs have changed the calculus of referral: a phone picture of an eardrum, a cornea, or a facial swelling, taken with consent and sent to a distant colleague, can turn a risky transfer into a plan. Build the relationship with the receiving facility before you need it, and agree on what your letters should contain. You are the examiner who finds the inturned lashes, the teacher who explains the salt water rinse, the coordinator who puts the malnourished child on vitamin A, and the trusted person whose advice decides whether a family travels at all.

To summarize, dental pain, eye complaints, and ear, nose and throat problems are the daily work of a remote clinic, and in each a few findings separate the routine from the emergencies that threaten sight or airway. Examine with light and time, treat pain first, drain what needs draining, and use antibiotics only as the escort to a procedure that removes the cause. Measure vision before and after, look behind the upper lid, keep the ear dry, pinch the soft part of the nose for 15 minutes, and look inside the mouth of every fever with a stiff neck. Know the doses you give, name the guideline they come from, and verify the current edition before you prescribe, because regimens are revised and guidance differs between countries. Do gently and well what your hands and your trays can do safely, document it clearly, and refer what deserves a specialist while the road is still open.
