# Chapter 10 — Arboviral Diseases

Arboviral diseases are infections transmitted by arthropods, chiefly mosquitoes and ticks, and they form one of the largest and fastest changing groups in tropical medicine. The term arbovirus describes not a family but a strategy, in which unrelated viruses pass from one vertebrate host to another inside the body of a blood-feeding invertebrate. Dengue, chikungunya, Zika, yellow fever, West Nile, Japanese encephalitis, tick-borne encephalitis, Rift Valley fever, Mayaro and Oropouche are the names to know, and they are not equally dangerous. What they share is a dependence on human behavior, because the water containers people keep, the houses they build and the journeys they take decide where these diseases appear. This chapter follows the guidance of the World Health Organization, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control, and it treats every regimen as current guidance rather than permanent truth.

Aedes aegypti, the yellow fever mosquito, is the central character in the urban story, and Aedes albopictus, the Asian tiger mosquito, is its expanding understudy. Both breed in small collections of clean water close to people, including discarded tires, flower pots, water storage jars and roof gutters. Both bite during the day, so a bed net used at night gives little protection. Aedes aegypti is adapted to human settlements, feeds preferentially on people and can complete its life cycle within a few meters of the bed where a patient sleeps. Aedes albopictus tolerates cooler temperatures and has spread across much of Europe and North America, which is why the map of these diseases keeps redrawing itself.

These viruses enter a person through the saliva of a biting mosquito or tick, multiply in local tissues and spread through the blood, producing a period of viremia during which the patient can infect further mosquitoes. The incubation period is short, usually 3 to 14 days, and the illness that follows is often indistinguishable from many other febrile diseases. Most infections are silent or mild, and the proportion that becomes severe varies enormously. The first lesson of arboviral medicine is that the diagnosis is rarely obvious from the fever alone, and the clinician who waits for a textbook picture will miss the patients who matter most. The second lesson is that severity often announces itself as the fever settles, so the apparent improvement is exactly when your attention must sharpen.

Dengue is caused by 4 closely related viruses, called serotypes 1, 2, 3 and 4, and infection with one serotype produces lifelong protection against that serotype only. A second infection with a different serotype is the dangerous event, because antibodies left from the first infection can fail to neutralize the new virus and can instead help it enter the cells that amplify the immune response. This phenomenon, called antibody-dependent enhancement, explains why severe dengue is far more common in a second infection and why a patient reporting a previous dengue illness deserves caution. The useful rule is not to predict severity from the serotype but to watch every patient through the window in which severity appears.

Dengue passes through 3 phases, and the names of those phases are the backbone of nursing surveillance. The febrile phase lasts 2 to 7 days and brings high fever, severe headache, pain behind the eyes, muscle and joint pain, nausea, vomiting and often a flushing rash. The critical phase begins as the fever subsides, usually between the third and the seventh day, and it lasts roughly 24 to 48 hours, during which the capillaries become leaky and fluid moves into the tissues. The trap for the unwary is that the patient often looks better at the very moment the critical phase begins, because the fever has broken and the pain has eased.

The warning signs of dengue tell you that the critical phase has arrived or is imminent, and the World Health Organization lists them explicitly so that they can be taught and remembered. They are abdominal pain or tenderness, persistent vomiting, bleeding from the gums or nose or into the gut, lethargy or restlessness, and fluid accumulation such as ascites or pleural effusion. Any one of these findings moves the patient from outpatient observation to inpatient care with intravenous fluids and close monitoring, and none should be dismissed as the ordinary misery of a viral illness. Persistent vomiting matters because it prevents oral rehydration, and lethargy is the sign most often missed when a quiet child is mistaken for an improving one.

Severe dengue is defined by shock, by significant bleeding, by severe organ impairment or by a combination of these, and it is an emergency that can kill within hours. Plasma leakage is the engine of the disease, because when the capillary wall becomes permeable, protein-rich fluid leaves the circulation, the hematocrit rises, the blood pressure falls and the organs are poorly perfused even though no blood has been lost. The shock of dengue is therefore distributive and hypovolemic at once, and it can remain compensated for a while before collapsing suddenly. Severe organ involvement can take the form of hepatitis, encephalitis, myocarditis or acute kidney injury, and it can occur with little obvious leakage, so a normal hematocrit does not exclude severe disease.

Fluid management in the critical phase saves lives, and it is also the intervention most easily done badly. Current World Health Organization guidance divides patients by the severity of leakage and by their ability to drink, giving oral rehydration salts to those who can take them and intravenous crystalloid, usually normal saline or a balanced solution, to those who cannot or who already show warning signs. A typical starting regimen for a patient with warning signs is 5 to 10 milliliters per kilogram of body weight per hour for the first 1 to 2 hours, followed by reassessment and stepwise reduction as the circulation improves, with the rate guided by urine output, hematocrit, blood pressure and perfusion. Colloid solutions are reserved for shock that does not respond to crystalloid, and blood transfusion is reserved for significant bleeding rather than for a low platelet count alone. These figures illustrate how the regimen is built rather than prescribe it, because guidance is revised as evidence accumulates, it differs between countries, and the current guideline must be checked before any fluid or drug is given. The nurse's most valuable contribution is not the choice of fluid but the frequency of reassessment, since a rate that was right 2 hours ago may be wrong now.

Aspirin and the other anti-inflammatory drugs are avoided in dengue for 2 reasons. They impair platelet function and irritate the stomach, so they increase the risk of bleeding in a patient whose platelets are already low. They can also mask the fever and malaise you are using to judge the phase of the illness, and they cause fluid retention that muddies the assessment of leakage. Acetaminophen, also called paracetamol, is the preferred antipyretic, and a common adult dose is 500 to 1000 milligrams by mouth every 6 hours, with the daily ceiling and the pediatric dose verified from the current guideline. Written instructions should name the medicines to avoid, because many over-the-counter preparations contain exactly the drugs that must not be used.

Recognizing the transition into the critical phase is the nursing skill this chapter exists to teach, and it is built on serial observation rather than on any single measurement. The transition usually occurs around the time the fever settles, so a patient who has been febrile for 4 days and now has a normal temperature is entering the window of greatest risk, not the window of safety. Record the temperature, heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate, capillary refill, urine output and mental state at a fixed interval, and compare each set with the last, because a falling systolic pressure and a rising heart rate in a stable patient are the earliest signs of compensated shock. Measure the hematocrit frequently, since a rise of 20 per cent or more from the baseline suggests significant leakage, and remember that it can fall later when bleeding occurs. Ask about and look for the warning signs at every assessment, and document the answers, because the nurse who notices a narrowed pulse pressure and cool hands has recognized severe dengue before the laboratory does.

Chikungunya is transmitted by the same Aedes mosquitoes and produces an illness whose signature is pain rather than leakage. After an incubation of 2 to 7 days, the patient develops sudden high fever and severe joint pain, often in the small joints of the hands, wrists, ankles and feet, with headache, muscle pain, a rash and sometimes conjunctivitis. The fever usually settles within a week, but the joint pain can persist for months or years, and many patients report chronic arthralgia that limits work and sleep. Infection during pregnancy near the time of delivery can be transmitted to the newborn, who may develop fever, poor feeding, lethargy, rash and, in severe cases, neurological disease, so neonatal monitoring is required when a mother is viremic at delivery. Treatment is supportive, with rest, fluids and acetaminophen, and other analgesics are withheld until dengue has been excluded.

Zika virus produces the mildest of the acute illnesses in this group and the most devastating of the congenital ones, an uncomfortable combination for anyone who counsels pregnant women. Most infections cause a low fever, a rash, joint pain, conjunctivitis and fatigue, and many cause no symptoms at all, so the acute illness is a poor guide to who has been infected. The virus can cross the placenta and damage the developing brain, causing microcephaly, brain malformation, vision and hearing loss and developmental delay, and the risk is greatest in the first trimester, though it is not confined to it. Zika can also trigger Guillain-Barré syndrome, an acute inflammatory polyneuropathy that causes ascending weakness, loss of reflexes and, in severe cases, respiratory failure, appearing days to weeks after the acute illness. Unlike the other arboviruses here, Zika is transmitted sexually as well as by mosquitoes and can persist in semen for weeks or months, so barrier protection and the timing of conception belong in the conversation. Prevention in pregnancy is therefore a layered strategy of avoiding areas of active transmission, preventing bites by day and by night, and using condoms with partners who may have been exposed.

Yellow fever has the longest and most consequential history of any arboviral disease, and it remains a serious threat across tropical Africa and parts of South America. The virus circulates in a jungle cycle among primates and forest mosquitoes, in an intermediate cycle at the edge of settlements, and in an urban cycle in which Aedes aegypti carries it from person to person. Most infections are mild or silent, but roughly 1 in 7 patients enters a toxic phase after an apparent improvement, with returning fever, jaundice, abdominal pain, vomiting, bleeding from the gut, kidney failure and shock. There is no specific antiviral treatment, so care is supportive, with fluids, correction of coagulopathy where possible, management of kidney injury and intensive monitoring. Because the disease is notifiable, the public health response begins when the clinician suspects it, and any patient with jaundice, fever and bleeding after travel to an endemic area should be protected from mosquitoes and reported at once.

The yellow fever vaccine is one of the great successes of preventive medicine, and it is a live attenuated vaccine given as a single subcutaneous or intramuscular dose. A single dose is considered sufficient for lifelong protection in most people, although a booster may be advised for certain groups and some countries require a certificate for entry, so current guidance must be checked before travel. The vaccine is contraindicated in infants below a certain age, in pregnancy and breastfeeding in most circumstances, in people with severe immunosuppression including advanced HIV infection or AIDS, and in those with a history of disease of the thymus gland, because the live virus can cause fatal disease of the internal organs or of the nervous system in these groups. Mild reactions of fever, headache, muscle aches and soreness at the injection site are common, and serious reactions are rare but real, so pre-travel counseling must weigh the risk of the disease against the risk of the vaccine.

West Nile virus is transmitted by Culex mosquitoes and maintained in a cycle involving birds, which are the amplifying hosts, and it has spread widely across temperate and tropical regions. The feared complication is neuroinvasive disease, which takes the form of meningitis, encephalitis or acute flaccid paralysis and appears in a small minority of those infected, with the risk rising steeply with age and with immunosuppression. West Nile encephalitis causes confusion, tremor, weakness and sometimes parkinsonian features, and recovery is often incomplete, with fatigue and cognitive difficulty persisting for months. There is no human vaccine and no specific antiviral treatment, so care is supportive, and the nursing role centers on airway protection, prevention of pressure injury and early rehabilitation. Because the virus is carried by ordinary backyard mosquitoes, prevention means eliminating standing water, using repellent and screens, and reporting dead birds where local surveillance asks for it.

Japanese encephalitis is the leading cause of vaccine-preventable encephalitis in Asia, and it falls hardest on children in rural areas where rice paddies and pig pens bring Culex mosquitoes, birds and people into close contact. Infection is usually asymptomatic, but when it invades the central nervous system it produces fever, headache, vomiting, confusion, seizures and focal neurological deficits, and it can progress to coma. There is no specific antiviral treatment, so management is supportive, with attention to seizures, raised pressure inside the skull, airway protection, nutrition and prevention of the complications of immobility. Many survivors are left with intellectual disability, behavioral change, motor weakness, speech difficulty or epilepsy, so the burden outlasts the acute illness by decades and places a lifelong demand on families and health services. Effective vaccines exist in inactivated and live attenuated forms, and they are given in multi-dose schedules whose intervals, ages and boosters differ between countries, so the current national schedule and World Health Organization guidance must be verified before any dose is given.

Tick-borne encephalitis is transmitted by Ixodes ticks and, less commonly, by unpasteurized milk or cheese from infected animals, and it occurs across Europe and northern Asia. The illness is often biphasic, with a first phase of fever, fatigue, muscle aches and headache that settles after about a week, followed by apparent recovery and then a second phase in which the central nervous system is involved. The second phase can present as meningitis, as encephalitis with confusion, tremor, seizures and paralysis, or as inflammation of the spinal cord with weakness of the neck, shoulders and arms, and the neurological damage can be permanent. There is no specific antiviral treatment, so care is supportive and directed at seizures, breathing, pressure area care, bladder management and early rehabilitation. Vaccination is effective and is recommended for people living in or traveling to affected areas, with schedules and boosters that vary by product and by country and must be checked against current guidance.

Rift Valley fever is a zoonosis of sheep, cattle, goats and camels, transmitted to people by mosquitoes and by direct contact with the blood, tissues, abortion products and fluids of infected animals. It appears in explosive outbreaks after heavy rain and flooding, when mosquito populations expand and animals abort in large numbers, so it is a disease of veterinarians, farmers and abattoir workers. Most human infections cause a self-limited febrile illness with headache and muscle pain, but a minority develop hemorrhagic fever, encephalitis or ocular disease with damage to the retina and lasting visual loss. There is no specific antiviral treatment approved for human use, so management is supportive, with strict attention to barrier nursing when bleeding is present and to the safe handling of specimens. Control depends on animal vaccination, on protecting livestock from mosquitoes and on the use of gloves by anyone handling animals or their tissues. This is One Health in practice, because a human outbreak cannot be controlled without controlling the animal outbreak.

Mayaro virus and Oropouche virus are emerging names that clinicians in Latin America and the Caribbean should recognize, and they show how quickly the arboviral map can change. Mayaro virus is maintained in forest cycles involving primates and Haemagogus mosquitoes, and it causes a febrile illness with prominent, sometimes prolonged joint pain that closely resembles chikungunya and is easily mislabeled without laboratory testing. Oropouche virus is transmitted by biting midges and by some mosquitoes, and it has caused large urban outbreaks in the Amazon basin and, more recently, elsewhere in the Americas, with fever, headache, muscle pain, joint pain, nausea and sometimes recurrent symptoms. A recent and deeply concerning finding is the possibility of transmission of Oropouche virus from mother to fetus, with reports of fetal death, congenital malformation and neurological disease in newborns. Diagnosis depends on PCR and antibody testing in reference laboratories, and the practical message is to include these viruses when the presentation fits but dengue and chikungunya tests are negative.

Laboratory diagnosis is a matter of matching the test to the day of illness, because the wrong test at the wrong time produces a falsely reassuring answer. During the first days of fever, when the virus is still in the blood, PCR on serum is the most useful test, and for dengue there is also a test for the non-structural protein 1 antigen that is sensitive early in the illness. After about the fifth day the virus is usually cleared and diagnosis depends on antibodies, and a single positive antibody result can reflect an old infection in someone who has lived in an endemic area, so paired samples taken 2 to 4 weeks apart are far more informative. Cross-reactivity is a serious problem within this group of viruses, so a positive dengue antibody test in a patient previously vaccinated against yellow fever or infected with Zika may not mean dengue, and confirmation may require a neutralization assay in a reference laboratory. The nurse's role in diagnosis is largely logistical, and it consists of drawing the right specimen at the right time, labeling it accurately, transporting it correctly and recording the day of illness.

Infection control for arboviral disease rests on a simple principle, which is that these viruses are not spread from person to person by casual contact but can be spread by the mosquitoes that feed on a patient whose blood carries the virus. The practical measure is therefore to protect the patient from being bitten inside the health facility, especially during the first days of illness. Place the patient in a screened room or under a bed net, use repellent and long clothing, and eliminate standing water in the ward and the courtyard. Standard precautions, including hand hygiene, gloves and safe handling of sharps and body fluids, apply as they do for every patient. Zika is the exception, because it can be transmitted sexually and from mother to fetus, and notification requirements should be met promptly for dengue clusters, yellow fever, Rift Valley fever and any suspected viral hemorrhagic fever.

Vector control for Aedes-borne disease is different from the malaria model that many clinicians know. Because Aedes mosquitoes bite during the day, bed nets treated with insecticide and used at night do not protect against them, and indoor residual spraying has a limited effect on a mosquito that rests in cool, dark corners and feeds by day. The effective measures are removing the containers in which the mosquito breeds, covering or treating water storage vessels, cleaning gutters and drains, and eliminating discarded tires, bottles and plastic containers that collect rain. Personal protection includes repellents with approved active ingredients, long-sleeved clothing, screened windows and doors, and fans that reduce landing and biting, and it should be recommended to the whole household. The nurse who explains the life cycle of the mosquito plainly, and who shows a family the containers in their own compound, changes behavior more effectively than a leaflet.

Counseling before travel is where much arboviral disease is prevented, and it belongs in the same visit as malaria prophylaxis and routine vaccination. The traveler should be told which diseases occur at the destination, which vaccines are available, how long before departure they must be given and which are not available at all, so that the decision to travel rests on accurate information. Yellow fever and Japanese encephalitis have effective vaccines, dengue has a vaccine licensed in some countries for people with documented prior infection, and chikungunya has a vaccine available in certain settings, but each has eligibility criteria that must be checked against current guidance before it is offered. The traveler should also be advised on repellent use, the timing of outdoor activity, clothing, accommodation with screens or air conditioning, and the importance of seeking care promptly for fever after return. Pregnant women and women who may become pregnant need specific advice about Zika and about sexual transmission. The nurse providing this counseling should give a written summary, because a spoken plan is easily forgotten, and should record the advice in the notes.

Special populations require modified surveillance and modified thresholds. Infants and young children with dengue can deteriorate rapidly and may show lethargy, poor feeding, irritability and cold extremities rather than the classic warning signs, so parents must be taught to return immediately if any of these appear. Older adults and people with diabetes, hypertension, heart disease, kidney disease or obesity have a higher risk of severe dengue and less reserve for fluid shifts, so the balance of fluid is narrower and monitoring must be closer. Pregnant women with dengue are at higher risk of severe disease and of adverse pregnancy outcomes, and those with Zika face the additional burden of fetal risk that requires specialist referral and long-term follow-up. Monitoring should therefore be individualized, defining the observations to be recorded, the interval between them, the thresholds that trigger a call and the person responsible, because a monitoring plan that exists only in someone's intention is not a plan at all.

Adverse effects and complications in arboviral care arise as much from treatment as from the disease itself, and the nurse is the person most likely to detect them. Fluid overload is the classic complication of treating dengue, and it appears as puffy eyelids, swelling of the limbs, raised pressure in the neck veins, crackles at the lung bases, breathlessness and a fall in oxygen saturation, and it is more likely when the critical phase was treated with more fluid than the circulation needed. Too little fluid produces the opposite failure, with persistent shock, falling urine output, acid accumulation in the blood and organ injury, so the margin is narrow and assessment must be continuous. Bleeding can follow intramuscular injections, unnecessary punctures of veins, insertion of a urinary catheter and other procedures in a patient with a very low platelet count, so every intervention should be weighed against its bleeding risk. Patients recovering from dengue, chikungunya and West Nile infection may also have fatigue, depression, sleep disturbance and cognitive difficulty that persist after the acute illness, and these deserve assessment rather than dismissal.

The logistics of managing arboviral disease in a resource-limited setting determine what is actually possible, and they deserve as much planning as the clinical algorithm. The first requirement is a reliable way to measure what you are treating, with working thermometers, blood pressure cuffs in pediatric and adult sizes, scales for weight, containers to measure urine, and a functioning centrifuge for hematocrit or a dependable laboratory to which samples can be sent. The second is a secure supply of the relevant fluid, of oral rehydration salts, of acetaminophen in adult and pediatric formulations and of equipment for intravenous access, with a stock system that flags shortages before they become crises. The third is a plan for referral and transport, because some patients will need oxygen, transfusion, dialysis or intensive care that the facility cannot provide, and an honest discussion with the family before transfer is part of good care. Where the workforce is stretched, task-sharing with trained nurses and community health workers extends the reach of surveillance and early warning, provided that supervision and referral pathways are clear.

The nursing role in arboviral disease is continuous and cumulative, beginning before the diagnosis is made and continuing long after the fever has gone. At the bedside, the nurse performs the serial assessment that detects the transition into the critical phase, manages fluid balance with precision, watches for the complications of treatment, protects the patient from further mosquito bites and provides the reassurance frightened families need. In the clinic, the nurse teaches families the warning signs to watch for, the medicines to avoid, the importance of oral fluids and the circumstances that require an immediate return. In the community, the nurse takes part in vector control, outbreak investigation, vaccination campaigns and education about water storage and waste disposal. None of these roles requires permission from anyone else, and each depends on the same skill, which is the ability to observe carefully and to act on what you see.

Surveillance turns individual cases into usable knowledge, and it depends on clinicians and nurses who report rather than wait to be asked. Many countries require notification of dengue, chikungunya, Zika, yellow fever, Japanese encephalitis, West Nile virus infection and Rift Valley fever, and the requirement usually attaches to suspicion rather than confirmation, so a report should not be delayed for a laboratory result. The World Health Organization, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control all publish surveillance data and outbreak notices that guide clinical suspicion and travel advice. Laboratory confirmation, case definition and the systematic recording of place and date are what allow a cluster to be recognized as an outbreak and a response to begin. The nurse is often the person who notices the pattern first, simply because the nurse is the one taking the observations, and that observation should be passed upward without hesitation.

To summarize, arboviral diseases are infections carried by mosquitoes and ticks whose severity ranges from a mild fever to fatal shock, and they share a dependence on the vectors that live among us. Dengue demands the most disciplined nursing attention, because 4 serotypes, the enhancing effect of a second infection and the abrupt onset of plasma leakage make the transition from fever to shock the central event to detect. Watch for abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, bleeding, lethargy and fluid accumulation, remember that the critical phase often begins as the fever breaks, and treat fluid as a drug that must be adjusted against frequent reassessment. Chikungunya brings chronic joint pain and neonatal risk, Zika brings congenital infection and Guillain-Barré syndrome and can be transmitted sexually, yellow fever and Japanese encephalitis have effective vaccines but no specific treatment once disease begins, and West Nile, tick-borne encephalitis, Rift Valley fever, Mayaro and Oropouche each carry their own patterns of disease. Prevention is largely a matter of controlling Aedes mosquitoes, avoiding bites during the day, using barrier precautions with sick animals, vaccinating where a vaccine exists and counseling before travel. All regimens in this chapter are current guidance rather than settled truth, because they are revised as evidence accumulates, they differ between countries, and the current guideline must be checked before any drug, fluid or vaccine is given.
