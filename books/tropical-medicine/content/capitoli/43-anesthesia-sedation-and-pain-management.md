# Chapter 43 — Anesthesia, Sedation, and Pain Management

Anesthesia clears a room in a remote clinic, because it names the part of medicine most nurses are taught never to do alone. Where an anesthetist exists, the surgeon cuts while somebody else owns the airway and the depth of unconsciousness. Far from that hospital, the nurse who holds the needle must also hold the airway, and the only monitor may be your own eyes and a hand on a pulse. This chapter is not a license to give general anesthesia; it is an account of the sedation, local anesthesia, and pain relief an advanced practice nurse can deliver safely with training. Where doses appear, they reflect the guidance of the World Health Organization and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and you must verify the current version and your national protocol before acting, because protocols are revised.

Everything that makes sedation safe is repetitive and a little dull, and the dullness is the point. The same checks and the same chart, performed the same way every time, prevent the catastrophic errors that no amount of talent can rescue. Four rules carry most of the weight: assess the airway before you sedate anyone, respect fasting, monitor continuously with one person whose only task is watching, and have oxygen, suction, and a rescue plan ready before the first drug. That observer is not a luxury, because in a small clinic the observer may be an assistant you taught to count breaths aloud, to call your name when the breathing changes, and to hold a mask on a face. The moment the observer also passes instruments, the monitoring has stopped and the patient becomes the least watched person in the room.

Before the procedure, walk the room and ask the questions aloud. Is the suction working and within one second of my hand, is oxygen flowing, is the mask the right size for this face, are the drugs drawn and labeled, and is the light good enough to see color? The surgical safety checklist of the World Health Organization is the most widely used version of this discipline, with a briefing before and a debriefing after, and adopting it in a one room clinic makes forgetting harder. Name the rescue drugs on the shelf before you need them: naloxone for opioids, flumazenil where a benzodiazepine was used, and glucose for the child whose sedation is really low blood sugar.

Airway assessment is a two minute conversation and examination that can save a life. Ask whether the patient has ever had trouble with anesthesia, whether a previous procedure was difficult, whether there is snoring at night, a hoarse voice, or a swelling in the neck. Then look at how far the mouth opens, whether the jaw slides forward, whether the neck bends and extends, whether the teeth are loose or missing, and whether a large tongue sits in a small mouth. A patient whose open mouth shows mostly the soft palate, whose chin is short, whose neck is stiff, or whose breathing is already noisy is a patient you do not sedate alone. Stridor, a changed voice, drooling, and a swollen tongue or floor of the mouth are absolute stop signs, and the right action is to sit the patient up, give oxygen, and arrange urgent transfer.

Fasting exists because a full stomach and a depressed cough reflex make a predictable disaster. Stomach contents reach the back of the throat, the reflex that would close the airway is asleep, and the lungs receive what the stomach held, which is called aspiration and can kill within minutes or cause a pneumonia that kills in days. Guidance for planned sedation in healthy adults commonly asks for two hours without clear fluids, four hours without breast milk, and six hours without light food, and the numbers differ for children and between national protocols, so check the guideline you work under. The fasting rule is never a reason to delay care for a patient who is bleeding, obstructed, or dying, and there the plan shifts to a rapid controlled technique with suction ready. If the airway is uncertain and the stomach is full, ask whether the procedure can wait or be done under local anesthesia.

Monitoring is a set of questions you ask the patient's body again and again, and the trend matters more than any single number. Keep a hand on the pulse, count the breathing for a full minute rather than estimating, watch the chest rise and the color of the lips, and measure the blood pressure every few minutes. If a pulse oximeter is available, a falling oxygen saturation warns you before the patient looks blue; without one, your eyes and ears do the same work, because noisy breathing and a rising pulse are the oldest monitors in medicine. An electrocardiogram, called ECG after this, is useful when the heart is at risk or the drug disturbs the rhythm, but it means nothing unless somebody watches the screen.

Local anesthesia is the safest anesthesia there is, and it works by blocking the channels in the nerve membrane so the impulse never travels. It produces numbness without unconsciousness, so the patient keeps their own airway, breathing, and protective reflexes, and that is why it sits at the top of the ladder. Lidocaine begins within two to five minutes and lasts one to two hours depending on the tissue and the dose, which makes it the workhorse of infiltration and field blocks. Longer acting drugs exist, but the longer they act the more toxic they can be to the heart, and bupivacaine has caused deaths after small accidental injections into a vein. Choose the shortest acting drug that will finish the procedure, and never keep topping up a block simply because the patient is still awake.

The maximum safe dose of lidocaine is the number that turns local anesthesia from safe into lethal, and it is worth knowing in milligrams and in milliliters. With plain lidocaine the ceiling in common guidance is about 4.5 milligrams per kilogram of body weight, and with epinephrine added the ceiling rises to about 7 milligrams per kilogram, because the blood vessels narrow and the drug leaves the tissue more slowly. Many protocols also cap the total at 300 milligrams plain and 500 milligrams with epinephrine, whatever the weight would allow. A 1 per cent solution holds 10 milligrams in each milliliter, so 300 milligrams equals 30 milliliters, and this arithmetic is the one most often done wrong at the bedside. For a patient weighing 60 kilograms, plain lidocaine allows about 270 milligrams, which equals 27 milliliters of 1 per cent, so write the running total on the packaging as you inject.

Local anesthetic systemic toxicity follows an order worth memorizing, because the earliest signs are easy to dismiss as anxiety. The patient reports a metallic taste, numbness around the mouth, or ringing in the ears, and may feel light headed or see shimmering lights. Next comes agitation, confusion, muscle twitching, and a rising pulse, and at that stage the level in the blood is high enough to cause a seizure. After the seizure the depression deepens, the breathing slows, the blood pressure falls, and the rhythm of the heart can deteriorate into a wide and slow arrest that is very hard to reverse. The early signs are subjective and a child may not describe them, so ask directly about taste in the mouth and tingling lips, and stop the injection the moment any of these signs appear.

Treatment begins with stopping the injection and calling for help, then securing the airway and giving oxygen, because low oxygen and acid in the blood make the drug more toxic to the heart. A seizure is treated with a benzodiazepine, such as diazepam 5 to 10 milligrams intravenously in an adult or midazolam in small titrated doses, and every pediatric dose must be calculated by weight from the current protocol before use. If the patient arrests or the rhythm becomes unstable, the antidote is an intravenous lipid emulsion, usually a 20 per cent preparation, given as a weight based bolus and then an infusion, and the exact regimen must be read from the current guideline because it is easy to get wrong. Cardiopulmonary resuscitation continues with prolonged effort, because recovery from local anesthetic arrest is sometimes delayed. Give no more local anesthetic, avoid lidocaine as an antiarrhythmic, and record the drug and dose before transfer.

Epinephrine added to lidocaine prolongs the block, reduces bleeding, and raises the safe dose, and it carries its own cautions. It narrows the vessels and can in theory reduce blood flow to a fingertip, a toe, or the nose, and although modern evidence suggests the danger has been overstated, many remote protocols still avoid it in digits and in skin that is already short of blood. Good technique matters as much as the drug: use the smallest needle that will do the job, inject slowly while withdrawing the needle, aspirate before each injection to be sure you are not in a vein, and warm the solution toward body temperature. Avoid injecting into infected tissue when you can, because the acid environment weakens the drug, and never inject against resistance in a closed space such as a finger.

The digital block is one of the most useful procedures in a remote clinic, and it is also where careless technique does permanent harm. Each finger has two nerves running along its sides, one toward the palm and one toward the back, and each is blocked by a small injection at the base, entering just above the web space, advancing toward the bone, aspirating, and injecting 1 to 2 milliliters of plain lidocaine before repeating on the other side. A ring block carries the same idea around a digit, with the anesthetic deposited as the needle is withdrawn until a complete ring of numbness surrounds the base. Use plain lidocaine without epinephrine, keep the total under the weight based limit, and avoid a tight bandage around the base of the finger. Test the block before you cut, wait five minutes if sharp pain remains, and treat a finger that turns pale, cold, and painful as a surgical emergency.

The fascia iliaca block changes the experience of a broken hip in a place where nobody can give a spinal. The target is the femoral nerve and the other nerves passing beneath the strong sheet of fascia over the front of the thigh, and the aim is to fill that space with local anesthetic. The landmark is the line from the front of the hip bone to the pulse in the groin, divided into thirds, with the needle entering at the junction of the outer and middle thirds and a distinct give as it passes through two layers of fascia. A common regimen is 0.3 to 0.5 milliliters per kilogram of 0.25 per cent bupivacaine, or an equivalent volume of lidocaine, up to the maximum safe dose, checked against the current protocol. Weakness of the front of the thigh is expected, but puncture of the femoral artery and nerve injury are the risks, so aspiration and slow injection are not optional.

Ketamine is the drug that makes remote sedation possible. It produces a dissociative state in which the patient is unaware of the world while the airway reflexes, the breathing, and the blood pressure are largely preserved, and it also produces deep analgesia, which is why it suits reducing a fracture or cleaning a burn. The usual intravenous dose for procedural sedation is 1 to 2 milligrams per kilogram given slowly, with the effect beginning in about a minute, while the intramuscular dose is 4 to 5 milligrams per kilogram with onset in about five minutes. It raises the heart rate, the blood pressure, and the secretions, and it can cause vomiting and, rarely, spasm of the vocal cords, so oxygen and suction must be ready. These doses follow widespread guidance, and the current protocol of the World Health Organization and of your national service must be checked before use, because pediatric and adult regimens differ.

The vivid dreams and hallucinations of ketamine are what patients remember and the reason some clinics refuse to stock it. They are more common in adults than in young children and are reduced by a quiet, dimly lit room, by keeping stimulation to a minimum, and by a calm voice explaining what is happening. A small dose of a benzodiazepine before the ketamine, such as midazolam 0.05 to 0.1 milligrams per kilogram intravenously, reduces the reactions, though it also deepens the sedation. Ketamine is not for everyone, and a patient with an uncontrolled rise in blood pressure, a recent heart attack, an unstable rhythm, or a severely narrowed aortic valve may not tolerate the surge in heart rate and pressure. A patient with a known psychotic illness may have a frightening emergence, and a patient with a full stomach, a bowel obstruction, or an airway already compromised risks vomiting and aspiration.

Once ketamine is given, the work becomes watching. Stay at the head, keep the airway in view, and watch the chest and the color without interruption, because the reflexes are preserved but not perfect and the tongue can still fall back. Record the pulse, the breathing, the blood pressure, and the oxygen saturation every five minutes, and describe the level of consciousness simply, as whether the patient responds to voice, to touch, or not at all. Vomiting is common, so keep suction ready and turn the patient onto the side as the effect wears off. Do not leave the patient alone until they can hold a conversation, sit up steadily, and drink without choking, and never send someone home alone to walk after a procedure that altered consciousness.

Pain control is not a separate subject from anesthesia; it is the same subject, and the analgesic ladder of the World Health Organization remains the clearest way to organize it. The first step is the non-opioid drugs, and in most remote clinics that means paracetamol, also called acetaminophen, and ibuprofen. Paracetamol for an adult is usually 1 gram by mouth every six hours, with no more than 4 grams in a day, and that ceiling falls in liver disease, severe malnutrition, and heavy alcohol use. For a child the usual dose is 15 milligrams per kilogram by mouth every six hours, measured with a syringe rather than a spoon, because the margin between a safe and a dangerous dose is small. Ibuprofen for an adult is 400 milligrams by mouth every eight hours with food, and it can injure the kidneys of a dehydrated or elderly patient and irritate the stomach, so verify these doses in the current guidance before prescribing.

The second step adds a weak opioid when the first is not enough, and tramadol is the drug most often available. An adult dose is 50 to 100 milligrams by mouth every six hours, up to 400 milligrams in a day, and the dose must be reduced in the elderly and in anyone with kidney or liver disease. Tramadol lowers the seizure threshold, so it is a poor choice for a patient with epilepsy, and it can cause a dangerous reaction with some antidepressants and other drugs that raise serotonin. Constipation, nausea, dizziness, and drowsiness are common, and the nausea is often worse on an empty stomach.

The third step is morphine, the drug most feared and most needed in a place with no surgeon. The fear comes from a story about addiction that does not fit the patient in acute pain, because dependence after a short course is not the disease of addiction, and untreated severe pain is the far commoner harm. The starting dose for an opioid naive adult is 5 to 10 milligrams by mouth every four hours, or 2.5 to 5 milligrams intravenously, with intramuscular and subcutaneous doses in the same range, and for a child about 0.1 to 0.2 milligrams per kilogram intravenously. Watch the breathing rather than the pain score, because respiratory depression is the effect that kills, and it appears as a slowing rate, deep sleep, and small pupils. Naloxone, 0.4 milligrams intravenously in an adult or 0.01 milligrams per kilogram in a child, reverses the breathing depression but also the analgesia, so it is given only when breathing is failing. Verify every dose against the current guidance of the World Health Organization and your national protocol, because opioid regimens are revised and differ between countries.

Morphine is a controlled drug, and the paperwork is part of the treatment. Keep it in a locked cupboard within a locked room, record every ampoule and tablet in a register that shows the balance and the signature, and have a second person witness the discarding of any unused portion, so that no one can be accused and no one can divert. Because supply is fragile, calculate a week of expected use and order ahead, and keep the stock where the temperature will not ruin it. The side effects are predictable: constipation is universal and needs a stimulant laxative, nausea usually passes, sedation settles as the dose is adjusted, and itching responds to an antihistamine. The myth of respiratory depression at correct doses frightens clinicians into giving too little, and the patient in agony who is then given a large rescue dose out of desperation is exactly the situation that causes harm.

There are analgesics that come from a person rather than a pharmacy, and they are not placebos when they are honest. Immobilizing a fracture, elevating a swollen limb, applying cold in the first day and warmth afterward, and positioning the patient so the injured part is supported can remove a surprising share of the pain. Slow breathing guided by your own counting reduces the panic that amplifies pain, and it costs nothing. Explain what will happen, when it will happen, and how long it will last, and never tell a patient that a treatment will not hurt when it will, since the lie destroys the trust the next reassurance needs.

Procedural sedation makes a patient comfortable enough for a painful procedure, and it sits on a continuum that nobody can control precisely. At one end is minimal sedation, in which the patient is calm and awake, and at the other is general anesthesia, in which the patient is unrousable and the airway may need support, and a patient can slip from one to the other with one extra milliliter. That is why the same standards apply to sedation as to anesthesia, with fasting, an airway plan, continuous monitoring, and a dedicated observer. Midazolam is a short-acting benzodiazepine, given intravenously in small increments of 0.05 to 0.1 milligrams per kilogram in an adult, and it produces calm and amnesia with a real risk of oversedation when combined with an opioid. The combination of a benzodiazepine with an opioid is the commonest cause of sedation disasters, so reduce each dose and increase the monitoring when both are needed. Every sedated patient needs defined discharge criteria, usually steady vital signs, a return to the baseline level of consciousness, and a responsible adult to stay with them.

Nitrous oxide is a gas that produces analgesia and light sedation, and where a cylinder and a mixing device exist it is one of the kindest tools in the clinic. A mixture of 50 per cent nitrous oxide and 50 per cent oxygen is safe to breathe for a conscious patient, it begins to work within a minute, and it wears off within minutes after the mask is removed, which suits a dressing change, a suture, or a joint reduction. It is not a general anesthetic at that concentration, but it must never be used without oxygen because the gas alone causes hypoxia. Avoid it in a patient with a collapsed lung, a bowel obstruction, a blocked middle ear, or a head injury with raised pressure, because it expands gas filled spaces, and repeated heavy exposure harms the staff and the patient's vitamin B12. Scavenging, a well sealed mask, and adequate ventilation protect the nurses who use it every day.

The most important skill in this chapter is knowing what not to attempt. Spinal anesthesia, epidural anesthesia, general anesthesia with a breathing tube, deep sedation of a child with a difficult airway, major abdominal surgery, and any procedure that requires paralysis are beyond the scope of an untrained clinician, however confident the moment feels. Regional blocks that approach the spine, the brachial plexus, the neck, or the eye belong to providers with supervised training, and a single weekend course does not create that competence. Saying no is itself a clinical skill: begin with what you can do, because a refusal that offers nothing sounds like indifference, and you can give pain relief, splint the limb, clean the wound, and organize transport. Then say plainly what you cannot do and why, such as the anesthesia for this operation is not available here, so we are sending you where it can be done safely. If the patient refuses transfer, document the conversation, the risks explained, and the refusal, with a witness.

The logistics are what make all of this possible or impossible, and they deserve the same attention as the pharmacology. A sedation kit should hold oxygen with a delivery device that fits an adult and a child, a working suction with a rigid and a soft tip, a bag and mask in several sizes, airways in several sizes, intravenous cannulas and fluids, and a means of keeping the patient warm. The emergency drug box should hold epinephrine, atropine, naloxone, flumazenil, diazepam or midazolam, dextrose, hydrocortisone, and an antihistamine, with the doses written on a card taped inside the lid. Check the cylinder pressure, the suction, and the expiry dates on a fixed day every week and record the check, because the equipment that fails is almost always the equipment nobody looked at.

Complications follow the drugs and the procedures, and each has a first move you should know without thinking. Anaphylaxis appears as sudden low pressure, wheeze, swelling, and a rash, and the treatment is epinephrine 0.5 milligrams of the 1 in 1,000 solution intramuscularly in the outer thigh for an adult, or 0.01 milligrams per kilogram for a child, repeated every five minutes as needed. Spasm of the vocal cords appears as a high pitched sound and then silence after a stimulus, and it is broken by stopping the stimulus, removing the debris, and applying positive pressure with a mask. Aspiration appears as coughing, a falling oxygen saturation, and a wet chest during or after sedation, and it is managed with oxygen, suction, positioning, and transfer. Cardiac arrest here is usually preceded by hypoxia or a drug error, so the first response to a falling saturation is always to stop, look at the airway, and give oxygen. Document every complication, and hold a short debrief with the team, because hidden complications repeat.

Some patients need the plan changed before the first drug is drawn. A child is not a small adult: the doses are per kilogram, the airway is smaller and more easily obstructed, the tongue is proportionally larger, the breathing is easier to depress, and a child loses heat quickly, so warmth and a parent's arms are part of the anesthesia. A pregnant woman in the second half of pregnancy has a full stomach, a compressed vein when she lies flat, and a greater sensitivity to opioid sedation, so she is positioned tilted and monitored closely. An elderly patient with a thin body and a slow liver needs smaller doses given more slowly, and a patient who is severely malnourished has little protein to bind the drug and little reserve to survive its side effects. A patient with human immunodeficiency virus, liver disease, kidney disease, or sickle cell disease may have altered drug handling and a lower tolerance for hypoxia, so check the interactions in the current reference rather than guessing.

Documentation is the memory of the procedure, and in a remote clinic it is also the supervision you do not have. Before the procedure, record the indication, the consent conversation and who gave it, the airway assessment, the fasting time, the baseline vital signs, the weight used for the dose calculation, and the allergies. During it, record every drug with its dose, its route, and its time, and the observations with the same time, so that the chart reads as a story rather than a set of disconnected numbers. After it, record the recovery, the discharge criteria that were met, the analgesia given and its effect, the instructions spoken to the family, and the follow up date, and write the complications in plain words, including the ones that resolved. The nursing role is broader than any technique, because you are also the system that teaches others to count a respiratory rate, to hold a mask, and to call for help without hesitation.

Put the pieces together in a composite patient. A woman of about 70 years arrives after a fall, with a shortened and externally rotated leg and pain that makes every movement a scream, and the nearest hospital with an anesthetist is a five hour drive over a rough road. You assess the airway, confirm she has not eaten for eight hours, place a cannula, and give a fascia iliaca block with the dose calculated from her weight after checking the local anesthetic limit and the current protocol. You splint the leg, give paracetamol and a small dose of morphine with a laxative and an antiemetic, monitor the breathing and blood pressure every fifteen minutes, and write down the times and doses. She sleeps on the journey, arrives stable, and the anesthetist at the hospital knows exactly what she received because your note travels with her. The procedure you did not do, the open reduction, was never yours, and the analgesia you did give made the transfer survivable.

To summarize, anesthesia in a remote setting is a discipline of limits, preparation, and watching rather than a set of heroic improvisations. Assess the airway before you sedate anyone, respect fasting, keep one person whose only job is monitoring, and have oxygen, suction, and a rescue plan ready before the first drug is drawn. Local anesthetics are safest when the maximum dose of lidocaine, about 4.5 milligrams per kilogram plain and about 7 milligrams per kilogram with epinephrine, is calculated with care, and toxicity is recognized by the metallic taste, the tingling lips, and the ringing ears and treated by stopping, oxygenating, controlling the seizure, and using lipid emulsion where it exists. Digital, ring, and fascia iliaca blocks extend safe analgesia to the finger, the toe, and the broken hip, while spinal, epidural, and general anesthesia remain the work of trained providers. Ketamine is the workhorse of remote sedation, with its preserved reflexes, its doses by weight, its emergence reactions that quiet and darkness reduce, and its short list of patients who should not receive it. The analgesic ladder of paracetamol, ibuprofen, tramadol, and morphine gives effective relief when the doses are calculated and the fear of morphine replaced by careful monitoring. Know what you can do, learn it thoroughly, and say no clearly and kindly to what you cannot, because honesty about limits is the foundation of safe sedation.
