# Chapter 39 — Research and Evidence Appraisal

A nurse practitioner in a district hospital holds a plastic cassette that has just turned positive in front of a child with fever. By the end of the consultation she will have made three decisions that no protocol can make entirely on her behalf. How much does she believe the cassette, given how it was stored and what she knows about malaria in her district this month? Does the study she read last year apply to this child, who is malnourished and possibly infected with something the study excluded? Is the guideline in front of her answering the question she is asking, or a different question that resembles it? Every one of those decisions is evidence appraisal, and evidence is not something that arrives finished. It is something a clinician finishes, in a particular place, for a particular patient.

Two instincts pull in opposite directions, and both have to be resisted. One says that guidelines exist for a reason and that a nurse who improvises causes harm, and that instinct is largely correct. The other says that the medicine practiced in high-income teaching hospitals has little to do with a clinic that has no reliable refrigerator and no night staff, and that instinct is also largely correct. A guideline is not the opposite of evidence; it is evidence that has already been interpreted, weighed against cost and feasibility, and written down by people who had a particular health system in mind. To follow it well, you have to understand what went into it, and to depart from it well, you have to be able to say why.

There is a small set of questions that can be asked of any piece of evidence, whether it concerns a drug, a diagnostic test, a staffing model or a bed net distribution. Was the comparison fair, was the measurement trustworthy, and does the result apply to the patient in front of me? Those three questions, asked without jargon, will get you most of the way through most of what you will read. The vocabulary changes with the design and the arithmetic changes with the outcome, but the underlying demand does not change. You are asking whether the answer you are being offered was produced in a way that earns your confidence for this person, in this place, today.

Begin with the designs, because the design tells you what question a study was even capable of answering. A case report or a case series describes one patient or a handful of patients whom a clinician found unusual or alarming, and it is often the first report of a new disease, a new complication or a new adverse effect. But it cannot tell you how common a problem is, cannot tell you whether an exposure caused an outcome, and cannot compare one treatment with another, because nobody was chosen to be a comparison. The cross-sectional survey measures exposure and outcome at the same moment across a defined population, so it can tell you how many people in a village have trachoma, how many households own an insecticide-treated bed net, or how many health workers can state the first-line drug correctly. What it cannot tell you is which came first, so it is a picture rather than a story.

The case-control study works backward, and its efficiency is its genius. You identify people who already have the outcome, find comparable people who do not, and compare what each group was exposed to in the past, which is how you study a rare disease without following a hundred thousand people for a decade. Its great weakness is that both exposure and outcome have already happened when the study begins, so the investigator is asking people to remember, and memory is unreliable and unequal between those who are ill and those who are not. The cohort study moves forward instead, following groups that differ in their exposure and counting what happens in each, so it can establish that the exposure came before the outcome and can measure how often the outcome occurs. Cohorts are honest about time but expensive, and they leak participants along the way, usually the ones who moved, died or became too ill to return.

The randomized trial is the strongest individual design for the narrow question of whether one treatment works better than another. Random assignment does not guarantee that the groups are balanced in every respect, but it leaves the way they differ to chance rather than to the judgment of the person doing the assigning. Blinding is the companion protection, because a patient who knows which treatment they received will report differently, and a nurse who knows will assess differently, even with the best intentions in the world. Even randomization cannot rescue a trial in which many participants were lost, in which the groups were compared on the wrong outcome, or in which the analysis quietly excluded the people who did badly, because the design is a ceiling on quality rather than a substitute for it.

Two designs sit above the individual study rather than inside it. A systematic review asks a single answerable question, searches for every study that has addressed it according to criteria written down in advance, assesses each study for bias, and describes what the whole body of work says. A meta-analysis goes one step further and pools the results numerically, which increases precision and can reveal a consistent small effect that no single trial was large enough to demonstrate. The power of these methods is real, and guidelines rest heavily on them, but their authority is borrowed, because a review of ten biased studies produces a confident and precise summary of ten biased studies. Publication bias is a particular hazard in tropical medicine, where negative results from low-resource settings travel poorly, so a review conducted to a published protocol with a search strategy you can inspect is worth more than one that does not say how it was done.

Now the concepts that explain most of the errors, beginning with bias. Bias is not the same as error, because random error is the wobble you get from chance and it shrinks as a study grows, while bias is a systematic distortion that gets worse rather than better as you add participants. Selection bias is who gets into the study in the first place, so a tuberculosis survey that recruits only people who reach a clinic has described the health-seeking behavior of the patients who could travel. Bias cannot usually be repaired by statistics after the fact, so it has to be prevented in the design and in the field.

Confounding is the idea that most often fools careful people. A confounder is a factor that is associated with the exposure and independently associated with the outcome, so it creates a link that looks causal and is not, as when poverty raises the risk of malnutrition and independently raises the risk of infection, so that a study finds the two associated and calls the association causal. A confounder can be controlled for if it has been measured and recorded, but every adjustment is only as good as the variable you adjusted with, and unmeasured confounders survive every technique. Effect modification is different, and the difference is worth holding on to, because it is the concept most often confused with confounding. A confounder distorts the truth, while an effect modifier is part of the truth, because it means the intervention genuinely works differently in different groups. A vaccine that protects children well and adults poorly is asking you to change who you vaccinate, not asking you to adjust the numbers.

Sensitivity and specificity are the properties of a test, and predictive value is what the patient in front of you actually needs to know. Sensitivity is the proportion of people who truly have the disease and are correctly identified by the test, so a highly sensitive test rarely misses disease when it is there, and specificity is the proportion of people who truly do not have the disease and are correctly identified as negative. Predictive value reverses the direction of the question, because it asks what the probability of disease is when the test is positive, and that probability depends on how common the disease is in the population being tested. Nowhere is that dependence more consequential than in tropical medicine, where a test that looks excellent in one district can mislead in the next.

Consider what happens when prevalence falls, because this is the arithmetic that most often goes wrong at the bedside. Suppose 100 people are tested and 10 of them truly have the disease, suppose the test detects 9 of those 10, and suppose that among the 90 people without disease the test is falsely positive in 14 of them. There are then 23 positive results, but only 9 belong to someone with the disease, so barely more than a third of the positives are true positives, even though the test looks excellent on paper. That is why a positive test in a person with no symptoms can lead to treatment for a disease they do not have and to the neglect of the disease they do have. As transmission declines and prevalence falls, the same test performs worse in exactly the same populations, so a positive result is a statement about probability rather than a verdict.

Treatment effects are usually reported in two ways, and the difference between them changes how you counsel a patient. A relative reduction describes how much the risk fell compared with the untreated group, while an absolute reduction describes how many actual people were spared the outcome. A drug that reduces the risk of a complication by one half sounds dramatic until you learn that the risk was 2 per cent without treatment and 1 per cent with it, so the absolute reduction is 1 per cent and 100 patients must be treated for one to benefit. That number, the reciprocal of the absolute reduction, is the number needed to treat, and it is a far better guide to a decision than the relative figure, because it can be set against the number needed to harm, the cost and the adverse effects of the same intervention.

Where clinical management appears in this chapter, the same rule that governs every other chapter governs here. Current guidance from the World Health Organization recommends intravenous artesunate at 2.4 milligrams per kilogram at the start of treatment, again at 12 hours and again at 24 hours, and then once a day until the patient can swallow, followed by a full course of artemisinin-based combination therapy by mouth. Those figures are the ones in the guideline at the time of writing, and they are not the point, because every number in a guideline carries the assumptions of the trials behind it, and those assumptions were made about particular patients in particular places with particular capacity. Regimens are revised, guidance differs between countries, and the current national guideline must be checked before prescribing. A nurse who can say where a dose came from, and what it was demonstrated to do, and what was not studied, is far safer than one who can recite it without that context, and monitoring is part of appraisal, because after artesunate delayed anemia from the destruction of red blood cells can appear a week or two after recovery.

The largest single problem in tropical evidence appraisal is not statistical but geographical. Most of the world's clinical research is conducted in high-income countries, on patients who differ from yours in nutrition, in co-infection, in genetics, in age structure and in what happens to them after they leave the clinic. A treatment tested in a well-nourished, well-monitored population with reliable laboratory support may behave differently where anemia is nearly universal and where a follow-up appointment competes with a harvest. The health system around the intervention matters as much as the intervention, because an effective drug that requires refrigeration, electricity, a functioning supply chain, a nurse who can give it and a patient who can return will not deliver its trial result where any of those links is missing. And the outcome that was measured may not be the outcome that matters, because a nurse who translates a laboratory value into a promise of survival, function or comfort has gone further than the data.

None of this means that evidence from elsewhere should be discarded, and the honest position is more interesting than either extreme. Mechanisms travel, because the way a drug kills a parasite, the way a mosquito finds a host and the way dehydration kills a child are biology, and they do not change at a border. Pathophysiological reasoning travels in the same way, which is why the same rehydration principles hold from a hospital in Europe to a village in the Sahel. Safety signals also travel more reliably than benefits, because harm that is real tends to appear wherever the drug is used, while benefit is vulnerable to the patients, the adherence, the dose and the system. The skill is to say which parts of a finding are universal and which are local, and then to be explicit about the assumptions you are making when you borrow.

Reading a guideline critically begins with the recognition that a guideline is not a summary of evidence alone but a set of judgments built on top of evidence. Ask who wrote it, when it was last revised, and what conflicts of interest were declared, because a guideline that has not changed in 15 years while its subject has changed repeatedly is a warning sign. The grading systems that most guidelines now use separate the certainty of the evidence from the strength of the recommendation, so it becomes possible to say that the evidence is weak and the recommendation is strong, or that the evidence is moderate and the recommendation is conditional. A strong recommendation resting on weak evidence is not automatically a failure, because it usually means that those who wrote it judged the values at stake, or the practical constraints, to be more important than the statistical uncertainty, and a recommendation is a promise about the balance of benefits and harms under stated conditions rather than a promise that a patient will do well.

National adaptation adds another layer, and the differences between international and local guidance are usually deliberate rather than careless. A country adjusts a recommendation for the drugs it can actually procure, for the resistance patterns in its own parasites and bacteria, for what its nurses are permitted by law to prescribe, and for what its budget can sustain. So the first-line drug in one country may differ from the first-line drug in its neighbor, and both may differ from the international recommendation, and all three may be defensible. The practical question is always whether the patient in front of you matches the population the recommendation was built for, because if she does not, the thoughtful response is not to abandon the guideline but to say why it may not fit and to write that reasoning in the record. Guidelines are also written for a system that may not be the system you work in, which means a recommendation to investigate before treating can be impossible to follow in a place with no laboratory, and the safe adaptation has to be reasoned aloud rather than improvised in silence.

Implementation science exists because the distance between what works in a trial and what works in a clinic is a research problem in its own right. An intervention with excellent trial results can fail completely at scale, and the failure is rarely a mystery once it is examined. A vaccine may sit in a cold chain that breaks, and a new protocol may be taught once and then forgotten. Implementation science studies these breakdowns systematically rather than blaming the frontline worker for them, and it asks about barriers and enablers at the level of the patient, the family, the clinician, the team, the clinic, the supply system and the policy environment. For the advanced practice nurse the practical implication is that you should evaluate the delivery of a program and not only its outcome, because an outcome that was never delivered is not evidence of a failed idea.

Operational research is the kind that a nurse can actually run in her own setting, and it is best kept small, specific and answerable. The question should come from a problem you can see, such as whether a checklist reduces missed doses on a ward, whether a short message to a phone improves return for a follow-up appointment, whether a different dressing lowers infection, or whether the time from arrival to treatment changes when the triage nurse is given a new role. The design should be one that your clinic can sustain, which usually means a before-and-after comparison, a comparison between two similar clinics, or a simple survey rather than a randomized trial. Collect as few variables as will answer the question, and decide in advance how the data will be recorded and analyzed, because a study that begins collecting and then asks what to do with the numbers usually produces a drawer of paper rather than a finding. Sample size does matter, and small numbers produce uncertain answers, so a study of 20 patients can suggest a difference that a study of 200 would erase. The nurse should also decide in advance what she will do with each possible result, and she should ask early whether the project needs ethical review, because work that tests a change in local care with aggregate data may be quality improvement, while collecting information from people to produce generalizable knowledge is research with human participants.

Research ethics in tropical settings begins from a history that a nurse should know and should not flinch from, because the syphilis study in Tuskegee, the deliberate infection experiments in Guatemala and the trials of the colonial era have left a legitimate suspicion that has to be earned back rather than dismissed. The populations that tropical research most often approaches are precisely the ones whose vulnerability makes ethical protection necessary, because they are ill, poor, dependent on a health system they cannot easily challenge, and often unable to read the consent document placed in front of them. They may be children, or women in households where a husband or an elder speaks for them, or people whose language the consent form does not use well. Vulnerability of that kind does not mean that research should not happen, because the diseases in question will not be solved without it, but it does mean that participation must be genuinely free and genuinely informed, and that the burden of making it so falls on the investigator rather than the participant.

Consent is the center of that work, and it is a process rather than a signature. Explaining the purpose, the procedures, the risks, the possible benefits, the alternatives and the right to withdraw at any time without losing care is not a formality to be hurried through at the end of a clinic day. In a low-literacy setting the conversation may need to be read aloud, translated, witnessed and documented with a thumbprint, and the nurse should be sure the participant understood rather than merely agreed. Free consent also has to be free in a practical sense, which means that an offer of care withheld from those who decline is not a choice but a coercion, and a token payment large enough to change a household's decisions is a pressure rather than a thank you. Community permission is a separate matter and does not replace individual consent, because a chief or a committee can open the door, but the person who takes part still has to decide for themselves. Children require the permission of a parent or guardian together with the child's own agreement as far as the child can give it, and consent requirements are revised between countries, so the current national guideline must be consulted before enrolling anyone.

Community engagement and benefit sharing are the parts of ethics that determine whether research leaves anything behind. Engagement means meeting the community before recruitment begins, listening to its leaders, its health workers, its teachers and its women's groups, and asking what questions they would want answered rather than announcing what will be studied. It means explaining the study in the language people actually use, answering suspicion directly, and being present in the community for the whole duration rather than only during enrollment. Benefit sharing means that the community receives something real and predictable, whether that is training for its health workers, medicine where it is ethical to provide it, equipment that will outlast the project, or results that are reported back honestly and in time to matter. Ancillary care is part of this, because research inevitably uncovers problems it did not set out to find, and a plan for what happens to a participant found to have severe anemia, or high blood pressure, or tuberculosis should be written before enrollment rather than improvised when the result arrives.

Ethics committees are the formal expression of this responsibility, and they exist at the institutional, the national and sometimes the regional level, and any research involving human participants is expected to pass review, including studies run by students, studies that use records rather than people, and studies that a nurse considers obviously harmless. The committee exists to be a check on the enthusiasm of the investigator, which is exactly the quality that makes good research possible and that also makes it easy to overlook the interests of the people being studied. One of the hardest questions such a committee has to answer is what the control group receives, because if you are testing a new treatment in a country where the internationally recommended standard of care is not available, the comparison group might receive what the local standard is, or what the global standard recommends, and the two answers are ethically very far apart. That question has no comfortable resolution in every case, and its difficulty is the reason the discussion belongs at the design stage, in public, with people who are not invested in the answer.

The nurse's place in research is more central than the typical description admits, and it begins with recruitment. The person who explains the study and approaches the family is usually a nurse, and that position carries a specific hazard, because a nurse who also provides care can make a refusal feel like a rejection of that care. The nurse is also the person who notices when enrollment is drifting toward the easiest patients and away from the ones the study was meant to help, which is a form of selection bias created in the clinic rather than in the protocol. Protecting participants means protecting their privacy as well, which in a small community may mean being careful about what is said aloud, how names are stored and who has access to the completed forms, and it means monitoring, because recognizing an adverse effect, recording it properly and reporting it within the required time is an obligation rather than a decision.

Data quality is a clinical responsibility, and this chapter would be dishonest if it treated it as clerical. A study is only as good as its forms, and the errors that ruin data are ordinary and recognizable: a missing weight, a date written in the wrong format, a value recorded without its unit, an identifier copied incorrectly from one page to another. Consistency matters as much as accuracy, because if three nurses measure a mid-upper arm circumference in three slightly different positions, the study will report a difference between nurses as though it were a difference between children. Good practice is unglamorous and takes only a little time: training the team together on the measurements before the study begins, checking the equipment against a known standard, recording the unit every time, filling the form completely before the participant leaves, and reviewing a sample of completed forms early enough to correct a systematic mistake while it can still be corrected. The nurse is usually the only person positioned to notice that a form is being filled in wrongly in real time, which makes her the last line of defense for the integrity of the whole study.

Data also carry duties beyond their accuracy. Confidentiality means that a completed form is a person, and it must live in a locked place, with coded identifiers where possible, and with access limited to those who need it. It means that a dataset shared with a collaborator abroad should not carry names, and that a photograph or a voice recording requires its own explicit agreement, because these are not the same as a signature on a clinical consent form. The nurse should also know what her country requires her to report, because some findings, such as a diagnosis of a notifiable infection, must go to the surveillance system regardless of what the study protocol says, and a researcher cannot promise more confidentiality than the law allows. These are the details that make a study trustworthy to the people who take part in it, and they also determine whether that community will consider taking part in the next study, when a different disease needs an answer.

To summarize, evidence appraisal is the ordinary clinical skill of asking whether a comparison was fair, whether the measurement was trustworthy, and whether the result applies to the patient in front of you, which means matching the study design to the question rather than to its reputation. Bias tilts a result and cannot be averaged away, confounding creates a false link that must be measured to be removed, and effect modification is a finding rather than a nuisance because it tells you for whom the intervention works. Sensitivity and specificity describe a test, while predictive value describes a patient, and predictive value changes as prevalence changes, so the same positive result can mean very different things in two clinics on the same day. Evidence generated elsewhere travels for mechanisms and for harms but only conditionally for benefits, so borrowing a result means stating your assumptions out loud. A guideline is evidence plus judgment, and reading it critically means finding out which is which and what the recommendation actually promises. Ethics is the framework that makes the work worth doing, and it lives in the consent conversation, in the accuracy of a form and in the honest return of a result. Regimens are revised, guidance differs between countries, and the current national guideline must be checked before prescribing, and the same discipline that makes you check a dose is the discipline that makes you ask where it came from. Hold all of it together and you will not merely follow the evidence; you will finish it, for the patient in front of you.
