# Chapter 65 — Knowing Your Limits

A thermometer is trusted because it converts a bodily change into a number that anyone can read, and the recognition of your own limit deserves the same status. A limit is a reading you take before you act rather than after, and it tells you where your training, your experience and your equipment stop being sufficient for the patient in front of you. Like the thermometer, it is a clinical instrument, and like the thermometer it is only useful when it is used honestly. The strange thing is that most clinicians reach for the thermometer before every consultation and almost never reach for the limit until something has gone wrong. The reasons are understandable, because training rewards action, patients arrive whether or not you are ready, and in a remote post there may be no senior colleague to impose the pause that a hospital would impose. A limit is also harder to see than a fever, since nothing on the outside marks a clinician who is about to attempt something beyond their reach. This chapter is about learning to take that reading, and about what to do with the answer.

Begin with the competence boundary, and accept that it is a real line rather than a feeling of nervousness. Everything that a nurse can do clinically falls into three zones. In the first zone are the skills you were trained in, have performed many times, and could now perform while tired, at night, with a frightened family watching. In the second zone are the skills you were trained in but have practiced rarely, or practiced long ago, or practiced only on a model. In the third zone are the skills you were never trained in at all, however well you understand the theory. The second zone is the dangerous one, because it feels like competence from the inside and looks like competence from the outside. A nurse who knows intellectually how to reduce a dislocated shoulder, deliver a breech baby, or place a chest drain may be convinced of their ability and still lack the repetition that makes the hands reliable. The boundary is not a judgement of character; it is a description of the evidence.

The distinction that matters most is between knowledge and volume. A manual can teach you the sequence of a procedure in an afternoon, and a workshop can let you perform it once on a model, and neither of those converts into the ability to manage the same procedure when it bleeds, when the anatomy is unusual, or when the patient stops breathing. Competence is built by repetition with feedback, because hands learn through correction rather than through explanation. A skill practiced twenty times under the eye of someone more experienced is a different thing from a skill practiced twice without anyone watching. This is why the honest question is not whether you know how to do something but how many times you have done it, how recently, with what outcomes, and with whom available should it go wrong. The answer to that question is the boundary, stated plainly.

A practical way to take the reading is to ask three questions before any significant act. Have I been trained and supervised in this, and can I name the person who trained me? Have I performed it often enough and recently enough that my hands know it without my having to reconstruct the steps? If it goes wrong, do I have the drugs, the blood, the oxygen, the equipment and the second pair of hands needed to rescue the patient? If any answer is no, the plan changes, and it changes before the first incision, the first dose, or the first attempt. These questions take perhaps thirty seconds, and they cost nothing when the answer is yes. Their whole value lies in being asked before the act rather than being remembered after it. The nurse who asks them habitually is not timid; the nurse who asks them habitually is the one who is safe to work with.

Beware the single good outcome, because it teaches a false lesson more effectively than failure does. A difficult delivery that ended well, a wound that healed after a closure you improvised, or an airway that opened after an attempt you were not trained for will all feel like proof of ability. Yet one survival may reflect the patient's youth, a generous anatomy, or simple luck, and none of those can be scheduled for the next patient. The right question after such an event is not whether you succeeded but whether you would be willing to repeat the same decision in the same conditions with the same resources. If the answer is that you would not, then the first outcome was not a competence, it was an exception. Recording the exception honestly is how a clinician keeps a boundary from drifting outward on the strength of a coincidence.

The boundary is not supposed to stay where it is, and a clinician who never expands it is not being careful but stagnant. Growth, however, must be deliberate and documented rather than accidental. The pattern that works begins with naming the specific skill, then obtaining training from someone qualified to give it, then performing it repeatedly under direct supervision, then having that supervision attest to the number and the quality of the performances, and only then beginning independent practice. Each stage has a gate, and the gate is passed by evidence rather than by enthusiasm. The supervisor should be named in advance, and their willingness to supervise should be confirmed rather than assumed. An expansion that happens because nobody was watching is not growth; it is drift, and drift is the mechanism by which most overreach occurs.

Keep a written log, because memory is generous and records are not. A log for clinical procedures needs only a few columns: the date, the procedure, the indication, whether it was performed under supervision, the immediate outcome, any complication, and the name of the supervisor or the person consulted. The log serves three purposes at once. It tells you honestly how much of a skill you actually have, it shows a supervisor or a licensing body what you have done, and it reveals the pattern of your complications before that pattern becomes a reputation. Review it at fixed intervals, perhaps every three months, and look for the skills that are decaying as well as the skills that are growing. A log kept only when a procedure goes well is a marketing document rather than a record.

Where case volume is low, the boundary shrinks unless something is done about it, and simulation and drills are how a remote team fights that decay. A skill used twice a year will not be available on the night it is needed, however well it was learned. Run drills for the emergencies that would otherwise be performed by someone out of practice, including the postpartum hemorrhage, the neonatal resuscitation, the anaphylaxis, the convulsing child, and the blocked airway. Run them with the actual equipment, in the actual room, with the actual team, and rotate the roles so that everyone has held each one. The organization employing you has a duty to fund refresher training, and the nurse has a duty to ask for it in writing rather than to hope it appears. Maintenance of competence is not a personal hobby; it is part of the job description that nobody writes down.

There is a well described trap in which the least experienced clinician is the most confident, because confidence is built from the inside while competence is visible only from the outside. This is why self-assessment alone can never be the whole answer, and why a colleague who watches you work is worth more than any amount of private reflection. Invite direct observation and ask for specific feedback rather than general reassurance, asking not whether the procedure went well but whether the steps were in the right order, what was slow, and what would have been done differently. Where video or a checklist is available, use it, because a recording shows what memory edits out. The clinician who can hear criticism without defending against it is the clinician who keeps learning after the formal training ends. Defensiveness is the enemy of the boundary, because it blinds the one person who most needs to see.

The boundary is also written into law and into institutional rules, and a nurse working remotely needs to know both. Scope of practice, standing orders, delegation arrangements and the protocols of a task-sharing program together define what you may do and under whose authority. The World Health Organization has published guidance on task sharing for maternal and newborn health and for other services, and many ministries of health have adapted that guidance into national documents, but the arrangements differ substantially between countries and are revised as policy changes. Before you rely on any authorization to perform a procedure, verify the current national scope of practice and the current program protocol rather than trusting what you were told at orientation. A skill you possess but are not authorized to use is still beyond your limit, and the fact that you could do something is never a legal or ethical justification for doing it.

The situation that exceeds the individual may exceed the clinic as well, and the two are not the same problem. A patient may present with a condition you personally cannot manage but that the clinic is equipped and staffed to manage, in which case the answer is to find or call the right colleague. Alternatively, the patient may present with a condition that is beyond the post itself, such as an obstructed labor requiring cesarean section, a ruptured ectopic pregnancy, a penetrating abdominal wound, a child needing ventilation, a patient needing dialysis, or a hemorrhage requiring blood that does not exist within a day's drive. In that second situation there is no internal answer, and the clinical task becomes stabilization, honest communication, and transfer or palliation. Confusing the two situations produces two opposite errors, which are abandoning a patient the clinic could have treated and attempting a rescue the clinic was never able to provide.

Saying that something is beyond you is a clinical act, and it must be said out loud at the time rather than explained afterward. The sentence is short and it needs to be practiced: I am not able to do this safely here, and we need to arrange the next step together. Say it to the team first, so that the plan can change while there is still time to change it, and then say it to the patient and the family in the language they use. What makes that sentence possible is not courage alone but a team culture in which the person who names the limit is respected rather than overruled. If the culture punishes the acknowledgement, the limit will be discovered later by a complication instead. The nurse who speaks early protects the patient and also protects the colleague who would otherwise be pulled into an attempt they did not choose.

Overreach is the failure mode this chapter exists to prevent, and its first error is almost never technical. The hands fail second; the decision fails first. The classic scene is a clinician alone, confronted by a patient who will certainly die without an operation or a procedure, deciding that the only humane choice is to attempt it even though the training, the assistants, the anesthesia, the blood and the postoperative care are all absent. That decision feels like courage in the moment, and it is usually described as courage afterward, especially if the patient survives. But the reasoning is flawed, because the alternative to an attempt is not always death; it is often transfer, temporizing, or a dignified death in the company of family rather than an agonizing death on an improvised table. The overreach that kills is not a single reckless act but a habit of mind that treats willingness as a substitute for capability.

The line between brave and reckless can be stated fairly precisely. Bravery accepts a risk that is known, proportionate to the benefit sought, and mitigated as far as the circumstance allows. Recklessness accepts a risk that is unknown, disproportionate to the likely benefit, or unmitigated because nobody paused to count what was missing. A nurse who performs a procedure within their competence to save a bleeding patient in a clinic with the right drugs at hand is brave, while a nurse who performs the same procedure for the first time, alone, at night, without the drugs to manage the complications, is reckless, even though the two look similar from the doorway. The distinguishing test is not the intensity of the desire to help but the quality of the preparation and the honesty of the assessment. Courage without an inventory is simply a wager with someone else's life.

Certain procedures have such a narrow margin that partial training is more dangerous than none, and they deserve to be named so that the boundary becomes concrete. Cesarean section, laparotomy for a ruptured viscus, craniotomy, open reduction of a fracture, spinal anesthesia, and definitive airway management in a child all require a team, and they require resources that a remote post rarely has, including oxygen, suction, blood, a functioning anesthesia supply, sterile instruments, and a person whose only task is to watch the patient. Being able to start such a procedure is not the same as being able to finish it, and the hardest part of most of them is not the first minutes but the recognition and management of what follows. Where these procedures are genuinely required, the correct intervention is usually pre-referral stabilization and urgent transport rather than improvisation. The nurse who declines to open an abdomen in a clinic with no blood bank has not failed the patient; the nurse has understood the patient's actual chances.

Competence decays with time and with the conditions of practice, which means the boundary must be re-read rather than remembered. A skill performed regularly in a well-lit clinic with help available is not the same skill when performed at night after a long shift with one assistant and a torch. Fatigue, hunger, illness and distraction all lower the ceiling, and the honest clinician adjusts the limit for the day rather than pretending that a stable limit exists. Illness in the clinician deserves the same seriousness as illness in the patient, because a nurse with a fever and a headache is not making good decisions. These are not excuses offered in advance; they are variables that belong in the assessment. The boundary is therefore not a fixed property of a person but a reading that changes with the day, the patient and the support available.

The limit is also a logistics problem, and this is the part of the assessment that clinicians most often neglect. A drug you can prescribe but cannot monitor is a drug you cannot safely give, because the adverse effects that require a blood count, an electrocardiogram, a potassium measurement or a glucose measurement may be undetectable where no laboratory exists. A procedure you can perform but cannot rescue is a procedure whose complications will become the patient's cause of death, including the pneumothorax you cannot drain, the hemorrhage you cannot replace, the respiratory depression you cannot reverse, and the anaphylaxis whose second dose of epinephrine has expired. Oxygen, blood, suture, sterile gloves, local anesthetic, reversal agents and a functioning light source are all part of the definition of what you are able to do. Before adding a capability, count the consumables that capability consumes, and count them honestly rather than by memory. A limit is often set not by skill but by the shelf.

When you decide that a patient must stay because transfer is impossible, monitoring becomes the substitute for capability, and it must be planned with the same care as a treatment. Write down what will be measured, how often it will be measured, who will measure it, and what number triggers the next action. For a patient receiving magnesium sulfate for eclampsia, for example, World Health Organization guidance describes a loading dose of 4 grams given intravenously over five to twenty minutes followed by an infusion of 1 gram every hour, with monitoring of the deep tendon reflexes, the respiratory rate and the urine output, because magnesium toxicity announces itself first through lost reflexes and then through respiratory depression. Regimens for eclampsia and for every other condition are revised as evidence accumulates, guidance differs between countries, and the current national or World Health Organization guideline must be checked before you prescribe or infuse anything named in this chapter. Monitoring is not a lesser form of treatment; in a remote setting it is the form of treatment that keeps the patient alive until help can come.

Every intervention you decide to perform carries adverse effects, and the duty to anticipate them belongs to the person performing the intervention. Before the act, say aloud what the three most likely complications are and what you will do when each appears. Lay out the rescue equipment before you start rather than after the patient deteriorates, because a search for a syringe in a crisis is a delay measured in brain tissue. Name the person responsible for watching the patient while your hands are busy, since a clinician whose eyes are inside a wound cannot also be watching the chest rise. Know your stop rules in advance, meaning the number of attempts after which you will stop, the amount of blood loss after which you will stop, and the degree of agitation after which you will stop. A complication recognized in its first minute is a clinical problem; the same complication recognized in its fifth minute is often a death.

Some rescue knowledge is specific enough that it should be present before the procedure that might require it. Local anesthetic systemic toxicity, for instance, presents with agitation, ringing in the ears, a metallic taste, seizures and then cardiac arrest, and its treatment includes stopping the injection, supporting the airway, giving oxygen, controlling seizures, and administering an intravenous lipid emulsion when it is available. Procedural sedation with ketamine can produce emergence reactions and, less commonly, apnea, which means that the drug should not be given by a nurse who has no way to support ventilation. Opioids given for pain can cause respiratory depression, and naloxone must be at hand if morphine is being used. Each of these is a concrete example of a general rule: the drug and its antidote, and the procedure and its rescue, belong together or not at all. Verify the current dosing and monitoring recommendations for any of these drugs before you use them, because both change.

You are also an object of monitoring, and this is the part of knowing your limits that nurses most often skip. Watch your own sleep, your own appetite, your own alcohol use, your own irritability, and your own physical symptoms, because each of them changes the ceiling on what you can safely do. A nurse who has not slept for two nights should not be the person making an operative decision, and a nurse with a fever in a malarial area should be a patient before being a clinician. Write down your own limits for the day at the start of the day, for example no procedures after eighteen consecutive hours awake, no independent sedation when alone in the building, and no long journeys after a night call. These rules sound rigid until the first time they prevent a catastrophe. Asking a colleague to cover, or to stand beside you, is a clinical intervention with a measurable benefit.

Backup is a component of the plan rather than a hope, and it must be built before it is needed. Identify by name the colleague you will call when a case exceeds you, and the second name for when the first is unreachable, and the third for when the telephone network is down. Write the numbers on the wall of the clinic and in the front of the register, not only in your own telephone, because the person who needs them may be the one on duty after you. Agree in advance what information you will transmit, in what order, and what you will have prepared before the call begins. Test the chain at intervals by making a call that is not an emergency, so that you learn the delays, the wrong numbers and the times of day when nobody answers. A backup that has never been tested is a story the team tells itself.

The chain needs redundancy, because a single contact is a single point of failure. The ladder usually runs from the nearest experienced colleague, to the district or referral hospital, to the regional specialist center, to a national or international telemedicine service that accepts images and messages. Each rung answers a different question, since the first may know the patient and the context, the second may accept a transfer, the third may advise on a rare diagnosis, and the fourth may provide an opinion when no one closer is available. Learn the hours of each service, the language in which it works, and the format its clinicians need, because advice arrives faster when the request is well formed. Where the internet is intermittent, use store-and-forward messaging with a clear photograph, a short history and a specific question, and expect the answer to come hours later rather than immediately. The purpose of the chain is not to make you omniscient; it is to make sure that being at your limit never means being alone.

A protocol is a limit made portable, which is why protocol-based practice and knowing your limits are the same discipline seen from two angles. A protocol carries the accumulated experience of people who saw many more cases than you will, and it tells you where to stop, what to check and when to escalate. But a protocol can only help if you follow it, and it can only be followed if it exists in the language and the format your team actually uses. When you deviate, and there are legitimate reasons to deviate, write down the reason, the alternative you chose, and who was consulted, because an undocumented deviation is indistinguishable from an error. When a protocol is wrong for your setting, adapt it openly with a named person accountable for the adaptation rather than ignoring it quietly. The protocol is not a substitute for judgement, but it is a far better base for judgement than memory under pressure.

Peer support is the mechanism by which limits are shared rather than carried alone, and it works best when it is routine rather than exceptional. Establish a regular case discussion in which difficult cases are presented, including the ones that went badly, and protect the time for it even when the clinic is busy. A mistake reviewed without blame is not a mistake excused; it is a mistake converted into knowledge that the whole team can use. The person presenting should be the person involved, and the discussion should move from what happened, to what the system allowed, to what will change. Confidential incident reporting that carries no punishment for honesty catches the near misses that never reached a patient, and near misses are the cheapest lessons available. If the only time a complication is discussed is when someone is being disciplined, nobody will ever report the next one.

When you have crossed your own limit, the response has a sequence, and the order matters. First, secure the patient, because the immediate clinical problem outranks your own discomfort. Then disclose to the patient and the family what happened, in plain language and without excuses, and answer their questions as fully as you can. Then write the record while it is fresh, including the times, the observations, the actions, the consultations and the outcome, because a record written a week later is worth much less. Then report the event to your supervisor and, where required, to the organization and the authorities, even when the patient came to no harm. Then take part in the review and accept the changes it produces, because the point of the exercise is to protect the next patient. Concealment is the one response that converts a single clinical event into a permanent professional one.

Humility is not a mood but a daily practice, and it consists largely of asking one question before acting. What am I missing, and who would know? The question is short enough to ask before every significant decision and specific enough to change the answer. Build pauses into the work where the question can actually be asked, including a brief before a procedure, a moment before a discharge, and a check before a transfer. Ask it of the least senior person in the room as well as the most senior, because the person holding the torch or mixing the drug often sees what the operator does not. The clinician who asks the question out loud teaches everyone present to ask it too, and that teaching may matter more than the answer received. Humility is not the absence of confidence; it is confidence that can tolerate being corrected.

Your limits should be written down where the whole team and the community can see them, because an unspoken limit gets tested by every emergency that arrives. Post the list of services the clinic provides, the services it does not provide, the days and hours it can provide them, and the arrangements for what it cannot. Then explain the same list to the community leaders, the traditional healers and the community health workers, so that the expectation matches the reality before a crisis rather than during one. Saying no is only acceptable when it is accompanied by an alternative, and the alternative is usually a plan describing what will be done here, what will be done there, who will decide, and how the patient will travel. Refusing to attempt something beyond the clinic is not the same as abandoning the patient, and the difference is visible in whether a plan exists. A clinic that says no clearly and stays involved keeps the community's trust; a clinic that says yes and fails loses it.

The transfer itself has a logistics and a documentation that determine whether the limit became a death. Stabilize before departure, because the most dangerous hour of any journey is the first, and a patient who is deteriorating in the clinic will almost certainly deteriorate faster on the road. Treat the reversible threats first, including the hypoglycemia, the dehydration, the seizures, the fever, the pain, and the hemorrhage that can be controlled with pressure. In severe malaria in a child, for example, the World Health Organization recommends a pre-referral dose of rectal artesunate to buy time for the journey, and the exact formulation, dose and eligibility differ between guidance versions, so verify the current recommendation before you give it. Send a written summary with the patient, containing the history, the examination findings, the treatment given with times, the results that exist and the question you are asking. Send an escort who knows the patient, and make sure the receiving facility knows the patient is coming.

Documentation is the nurse's memory, the patient's continuity and the clinician's protection, and it deserves to be treated as clinical work rather than as clerical residue. A good record of a case that exceeded you contains what you observed, what you decided, what you did, what you declined to do and why, whom you consulted and when, what you advised the patient, and where the patient went next. Write the reasoning as well as the action, because a record that shows only the intervention cannot explain a decision that was correct for the setting. Sign it, time it, and keep it legible, since an illegible record protects nobody. When a patient is transferred, a copy travels with them and a copy stays in the register, so that the next clinician can reconstruct the story without guessing. The record of a limit honestly documented is what allows the system to learn; the same limit undocumented is what allows it to repeat.

The team around you has its own limits, and recognizing them is part of the same discipline. A community health worker, a traditional birth attendant, a cleaner who has become an assistant and a junior colleague all have boundaries that deserve the same honesty you ask of yourself, and none of them benefit from being flattered into a procedure they cannot perform. Teach by naming the boundary rather than by testing it, which means saying what a person may do, what they must call you for, and what nobody in this clinic does. Delegate with the same care you would want if the roles were reversed, which means the training, the supervision, the log and the escalation path. Respect the experience of staff who have worked in the community for years, because their knowledge of the place and its people is a clinical resource that no course provides. The team that shares an accurate map of its own limits is far safer than the team that assumes everyone can do everything.

The system employing you carries a duty that no individual can discharge alone, and it is fair to name that duty out loud. An organization that posts a single nurse in a remote clinic without supervision, without refresher training, without a functional referral pathway and without the authority to refuse an unsafe task has not shared the limit; it has transferred the risk to the least powerful person in the chain. Supervision should be scheduled rather than occasional, and it should include a review of the log, the complications and the difficult decisions. Continuing education should be funded and counted as working time, because competence belongs to the service rather than to the individual's spare hours. Most importantly, the organization should publicly support a clinician who declines to perform a procedure beyond their training, so that the refusal is understood as policy rather than as personal failure. A nurse who must choose between professional safety and continued employment will eventually make the wrong choice.

Across a career, the boundary moves many times, and the task is to keep the record of it honest. Early practice expands quickly, then plateaus, then partially decays, and it may contract further with age, illness or a long period away from procedures. Each of those changes should be acknowledged rather than resisted, because a limit that is denied does not disappear; it simply becomes invisible to the person who most needs to see it. The most experienced nurse in a district is not the one who can do everything but the one who knows precisely what they can do, what they cannot, and who to call for the difference. This is the promise with which this book ends: knowing your limit is not weakness but the highest form of competence, because the clinician who says this is beyond me and then stays with the patient has protected that patient twice, once by not harming them and once by not leaving them. The limit, honestly read and openly spoken, is not the edge of your usefulness but the shape of your safety.

To summarize, the recognition of your own limit is a clinical instrument as real as the thermometer, and it must be read before you act rather than after. Know the three zones of your competence, beware the second one, and distinguish the number of times you have performed a skill from your knowledge of its steps. Expand the boundary deliberately under supervision, keep a log, review it honestly, and maintain your skills with drills when the case volume is low. When a case exceeds you or the clinic, say so out loud at the time, arrange the backup you have already built, and choose transfer, temporizing or palliation over the improvisation that kills. Monitor the patient you keep, anticipate the adverse effects of what you do, and document the decisions, the consultations and the limits so that the next clinician and the next patient are safer. Humility, practiced as a question before every act, is what keeps competence honest, and honesty about the limit is the form of courage that this work actually requires. The patient is best protected by the clinician who knows what they cannot do, says it plainly, and does not walk away.
