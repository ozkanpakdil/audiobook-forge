# Tropical Medicine for the Advanced Practice Nurse — editorial plan

This file is the binding contract for every chapter. Read it fully before writing.

## What this book is

A narrated clinical course in tropical medicine for the advanced practice nurse. It
covers the neglected tropical diseases, the vector-borne and enteric infections, the
special populations they affect, and the public health systems built around them. It
then goes where most textbooks stop: the core clinical skills of remote practice, from
emergency obstetrics to point-of-care ultrasound; decision-making when diagnostics,
evacuation, and a second opinion are unavailable; the logistics of drugs, power, water
and communication; and the systems and people around the clinician, ending with the
nurse's own safety, health and limits.

It is written to be **listened to**, not skimmed on a screen. The listener is a nurse
practitioner who may be driving, walking, or setting up a field clinic. Nothing in the
audio can be re-read, looked up, or highlighted. If a sentence only works when seen on
a page, it does not belong in this book.

## The rule about doses

Doses, regimens, and treatment durations **are included**, because a tropical medicine
course without them is useless. They are always framed as follows:

- Every clinical chapter names its source in the text, in prose, as the World Health
  Organization, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, or the European Centre
  for Disease Prevention and Control.
- Every clinical chapter contains, in the flow of the prose and without a heading, at
  least one sentence telling the listener that regimens are revised, that guidance
  differs between countries, and that the current guideline must be checked before
  prescribing.
- The book opens with a short spoken notice that sets out this limitation plainly.

Never present a dose as settled truth. Present it as what current guidance says, which
is a different and more honest claim.

## Contract of style

**Headings.** Exactly one heading per file, on the first line, in this form:

    # Chapter 7 — The Syndromic Approach to Tropical Fever

with an em dash. Nothing else in the file may begin with a hash. The appendices use
`# Appendix A — ...` instead of a chapter number.

**Prose only.** Paragraphs of four to eight sentences, separated by one blank line.

Never use: bullet points, numbered lists, tables, columns, block quotes, horizontal
rules, footnotes, endnotes, citations, hyperlinks, DOIs, figure references, table
references, italics or bold markers, asterisks, underscores, backticks, or pipes.

**Nothing that a voice cannot say.** No abbreviations or acronyms, with these
exceptions, which are spoken as letters and are unambiguous in English: HIV, AIDS,
PCR, BCG, CDC, ECDC. Everything else is written out in full every time it appears:

| Never write | Always write |
| --- | --- |
| WHO | the World Health Organization |
| TB | tuberculosis |
| MDR-TB | multidrug-resistant tuberculosis |
| NTD, NTDs | neglected tropical disease, neglected tropical diseases |
| PPE | personal protective equipment |
| ORS | oral rehydration salts |
| WASH | water, sanitation and hygiene |
| DALY | disability-adjusted life year |
| IRIS | immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome |
| LLIN, ITN | insecticide-treated bed net |
| IRS | indoor residual spraying |
| RDT | rapid diagnostic test |
| ACT | artemisinin-based combination therapy |
| MDA | mass drug administration |
| ART | antiretroviral therapy |
| VHF | viral hemorrhagic fever |
| ICU | intensive care unit |
| IMCI | integrated management of childhood illness |
| IMAI | integrated management of adult illness |
| mhGAP | the mental health gap action program of the World Health Organization |
| MSF | Doctors Without Borders |
| NGO | nongovernmental organization |
| POCUS | point-of-care ultrasound |
| FAST | focused assessment with sonography in trauma |
| CPR | cardiopulmonary resuscitation |

The trap is that a voice reads `WHO` as the word "who", `WASH` as "wash", `DALY` as
"Daly", and `IRIS` as the name "Iris". Those four are the ones that most reliably ruin
a sentence, so watch for them.

Four short forms are tolerated because they are the spoken name of the thing and the
voice reads them as letters: **CT**, **MRI**, **ECG** and **EEG**. Spell them out in
full at their first mention in each chapter (computed tomography, magnetic resonance
imaging, electrocardiogram, electroencephalogram), then use the short form freely.

**Numbers and units.** Write the number in digits and the unit in full words:

- Correct: 500 milligrams, 20 milligrams per kilogram, 1.5 grams, 10 milliliters,
  38.5 degrees Celsius, 4 weeks, 6 months, 12 hours.
- Wrong: 500mg, 500 mg, 20mg/kg, 38.5°C, q6h, 1/2 tablet.

Spell out frequency and route, never abbreviate them: "every six hours", "twice a
day", "by mouth", "intravenously", "intramuscularly". Never write `q6h`, `b.i.d.`,
`PO`, `IV`, or `IM`.

Write ranges with the word "to", never a dash: "two to three days", "20 to 25
milligrams". Write fractions as words: "one half", "one quarter".

**No symbols at all.** Do not use per cent signs, equals, plus, arrows, degree signs,
ampersands, angle brackets, or greater-than and less-than marks. Write "per cent",
"equals", "leads to", "degrees", "and", "greater than", "less than".

**Centuries in words.** "the nineteenth century", never "the 19th century", never
"the XIX century".

**Organisms.** Give the binomial in plain text on first mention with the common name
beside it, then use whichever is easier on the ear:

    The parasite Plasmodium falciparum, the species responsible for the most severe
    form of malaria, ...

No italics markers anywhere; the voice cannot hear them.

**Letters.** Use only Latin letters, ordinary punctuation, and the em dash in the
heading. No Cyrillic, Greek, or mathematical characters. Accented Latin letters are
fine.

**Ending.** The final paragraph of every chapter begins, without any heading, with the
exact words `To summarize, ` and closes the chapter.

**Length.** Chapters run 4,000 to 4,500 words. Appendices run 2,000 to 3,500 words.
Going a little over is fine. Coming in badly under is not.

**Voice.** Second person where you address the clinician, third person for the
medicine. Clinical, unhurried, concrete. Explain the mechanism before the management,
so the listener understands why a step exists before hearing what the step is.

**Patients.** Any case material is a composite. Invent no identifying details that
could point to a real person, and never present a case as a named individual's record.

**Write the file once.** Compose it carefully, save it, verify it, and stop. Do not
rewrite, restructure, or trim the file afterward.

The appendices cannot be tables or lists, so each one is written as prose that
explains how to use the information, walking the listener through it in order. A drug
formulary becomes a tour of the drugs by class, with the doses spoken aloud. An
incubation chart becomes a walk through the time windows. The glossary becomes a
sequence of definitions delivered as sentences.
