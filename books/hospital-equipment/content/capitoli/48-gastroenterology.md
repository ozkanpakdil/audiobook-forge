# Chapter 48 — Gastroenterology

The gut is a muscular tube some 7 to 9 meters long, folded into the abdomen and reachable from either end only through a narrow opening. It is collapsed when empty, dark inside, wet, and constantly moving, so a physician who wants to look at its lining cannot do so from outside the body. Gastroenterology solved this problem with instruments that travel into the tube itself, carrying light in and an image out. Every device in this chapter is a variation on that single idea, and every hazard follows from the fact that the instrument is in contact with living tissue for the whole of its journey.

The physical principle is total internal reflection. Light traveling through a glass fiber strikes the boundary between glass and air at a shallow angle, and instead of leaving the fiber it is reflected back inside, so light can be guided around bends with little loss. The first flexible gastroscopes used one bundle of fibers to carry light to the tip and a second, carefully ordered bundle to carry the image back, and the image bundle worked only because each fiber kept the same position at both ends. The modern instrument replaces the image bundle with a small solid-state sensor at the tip, wired back through the shaft, so the picture arrives as an electrical signal while illumination still comes from fibers. This division, fibers for light and a wire for the image, explains why a damaged endoscope may lose brightness while keeping a perfect picture, or lose part of the picture while the illumination remains.

At the operator's end sits the control section, the only part the examiner touches. Two large wheels bend the tip up and down and left and right, and each has a lock that holds a position while the hands do something else. A set of valves admits air to open the collapsed gut, admits water to wash the lens, and applies suction to clear fluid and hold tissue. The insertion tube runs from this section to the tip, a thicker umbilical cord reaches the light source and the processor, and a small biopsy port admits forceps, snares, and needles. The tip angle, the air flow, and the water flow must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The upper endoscope, often called the gastroscope, passes through the mouth and esophagus into the stomach and the first part of the small intestine. Its working length is usually about 100 centimeters, its insertion tube about 9 to 10 millimeters, and its working channel about 2.8 millimeters, wide enough for biopsy forceps but not for every therapeutic accessory. The shaft is marked at intervals so the examiner can judge how far the tip has traveled, and the markings are read as the instrument is withdrawn rather than as it is pushed. A thinner pediatric instrument of about 5 to 6 millimeters gives comfort at the cost of a smaller channel, and a therapeutic instrument of about 12 millimeters carries a channel wide enough for a clip or a snare. The sizes, the depth of insertion, and the accessory that fits the channel must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The colonoscope is longer and thicker because the colon is longer, more folded, and entered from below. Its working length is typically 130 to 170 centimeters and its tube about 12 to 13 millimeters, and it must negotiate a mobile, looping organ that can be pushed ahead of the tip. The examiner steers by torque as much as by tip deflection, and a loop in the sigmoid or the transverse colon is recognized and removed before the tip can advance, because pushing harder against a loop only stretches the bowel and risks a perforation. Many instruments offer variable stiffness, in which a cable or a rotating collar stiffens the shaft so that it transmits force instead of folding. The stiffness setting, the insertion technique, and the degree of air insufflation must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The small intestine lies between these two reachable zones and is the hardest part of the gut to see. Balloon enteroscopy solves the reach problem with an overtube that carries an inflatable balloon, and sometimes a second balloon on the endoscope itself, so that the two surfaces grip the bowel wall alternately. The examiner advances the scope, inflates the overtube balloon to hold the bowel, advances the overtube, deflates, and repeats, effectively pleating the intestine onto the instrument. This reaches depths the older push enteroscope could not, at the cost of a long procedure and a real risk of mucosal injury from the repeated gripping. The balloon pressures, the inflation sequence, and the maximum insertion depth must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The duodenoscope is the most specialized and the most dangerous instrument in the endoscopy unit, and its shape explains why. It looks sideways rather than straight ahead, so that the examiner can see the far wall of the duodenum and the small opening where the bile and pancreatic ducts enter. At its tip sits a small hinged lever called the elevator, which lifts the tip of a catheter or a wire so that it can be aimed into that opening, and because a side-viewing instrument cannot see the path ahead, it is passed under direct vision with a straight-viewing scope or with fluoroscopy. The elevator and its recess are the heart of the contamination problem, because they are difficult to brush and they trap fluid, biofilm, and bacteria that survive an ordinary cycle. Duodenoscopes have transmitted resistant organisms between patients even when the reprocessing steps appeared to have been followed, so they require a stricter, documented, sometimes cultured routine that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The light source is a separate box on the trolley that sends concentrated white light down the fiber bundle. Older units used a halogen lamp, later ones a xenon arc lamp that is closer to daylight, and newer ones a light emitting diode, or LED, array that runs cooler and lasts longer. The end of the light cable and the tip of the endoscope become hot during a long procedure, and a scope left switched on against a drape or the patient's skin can burn, so the source is placed in standby whenever the instrument is not in the patient. The lamp has an hour meter so its aging can be predicted, and the unit usually carries a lamp failure alarm and an over-temperature alarm, and the intensity setting, the standby behavior, and both alarm thresholds must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The processor turns the sensor's electrical output into a picture and controls the appearance of that picture. It synchronizes the readout of the sensor, corrects the video signal, sets white balance so that tissue colors are rendered as the examiner expects, and sends the result to a monitor. Advanced processors add optical or digital contrast enhancement, in which selected wavelengths make surface vessels and mucosal patterns stand out, because early cancers often change the pattern of blood vessels before they change color. Magnification, either optical by zooming the lens or digital by enlarging the image, is useful only when the surface is clean and the tip is close, since enlargement of a blurred image is still blurred. The enhancement mode, the magnification, and the balance procedure must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The monitor sits at eye level on the trolley so that the examiner's head and the screen do not fight each other for hours, and it must be bright enough to be read in a lit room, since a dim screen hides exactly the subtle findings the examination is for. The recording system captures the live image, still photographs, and often the video of the whole procedure, with the patient's identifiers attached, and stores them in the picture archiving and communication system, or PACS, which is the hospital's image store. Stills and video may be exported in the standard medical imaging format called Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine, or DICOM, so they can be reviewed beside radiology. The retention period, the labeling convention, and the export format must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

An endoscope can only look, and everything that treats is an accessory passed through its channel, so these accessories are consumables that must match the channel and the task. Biopsy forceps are flexible cables with two small cups that are closed by a handle, and a dull forceps crushes rather than cuts, producing a specimen the pathologist cannot read. Snares are loops of wire opened around a polyp and tightened while electrical current is applied, and they come in different shapes and diameters for different lesions. Injection needles are thin retractable needles used to inject a solution beneath the mucosa or beside a bleeding point, and injecting too deeply or too superficially defeats the purpose. These accessories are usually single use, because reuse after cleaning removes the lubricant, blunts the cups, and leaves protein in a joint that cannot be cleaned, and the size, the electrical settings, and the reuse policy of every accessory must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Bleeding inside the gut is controlled with devices that either mechanically pinch the vessel or heat it closed. A through-the-scope clip is a jaw opened outside the patient, advanced along the channel, pressed onto the bleeding point, and released, leaving a permanent or slowly absorbed clasp across the tissue. Thermal therapy uses a probe with a heated tip, a bipolar probe that passes current through the tissue between two electrodes, or a jet of ionized gas that conducts current to the surface without touching it, and all three coagulate by raising the tissue temperature until the proteins denature and the vessel seals. Too much energy desiccates the tissue until it chars, and a charred surface hides the vessel that is still bleeding underneath, while a spurting artery needs a mechanical or injection method before or instead of heat. The energy level, the application time, and the probe selection must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Capsule endoscopy removes the shaft altogether. The patient swallows a capsule the size of a large vitamin, which contains a lens, a light source, a battery, a transmitter, and a small antenna, and which passes through the gut by the ordinary movement of the bowel. The capsule photographs the mucosa as it travels and sends the images to a recorder worn on a belt, so the examination needs no sedation, no insufflation, and no insertion, and the patient walks around while it happens. Its limits follow from its design, because it cannot steer, wash the surface, biopsy, treat, or send back anything but images, and the retention of a capsule in a narrowed segment is the characteristic serious complication. The battery life, the recording interval, and the exclusion criteria must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Endoscopic ultrasound puts a high-frequency sound transducer on the tip of an endoscope, so that the wall of the gut and the organs immediately beyond it can be seen without the intervening gas and bone that spoil an external scan. High frequency gives fine detail but shallow penetration, so a probe at 12 to 20 megahertz images a few centimeters while a lower frequency reaches further, and the examiner chooses the compromise that fits the question. Radial probes sweep a circle around the shaft and produce an image like a slice across the gut, while linear probes look forward along the shaft and leave a path for a needle to pass into a lymph node, a cyst, or a pancreatic mass. The tip is coupled to the tissue with a water-filled balloon or by filling the gut with water, because sound does not travel through air, and the frequency, the balloon volume, and the needle path must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Everything that enters the gut is contaminated with blood, mucus, and a dense population of bacteria, and the instrument is hollow, long, and heat-sensitive, so it cannot simply be steamed. Reprocessing a flexible endoscope is a chain of steps in which each one protects the next, and a break anywhere in the chain leaves a device that looks clean and is not. The chain begins at the bedside, within minutes of the instrument leaving the patient, because delay lets blood and mucus dry into a film that later steps cannot lift. The insertion tube is wiped, the channel is flushed with detergent solution, and air is passed to expel the fluid before the scope travels to the reprocessing room. The interval allowed before bedside cleaning, the detergent concentration, and the flush volume must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A flexible endoscope is a sealed tube, and the single most important test of its integrity is the leak test. The scope is pressurized with air at a low pressure through its leak test port and then submerged in water, and any breach in the covering, the bending section, or the channel walls releases bubbles that show where the damage is. The test is done before immersion cleaning so that water is not drawn into a punctured channel, where it would reach the electronics and the optics and destroy the instrument, and it is repeated whenever the scope has been struck, bitten, or used on a patient with a sharp foreign body. The test pressure, the immersion time, and the required response to bubbles must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Manual cleaning comes before any machine, because a disinfectant cannot penetrate a layer of dried organic matter. The scope is fully immersed in a basin of detergent solution and every external surface is wiped, including the control section, the insertion tube, and the bending section, with attention to the rough areas where the covering is bonded. Every channel is brushed, including the suction channel, the air and water channel, the biopsy channel, and the elevator recess on a duodenoscope, and the brush is pushed through until it emerges at the far end before the bristles are pulled back. The brush diameter must match the channel, because a brush too small does not touch the walls and a brush too large jams and can scratch the lining, and the brush itself is a contaminated item that must be cleaned or discarded between scopes. The brush sizes, the number of passes, and the detergent contact time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The washer-disinfector automates the part of the chain that humans do inconsistently. The scope is placed in a tray with its channels connected to ports on the machine, and the machine pumps detergent, rinse water, and disinfectant through the lumens at a controlled flow, temperature, and time, so that every internal surface sees the chemical rather than only the outside. Some machines also test for channel blockage by measuring flow, and a channel that no longer accepts the expected flow is flagged. The cycle is only valid if the connectors are correctly attached, the channels are not occluded, and the chemicals are at the correct concentration, because a cycle that runs with a loose connector washes the outside of the scope and leaves the inside untouched. The cycle parameters, the flow check, and the connector configuration must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

High-level disinfection is a chemical process that kills all bacteria, most viruses, and fungi, and leaves some bacterial spores, which is why it is not called sterilization. The chemistry most often used is a dialdehyde solution, usually glutaraldehyde, which cross-links the proteins of the microbial cell, while ortho-phthalaldehyde is less irritating to the airway and peracetic acid acts by oxidizing and is often used in automated processors. Concentration, temperature, and contact time act together, and a dilute or cold or short exposure may fail even though the instrument was submerged. Test strips confirm that the active concentration is above the minimum effective value, the solution is discarded after its stated reuse life or sooner if the strip fails, and the room must be ventilated because the vapors irritate the eyes and the airways. The concentration, the temperature, the contact time, the test method, and the ventilation requirement must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Drying is not cosmetic, because a drop of water left in a channel is a place where bacteria multiply between patients. After disinfection the scope is rinsed with water of a defined quality, purged with air, and often flushed with alcohol, which mixes with the remaining water and evaporates with it. The instrument is then hung vertically with its valves removed, in a cabinet that circulates filtered air through the channels and keeps the surfaces dry. The cabinet is a device in its own right, with a filter, a fan, and sometimes a timer and a record of when the scope was placed inside, and its purpose is to prevent the regrowth that happens in a damp, closed space. Storage time has a limit, and a scope stored beyond it is reprocessed before use, and the rinse water quality, the drying time, the cabinet airflow, and the maximum storage time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Endoscopy equipment speaks in alarms, and each one points to a different part of the chain. The processor alarms on lamp failure, on overheating, and on a disconnected scope or cable, and a dark monitor during a procedure requires a spare lamp and a spare scope rather than a repair attempt on the patient. The reprocessing machine alarms on low disinfectant concentration, on a blocked channel, on low temperature, and on an incomplete cycle, and the only correct response to an incomplete cycle is to repeat the whole cycle rather than to release the scope. The storage cabinet alarms on loss of airflow or a saturated filter, while the leak tester simply shows bubbles. The alarm behavior, the response to each alarm, and the records to be kept must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The common operator errors in gastrointestinal endoscopy are few and expensive. The first is to advance against resistance, because the gut wall is thin and a loop that will not straighten is a warning rather than an obstacle, and a perforation may be silent at the moment it happens and declared later by pain, fever, and a rigid abdomen. The second is to insufflate without limit, because distension is uncomfortable and in a patient with a weak wall it can cause a perforation, so the abdomen is watched as the examination proceeds. The third is to pass an accessory through a channel that is too small or a needle that is too short for the lesion, and the resulting jam damages both the accessory and the lining. The fourth is to silence an alarm or accept an incomplete reprocessing cycle because the list is long, and that shortcut is how a clinic transmits infection. The resistance that should stop the advance, the insufflation limit, the accessory and channel match, and the alarm response must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The characteristic failures of a flexible endoscope are physical, and most are visible before they are dangerous. A bite from a confused patient cracks the covering of the bending section, and the leak test then bubbles, admitting fluid into the optics so the picture fogs or dims permanently. A channel punctured by a needle or an over-sized accessory leaks during the leak test and later cannot be properly cleaned, because fluid bypasses the lumen walls. A broken fiber bundle shows as black dots in the image, and the number of dots only grows, while the angulation mechanism can lose its range or become stiff so the tip no longer reaches the angle needed to see a flexure. Each finding has a defined action, whether removal from service or continued use with a limitation, and the acceptance criteria for each must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The consumables of an endoscopy unit are as important as the instruments, and running out of one stops the list. Every patient needs a bite block to protect the instrument from the teeth, a lubricant, and often a topical anesthetic for the throat. The unit needs suction canisters and tubing, basins and detergents for manual cleaning, channel brushes in several diameters, leak testers, and the disinfectant with its test strips. There are specimen containers with fixative, labels that survive the fixative, and a documented chain from the patient to the pathology laboratory, plus the accessories themselves, forceps, snares, needles, and clips, each with an expiry date and an intact package. The staff who reprocess the instrument wear personal protective equipment, because the detergent, the disinfectant, and the patient's secretions all splash, and the stocking levels, the expiry checks, and the storage conditions of every consumable must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The field position begins with a hard fact, which is that a flexible endoscope is one of the least field-friendly devices in medicine. It needs a light source, a processor, a monitor, clean water, a disinfectant, a drying cabinet, and a trained person to reprocess it, and none of those are present in a tent at the end of a road. What a mobile or frontier clinic can realistically carry is one general-purpose upper endoscope, a portable battery light source, a small monitor, a suction pump, a basin, brushes, and a supply of disinfectant. That single instrument is asked to do everything, from diagnosis to foreign body removal to band treatment of bleeding veins, and its service life in that setting is short. The field protocol must therefore be built around protecting the one scope the clinic has, and the cleaning steps become the most important clinical activity of the day.

Field cleaning is manual from beginning to end, and it is slower than a machine cycle, so it must be organized. The bedside flush happens immediately, with a syringe of detergent solution pushed through the channel until it runs clear, and the scope is carried to a dedicated basin used for nothing else. Every channel is brushed with a brush that fits, and the brush is itself cleaned and disinfected between patients or discarded, because a dirty brush inoculates the next scope. The disinfection step uses the same chemistry as the hospital, but at field scale, which means a covered container, a timer, and a person whose only task is to watch that timer, and the contact time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Drying is done by pulling air through the channel with a syringe and hanging the scope vertically, straight, in a dust-free place, because a scope coiled in a bag stays wet and grows biofilm.

Power is the other field constraint, and it shapes the equipment list. A battery light source and a battery monitor let the examination run during a power cut or where there is no supply at all, and a small generator can recharge them between sessions, but a generator shared with a refrigerator and a centrifuge may be overloaded when the scope is switched on. A portable unit usually gives up image enhancement, magnification, and high-definition resolution, so the field examiner sees a smaller, softer, less colorful picture and must compensate by cleaning the lens, keeping the tip close, and examining slowly. There is no recorder in most field kits, so the findings are drawn and written by hand, with the location, the size, and the appearance recorded in words. The battery capacity, the charging routine, and the generator load must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

What the field variant gives up is the entire therapeutic and confirmatory layer of the specialty. There is no second scope to substitute when the first one leaks, no duodenoscope with an elevator, no balloon enteroscope, no endoscopic ultrasound probe, and no capsule program with its recorder and its reading station. The field clinic can see, photograph poorly, biopsy if it has a working channel and a sterile forceps, and remove a foreign body with a snare or a basket. What it demands instead is discipline, because one scope must be cleaned perfectly every time, and staffing, because the person who holds the scope cannot also watch the disinfection timer. It also demands water of a known quality, since rinse water that is contaminated contaminates the instrument after it has been disinfected, and the scope model chosen, the accessories carried, and the water treatment used must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The characteristic field failure is not a broken instrument but a contaminated one. A frontier clinic that cannot perform every step of the chain, or that shortens the contact time because a queue is waiting, can transmit blood-borne viruses and resistant bacteria on a scope that appears clean and smells of disinfectant. The temptation to reuse single-use forceps and needles is strongest where supply is uncertain, and it must be resisted because their joints cannot be cleaned and their cutting edges fail. Sterilization is not the answer for a heat-sensitive scope, so the field must accept high-level disinfection done properly rather than sterilization done badly. A scope that cannot be leak tested, or that fails the test and cannot be repaired, is taken out of service, because a scope with an internal breach cannot be cleaned at all. The disinfection chemistry, the contact time, the reuse limits, and the criteria for removing a scope from service must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Before every examination the operator performs a short sequence of checks that catches most problems while there is still time to fetch another instrument. The scope is inspected along its whole length for cracks, kinks, and rough patches, the bending section is flexed through its full range to feel for stiffness, and the tip is checked for a clean lens and a clear channel. The leak test is performed and read, the light source is switched on to confirm brightness, and the image is checked on the monitor for fog, spots, or color error. The disinfectant is tested for concentration, the expiry dates of the accessories are read, and the spare bulb, spare scope, and suction are confirmed ready. Between services, a biomedical technician checks the electrical safety of the trolley, the calibration of the light source and processor, the performance of the washer-disinfector, and the airflow of the storage cabinet, and the check intervals, the electrical tests, and the calibration tolerances must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The hazards of this specialty deserve to be named plainly. Perforation and hemorrhage are the immediate mechanical dangers of pushing and cutting inside a thin, vascular wall, and both can begin during a routine examination. Infection transmission is the delayed danger, carried by an instrument that is difficult to clean and shared among many patients. Sedation adds its own risks, so the patient's breathing, oxygen level, and heart rhythm are monitored throughout, with suction and resuscitation equipment immediately at hand. Electrical safety matters because current is deliberately applied to tissue through accessories and because a wet scope and a wet floor surround the trolley, and the monitoring standard, the resuscitation readiness, and the electrical testing of every device must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

To summarize, gastroenterology is the discipline of seeing and working inside a dark, moving, muscular tube, and it does so with flexible instruments that guide light and images along their length. The upper endoscope, the colonoscope, the duodenoscope, the enteroscope, and the ultrasound endoscope are each shaped by the anatomy they must follow and the task they must perform. Everything that treats the gut is a consumable passed through a narrow channel, from biopsy forceps and snares to clips and thermal probes, and each has a defined match to the channel and to the lesion. The capsule trades control for comfort, imaging the small intestine while it drifts and treating nothing. The reprocessing chain, from the bedside flush through the leak test, the brush, the washer-disinfector, the high-level disinfectant, and the drying cabinet, is the part of the specialty that protects the next patient, and in the field one scope must carry the whole service, so a clinic that cannot clean it properly should not use it at all.
