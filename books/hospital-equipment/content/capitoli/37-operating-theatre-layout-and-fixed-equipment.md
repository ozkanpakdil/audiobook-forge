# Chapter 37 — Operating Theatre Layout and Fixed Equipment

An operating theatre is not a room that contains equipment but a machine in its own right, built to keep living particles away from an open wound. Contamination always travels on a carrier, whether air, water, a hand, an instrument, or dust, so the room controls the carriers rather than the organisms. A wound is warm, wet, and exposed, and any organism landing on it may multiply, so the design excludes contamination rather than killing it after arrival. The theatre is therefore a system of zones, pressure steps, filtered air, cleanable surfaces, and limited traffic, and this chapter describes it in a well-resourced hospital and then in a mobile or frontier clinic where none of it exists.

The sterile field is a concept before it is a place, being the region of the patient, the drapes, the instruments, and the air directly above them that is treated as free of living organisms. Its boundary is held by gravity, by distance, and by materials: a sterilized drape kept dry, a gown and gloves worn by a scrubbed person, and a rule that treats everything below waist level and beyond the drape as unsterile. Because organisms are carried rather than walking, a dry cloth is a barrier only while it stays dry, since moisture wicks organisms from an unsterile surface to a sterile one. The commonest operator error is the unthinking one, the hand that adjusts a lamp above the wound or the tubing lifted back after trailing on the floor, and no alarm reports a broken field, so the circulating nurse watches the boundary.

A modern theatre is laid out as zones of decreasing contamination, and the layout itself is equipment. The outer transfer area receives the patient and stops street clothing, outside equipment, and family. The clean zone holds the scrub sinks and the closed storage of sterile supplies, while the restricted zone is the operating room itself, entered only by staff in theatre clothing, caps, and masks. A theatre that lacks these zones still works clinically and pays for the lack in surgical site infections, so the zoning and traffic rules must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the room is used for a patient.

The operating table holds an unconscious body in a chosen position for hours without injuring it and without shifting under the surgeon's hands. It is a column or pedestal carrying the weight to the floor, a top divided into sections, and a mechanism that raises, lowers, tilts, and flexes those sections. The hydraulic version pushes an incompressible fluid into a cylinder and holds position when the pump stops, while the electric version uses a motor with a screw or gear, quieter but slower and dependent on power. The joints between sections are the weak points where fluid leaks and skin can be trapped, and the maximum load and locking procedure must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before a patient is placed on the table.

The movements of the table each change what the surgeon can reach: Trendelenburg position lowers the head and raises the feet, lateral tilt rolls the table, and the kidney bridge arches the flank. The table also takes attachments, including arm boards, leg holders or stirrups for pelvic surgery, shoulder supports and straps that stop a patient sliding on a steep tilt, and a headrest for prone surgery. The positions these create, supine, prone, lateral, lithotomy, and sitting, carry mechanical hazards, because a patient who cannot report numbness can suffer a stretched nerve, pressure damage from a hard edge, or a fall from a table tilted before the straps are applied. Every pressure point, strap tension, and position limit must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the patient is positioned.

Before every case the table is checked in a fixed order, because the failure that matters happens after the patient is asleep and draped. The operator releases the brakes, runs each movement through its full range, listens for a change in the motor's note, looks for fluid under the column, and confirms that the table locks solidly. The foot switch is checked for a stuck button, since a table that keeps moving after the button is released is a serious hazard, and a table that drifts, sinks, or grinds is removed from service. Maintenance covers the hydraulic fluid and seals, the supply cord, and the battery that lowers the table when the mains fails, and the service interval and load test procedure must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the table returns to use.

The surgical light exists because the eye needs far more light on a deep, wet, moving field than a room can provide, and its design follows from intensity and heat. Intensity is measured in lux, and any intensity setting, focus, or filter specification must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The halogen lamp is an incandescent filament in a gas-filled envelope, and most of its energy leaves as heat, so it needs a large housing and a heat filter and can still feel warm on the surgeon's neck. The light-emitting diode, or LED, lamp produces light at a semiconductor junction, wastes far less energy as heat, lasts much longer, and allows a flatter head, while both types use a reflector and a ring of lamps to reduce the shadow cast by the surgeon's hands.

The light head hangs from an arm with joints and counterweights, and a joint that has lost its friction lets the head drift over the field or drop without warning. The sterilizable handle at the center of the head, or the disposable cover over it, lets a scrubbed person reposition the light without touching the unsterile body of the lamp. The common operator error is to grab the unsterile arm, contaminating the hand and then the field, or to over tighten a joint until it strips, and a light that flickers, dims, or cuts out is repaired before the next case. Every theatre needs a tested backup of a battery lamp, a head torch, or a second ceiling light, and its charge state must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the room is used for a patient.

Ceiling pendants and booms bring gases, power, suction, and data to the point of use without a floor full of hoses, and they are the equipment most often overloaded. A boom is a column or articulated arm fixed to the structural ceiling, carrying outlets, gas hoses, sockets, and often a shelf for a monitor or pump. The load hangs from the building, so the ceiling fixing and each joint brake carry the whole weight, and a boom rated for a stated load must not also hold a pump, a monitor, and a heavy bag. The outlets are color coded and indexed by gas, and the common errors are to force a hose onto the wrong outlet with an adapter, to leave a joint unbraked, or to park the boom over the sterile field, so the load limit, brake test, and gas outlet check must each be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Ventilation is the part of the theatre that is invisible and works hardest and longest. Clean air is delivered at the ceiling, sweeps the room in a controlled direction, and leaves near the floor, carrying particles away from the wound rather than across it. Outdoor air is filtered, conditioned for temperature and humidity, and supplied at a rate measured in air changes per hour, which is how many times the whole volume of the room is replaced in an hour. Above the table the supply is usually a unidirectional or laminar flow field made possible by a high-efficiency particulate air filter, and the air change rate, filter efficiency, temperature, and humidity set point must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the room is used for a patient.

The pressure of the theatre relative to its corridor is a deliberate choice and one of the cheapest defenses against infection. The room is usually held slightly above the corridor, so that when a door opens the air flows outward, while for a patient with a contagious airborne infection the relationship is reversed and the room is held at a lower pressure so air is exhausted through a filter. The difference is small and is measured in pascals, and a difference that is too large makes doors hard to open and whistle, while one that is lost leaves the room at the mercy of the corridor. A pressure monitor with a gauge and an audible alarm is the standard way to know the state of the room, so the set point and alarm band must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The operator check on ventilation is short and is done before the first case and after every door has been opened. The gauge is read and written down, the alarm is tested with its test button, and the room is walked for a draught that should not be there, because a door propped with a chair or taped back defeats the pressure step completely. Filters load with dust, and a loaded filter lowers the air change rate while the fan works harder, so the pressure drop across the filter is monitored and the filter changed on a schedule. A ventilation failure during a case is serious, and the fallback is to stop if the case can be stopped, close the wound, and leave the room, while the filter change interval and pressure drop limit must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The electrical supply of a theatre is arranged differently from that of a ward, and the difference is a transformer. In the usual arrangement the room is fed by an isolated or ungrounded system, in which a transformer separates the mains so that neither of the 2 supply conductors is connected to earth and a single fault to earth cannot complete a circuit through a person. A line isolation monitor watches the leakage to earth and sounds an alarm when it crosses a threshold, and that alarm is a warning that the margin has been used, so equipment is removed until the leak is found. Critical circuits, meaning the table, lights, anesthetic machine, monitors, and life support devices, are usually backed by an uninterruptible power supply, or UPS, and by the emergency generator, and every threshold, battery condition, and generator test record must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Power and gas reach the patient through a clutter of cords and hoses, and that clutter is where the mechanical and electrical hazards of the room meet. A cord across the floor trips a person carrying a sterile tray, a cord under a wheel can be crushed and its conductors exposed, and a cord coiled around a metal rail can overheat. The common errors are the extension lead, the multiple adaptor, and the cord pulled out by its cable instead of its plug, all of which loosen the connection the safety of the device depends on. Every cord, plug, and strain relief is inspected before use, a damaged one is removed at once, and the inspection routine and the acceptance rules for borrowed equipment must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The anesthetic machine stands at the head of the table so that the person watching the patient, the breathing circuit, and the controls never has to turn away. It is connected to pipeline outlets or cylinders, its hoses run without a loop that can be tripped over, and its scavenging system carries waste gas out of the room to a safe discharge. It must be close enough that the circuit reaches the airway without tension, and its wheels are locked, because a machine that rolls during induction pulls the circuit and can dislodge an airway. The check before every case covers the gas supply and its alarms, the vaporizers, the circuit and its seals, the absorber, the ventilator, and the monitoring, and every pressure, flow, concentration, and alarm limit must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Instrument tables are the working surface of the sterile field, and their arrangement is a matter of reach and gravity. The back table is the large draped surface where instrument sets are opened and laid out in the order they will be used, and the Mayo stand is a small table on a single pedestal brought over the patient for the current step. Because a drape is a barrier only while it is dry, a wet table is a contaminated table, so the tables are placed where the scrub nurse reaches the surgeon in one step and nothing passes over the open wound. The errors are to stack instruments so high that a hand knocks them, to leave a blade facing upward in a tray, or to lay a wet towel at the drape's edge, and the weight a table can carry must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Three smaller items complete the working field: the electrosurgical or diathermy unit, the suction source, and the receptacles that receive what the case discards. The diathermy unit sends a radiofrequency current through an active electrode to cut or coagulate tissue and returns it through a large return electrode on the patient's skin, so its placement and full contact are safety critical. Suction keeps the field clear of blood and irrigation fluid, so a blocked tip, a full canister, or a kinked tube stops the surgeon, and waste receptacles are placed where the discarding hand naturally goes, because a needle dropped into a general bin is an injury waiting to happen. A counts board records the sponges, needles, and instruments in the field, the counts are checked aloud before the wound is closed, and every energy setting, suction pressure, and alarm threshold must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Doors and the number of people in the room matter as much as the ventilation, because people are the largest source of the particles that carry infection. Every person sheds skin scales, and the rate rises with movement and talking, so a room filling with observers defeats the ventilation trying to clear it, while an open door destroys the pressure step and admits corridor air. The door therefore stays closed except for a patient, a trolley, or a needed supply, and the number of people is limited to those with a defined role while movement in and out is minimized. Caps, masks, and theatre clothing reduce shedding and are worn by everyone including visitors, and the traffic rules, dress requirements, and permitted occupancy must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The room is built of materials chosen so that it can be cleaned, and cleaning is a physical process rather than a ritual. Floors are seamless or coved where they meet the wall so dirt cannot collect in a corner, walls are smooth, and the table, booms, and housings are finished so a cloth passes over them without a seam to catch a soiled edge. Cleaning lifts organisms into a cloth or a solution and carries them away, which is why a surface wiped with a dirty cloth is redistributed rather than cleaned, and why the cloth is changed between areas. Between cases the room receives a turn over clean of the table, light handles, arm boards, and floor, and at the end of the day a terminal clean, while the cleaning agent, its dilution, and its contact time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Cleaning carries its own hazards and its own consumable list. Liquid pushed into a switch, an outlet, or the grille of a machine can bridge insulation and destroy the device, and a strong disinfectant can attack the plastic and the labeling of an instrument, so the agent is matched to the surface and a powered device is never sprayed. The consumables are easy to forget: light handle covers, sterile drapes, table pads and straps, gas hoses, suction canisters and liners, diathermy return electrodes, filter elements, and the caps, masks, and gowns worn by the staff. A theatre that runs well keeps a par level of each and checks a written list daily, because a missing handle cover or return electrode stops a case as surely as a broken light, and the storage conditions, expiry dates, and single-use policy must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Behind the daily turn over sits a maintenance program that only technical staff can perform, and that program is what keeps the fixed equipment fixed. Electrical safety is tested on a schedule, the earth path and leakage of each device are measured, and the line isolation monitor is checked against a known leak. The booms and light arms are inspected for ceiling fixings, brakes, and joints; the table for hydraulic leaks and its battery; the ventilation for air change rate and filter loading; and the gas outlets for identity, flow, and leaks. Each test has a pass value and a service interval that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol, a register records what was done and by whom, and the failure mode most easily missed is the intermittent one.

The failure modes of the theatre itself are worth naming in advance, because the response must be decided before the pressure is high. If the table fails with a patient on it, the case is brought to a safe stopping point, the wound is packed and covered, and the patient is moved to a trolley with the position controlled by hand. If the light fails the backup lamp is deployed, if the ventilation alarm sounds the door is closed and the case is stopped if it can be stopped, and if the gas supply or suction fails the machine switches to cylinders and suction changes to a portable pump. If the mains is lost the emergency circuits and the uninterruptible power supply carry the critical devices, and every fallback and its trigger must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol in advance of the emergency.

In a mobile clinic or a frontier hospital the surgical need is the same and the architecture is absent, so the room and its discipline are improvised from different materials. The typical field theatre is a clean room with a table, a lamp on a stand, and sterile drapes processed locally, and its performance depends far more on the behavior of the team than on the building. It gives up the pressure cascade, the filtered unidirectional air, the isolated power, the booms, the adjustable table, the piped gases, and the central sterile supply. It demands instead a set of rules that substitute for the architecture, applied without exception, because there is no monitor to notice when they are broken, and when that discipline holds the field theatre can achieve acceptable surgical infection rates.

The first demand is a room separable into a clean area and a soiled area, and the separation can be as simple as tape on the floor. One end holds the table, the instrument table, the lamp, and the sterile supplies, while the other holds discarded sponges, used instruments, soiled linen, and waste, and the 2 ends never mix. A hand washing point with clean water, soap, and a means of drying stands at the boundary, windows and doors are screened so dust and insects are excluded, and a fan blows from the clean side toward the soiled side rather than across the wound. The room is cleared of clutter, because clutter is a reservoir of dust, and the layout rules for a given facility must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The field operating table is often a plain wooden or metal table with a firm top, and because it does not articulate, position is made with pads, sandbags, folded linen, and straps. Trendelenburg position is achieved by propping the foot of the table and securing the patient, and every such improvisation is tested with the patient's weight on it before the case, because a prop that slips under a straining abdomen is a fall. The table is checked for stability, a level top, splinters or sharp edges that would press into skin under anesthesia, and the space beneath it for the surgeon's legs and the suction tubing. Padding is placed at the heels, elbows, shoulders, and head, a table that is too high or too low makes the surgeon work awkwardly, and the stability check and the load the improvised table can bear must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Lighting is the field constraint surgeons notice first, because the illumination of a hospital light cannot be matched by a lamp on a stand. The usual solution is a battery or solar charged light-emitting diode lamp on an adjustable stand, chosen because it runs cool, draws little current, and gives a bright white field from a small head. The stand is weighted so it cannot be knocked onto the patient, the lamp sits above and slightly to the side of the wound so the surgeon's head does not eclipse the field, and a head torch is the usual backup for deep work. The characteristic failure is not a burnout but a slow dimming as the battery discharges, so the charge state and spare battery are checked before the first incision, and the intensity and charging routine must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Sterile supplies are the hardest field constraint, because everything touching the wound must have been processed to a standard the room cannot verify. Where a central sterile services department is absent, instruments are cleaned and then sterilized by boiling in water for a stated time, by steam under pressure in a small autoclave or pressure cooker, or by a chemical agent where the material cannot tolerate heat. Boiling does not destroy every organism and does not leave instruments dry and wrapped, so a boiled instrument is used immediately, while a pressure cooker gives a genuine steam cycle when the air is driven out and the pressure held. The field therefore demands a written processing routine, a log for each cycle, and indicator strips or tape, whose time, temperature, and interpretation must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Power in the field comes from a generator, a battery, or a solar array, and none behaves like the mains. A small generator can sag when a large load starts and can carry a waveform the device was not designed for, so sensitive equipment runs from a battery or a voltage stabilizer rather than the generator directly. There is no isolated supply and usually no line isolation monitor, so the defense against electric shock is a residual current device, a good earth connection to a rod driven into the ground, and the discipline of keeping water away from electrical equipment. Gases come from cylinders with a pin index and a regulator that is checked for identity and turned off when not in use, and the electrical arrangements and cylinder handling rules must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Maintenance in the field is a stock list and a habit rather than a service contract, because no engineer will arrive and no spare will be shipped in time. The stock includes spare lamps and charged batteries, spare gas hoses and regulators, spare suction tubing and canisters, seals for the sterilizer, cleaning agent, indicator tape, suture, gloves, and tools to repair a table. The habit is a daily check mirroring the hospital check in fewer steps, and the common error in a low-resource setting is to postpone a small repair until it becomes a large failure, or to fit a part that does not belong because the correct one is unavailable. Equipment is kept under cover, away from dust, moisture, rodents, and direct sun, with a named person responsible for it, and the spare parts list and storage conditions must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The comparison between the 2 versions describes what each buys rather than which is better. The hospital theatre buys its low infection rate with filtered air, pressure control, isolated power, an adjustable table, piped gases, and a central sterile supply, at the cost of capital, energy, and maintenance. The field theatre gives up those systems and buys mobility, low cost, and independence from the grid, at the cost of a far greater reliance on the behavior of the team and the processing of instruments. The hospital version can fail in a way nobody notices, such as a loaded filter or a drifted pressure, while the field version fails visibly, such as a lamp that dims or a table that rocks, so it demands more discipline per case and not less.

To summarize, an operating theatre is a machine for controlling contamination and for holding a patient safely while the body is opened, and its fixed equipment serves those 2 purposes rather than decorating the room. The zones and the sterile field define where clean ends and dirty begins, the table positions the patient, the lights and booms bring light and services to the field, and the ventilation, the pressure step, the isolated supply, and the traffic rules keep the environment from loading the wound. The hospital version does this with architecture that must be maintained and monitored, while the field version gives up the architecture and substitutes discipline, a charged lamp, a stable table, a written processing routine, and a controlled room. In both settings the operator check, the cleaning, the consumable list, and the maintenance register are the parts no purchase order includes, and they decide whether the room helps the patient or harms the patient.
