# Chapter 44 — Surgical Drapes, Gowns, Gloves, and Counting Systems

A sterile field is not a place but a boundary, and the drapes, gowns, and gloves of an operating room exist to hold that boundary against two things that always travel together, microorganisms and fluid. A barrier resists that passage by two separate mechanisms, and confusing them is the origin of most breaches of technique. The first is a mechanical screen, in which a fabric stops a particle larger than its pores, and the second is a resistance to liquid, in which a surface repels water even where the openings are wide enough to admit it. A loosely woven cloth can screen dust and still allow a thin film of blood to wick through by capillary action, while a non-woven fabric can be nearly impenetrable to fluid and still fold into a pack the size of a hand. The consequence is that a barrier is only as good as its weakest point and only for as long as it stays dry, so a drape that is intact at the first incision can become a wet channel within minutes. Every decision about draping is therefore a decision about fluid, time, and contact.

The fabrics themselves fall into two families, and the difference between them is the difference between a net and a sheet. A woven textile is made by interlacing threads, so it has a regular grid of pores and a tendency to wick fluid along its threads from a wet area to a dry one. A non-woven textile is made by bonding fibers into a mat whose random orientation removes the straight capillary path that a woven cloth provides. Barrier performance is stated as a resistance to liquid under pressure, measured as the height of a column of water that the fabric holds before water appears on the far side, and as a weight in grams per square meter, and a surgical drape commonly falls between 20 and 60 grams per square meter, a range that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The choice between a heavier and a lighter fabric is the choice between a stronger resistance to fluid and an easier drape that fails sooner when it is wet.

Drapes are shaped for the body part they cover, and the shape is a mechanical answer to a specific problem. A plain sheet is the universal barrier and is laid first to establish the field, a fenestrated drape carries a window that frames the incision, and a split drape opens along one edge so that it can be wrapped around a limb or a head without lifting what is already in place. An adhesive edge bonds the fabric to the skin, and its purpose is not comfort but the elimination of the gap through which fluid and organisms travel from unprepared skin to the wound. An aperture is often surrounded by a reinforced ring or a fluid collection pouch, which turns the window into a shallow basin and directs irrigation and blood away from the operator's feet. The operator must confirm after opening the pack that the drape matches the planned procedure, because a drape of the wrong shape invites improvisation at the worst moment.

Draping follows a direction, and the direction is always from the sterile toward the unsterile. The first sheet is placed at the incision and the field is enlarged outward, so that the operator never reaches across a covered area to place a new one. The drape is handled by its corners and its cuffs, and the hands stay inside the sterile boundary while the fabric unfolds under its own weight rather than being shaken open above the field. Once placed, the drape defines the rules of the room, because the table top is sterile, the hanging part below the table level is not, and the boundary between them must never be crossed by an instrument, a hand, or a cable. A drape that has been lifted and repositioned has lost its contact with the reason for its placement, and a drape over a patient who moves or breathes must be checked again. The common error is to reach over an unprepared area to retrieve a dropped instrument, which drags the sleeve across a contaminated surface, and the correct response is to step back and ask for a replacement.

The hazards of draping are fluid, fire, and concealment. Strike-through is the moment fluid reaches the far side of the fabric, and it converts the barrier into a bridge, so any wet spot or dampness felt through the glove is treated as a contamination of the field and covered with a fresh drape. Fluid collects in the folds of a drape and in the pouch of a fenestration, and a pool that sits against the skin macerates it, hides bleeding, and can spill when the patient is moved. An oxygen-enriched atmosphere under a drape is a fire risk when an energy device or a laser is used nearby, and it must be managed according to the local fire protocol, with every concentration, flow rate, and alarm threshold confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Cables and cords are laid over the drape rather than under it, because a cord that crosses the field can drag without warning. The characteristic warning of a draping failure is a change in appearance rather than a sound, a spreading wet area, a stain at the edge of an adhesive strip, or a gap that appears when the patient is repositioned.

A surgical gown is a personal barrier, and its performance is judged at the places where the body meets fluid. The fabric is rated by the level of liquid and viral penetration it resists, and the rating rises with the amount of fluid, the pressure behind it, and the duration of contact, with the highest level reserved for procedures that produce a large volume of blood under pressure. Reinforcement panels are added at the forearms and the chest because those are the surfaces that press against a wet field, and a gown is only as protective as the part of it that touches the patient. The closure runs down the back and is secured with ties, a hook-and-loop strip, or a wrap-around flap, and the back of the gown is never considered sterile because it is not designed as a barrier. Cuffs are usually knitted, which is comfortable and absorbent, and that same absorbency is why a saturated cuff must be changed rather than ignored.

The gown fails first where it is wettest, and the sequence of failure is predictable. Fluid wicks up a woven sleeve, spreads across a reinforcement panel, and reaches the skin at the forearm or the abdomen, often without the wearer noticing because the inner surface is already damp with sweat. A gown that is soaked at the front and the sleeves is no longer a barrier even though it is still intact, and the only remedy is to leave the field, remove the gown, perform hand hygiene, and put on a fresh one. The common error is to keep working in a wet gown because the case is nearly finished, and the second common error is to tie the gown at the front, which pulls the sterile front panel open and exposes the wearer. The characteristic warning is the cold, heavy feeling of a saturated sleeve and the visible darkening that spreads upward from the cuff, and both are signals to change rather than reasons to continue.

A surgical glove is a thin elastomer film that is stretched over the hand, and its protection depends on the film staying continuous under tension. Natural rubber latex is the most elastic of the common materials, so it conforms to the fingers, seals at the wrist, and recovers its shape after being stretched, but its proteins are the cause of the most serious glove allergy. Nitrile is a synthetic rubber that resists oils, punctures, and many chemicals better than latex and contains no natural rubber protein, which makes it the usual choice for the latex-sensitive wearer, though it is less elastic and tears more readily at a sharp edge. Vinyl is the least expensive and the weakest barrier, since it is stiff and tears easily. The strength of any film is stated as a tensile measurement, a resistance to puncture, and an average thickness that is often near 0.2 millimeter at the fingertip, and every such specification must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Gloves are supplied sterile and non-sterile, powdered and non-powdered, and the choice is a clinical decision rather than a matter of convenience. A sterile surgical glove is packed as a pair, sized to the hand, and put on by the closed technique so that the outer surface is never touched by bare skin, while an examination glove is supplied in a box and may be non-sterile. Powder was once used to make a tight glove easier to pull on, but the particles it leaves in a wound can provoke inflammation and adhesion formation, and the powder also carries latex protein into the air and into the tissues, so non-powdered gloves are now the standard wherever they are available. Size matters physically, because a glove that is too tight restricts the blood flow of the hand, tires the fingers, and tears at the cuff, while one that is too loose wrinkles at the fingertips and reduces the sense of touch. Double gloving adds a second film that can be removed when it is contaminated. The common error is to choose a glove by habit rather than by fit, and the characteristic warning of a failing glove is the sudden looseness, the damp feeling at a fingertip, or a visible tear at the web of the thumb.

The allergy problem is a hazard of the glove itself, and it takes two distinct forms that must not be confused. Immediate hypersensitivity to natural rubber latex proteins is a systemic reaction that can begin within minutes of contact and can progress to difficulty in breathing, a fall in blood pressure, and shock, and it is a medical emergency rather than a skin complaint. Delayed contact dermatitis is a reaction to the chemicals used to accelerate the curing of the rubber, and it appears hours later as redness and itching at the back of the hands. Powder increases the risk of the immediate form because it binds the protein and carries it into the air as an aerosol that a sensitive person can inhale, so a facility that cares for a latex-sensitive patient or worker should remove powdered gloves from the area entirely. The response to a known latex allergy is a latex-free environment, with nitrile or another synthetic glove for every person in the room. The characteristic warning is the itching, the rash, or the wheeze that appears soon after a glove is put on, and the common error is to dismiss it as dryness of the skin.

The cap, the mask, and the shoe cover are small barriers with specific jobs, and each has a physical reason for its shape. A surgical cap contains hair and skin scales so that they do not fall into the field, and it must cover the whole crown rather than sit on the back of the head. A mask filters the air that passes through it by three mechanisms, direct interception of larger particles, inertial impaction of particles that cannot follow the airstream around the fibers, and diffusion of the smallest particles, and a layer of electrostatic charge increases the capture of the fine ones, which is why a mask must not be crushed, folded, or wetted before use. A surgical mask is tested on particles of about 1 micrometer and is expected to capture a high proportion of them, and the exact efficiency figure must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The mask works in two directions at once, protecting the wearer from a splash and the patient from the wearer's own respiratory droplets, and a mask worn below the nose, left hanging around the neck, or reused for a second case does neither. A valved mask makes exhalation easier but allows unfiltered air to leave, so it is not a substitute for source control in a sterile field.

The counting system is an accounting device, and it exists because a human being cannot see the whole of a wound and cannot remember every item that passes through it. Counting works by comparison, since a number recorded before the case begins is compared with a number recorded at fixed points during and after it, and that comparison is the only evidence available that nothing has been left behind. The items counted are the instruments on the tray, the swabs and sponges used to mop and to pack, the needles loaded onto holders, and the blades fitted to handles. A count is not a formality but a control, and its reliability depends on the same principle as any other measurement, namely that the same method is used every time so that a difference means something. The count must be written down rather than remembered, and the record must stay visible to both counters.

The count is taken at fixed moments, and the moments are chosen so that no item can leave the field unrecorded. The first count is taken before the incision, when the tray is complete and the field is still clean, and it establishes the number against which every later count is compared. The second is taken before a cavity or a deep layer is closed, the third before the skin is closed, and the fourth at the end of the procedure, and a local protocol may add a count when a new item is added or a member of the team changes. The scrub person and the circulating person count together, aloud, item by item, using the same order and the same names every time. Interruptions spoil a count, so the counting pair stops speaking to anyone else until the number is agreed. Swabs are counted by type and by number, needles are counted as loaded and as returned, and a needle that has been bent or broken is kept and counted as a separate item. Every count threshold and every check point a local protocol defines must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

When a count does not match, the discrepancy is treated as a retained item until it is proved otherwise, and the proof is never the word of a tired person. The first action is to stop the closure, to keep the field and all the used materials untouched, and to recount the same category in the same way. The second action is a search of the field, the floor, the drapes, the suction canister, the trash, and the linen, and the third is a search of the patient, which is a clinical decision made by the surgeon. A radiograph is taken when the missing item is radiopaque and the search has failed, and the patient is not closed and the team is not dismissed until the item is found or its absence is established by an agreed method. The incident is documented with the item named and the times of each count. The characteristic alarm of the counting system is precisely this mismatch, and the common error is to accept a verbal explanation such as the swab is probably in the bin without looking in the bin.

A retained surgical item is called a never event because it should not happen at all, and its mechanism is always the same combination of a small object, a deep cavity, and a count that was believed rather than performed. A sponge is the commonest offender because it is soft, it changes shape when it is soaked, and it may be indistinguishable from the tissue around it once it is buried, while a needle, a blade, a screw, or a broken instrument tip can travel beyond the reach of a finger. The consequence is not merely a foreign body, since a retained sponge can cause infection, obstruction, and erosion into a vessel or a bowel. The radiograph is the instrument that finds such an item when the search of the field has failed, and its physics is the same absorption of X-rays that produces every other image. A radiopaque marker is woven into or bonded onto a surgical sponge so that it casts a distinct shadow, and the marker must be dense enough to be seen through the patient and the table. A single film of the whole operative area is taken, and in some protocols a second view is added in another direction, because an object can be hidden along one projection and revealed along another. The technique has limits, since a very small needle fragment or a non-radiopaque material may be invisible. Every exposure setting, projection, and detection threshold used in this search must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the common error is to accept an unread film as evidence that the field is clear.

Reusable drapes and gowns are reprocessed rather than discarded, and their life is limited by the gradual loss of the barrier that makes them worth keeping. The laundering cycle uses water at a temperature set by the protocol, commonly between 60 and 71 degrees Celsius, a detergent chosen for the fabric, and a rinse that removes the residue of both, and every temperature, concentration, and cycle time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Heat and mechanical agitation shrink the fibers, open the weave, and wear away any water-repellent finish, so a gown or a drape that has been through many cycles looks sound and still lets fluid through. Reusable woven textiles are inspected after every wash for holes, thin areas, broken seams, and lost ties, and the person inspecting must hold the fabric against a light to see the weave that the eye misses in a folded pile. Patches, darns, and replacements of ties and hook-and-loop closures are repairs of convenience rather than of barrier. The number of wash cycles a fabric can tolerate is stated by its maker, and the count of cycles must be recorded so that the item is withdrawn before its barrier fails.

Items labeled for single use are not designed to survive reprocessing, and the reason is physical rather than commercial. A single-use drape is often made from a light non-woven fabric whose fibers are bonded with a finish that is removed by detergent and heat, and a single-use gown may have a coating that cracks when it is dried and folded again. A single-use glove cannot be laundered, because the film absorbs the detergent, loses its elasticity, and develops pinholes that are invisible to the wearer, so a washed glove is a false barrier. The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has set conditions under which some single-use devices may be reprocessed by a regulated third party, and those conditions include validation of cleaning, sterilization, and function. The World Health Organization advises against the reuse of single-use items wherever supplies allow an alternative, and the practical rule is that an item must be reprocessed only when its maker permits it and when the method has been validated. Every such permission and every cycle parameter must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the common error is to treat a package that still looks intact as proof that the item inside is still sterile.

Sterile drapes, gowns, and gloves arrive in packs that are themselves devices, since the pack must let the sterilizing agent in, keep organisms out, and open without contaminating what is inside. The central sterile services department (CSSD) assembles the pack, adds a chemical indicator that changes with the cycle and a biological indicator where the protocol requires one, and records the load so that every item can be traced to the cycle that processed it. The pack is stored in a dry, cool, dust-free place, handled gently, and kept away from sunlight, because compression, moisture, and ultraviolet light all degrade the wrap and the contents. A pack that is dropped, punctured, damp, or past its expiration date is treated as unsterile. Opening the pack is a technique in itself, since the outer wrap is peeled back without reaching over the sterile contents, and the sterile person lifts the item from the inside of the wrap. A hospital steam autoclave for these packs commonly runs at 134 degrees Celsius for about 3 minutes or at 121 degrees Celsius for about 15 minutes, values that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The characteristic warning of a storage failure is a wrap that feels damp, a seal that has lifted, or a stain on the paper, and the common error is to use the last pack in the cupboard because the cupboard is empty.

The field version of the sterile field begins with the recognition that a mobile clinic cannot carry a sterile pack for every case and often has no autoclave at all. The drape becomes a woven cotton sheet or a length of cloth that is washed, boiled, dried in the sun, and folded for the next patient, and its barrier is weak because a woven fabric wicks fluid along its threads. What the field version gives up is the non-woven barrier with a measured resistance to liquid, the adhesive edge that seals to the skin, the fenestration that frames the incision, and the single-use pack that guarantees a known performance. What it demands instead is a discipline of its own, since the cloth must be washed with soap and clean water, rinsed, boiled for the full time the local protocol requires, often 15 to 20 minutes after the water returns to a rolling boil, and dried completely in direct sunlight or over a fire. That boiling time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, because a cloth that is merely warm is not sterile and a cloth that is still damp will grow organisms in storage. The field operator must also accept a smaller field and a shorter case, because the barrier is only trustworthy while it is dry. The common error is to hang a damp cloth in a closed cupboard overnight and to treat it as ready in the morning.

In the field, the gown is often a clean cotton gown or a plastic apron, and the glove is the item that decides how safe the case can be. A clean cotton gown can be washed and boiled and will serve as a barrier against gross contamination, but it is not a fluid barrier, so the wearer must avoid pressing against a wet field and must change it when it is soaked. A glove is the one item that must never be reused, because the film stretches, loses its elasticity, and hides pinholes that no eye can see, so a washed glove is a false promise of protection. What can be washed is the woven cloth, the cotton gown, the reusable instrument wrap, and the linen, and what must be burned is the single-use item that cannot be cleaned, including the used swab, the soiled dressing, and the used examination glove. What must never be reused is the needle, the blade, the syringe, the single-use glove, and the single-use drape or gown whose wrap has been opened, because their safety depends on a property that washing cannot restore. The characteristic warning in this setting is the same as in the hospital, a wet gown, a torn glove, and a count that does not reconcile, and the common error is to stretch a limited supply of gloves by reusing them.

Counting in the field is harder because there are fewer people and fewer items. A single operator may be both the scrub person and the circulating person, so the count must be written on a card or a board that stays in view and must be repeated aloud. The field count starts before the incision, is repeated before every closure, and is finished before the wound is dressed. The radiograph that a hospital uses to find a retained item is often unavailable, and when it is unavailable the operator must rely on a careful search, on the use of a fixed number of swabs with a known total, and on the rule that no closure begins until the count reconciles. Every count threshold and every local protocol for a missing item must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, because the field version has no second system to catch what the first one missed. The common error is to count from memory at the end of a long day, when fatigue makes the number agree with what the operator hopes rather than with what is on the tray, and the characteristic warning is a swab that cannot be placed.

To summarize, the sterile field is a boundary that is held by drapes, gowns, and gloves, and it is defeated by fluid rather than by visible dirt. Drapes and gowns work as mechanical screens and as liquid barriers, and their performance is stated in grams per square meter and in a resistance to water under pressure, values that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Woven fabrics wick along their threads, non-woven fabrics block by random fibers, adhesive edges close the gap to the skin, and a dry intact barrier becomes a channel the moment fluid reaches the far side. Gloves are elastomer films whose protection depends on continuity and fit, and their materials carry different risks, with natural rubber latex able to cause an immediate and dangerous allergy while nitrile avoids the protein. Caps, masks, and shoe covers complete the barrier, and a mask protects in both directions only when it is fitted, dry, and worn over the nose and mouth. The counting system is an accounting device rather than a ritual, and it works by comparing a written number taken before the incision with numbers taken before the closure of a cavity, before the skin is closed, and at the end, with a mismatch treated as a retained item until a search and, where it is available, a radiograph prove otherwise. In the field, cloth is washed, boiled, and dried, the count is kept on a card by one pair of hands, and the glove is the one item that must never be reused, because a barrier that has lost its property cannot be restored by hope.
