# Chapter 9 — Vascular Access

Every route into the circulation begins with one physical idea, a tube placed inside a blood vessel so that fluid can travel along a path that skin would otherwise seal. Flow through a narrow tube depends enormously on its width, because the radius enters the resistance calculation raised to the fourth power. Halve the internal diameter and resistance rises about sixteen times, so the difference between two neighboring gauge sizes is far larger than the number on the package suggests. Around that idea sit a pressure gradient, a length of soft tubing, a vessel wall, and a fluid with its own thickness, and any one of them can defeat the others. A clinician who cannot draw blood back through a fine cannula should suspect the diameter before the syringe.

A vein is not a rigid pipe but a soft collapsible tube with thin walls, low pressure, and valves, so it flattens when tissue presses inward. An artery is the opposite object, with a thick muscular wall, a high pulsatile pressure, and a deeper position that makes it harder to find and more dangerous to puncture. Because a vein collapses, access depends on filling it, and the tourniquet does this by blocking outflow while leaving inflow open. Left in place too long, it raises the pressure until fluid leaks into the tissue around the vein. Every tourniquet time and pressure limit must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Vascular access is the family of devices that make a temporary or long-lasting door into that system. The hospital keeps a graded inventory: short plastic cannulas for the hand and forearm, long catheters whose tips rest in the great veins near the heart, midline catheters that stop short of the chest, implanted ports hidden beneath the skin, stiff arterial catheters for pressure watching, and intraosseous needles that enter the marrow rather than a vessel. Each step buys reliability, duration, or monitoring, and also a larger infection risk and a greater chance of serious harm. The choice is never the biggest device that fits but the smallest that will do the job for as long as the job requires. Each rung is described here first as the hospital device and then as the field variant used in a mobile or frontier clinic.

The peripheral cannula is the most common device in the hospital and the one most often taken for granted. It is a thin plastic tube mounted over a hollow metal needle, with a transparent flashback chamber at the back and an angled bevel at the tip. The chamber fills with blood the instant the tip enters the vein, and the operator then advances the plastic off the needle and connects the hub to a cap or an extension set. The design carries three jobs at once: cut a small hole, leave a soft tube where the metal was, and seal against the skin so nothing leaks out or enters. The operator who feels resistance is usually feeling the vessel wall rather than the lumen.

Cannulas are named by gauge, and the naming runs opposite to intuition, because a larger gauge number means a smaller internal diameter. A 14 gauge cannula is for rapid fluid or blood, a 20 gauge is an everyday adult size, and a 26 gauge is fine enough for a fragile neonatal vein. Each gauge carries a colored hub that follows a published international color code, so orange, grey, green, pink, blue, yellow, and violet each belong to a size, and the color is a label rather than a guarantee. Standard lengths run from about 2 to 5 centimeters, and a stiff plastic threads easily while a softer polyurethane is kinder to the vein wall over days. Every gauge, length, and material choice must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The veins that suit a cannula are the ones a person can see and feel, such as the dorsal veins of the hand, the forearm veins, and the large vein in front of the elbow. The rule is to start as far from the heart as the treatment allows and to move toward it only when a distant site has failed, because each failed attempt scars the vessel it touched. A vein that is firm, cord-like, red, or painful is already inflamed, and skin that is broken, infected, or burned is never a site. Certain limbs are off limits, including an arm with a dialysis fistula, an arm on the same side as a removed breast with lymph nodes taken, a paralyzed arm, and a limb swollen from poor lymphatic drainage. A cannula placed there can destroy the only route a patient has for dialysis.

Insertion is a sequence of small decisions rather than one motion. The operator fills the vein with a tourniquet, chooses the straightest segment, cleans the skin and lets the antiseptic dry, and enters at a shallow angle with the bevel facing up. The flash of blood confirms the tip is in the lumen, and only then is the plastic advanced while the needle is held still. The common operator error is to push the whole assembly forward after the flash, which drives the needle through the far wall and leaves the plastic outside the vein. Another is to withdraw the needle while the plastic is only partly in, which drags the sharp point back through the soft tube and shaves its tip.

The hazard that belongs with every sharp placed in a vein is the injury the operator can suffer while placing it. A needle that has just been inside a patient carries that patient's blood, and a hand that recaps it or drops it into a pocket transfers the risk to whoever handles laundry or waste. A safety cannula that retracts or shields its needle reduces this, but its protection is defeated by habit, whether by holding the device in the mouth, laying it on the bed, or engaging the safety too late. The discipline is one sharp, one use, and immediate disposal into a rigid container at the point of use. The needle is never recapped, bent, or removed from the plastic by hand.

Keeping a cannula in place is a mechanical problem rather than a clinical one. Every time the patient moves a wrist or flexes an elbow, the tube pivots in the skin, the tip pistons in the vessel, and the entry point slowly widens. A transparent dressing lets the operator see the site without lifting it, and a manufactured stabilization device grips the hub and spreads the pull across a wider area of skin. Tape alone is a poor substitute, because it loosens when damp and a loop around a limb can act as a tourniquet. The dressing is changed when it is soiled or loose, and the site is inspected every shift. Dressing intervals, often every 5 to 7 days for a transparent dressing and every 2 days for gauze, must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Extravasation is the failure mode that turns a routine cannula into an emergency. It happens when the tip leaves the lumen and fluid continues to flow into the tissue, because the pump keeps pushing while the pressure it senses is still below its occlusion threshold. The early signs are pain, a cool or taut swelling, and a slowing infusion, while the later signs are blistering, blanching, and a hard area that may go on to die. Some medicines are vesicants, meaning they destroy tissue on contact, which makes extravasation a chemical burn rather than a mechanical one. The characteristic alarm is the occlusion alarm of the infusion device, and it sounds late, after the tissue is already flooded. The common operator error is to reset the pump and continue when the rate falls, instead of stopping, disconnecting, and looking at the limb.

Flushing a catheter is a small procedure with real physics behind it. The pressure a syringe generates depends on the force of the hand and on the area of the plunger, so a small syringe concentrates the same force onto a smaller area and produces a much higher pressure than a large one. A 1 milliliter syringe can therefore exceed the pressure a thin catheter wall can tolerate, rupturing the catheter or forcing clot into the circulation. A 10 milliliter syringe is usually the safe adult choice, and that size and the maximum pressure stated by the maker must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A steady push with a brief pause clears the catheter wall better than a smooth stream, and finishing with positive pressure prevents blood from being drawn back into the tip.

After the flush comes the lock, the small volume of fluid left in the catheter to keep it open between uses. Two solutions dominate: ordinary saline, and saline with a small amount of the blood thinner heparin. The choice depends on the device and the patient, and a typical heparin lock may contain 10 to 100 units per milliliter in a volume of 3 to 5 milliliters, figures that vary so widely they must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The connector at the end of the line also touches blood, so it is scrubbed with antiseptic and allowed to dry before every entry, a practice called scrubbing the hub. Needleless connectors reduce sharp injuries but add a hidden surface where blood can stagnate, and their scrub time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A cannula that stays in place can inflame the vein, a condition called phlebitis, through chemical irritation from the fluid and mechanical irritation from the tube. The classic signs are pain along the vein, redness, warmth, and a palpable cord under the skin. Infection can follow phlebitis, and bloodstream infection from even a peripheral cannula is possible, which is why the site is inspected every shift. The device is removed at the first sign of phlebitis, infiltration, or leakage, and it is removed as soon as it is no longer needed rather than left in place as insurance. Routine replacement intervals, often every 72 to 96 hours, must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The central venous catheter is the next rung, and the physical difference is position rather than size. It is a long soft tube inserted through a large vein in the neck, the chest, or the groin and advanced until its tip rests in the great vein just above the heart, where flow is fast and the vessel is wide. That position makes it useful: a medicine that would burn a small vein is diluted in a torrent of blood, fluid can be given at a high rate, and the pressure inside the chest can be measured directly. Central catheters are described by their number of separate channels, or lumens, usually 1 to 4, and by their length, often 15 to 30 centimeters. Each lumen has its own hub and lock, and a mistake in one can contaminate the others. The number of lumens, the length, and the French size, often 4 to 8 French, must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The sites used for a central catheter each carry their own anatomy and their own characteristic harm. The internal jugular vein runs down the side of the neck beside the carotid artery, and the operator can often see it, but the artery lies close and can be entered by mistake. The subclavian vein passes under the collarbone, which gives a stable tunnel that is hard to infect, but the needle travels near the top of the lung and can cause a collapsed lung called pneumothorax. The femoral vein in the groin is easy to reach in an emergency, but it is close to the skin, moist, and colonized by bacteria, so it carries the highest infection risk. The choice among the three is guided by the patient's anatomy and the availability of ultrasound.

Almost all central catheters are placed by a technique named after Sven Seldinger, and each step exists to make the next one safer. A thin needle enters the vein, and a flexible wire is passed through it into the vessel, soft enough to follow the curve of the vein without tearing it and stiff enough to guide the rest. The needle is removed, a dilator is threaded over the wire to widen the track through the skin, and the catheter is slid over the wire into place before the wire is withdrawn. The wire is the safety line, because it keeps the track open and keeps the catheter on course. The common operator error is to lose control of the wire, either letting it advance into the heart or pulling it out while the catheter is short of position. A wire that cannot be accounted for is a reason to stop and image.

Because central catheters reach the heart, they carry the most serious infections in the hospital, and the insertion bundle is the set of practices that reduces them. The bundle usually means hand hygiene before and after, full sterile barrier including cap, mask, gown, and a large drape, skin preparation with an antiseptic that is allowed to dry, the widest possible choice of site, ultrasound guidance, and a daily review of whether the line is still needed. When ultrasound is used, the probe is enclosed in a sterile cover with sterile gel, because gel and probe surfaces that touch the skin can carry organisms into the track, and the cover and gel choice must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The antiseptic is frequently a chlorhexidine solution at 2 per cent, and its strength and required drying time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A central line placed in a hurry without a barrier will earn its infection within days.

Three hazards deserve to be named at the point of insertion rather than the point of harm. The first is the collapsed lung that follows a needle grazing the pleura, which is why the patient is watched and imaged after a subclavian or jugular attempt. The second is arterial puncture, which produces a bright red pulsatile return and, if unrecognized, leaves a catheter sitting in an artery. The third is air embolism, and its physics is the reason the patient is placed head down before a central line is opened, because the chest cavity has a slight negative pressure during breathing and an open hub below the height of the heart can suck air into the vein. The characteristic alarms are the air in line alarm of the infusion device and the low pressure alarm of a pressure monitoring system. Every alarm threshold and response protocol must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

After insertion, the position of the tip is confirmed before the line is used for anything that could injure the heart. A chest image shows the catheter against the anatomy, and the tip should sit at the junction of the great vein and the right atrium rather than inside the heart, where it can irritate the rhythm or perforate the wall. The tip can migrate later, because a cough, a deep breath, or a raised arm can move it several centimeters, which is why the external length is recorded at insertion. Daily care then keeps the outside world away from the blood: the dressing and connectors are changed on schedule, unused lumens are locked, and the necessity of the line is reviewed every day. Dressing and connector intervals, often every 5 to 7 days for the dressing and every 3 to 7 days for the connector, must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The peripherally inserted central catheter, PICC for short, is a long thin tube placed in a vein of the upper arm and threaded centrally until its tip reaches the same great vein as a conventional central catheter. Its physics is a compromise: it avoids the neck and chest entirely, so it cannot cause a collapsed lung, but it travels a longer distance through smaller veins, so it more readily causes a clot along its path. The vein is measured with an ultrasound probe, and the catheter is chosen to occupy only a fraction of the vein's width, because a catheter that fills the vessel leaves no room for blood to flow. A typical PICC is 50 to 60 centimeters long and 4 to 6 French in diameter, and those figures and the measurement method must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A PICC suits weeks to months of therapy.

The midline catheter and the implanted port sit on either side of the PICC in duration and complexity. A midline is placed in the same arm veins but is shorter, and its tip stops in the upper arm rather than the chest, so it cannot carry the concentrated solutions that a central tip can dilute, though it suits ordinary antibiotics or fluids over one to four weeks. An implanted port is a small chamber with a self-sealing rubber septum placed beneath the skin, which gives it a lower infection risk and almost no care between uses, so it suits months or years of intermittent therapy. Access requires a special non-coring needle whose side opening and flat tip push through the septum without cutting a plug of rubber, while an ordinary sharp needle will core the septum and destroy the seal. The common operator errors are to treat a midline as a central line and to access a port without confirming the needle is fully seated.

The arterial line is a different instrument because it measures rather than delivers. A small stiff catheter sits in an artery, connected by short stiff tubing full of saline to a transducer, a chamber with a flexible diaphragm that moves as the pressure wave arrives and converts that movement into an electrical signal. The monitor draws the wave and derives the systolic, diastolic, and mean pressures from it, and the shape of the wave carries information no number can hold. Two rituals make the reading trustworthy: leveling the transducer at the height of the heart, and zeroing it to the atmosphere so the monitor knows where zero is. The leveling point is often described as the phlebostatic axis at the fourth intercostal space, and that landmark, the zeroing procedure, and the flush pressure must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The tubing in an arterial system must be short, stiff, and free of bubbles, because of the physics of a vibrating column of fluid. A soft or bubbly system absorbs the sharp edge of the pressure wave, a fault called damping, and it understates systolic pressure while overstating diastolic. A system that is too stiff or too long can instead ring, a fault called resonance, which exaggerates the systolic peak. The square wave test reveals both: the operator briefly opens the fast flush valve, sending a flat-topped pressure step into the tubing, and watches how the trace settles. A good system settles in one or two small oscillations, a damped system climbs slowly and flattens, and a resonant one oscillates for several cycles. The flush pressure and the test criteria must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The hazards of an arterial line belong with the line itself. If the tubing disconnects at the hub, the artery can bleed quickly and, under a drape or a sleeve, invisibly, so the line is always secured, labeled, and inspected. If the catheter blocks, the tissue beyond it loses its blood supply, which is why the fingers or toes are checked for color, warmth, and pulse every shift. If air enters during a flush, it can travel to the hand or, rarely, to the brain, so the line is never left open to the air. The characteristic alarm is the low pressure alarm, which may signal a disconnected line, and the common operator error is to silence it before looking at the arm. Sampling from the same line requires the syringe to be prepared with a small amount of heparin, any air bubble to be removed at once because air changes the gas values, and the puncture site to be pressed firmly after the needle is withdrawn, typically for 5 minutes and for 10 minutes or more when blood clots slowly, times that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Intraosseous access ignores veins entirely, and its principle is that the marrow cavity of a long bone is a rigid chamber filled with a spongy network of small vessels that does not collapse when the circulation fails. In shock, the peripheral veins empty, but the marrow space still connects to the central circulation through veins that do not depend on blood pressure, so a needle through the tough outer cortex reaches the heart even when every surface vein has vanished. The device is either a manual needle with a removable trocar or a powered drill that spins a designed needle through the cortex in seconds, and needle sizes run from about 15 gauge to 18 gauge with lengths from about 15 to 45 millimeters, chosen by tissue depth and site. In adults the usual sites are the upper shin bone below the knee, the upper arm bone, and the lower shin bone above the ankle, while in children the shin bone is first choice. Correct placement is confirmed by the needle standing without support and by a flush that runs without swelling the tissue, and every size, depth, and site rule must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The complications of intraosseous access are the price of its speed. Fluid or medicine that leaks out of the cavity can raise the pressure in the muscle compartment and cut off its blood supply, the bone can become infected with osteomyelitis, and a poorly aimed needle can fracture it. The characteristic alarm is the high pressure alarm of the infusion device, which sounds when fluid is going into tissue instead of marrow, and the common operator error is to accept a needle that wobbles or a flush that meets resistance. A needle left in place is also a route for infection, so it is removed as soon as a reliable intravenous route is established, usually within 24 hours, and that dwell time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. In a frontier clinic the ladder is shortened, and intraosseous access becomes the emergency default, because there is often no ultrasound probe, no experienced operator, and no time to wait. A powered drill depends on batteries that lose their charge silently in heat, so spare cells and a manual needle are carried together, and every battery state must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Peripheral access in the field is a skill of the hands rather than the screen. Without an ultrasound probe, the operator relies on a tourniquet, gravity, warmth, and gentle tapping to make a vein visible, and on the fingertips to judge its depth. The external jugular vein in the neck is often visible when arm veins have collapsed, but it lies close to the carotid artery and the airway, and when nothing can be found the clinician must decide quickly between an intraosseous needle, a surgical cutdown, and transfer. Central access in a frontier clinic is rare and should be rare, because a cutdown demands sterile gloves, an anesthetic, a scalpel, forceps, and a catheter that may be improvised poorly. Securement changes too, because transparent dressings and sterile applicators are replaced by gauze and tape, which hold moisture, loosen when wet, and can tear fragile skin. A port or a PICC without its proper supplies is often best left alone until the patient reaches a better facility, and every substitute considered must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The consumables of vascular access are many, and their storage decides whether they work. They include cannulas in every gauge, central catheter kits with their wires and dilators, PICCs and midlines, non-coring needles, arterial catheters and transducer sets, intraosseous needles, syringes, flush vials, antiseptics, dressings, securement devices, and tourniquets, and most are single use, so a torn or damp wrapper is a discard. Heat and sunlight age the plastic and dry the lubricant on a needle. Cleaning divides these items into two groups: needles, cannulas, catheters, flush vials, and dressings are used once and discarded, while transducers, tourniquets, and probes are cleaned or disinfected according to their own instructions, and any reprocessing method must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Maintenance is the maintenance of attention: each shift the operator checks the site, the labels, the locks, and the spare stock. The failure modes form a short catalogue, in which the catheter can leave the vessel, block, inflame the vein, become infected, admit air, or injure the operator, and each is made more likely by haste and an unclean hub.

To summarize, vascular access is a ladder of devices built on one physical truth, that flow through a narrow tube depends on its width. The peripheral cannula is the everyday tool, placed by hand, secured by dressing, flushed and locked with care. The central catheter reaches the great veins and buys dilution and monitoring at the price of infection, collapsed lung, and air embolism. The peripherally inserted central catheter and the midline trade location for duration, the implanted port hides beneath the skin and needs its special needle, the arterial line measures rather than delivers and lives or dies by leveling and zeroing, and the intraosseous needle finds the one space that does not collapse. In a frontier clinic the ladder shortens to the devices that need no power, no imaging, and no sterile supply chain, and intraosseous access becomes the emergency default. Whatever the setting, the operator's duties are the same: confirm every setting and threshold against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol, know the hazard before it happens, look at the patient before the screen, and remove the device as soon as it is no longer needed.
