# Chapter 16 — Ventilation

Breathing is a mechanical event before it is a chemical one, and every machine in this chapter reproduces that event from outside the body. A healthy person breathes because the diaphragm drops and the rib cage lifts, lowering the pressure inside the chest below the pressure of the atmosphere, so gas flows down that gradient into the alveoli and expiration follows as the elastic lung recoils. The work of breathing is the price of moving gas against the resistance of the airways and the stiffness of the lung and chest wall, and illness raises that price until the patient cannot pay it. A ventilator is any device that takes over or supports that work, and the manual bag, the transport machine, and the intensive care unit ventilator are all members of one family. Pressure, volume, flow, and time are the four quantities behind every setting on these machines.

A stiff lung and chest wall need a large pressure to move a small volume, which is called compliance, while narrow airways force gas to be pushed harder for the same flow, which is called resistance. Compliance and resistance together set a time constant, so a lung with a long time constant needs more time to exhale before the next breath. A ventilator can control the volume it delivers and let the pressure rise as it will, or control the pressure and let the volume follow, and these are the two fundamental strategies of mechanical breathing. A fixed volume pushed into a stiff lung raises the pressure, and a fixed pressure into a stiff lung lowers the volume, so the same dial setting produces different results in different patients and the patient must be watched rather than the number.

The self-inflating bag is the most important breathing device in the world, because it works when everything else has stopped. It is a bag of flexible rubber or silicone that springs back into shape after it is squeezed, and that elastic recoil is its physical principle, because the re-expanding bag draws gas in, which the operator then pushes through a one-way valve into the patient. Sizes follow the patient: a small bag of about 250 milliliters for a newborn, a medium bag of about 500 milliliters for a child, and a large bag of about 1600 milliliters for an adult, values that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The bag needs no compressed gas and no electricity, and it delivers room air to a patient whose lungs have stopped moving, but a small bag used on an adult will not ventilate adequately and a large bag used on a newborn can injure the lung.

Three parts of the self-inflating bag fail in their own ways. The patient valve is a one-way valve at the mask or tube end that lets gas into the patient and directs exhaled gas out through a side port, so if it sticks or is assembled wrongly the patient receives little or nothing. The inlet valve opens as the bag refills so that fresh gas enters and exhaled gas does not return, and a torn or dirty inlet valve lets the patient rebreathe carbon dioxide. The reservoir is a soft bag or corrugated sleeve behind the inlet valve that holds a reserve of oxygen, and the oxygen tubing feeds it from a cylinder or wall outlet at a flow of 10 to 15 liters per minute, a range that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, so a bag run without its reservoir delivers only room air.

Before use the operator should squeeze it with the patient port occluded and watch for the valve to move, listen for the leak of a cracked bag, and confirm that the reservoir fills. The common error is to hold the mask loosely against the face so that most of the gas escapes around the seal, and the correct technique is to lift the jaw forward with one hand while the other holds the mask down and squeezes. Squeezing too fast and too deeply drives gas into the stomach, causes vomiting and aspiration, and raises the pressure in the chest until the blood returning to the heart falls. A bag can also be reassembled with its valves reversed by a well-meaning cleaner, so a reprocessed bag must be tested before it returns to the trolley, and reusable bags are washed, disinfected to the highest level appropriate to their material, dried, and stored open.

The flow-inflating bag looks like a self-inflating bag and behaves completely differently, and knowing the difference is a mark of competence. It is soft and thin-walled, with no elastic recoil, so it does not refill by itself; it stays partly inflated only while gas flows into it continuously, and the operator controls the pressure by how firmly the patient port is occluded with the thumb. Squeezing raises the pressure, releasing the thumb lets the lung empty, and the feel of the bag tells the operator how well the lung is filling, which is why it is prized in anesthesia for children and for patients with poor compliance. It can supply continuous positive airway pressure by itself, but if its gas supply fails the bag deflates and the patient receives nothing, and the common error is to wait for it to refill on its own.

The transport ventilator carries ventilation between the intensive care unit, the imaging suite, the operating room, and another hospital. Its gas may come from small cylinders of compressed oxygen and compressed air, or from a turbine that spins at high speed and draws the breath from room air while blending oxygen into it, which frees the machine from a compressed air supply and lets it run on a battery and a single oxygen cylinder. Battery life is measured in hours rather than days, with a typical endurance of 2 to 6 hours, a figure that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and it falls as the oxygen concentration, the positive end-expiratory pressure, and the minute volume rise. Its modes are deliberately few, usually volume control, pressure control, and a spontaneous or continuous positive airway pressure mode, without the fine synchrony of a full intensive care machine. The settings for tidal volume, respiratory rate, positive end-expiratory pressure, and inspired oxygen must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and rechecked after every move.

In a moving ambulance the flow sensor can drift, the circuit can disconnect at a hidden joint, and the oxygen cylinder can empty faster than the gauge suggests because the machine draws a higher flow than the planned minute volume. The characteristic alarms of the transport ventilator are the high-pressure alarm when the circuit kinks against a stretcher rail, the low-pressure or disconnect alarm when a fitting separates, and the low-battery alarm that gives only a short warning before the machine stops. The operator must test the machine with a test lung before leaving, confirm that the alarm is audible above the engine, and carry a self-inflating bag and a spare cylinder as the guaranteed fallback. The common error is to trust the display during the move instead of checking chest movement, oxygen saturation, and exhaled carbon dioxide, so a transport begun without this discipline leaves the patient watched by no reliable observer.

The intensive care unit ventilator is a life-support device of the same rank as a dialysis machine, and it deserves to be understood as a system rather than a box. Gas arrives from the wall pipeline as oxygen and as medical air, and a blender mixes them to the chosen fraction of inspired oxygen; valves then regulate flow, an inspiratory sensor measures the volume on its way to the patient, and an expiratory valve and a second sensor measure the gas coming back. The breath is generated by a piston, a bellows, a compressor with proportional valves, or a turbine, and most intensive care unit ventilators have an internal battery that allows 30 to 60 minutes of continued ventilation during a power failure, a figure that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The gas supply, the electricity, the battery, and the alarm system are all part of the therapy, and the machine must be calibrated and self-tested at intervals set by its maker.

The modes of ventilation are the rules by which the machine decides when to give a breath and how large it should be, and their names describe the rule rather than the disease. In assist-control mode the machine delivers a full breath at a set rate and also delivers one whenever the patient triggers it, so the patient can increase the rate but cannot reduce the size of the breath. In volume control the machine pushes a fixed tidal volume at a set flow over a set time, and the pressure rises or falls as the resistance and compliance of the lung dictate; a typical adult setting is 6 to 8 milliliters per kilogram of predicted body weight, a range that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. In pressure control the machine holds a fixed pressure above the baseline for a set time, and the volume delivered depends on the patient's lung, so a leak or a stiff lung produces a smaller breath without any change on the dial. The respiratory rate is set between about 10 and 20 breaths per minute in a typical adult, a range that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the fraction of inspired oxygen is set to the lowest value that achieves the target oxygen saturation.

Two further modes let the patient do more of the work and are used to separate a patient from the machine. Synchronized intermittent mandatory ventilation delivers a set number of full breaths, each synchronized to the patient's own effort so that the machine does not fight the patient, and between them the patient may breathe spontaneously. Pressure support adds a fixed boost to every spontaneous breath, and its level is usually adjusted between 5 and 20 centimeters of water, a range that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Positive end-expiratory pressure holds a baseline pressure in the airway at the end of expiration so that the alveoli do not collapse, and it is commonly started at 5 centimeters of water and adjusted upward, values that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. These modes demand a reliable respiratory drive, so they are not used in a deeply sedated or paralyzed patient, and the operator error is to choose a mode by habit rather than by the patient's effort, which produces a patient who fights the machine.

Three pressures inside a ventilated lung matter more than all the others. The peak inspiratory pressure is the highest reached during a breath, the plateau pressure is held briefly when flow stops and reflects the stretch of the alveoli, and the positive end-expiratory pressure is the baseline remaining at the end of expiration. A plateau pressure above 30 centimeters of water is associated with injury to the lung, a threshold that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the difference between the plateau pressure and the positive end-expiratory pressure, called the driving pressure, is kept as low as the patient allows. When a patient breathes rapidly and the lung has a long time constant, gas can be trapped and the end-expiratory pressure can rise above the set value, a condition called auto-positive end-expiratory pressure, which reduces the blood returning to the heart and lowers the blood pressure, and the high-pressure alarm is the signature of this problem.

Non-invasive ventilation supports breathing without a tube in the trachea, and its physics is the physics of a splint. Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) delivers a single steady pressure throughout the breath, usually set between 4 and 15 centimeters of water, a range that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and that pressure holds the small airways and alveoli open, reduces the work of breathing, and can keep an obstructed upper airway from collapsing during sleep. The interface decides the success of the therapy: a well-fitted nasal mask, an oronasal mask, a full-face mask, or a helmet, chosen for the patient's face and for the pressure required. A leak at the edge of a mask is not a small nuisance, because the machine cannot distinguish the gas that leaks from the gas the patient receives, so the pressure and the volume at the patient fall silently, and the common error is to tighten the straps until the skin breaks down.

Bilevel positive airway pressure (BIPAP) adds a second pressure to the idea of CPAP, and that second pressure is what makes it a ventilator. The higher pressure, called the inspiratory positive airway pressure, helps the breath enter, and the lower pressure, called the expiratory positive airway pressure, keeps the airway open during expiration, so the difference between them is the amount of support the patient receives. Typical settings are an inspiratory pressure between 8 and 20 centimeters of water and an expiratory pressure between 4 and 10 centimeters of water, values that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and because the patient triggers every breath the mode is comfortable and is widely used for acute exacerbations of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and for pulmonary edema. The hazards are those of CPAP and are worse, including a leaking mask, a patient who vomits into a sealed interface, and a stomach distended by gas, and a patient who is drowsy or not improving within the first hour needs reassessment for an invasive tube.

The circuit is the set of tubes that carries gas from the machine to the patient and back, and its design has clinical consequences. A dual-limb circuit has a separate inspiratory limb and expiratory limb joined at a Y-piece, with one-way valves that keep the two streams apart, and it allows the exhaled volume to be measured accurately. A single-limb circuit carries gas to the patient and relies on a deliberate leak at the mask or an exhalation valve near the patient to let gas out, and it is lighter and easier for non-invasive ventilation but less precise for monitoring. The tube is corrugated plastic that can stretch, kink, or disconnect, and its expiratory limb collects water as the warm humidified gas cools, so the circuit must be positioned so that condensate drains away from the airway and replaced at the interval the maker and the local protocol specify.

A ventilator delivers dry gas, and the nose and upper airway that normally warm and moisten inspired air are bypassed by the tube, so humidity must be added. The heated humidifier passes the gas over a chamber of sterile water on a heated plate, and a temperature probe at the patient end controls the plate so that the gas arrives warm and saturated; the usual target is a temperature near 37 degrees Celsius at the airway, a value that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The alternative is a heat and moisture exchanger, a small cartridge of foam or paper between the tube and the circuit that captures heat and water from the exhaled gas and returns them to the next breath, and it needs no power and no water but adds resistance and dead space. The heated humidifier risks a burn to the airway if the probe fails, and the exchanger risks blockage when secretions dry inside it, so water in the circuit, a dry chamber, and a cartridge left beyond its rated life are three of the most preventable faults in ventilation.

Filters sit at the boundary between the patient and the machine and do two jobs at once, protecting the patient from the machine and the machine from the patient. A mechanical filter is a dense mat of fibers that traps particles and microorganisms, and an electrostatic filter uses a charged medium to attract particles too small to be caught mechanically. A filter in the inspiratory limb protects the patient, while a filter in the expiratory limb, or at the expiratory valve, protects the machine and the ward, and in a patient with a transmissible respiratory infection the expiratory filter is the single most important infection control measure in the circuit. Filters have a rated life measured in hours or days, a limit that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and a wet, blocked, reused, or autoclaved filter is no longer a filter, so every filter should be inspected at the start of each shift.

The alarms of a ventilator are its language, and every one names a specific physical event rather than a vague malfunction. The high-pressure alarm sounds when the pressure in the circuit rises above the set threshold, and it usually means a kink, a blocked tube, secretions, a cough, or a stiffening lung, while the low-pressure or disconnect alarm sounds when the pressure or volume falls below the threshold and means a leak, a separated circuit, a displaced tube, or a patient who has stopped triggering the machine. The apnea alarm reports that the patient has stopped breathing for longer than the set interval, usually 15 to 30 seconds, a threshold that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the inspired oxygen concentration alarm, the power failure alarm, and the battery alarm each report a different failure whose threshold must also be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The characteristic operator error is to silence an alarm without looking at the patient, so the correct habit is to go to the patient first, check the chest and the circuit, and then read the screen.

At the extreme end of respiratory support stands extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, a technology that removes gas exchange from the lung altogether. Blood is drained from a large vein, pumped through a membrane oxygenator that adds oxygen and removes carbon dioxide across a thin permeable film, warmed, and returned to the patient, so that the lung can rest while it heals or while a transplant is arranged. The circuit needs a pump, an oxygenator, a heat exchanger, large cannulae, and a supply of oxygen, and the patient usually needs continuous anticoagulation to keep the circuit from clotting. The infrastructure it demands is the real subject: a trained perfusion team at the bedside continuously, a supply of blood products, a laboratory that can measure clotting within minutes, a protected power supply, and a spare circuit ready to be exchanged, with blood flow rates of 2 to 5 liters per minute in adults that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A ventilator that is not maintained is a ventilator that will fail at the worst moment, and its maintenance is mostly a matter of consumables and of checking what wears. The breathing circuit, the humidifier chamber, the heat and moisture exchanger, the filters, the flow sensors, the expiratory valve membrane, the oxygen sensor, and the internal battery all have a limited life, and each has a replacement interval that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Flow sensors are delicate and are damaged by water, secretions, and rough handling, so a sensor that has been dropped or allowed to get wet must be recalibrated or replaced, and the internal battery ages quietly and may hold only a fraction of its rated time after a few years. The exterior, the screen, and the trolley are wiped with a compatible disinfectant, the intake filter is cleaned or changed on schedule, and the compressor, the valves, and the internal tubing belong to the biomedical technician, whose service intervals are set by the maker and recorded in the equipment register.

The pre-use check of a ventilator is a fixed sequence that follows the gas from the wall to the patient, and it takes only a few minutes when it is done in the same order every time. The operator confirms that the machine is plugged into the correct outlet, that the gas hoses are connected to oxygen and to medical air and not crossed, that the cylinder reserve is full, and that the internal battery is charged, and then inspects the circuit for cracks, for a secure connection at every joint, and for a water trap that is empty and correctly positioned. The machine is run through its self-test with a test lung attached, and the operator watches for a leak, for an accurate delivered volume, and for the alarm to sound when the test lung is squeezed or removed. The alarms are tested deliberately, because an alarm that cannot be made to sound will not sound when it matters, and the thresholds and the settings are checked against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use and against the written order for the patient.

In a mobile or frontier clinic the ventilator is a different animal, and the difference is measured in what it cannot do. The usual field machine is a compact turbine or compressor-driven device in a padded case, powered by a rechargeable battery and a solar panel, with a single gas inlet for oxygen enrichment and no dependence on a compressed air pipeline. It gives up the precise flow sensing, the fine synchrony, the full alarm set, and the control of humidity that a hospital machine provides, and it typically offers only volume control, pressure control, and a spontaneous or continuous positive airway pressure mode. Its oxygen consumption is often higher than the operator expects, so a cylinder that would last hours on a hospital machine may last a fraction of that time, and its battery life depends on the mode, the pressure, and the temperature, values that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

When the field ventilator is absent or has failed, the fallback is the manual bag, and around it a set of simpler supports a clinic can build. CPAP can be improvised for a newborn with a bubble circuit, in which the expiratory limb of a nasal interface is placed under a measured depth of sterile water so that the depth sets the pressure: a depth of 5 centimeters of water gives about 5 centimeters of water of pressure, a relationship that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The oxygen source may be a concentrator, whose limited flow must be shared with the circuit, and the interface is a short pair of nasal prongs that must not block the nose, while the circuit demands a steady supply of sterile water and a person who watches the chest and the bubble pattern continuously because the system has no alarms of its own. For an older child or an adult, a tight-fitting mask with a controlled leak and a continuous flow from a concentrator can provide a crude CPAP, but the pressure is unknown and the choice between a machine and a manual bag is often settled by the state of the battery.

The manual bag is what remains when everything else fails, and it is the one ventilator that has never needed electricity, a compressor, or a service contract. Its discipline is simple to state and hard to perform: the operator holds the mask with a proper seal, lifts the jaw, squeezes at a rate of about 10 to 12 breaths per minute for an adult, a rate that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and watches the chest rise with each squeeze rather than the bag. The volume delivered is judged by the visible chest movement and the feel of the bag, because a bag has no display and no flow sensor, and the common error is to squeeze harder than the lung can accept, so two rescuers and a change of operator every few minutes keep the quality of the breaths from falling. In a prolonged resuscitation the operator should watch for the stomach filling with gas, for a chest that rises on one side only, and for an oxygen saturation that fails to improve, because the manual bag is also the correct answer during a ventilator failure.

Maintenance in the field is a routine built from what is available, and it is the difference between a machine that lasts and a machine that becomes furniture. Filters are washed or replaced on the maker's schedule, batteries are cycled and stored charged, the circuit is inspected for cracks before every use, and the machine is protected from dust with a cover when it is not running. Cleaning follows the same principles everywhere: the external surfaces and the reusable patient interface are cleaned first to remove organic material, then disinfected with a compatible product, and then dried, while single-use items are discarded rather than reprocessed. Reusable components that are meant to be sterilized must be reprocessed by a validated method, with the cycle confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the written log of checks, faults, and repairs is the only way an absent engineer can understand what has been happening.

The hazards of mechanical ventilation are grouped around pressure, volume, oxygen, infection, and power. Too much pressure or too much volume injures the lung and can rupture it, producing air around the lung or in the chest wall, while too much oxygen over days injures the lung and, in a premature newborn, damages the retina, so the fraction of inspired oxygen is lowered as soon as the oxygen saturation allows. A patient who lies ventilated with a tube in place is at risk of ventilator-associated pneumonia, which is reduced by hand hygiene, by keeping the head of the bed raised, by oral care, and by avoiding unnecessary circuit changes. A circuit that disconnects, a tube that is displaced, a battery that empties, and a gas supply that fails are the mechanical emergencies, and the common operator errors repeat across every setting: the wrong mode, an alarm silenced, a humidifier run dry, a mask strapped too tight, and a bag squeezed too hard.

To summarize, ventilation is the replacement or support of the work of breathing, and every device that does it manages pressure, volume, flow, and time. The self-inflating bag turns the elastic recoil of a rubber bag into a breath that needs no gas and no power, while the flow-inflating bag trades that independence for a controllable pressure that demands a continuous supply. The transport ventilator buys portability by giving up modes, precision, and endurance, and its battery and gas supply must be planned before the journey rather than discovered on the way. The intensive care unit ventilator is a system of blender, valves, sensors, circuit, humidifier, filters, and alarms, and CPAP and BIPAP support the breath through an interface whose fit decides the outcome. In the field the same tasks are met with a rugged machine, a bubble circuit, a solar panel, and a manual bag, and the manual bag is never obsolete. Whatever the setting, the discipline is identical: confirm every setting against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol, check the whole chain from gas supply to chest movement, watch the patient rather than the dial, and keep a hand-ventilation plan ready for the moment the machine stops.
