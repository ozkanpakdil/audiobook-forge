# Chapter 30 — PACS and Imaging Information Technology

A picture archiving and communication system, called PACS, takes an image from the moment an exposure ends and keeps it available, findable, and readable for the life of the patient record. The principle behind it is easy to forget, because a medical image is not a photograph but a rectangular grid of numbers, and every decision made from it is made from those numbers. Once an image exists as numbers it can be copied without loss, sent through a cable, stored on a disk, and compared with an older study. Every parameter, capacity, compression level, brightness setting, and alarm threshold named in this chapter must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The image begins as a measurement of how much signal reaches each small region of the patient, whether that signal is a beam of radiation, a radio wave, or a returning echo. Each measurement is written into a matrix counted in picture elements, so a chest image may be 2,000 picture elements across, and the number of gray shades each element can take is its bit depth. Contrast is therefore not fixed at acquisition but chosen later by the reader, which is the most important consequence of the digital form. The bit depth, matrix size, and reconstruction parameters of each modality must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The standard format that carries these numbers between machines is called Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine, called DICOM, and its design explains why equipment from different makers can usually talk to one another. A DICOM object is a self describing file, a header of tagged fields followed by the pixel values, where each tag has a number and a defined meaning, so the header records the patient's identity, the modality, and the acquisition settings. Images are organized beneath it in a hierarchy of study, series, and instance, each level carrying a unique identifier generated so that no two objects anywhere can collide. The tags, their permitted values, and the matching rules of those identifiers must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The order that starts an examination usually lives in the hospital information system, and the bridge between that system and the imaging equipment is a messaging standard called Health Level Seven, called HL7. A message carries the patient's identity and the requested examination to the imaging system, which turns it into a worklist that the technologist sees at the console of the modality, so the technologist selects the correct entry instead of typing the details. A worklist that fails to refresh leaves yesterday's names on the screen or an empty list, and the characteristic alarm is a query failure or a stale list warning at the console. The message fields and the refresh interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The commonest and most damaging operator error in this chain is to bypass the worklist and type the patient's details by hand, or to pick the wrong name from a list of similar names. A name spelled differently in two systems, a date of birth in the wrong order, or an identifier read from the wrong wristband can all put a study under the wrong person, and the error is invisible in the image itself. The hazard is not only the mislabeled patient but also the missing study for the patient who should have been imaged, because a clinician who finds no image may repeat the examination and add dose. The written identification routine and the acceptable matching fields must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Once the images are accepted, the modality sends them onward, and the path they take is called the acquisition to archive chain. A larger department usually sends first to a verification station where a technologist checks the study for completeness and correct labeling before releasing it to the long term store. The exchange is a request and a response, in which the sender offers the objects, the receiver acknowledges them, and the receiver may commit to having stored them durably before the modality deletes its local copy. A modality that fills its own disk because nobody cleared the queue will refuse the next patient, so the outgoing queue must be watched, and the chain steps and commitment rules must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Compression is where bandwidth and storage are traded against diagnostic detail, and the distinction that matters is whether the process is reversible. Lossless compression rewrites the numbers more compactly and restores them exactly, usually achieving a modest reduction, and it is the default for images that may be measured. Irreversible compression discards detail the eye is unlikely to miss and can reduce a file greatly, but it alters the numbers, so measurements and computer aided detection may change with it and it must not be applied to quantitative work without a written policy. The permitted method and ratio for each examination type must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The archive is the hospital's memory for images, and it consists of servers running the archiving software and disk arrays holding the data, arranged so that a failed disk can be replaced without losing a study. Older studies migrate to slower and cheaper storage or to tape while recent ones stay on fast disk, and growth is relentless because every year adds more examinations. The characteristic alarm of an archive is a capacity threshold warning followed by a refusal to accept new studies when the last reserve is consumed. A backup is not a copy that exists but a copy that can be restored, so the archive keeps more than one copy, on separate storage and ideally in a separate building, because fire, flood, theft, and electrical damage take the primary copy and its neighbors together. The practice that proves a backup is the scheduled restore test, in which a sample of studies is recovered onto a spare system and found complete and readable, while physically separated copies are also the defense against extortion software. The retention periods, backup schedule, separation distance, and restore test interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The workstation is where an image becomes a diagnosis, and a viewing program opens a study with a hanging protocol, a set of rules that lays out the series in the order the reader wants them. It allows several studies of the same patient to be scrolled together, so that today's image is compared with last year's in the same position, and it shows the series as a stack that can be paged through until the finding is located. It also reconstructs slices in other planes from the same acquired data and fuses one examination with another on the shared position data. The default layout, comparison rules, and available reconstructions must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Windowing shows most clearly why a digital image behaves differently from a film, and it follows from the way stored numbers are mapped to visible brightness. The stored values span a wide range, but a screen can show only a limited number of gray shades, so the software chooses a window, a band of stored values, and stretches that band across the display while everything below becomes black and everything above becomes white. The center of the window sets which tissue is given middle gray and the width sets how much contrast is shown, and the same stored image therefore supports several appearances. The program also measures, converting picture elements into physical length using the spacing recorded in the header and rendering the stack into a rotatable volume. The window and level values, measurement conventions, calibration factors, and saved presets must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The diagnostic display is a liquid crystal display, called LCD, and it is a different class of device from an office screen. A liquid crystal cell does not emit light of its own, but twists to control how much light from a light emitting diode, called LED, backlight passes through a polarizing filter. The luminance of the panel, the uniformity of the backlight, and the contrast between the darkest and brightest regions all affect whether a faint lesion can be seen, and the display is calibrated so that the gray shades it shows follow the standard display function. A display drifts as it ages, so a scheduled quality control program uses a photometer to measure the luminance at several brightness levels and a test pattern to reveal banding, smudges, and dead picture elements. The ambient light in the room is part of the system, because a bright window or ceiling lamp reflected in the glass reduces the perceived contrast. The commonest operator error is to read on an ordinary office screen, an uncorrected mobile screen, or a display whose calibration has lapsed, and the characteristic reminder is a calibration due warning that should stop reading rather than be dismissed, with the calibration interval, luminance targets, and ambient light level confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Teleradiology sends the images away from the building, and the physical limit that governs it is bandwidth, the rate at which data can cross the link. A study of several hundred megabytes over a slow connection takes many minutes, and a queue of studies can exhaust a link shared with other hospital traffic, so transmission is often scheduled after hours or prioritized by urgency. The link is usually a wired connection in which a cable carries signals to a switch and the switch forwards them by address, and it may cross an encrypted tunnel so that images are unreadable to anyone between the two sites. The link capacity, encryption method, and priority rules for urgent studies must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Because bandwidth is finite, the reporting workflow is designed around what can be sent and when. The images may cross the link compressed, the report returns through the hospital information system as a structured message, and the acknowledgment that a report has been received is as important as the report itself. A preliminary verbal report by telephone may save a life when the link is down, but it must be written down and later matched to the final report, because a message passed verbally is easily lost or altered. A study that is sent but never acknowledged leaves the referring clinician waiting, so the receiving service keeps a list of unread studies, and the characteristic alarm is a rising count of unread examinations. The compression settings and turnaround targets must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Film printing has not disappeared, and the printers that survive are dry thermal devices that use heat rather than wet chemistry. A thermal print head is a row of tiny heating elements that transfer dye or develop a silver based layer in the film as it passes, and the density of each spot is set by the heat applied to it. The printer is calibrated so that the densities it produces match the display, because a film that is too dark or too light forces the clinician to guess, and the print head and its rollers accumulate dust and must be cleaned on schedule. The consumables are the film in its several sizes and the donor materials. The density, film size, and cleaning interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The network is not an accessory to the imaging chain but the chain itself, and its design decides whether the department can work. A hospital network is built in layers, with cabling running from each device to a switch, switches connecting to one another and to a central router, and the imaging traffic separated from other traffic so that a large transfer does not starve the clinical systems. Wireless links are convenient for a portable device but they are shared and variable, so a fixed workstation or an archive is connected by cable, and every connection has a backup path because a single switch or cable can isolate an entire room. The server room itself needs an uninterruptible power supply, called UPS, cooling from the heating, ventilation, and air conditioning system, and limited logged access. Network addresses, battery test interval, room temperature alarm threshold, and access rules must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Images and reports are among the most sensitive records a hospital holds, and the security that protects them rests on three ideas, that only the right people may see them, that every access must be recorded, and that the data must remain unreadable to anyone who intercepts it. Access begins with a unique account for each user and a password that is not shared, because a shared login makes the audit trail worthless, while a smart card or a token adds a second proof of identity. The audit trail records who opened which study and when, encryption protects data in transit and at rest, and backups are kept in a form that extortion software cannot reach. The password policy, audit retention, and encryption standard must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Availability is the property that clinical decisions depend on, and it is measured rather than assumed. A well designed service removes single points of failure, so there are two servers, two network paths, and duplicate power supplies, and the software moves the workload to the surviving side when one fails, a behavior that must be tested rather than trusted. Monitoring watches disk space, queue lengths, response time, and temperature, and it raises an alert before a user notices a problem, because a slow archive is a warning while an unavailable archive is a crisis. The measure that matters to the ward is the number of minutes in a month when a clinician could not see an image, so the failover test interval, monitoring thresholds, and accepted downtime target must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Downtime procedures are the equipment that keeps a hospital working when the electronics do not, and they are useless if they are first read during the outage. The written plan names who decides that the system is down, who is called, how long the switch to paper will last, and how the work is recorded while the network is unavailable. Radiographers keep a paper log of every examination with the patient's identifier, the time, and the body part, and after recovery comes the reconciliation, in which each paper entry is matched to its study and each study is verified as archived. A study produced during downtime can otherwise be lost forever, so the plan is rehearsed at least once a year, and the notification list and reconciliation method must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Maintenance of this chain is mostly preventive and mostly unglamorous, and it is the part most often neglected because everything appears to work. The schedule covers the archive servers and their disks, the network switches and their power supplies, the workstations, the printers, and the display calibration, and each item has an interval, a task, and a record. Dust is the quiet enemy of every one of these, because it blocks airflow in a server, fouls a connector, and scratches a print head. Every device also has a characteristic way of failing, since the archive fails by filling, the network fails by isolating one room, the display fails by drifting silently, the printer fails by streaking, and the modality fails by keeping images in a queue nobody reads. The service intervals and spare parts list must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The operator's start of shift check catches most of the faults that would otherwise waste a day, and it takes only minutes. The technologist confirms that the modality is on the worklist, that the list shows today's patients and not yesterday's, and that the archive is reachable by sending a test object or opening the most recent study. The outgoing queue is inspected for unsent studies, the free space on the archive is read, the calibration status of the display is noted, and the printer is checked for film and for a clean test print. The server room temperature and the status of the uninterruptible power supply are glanced at, and any warning is written in the log rather than dismissed. The worklist query interval, acceptable free space level, and test object must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The consumables of this chapter are few and cheap, and a single missing item can still stop the service. They include the film and donor materials for the thermal printer, the cleaning kits for print heads and display glass, the lint free cloths, and the approved cleaning solution. There are the removable storage media used to carry studies out of the building, the labels and wristbands with their printed identifiers, and the barcode and radio frequency identification, called RFID, supplies that link a patient to an order. There are also the batteries for the uninterruptible power supply, the patch cables and connectors that seem to vanish, and the blank paper logs for the downtime box. The expiry dates, cleaning agents, and labeling supplies must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Cleaning the hardware of the imaging chain is a clinical task with a technical edge. Keyboards, mouse, console, and tablet are wiped with a compatible disinfectant between users, and the correct agent is chosen after checking the maker's list, because alcohol attacks some plastics, bleach corrodes metal, and a wet cloth can drive liquid into a vent. Display glass is cleaned with a soft dry cloth first and an approved solution applied to a cloth rather than sprayed onto the panel, since fluid running into the bezel can damage the backlight, and cables are inspected rather than scrubbed. The cleaning agents, dwell times, and approved surfaces must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

In a mobile or frontier clinic the archiving chain described above does not exist. There is no archive, no radiologist on site, no reliable network, and often no electric supply stable enough to run a server, so the entire information system for imaging is reduced to what can be carried and what can be transmitted. What survives is the image itself and a way to move it, whether a film, a memory card, or a photograph taken from a screen. An improvised transfer that violates the standard can still save a life, and the same transfer can also hide a fracture, mislabel a patient, or expose a record to strangers, so the acceptable compromises must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The field variant most often seen is a photograph of a screen taken with a mobile telephone and sent through a messaging application over a weak link, and it breaks the standard at every level. The photograph captures only the window that happened to be displayed, so the reader cannot change the window and cannot see the tissue that was mapped to black or to white, and it captures a perspective view of a panel, so distances are distorted and geometric measurements are meaningless without a known scale in the frame. The link compresses the picture irreversibly and may reduce it to a small number of picture elements, so fine detail is destroyed and compression artifacts can mimic or hide a line. The metadata is lost entirely, because the header does not survive the camera, and the patient's name may travel visibly in the corner of the frame while the file carries no verifiable identity.

When a photograph must be used, discipline turns a poor copy into a defensible one, and the rules are easy to remember. The camera is held square to the screen and steady, the room light is dimmed so that no reflection crosses the image, and the frame includes the whole image rather than a corner of it. The screen is set to the window the radiologist would want, usually the standard soft tissue or bone setting for that examination, and the brightness is not adjusted afterward, because any enhancement is irreversible. A ruler or a known object is included if a measurement may be requested, the patient's identifier is either fully visible or fully excluded but never partly legible, and the picture is sent with the clinical question, the date, and the sender's identity. The display setting and frame contents must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Where no worklist exists, the paper request form becomes the worklist, and it must carry the fields a digital list would have carried. The form names the patient, the identifier, the date of birth, the requesting clinician, the clinical question, and the examination requested, and it stays with the patient or in a single bound book rather than on loose slips that blow away. The radiographer enters the same details into the modality by hand, which is precisely the step that causes wrong patient studies, so a second person reads the details back before the exposure is made, and a serial number is written on the form and on the image so that the two can be reunited later. The fields required on the form, the verification routine, and the retention of the book must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A district hospital may run a small version of the full chain, and its compromises are instructive. Such a site might have one modality, one workstation, and a single archive server in a locked room, with images kept on local disks and copies written to removable drives carried to a second building each evening. It gives up the redundancy and automatic migration of a large archive, so capacity is managed by hand and old studies are deleted only under a written retention policy and only after a verified copy exists elsewhere. It also gives up the specialist engineer, so the local technician must recognize a failing disk, a full volume, and a failing battery and must be able to restore a backup. The retention policy, backup rotation, and deletion procedure must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Where no network connects two sites, the study travels physically, and the equipment of that journey is a disk, a memory card, or a film jacket in the hands of a person. A disk carried by hand delivers more data in an hour than a weak link delivers in a day, provided it is labeled, protected, and signed for at both ends. The hazard is the loss of the medium or the loss of the chain of custody, because an unlabeled disk in a bag is both a privacy breach and a missing study. The receiving site must be able to read the format written by the sending site, which is why the universal standard is used, and the labeling, encryption, and accepted file format must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

In a truly remote clinic there is no archive at all, and the film jacket in a cupboard is the only record. The radiographer keeps a logbook of every exposure, files the film in a numbered jacket, and becomes, in effect, both the archive and the worklist at once, so that log must be protected by training, supervision, and a second copy. Images and reports leave by telephone, by short message with a photograph, or with the patient on the next transport, and the receiving clinician is told explicitly what was seen, what was not seen, and what could not be assessed. The referral note must state the clinical question, the relevant history, the results of tests already done, and the patient's ability to lie still. Ultrasound answers many questions without film, and a referral to a center with computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging is planned early rather than after a trial of treatment, so the logbook fields, referral content, and transport of the film must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

To summarize, the image that leaves a modality is a grid of numbers with a header describing them, stored in the standard called DICOM, and the worklist built from messages in the standard called HL7 ties those numbers to the correct patient. The archive holds them on servers and disk arrays and is only as good as its tested backup and its separated copy, while the workstation turns them back into a picture through windowing and measurement and the diagnostic display must be calibrated. Teleradiology carries them over a link whose bandwidth and compression set the limits of the reporting workflow, film printing survives in the thermal printer, and the whole service depends on network design, security, privacy, cooling, power, and a written downtime procedure that is rehearsed rather than discovered. The hazards are the wrong patient in the header, the uncalibrated screen, the unacknowledged study, the untested backup, and the unprotected record, while the commonest operator errors are hand typing an identity, ignoring the outgoing queue, dismissing a calibration warning, and reading on an ordinary screen. In the field none of this infrastructure travels, so a photograph of a screen over a weak link, a paper logbook, a labeled disk carried by hand, and an early referral take its place, and every one of those substitutes violates the standard in a way that must be understood rather than ignored. Every parameter, capacity, compression level, brightness setting, and alarm threshold must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.
