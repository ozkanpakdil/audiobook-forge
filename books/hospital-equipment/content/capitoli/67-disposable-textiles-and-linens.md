# Chapter 67 — Disposable Textiles and Linens

Cloth is equipment, and it is the equipment that a hospital touches more often than any other. A sheet lies under every patient, a gown covers every body, a towel dries every hand, and a drape defines the sterile field in every operation. The physical principle that governs all of them is simple. A woven textile is a lattice of threads, and between the threads there are gaps, so the fabric behaves as a selective barrier whose performance depends on the size of the gaps, on the surface chemistry of the fiber, and on the pressure applied from one side. When the gaps are small and the fibers repel water the cloth keeps liquid out, and when the gaps are wide or the liquid is pushed hard the cloth lets it through. Every clinical property of linen, from absorbency to barrier protection to warmth, follows from that geometry.

Three physical ideas carry the whole subject, and an operator who holds them can predict how a textile will behave. The first is capillary action, the tendency of a narrow space to draw liquid along itself against gravity, which is why a cotton towel wicks water away from skin and why the same property pulls contaminated fluid through a gown that has been wetted. The second is strike-through, the passage of liquid through a fabric under pressure, which is why a drape that repels a splash may still leak when a wet instrument rests on it. The third is the difference between a liquid barrier and a particle barrier, because a tightly woven cloth may stop droplets while still letting airborne organisms settle through. These ideas explain why two textiles that look identical from a meter away can perform completely differently against infection.

Textiles reach the patient in two forms, and the title of this chapter names only one of them. Disposable textiles are made from non-woven fiber, used once, and discarded, and they dominate the operating theatre, the isolation room, and the emergency department because they arrive sterile and leave as waste. Reusable textiles are woven from cotton or from a cotton and polyester blend, washed and reprocessed many times, and they remain the backbone of beds, wards, and clinics because they are cheaper over a thousand uses and gentler on the skin. The same chapter must therefore describe both, since a ward typically runs on reusable linen while its theatre runs on disposables, and the choice between them is decided by barrier performance, cost, laundry capacity, and the reliability of supply. The consumable side of the subject, the soap, the chemical, the bag, and the thread, matters as much as the cloth, because a laundry without consumables is a room full of dirty fabric.

Bed linen is a system rather than a single object, and each layer has a task. The mattress cover is the barrier that keeps body fluids out of the mattress foam, where they would be impossible to remove and would feed organisms for years. The fitted sheet is the working surface, smooth so that it does not crease under a pressure area and closely fitted so that it does not ruck and wrinkle. The draw sheet is a narrow sheet placed across the middle of the bed under the patient, and it exists so that two or three staff can slide a patient rather than lift, which protects both the patient's skin and the worker's back. The pillowcase covers the pillow, the underpad absorbs small amounts of urine or wound fluid, and the top sheet and blanket regulate warmth. A missing mattress cover or a torn fitted sheet is therefore not a cosmetic fault, because it defeats the barrier and the friction control of the whole bed.

The material of bed linen is chosen for two properties that pull in opposite directions. Cotton absorbs water readily, feels cool and soft against skin, and tolerates repeated hot washing, but it wrinkles, it shrinks, and it dries slowly. Polyester cotton blends resist wrinkling, dry quickly, and hold their shape, but they absorb less and they can feel hot and clammy against a feverish patient. The hospital therefore keeps a mixed inventory, and the operator learns to match the cloth to the patient rather than to the shelf, giving the absorbent cotton to the patient who sweats and the quick-drying blend to the ward whose dryer is overloaded. The common operator error in this small decision is to use a plastic-backed underpad directly against the skin, which traps moisture and macerates the skin within a few hours.

Patient gowns are textiles with a designed failure point, because they must open for examination and close for dignity. The standard gown ties at the back of the neck and at the waist, which allows a clinician to expose the chest, the abdomen, or the back with one movement, and the pediatric gown does the same in a smaller size with a brighter print. A gown that has lost its ties is no longer a gown, because the patient holds it closed with one hand and the examination becomes a struggle for both people. The common operator error is to keep a torn gown in circulation because it still covers the patient, and the result is a garment that falls open during transport in a corridor. The safety hazard that follows is a fall, because a patient who is holding a gown cannot use both hands for the walker or the rail, so the ties must be checked at every change.

Towels and blankets are the warm, bulky part of the inventory, and their bulk is exactly why they carry organisms from one patient to the next. A bath towel is used wet, left damp, and shared by habit in some wards, and a warm damp cloth held against skin is an efficient way to transfer organisms. The blanket is worse, because a heavy blanket is washed less often than a sheet, sits in contact with skin for hours, and is often shaken over the bed, which releases a cloud of fibers and organisms into the air of the room. The physical remedy is the same in every hospital, which is that towels must be single use per patient and blankets must be covered by a sheet or replaced by a washable cover. The thermal blanket used in the operating theatre or the warming cabinet adds another rule, because the warming cabinet temperature and the blanket's heat setting must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The operating theatre uses textiles that are engineered rather than merely washed, because the drape defines the boundary between sterile and non-sterile space. A surgical drape must resist the striking through of blood, irrigation fluid, and saline under the weight of instruments, it must not shed lint into an open wound, and it must not burn when a cautery device touches it. Reusable theatre drapes are woven tightly from a polyester and cotton blend, often with a reinforcement layer at the working area, and they are inspected for holes after every wash because a pinhole becomes a channel for fluid. Disposable drapes are built from non-woven fiber and are supplied sterile in a pack, and their barrier performance is graded against a formal scale so that a drape for a short procedure is not used for a long one. The specific grade, the weight, and the fluid resistance required for each procedure must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the pack is opened.

The structure of a non-woven textile explains its performance. Instead of interlaced threads, the fabric is a mat of fibers bonded by heat, by pressure, or by a chemical binder, and the fibers may be continuous filaments or short staple strands. A common three-layer construction places a layer of strong spunbonded filaments on each side of a layer of fine meltblown fibers, and the fine middle layer is the barrier while the outer layers carry the strength and the softness. A woven reusable drape achieves its barrier with a high thread count and a calendered surface, which flattens the threads and closes the gaps between them. The trade is written into the physics, since the tighter and denser the structure the better the barrier and the poorer the breathability, so a gown that protects completely also traps heat and moisture next to the surgeon.

The laundry flow is the part of the subject that decides whether clean linen is genuinely clean, and its governing rule is that dirty linen and clean linen must never share a path. The building is divided into a soiled side, where linen arrives, is weighed, sorted, and loaded into machines, and a clean side, where it is unloaded, dried, ironed, folded, and stored. The boundary between the two sides is a physical barrier, usually a wall with a pass-through washer or a doorway that only one direction of travel crosses, and the workers change garments or wash hands when they cross it. The one-way route exists because a clean sheet carried back through the soiled room is contaminated by the air and the surfaces around it, and no amount of folding will undo that. The common operator error is to walk a clean trolley into the soiled area to save a trip, and that single trip defeats the entire design of the plant.

The route in detail begins at the bedside, where the used linen is placed into a bag without being shaken or sorted. The bag is closed, carried on a dedicated soiled trolley that never carries clean goods, and delivered to the soiled linen room. There the load is weighed, because weight is the currency of a laundry contract and the trigger for the wash formula, and it is sorted only if the local protocol permits sorting before disinfection. The linen then enters the washer, passes through the wash stages, and emerges on the clean side, where it is transferred to a dryer, then to an ironer or a folding table, and finally into a clean linen store. A clean trolley with a closed or covered top then carries it to the ward, and the ward stores it in a cupboard that is used for nothing else. Every setting, weight, and cycle time along that route must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the plant is run.

Segregation begins where the linen is removed, and the categories are usually marked by the color of the bag. General ward linen, linen from an infectious patient, linen contaminated with blood or body fluid, and theatre linen are the common groups, and each demands a different handling and sometimes a different wash formula. The color code itself is local, so a green bag means one thing in one hospital and something else in the next, and the operator must therefore learn the code of the building rather than of the profession. Shaking soiled linen is the cardinal error, because it releases a cloud of fibers, dust, and organisms into the face of the person doing it, and that cloud settles on the floor, the trolley, and the clean doorway. The linen must be lifted with gloved hands, folded inward on itself if it is heavily soiled, and dropped into the bag without being compressed or fanned.

Thermal disinfection is the killing of organisms by heat, and its principle is that proteins and membranes lose their shape when they are held above a certain temperature for long enough. The relationship between temperature and time is a trade, so a higher temperature needs a short hold and a lower temperature needs a long one. A common thermal disinfection cycle holds the load at 71 degrees Celsius for 3 minutes, and a gentler cycle holds it at 65 degrees Celsius for 10 minutes, and both of those figures must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before a cycle is selected. The temperature is measured at the coldest point that the machine can reliably control, and the hold time begins only when that point reaches the set value, which is why a display that shows the set point is not evidence that the load was disinfected. The characteristic alarm of this equipment is a cycle abort, because the controller did not see the set point reached or held, and the load must then be treated as soiled and reprocessed.

Chemical disinfection is used for fabrics that cannot take heat, and its principle is that an oxidizing or surface-active chemical damages the organism's proteins, its membrane, or its genetic material on contact. The common agents are chlorine-releasing compounds, peracetic acid, and quaternary ammonium compounds, and each has a spectrum of activity that is narrower than heat and a sensitivity to soiling that heat does not have. A chlorine-releasing solution is typically made up to a concentration measured in parts per million of available chlorine, and it must remain in contact with the fabric for a defined number of minutes, and both the concentration and the contact time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The critical point is that a heavy soil load consumes the chemical before it reaches the organism, so chemical disinfection is a second step after cleaning and never a substitute for it. Chlorine also attacks cotton and damages the fibers, so repeated chemical disinfection shortens the life of the linen and leaves it thin and grey.

A modern wash process is a sequence rather than a single immersion, and each stage has a task. The prewash removes the bulk of the soil in warm water and is often drained before the main wash begins, because soil carried into the main wash neutralizes the detergent and the bleach. The main wash carries the detergent and sometimes a bleach at the temperature and the time that the formula demands, and the intermediate rinses remove the chemical before the fabric is exposed to the next one. The final rinse removes every trace of detergent and disinfectant, and a souring agent may be added to bring the fabric back to a skin-friendly acidity, followed by a softener that makes the cloth pleasant to handle. The operator must understand that the rinse is not a formality, because a sheet that smells of chlorine or of soap irritates the skin of the next patient.

Detergent dosing is the daily judgment of the laundry, and it is measured rather than guessed. The main wash normally uses an alkaline detergent, often with enzymes that digest protein and fat, and the formula may add an oxygen or chlorine bleach for stain removal and a souring agent for the final rinse. An automatic dosing pump draws the chemical from a container and injects the set volume into the machine, and the pump is calibrated at intervals, because a worn pump delivers less chemical than the display claims. Under-dosing leaves soil and organisms on the fabric, and over-dosing leaves a residue that irritates skin, damages fibers, and wastes money, so the dose must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol for the machine and the water supply. The common operator error is to top up the dosage by eye when the linen still looks grey, which hides the real problem, which is usually hard water, an overloaded machine, or a failed pump.

Water hardness is the invisible variable that decides how much detergent a laundry really needs. Hard water contains dissolved calcium and magnesium, and those ions react with soap and with detergent to form an insoluble scum that deposits on the fabric and inside the machine. Hardness is quoted as a concentration in milligrams per liter of calcium carbonate or in parts per million, and the value for a given supply must be confirmed against the local water test and the manufacturer's instructions before the wash formula is fixed. Soft water allows a smaller dose of detergent and a cleaner rinse, so a laundry that has switched to a softened supply and kept the old formula will over-dose and leave residue. The physical signs of hard water are grey, stiff, and scratchy linen, a white scale on the heating elements, and a machine that needs more energy to reach its temperature.

Drying is the removal of the water that the rinse left behind, and its physics is that warm air carries moisture away while the fabric gives it up. A tumble dryer forces heated air through a rotating load, and the temperature and the cycle length are chosen for the fiber, because cotton tolerates more heat than polyester and an overheated synthetic load can shrink, glaze, or even melt. Over-drying damages every fiber and consumes energy, so the machine is judged on whether the load leaves dry rather than bone dry, and the temperature setting must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before a load is started. Ironing adds heat, moisture, and pressure together, and a flatwork ironer passes a sheet between a heated cylinder and a padded roller so that a large item is pressed in one movement. The hazard of the ironer is the same as the hazard of any hot surface, which is a burn to the hand or the forearm, and the safety guard and the emergency stop must be tested at the start of every shift.

Transport is where clean linen is most easily lost, so the equipment that moves it is designed around the separation of clean and dirty. A soiled trolley is a closed or lidded container that holds bagged linen and is washed or disinfected at the end of the route, and a clean trolley is a separate cart with shelves and a cover that carries folded linen from the store to the ward. The two are never the same cart, and the clean cart never enters the soiled room, because a cart that has been in the soiled room carries organisms on its wheels and its frame. A ward linen cupboard must be dry, dust-free, and used only for linen, because a cupboard that also holds cleaning chemicals or a dripping mop bucket will spoil the stock above it. Radio-frequency identification, or RFID, tags sewn into reusable linen allow a laundry to count and trace its stock, and the technology turns the invisible loss of linen into a number that can be managed.

Linen from an infectious patient demands special handling from the moment it leaves the bed. The linen must be bagged in the room, at the point of use, without being sorted or shaken, and the bag must then be closed and labeled before it leaves. If the outside of the bag is contaminated, a second bag is placed over the first, and the outer bag is removed on the clean side of the boundary so that the contaminated surface never travels further. The worker wears personal protective equipment, or PPE, including gloves and an apron, and performs hand hygiene after the bag is closed, because the organisms on linen are carried on the hands rather than in the air. The most serious hazard in this whole chapter is the sharp that a patient or a clinician has left in the linen, because a needle hidden in a sheet can pierce a hand during bagging, and every bag must be handled as though a sharp were inside it. A puncture-resistant container for sharps must stand beside the bed, and the operator must never compress a bag of linen with the hand or the knee.

The characteristic alarms of this equipment are the alarms of the machines that wash it, and the operator must learn them by sound and by sight. A washer-disinfector announces a failed disinfection cycle with a buzzer and a flashing fault indicator, and the cause may be a door that unlocked early, a heating element that did not reach its set point, a dosing pump that delivered no chemical, or a drain that did not empty. The door interlock alarm is a safety alarm, because it prevents the door from opening while the drum turns or while the water is hot, and defeating that interlock is a serious hazard to the operator. The alarm threshold and the response to every one of these faults must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the plant is run, and the rule for a failed cycle is always the same, which is to reprocess the load as soiled rather than to reason about how clean it might be. A laundry that silences its alarms to keep the flow moving is a laundry that is disinfecting nothing.

The stock level that a ward needs is a calculation rather than a feeling, and it is built from consumption. A ward measures its linen use per patient per day, usually in kilograms, and multiplies that figure by the number of beds and the number of days that the laundry cycle takes, adding a safety margin for the ward's peak demand. The result is the par level, the quantity that must be on the shelf at the start of a shift, and a stock that falls below it is the quiet alarm of this chapter, because the shortage is noticed only when a bed is being made. The par level must be recalculated when the ward's case mix changes, when the laundry's turnaround time lengthens, and when a new procedure adds to the demand, and the figures used must be confirmed against the local protocol and the manufacturer's instructions for the textile before the order is placed. A ward that holds too much linen is as badly managed as a ward that holds too little, because an overfilled cupboard buries the oldest stock and encourages it to be damaged and lost.

Theft and loss consume a linen inventory faster than wear, and the loss is usually invisible until a count is taken. Sheets leave a hospital as cleaning rags, as dust covers, as paint cloths, as car covers, and as fabric for uniforms, and a proportion of every inventory simply disappears into the community around the gate. Some of the loss is innocent, since linen that is heavily contaminated is discarded with the waste and linen that is used to clean a spill is thrown away, and some of it is not, and the management response must distinguish the two without insulting the staff. Physical controls include a locked store, a signed issue book, an annual or quarterly count against the register, and the RFID tag that makes the loss of a single item visible. The operator's part is to report a shortage early and to resist the habit of stashing a private reserve of clean linen in a cupboard, because a hidden reserve distorts the par level and creates the shortage that the next shift suffers.

Failure modes in textiles are mostly slow and visible, which is a gift to the operator who looks. Lint and pilling appear first, because the surface fibers break and ball up, and a pilled sheet is abrasive against skin and sheds particles into the air. Holes and thin patches follow, and a hole in a theatre drape is a barrier failure, a hole in a sheet is a route for fluid to reach the mattress, and a hole in a gown is an exposure for the wearer. Lost ties, broken buttons, and torn hems are mechanical failures, and chemical damage from repeated over-bleaching shows as a fabric that has lost its body and tears along a line rather than at a point. Damp storage produces mildew, which leaves a musty smell and dark spots that washing may not remove, and a load stored wet in a warm room can grow organisms on the cloth itself. The most dangerous failure is the retained object, because a sharp or an instrument that reaches the laundry in a sheet can injure a worker, and a lost instrument reaches the patient as a missing count.

The operator's checks before linen reaches a patient are short, physical, and decisive. The item is held up to the light, which reveals a thin patch or a pinhole that the eye misses at arm's length. The ties, the seams, and the hems are pulled gently, because a stitch that has rotted will part under a light tug rather than during the night. The cloth is felt for dampness and smelled for chlorine, mildew, or detergent, since a residue is a chemical exposure for the next patient. The barrier side of a drape is inspected for a breach, and the load is inspected for a sharp before it is handled with bare hands. Every item that fails a check is removed from circulation and routed to repair or to waste, and the item that is quietly returned to the shelf is the item that fails at the worst moment.

The field variant begins where the laundry plant ends, and it changes the equipment rather than the principle. A mobile or frontier clinic has no boiler, no tunnel washer, no dryer, and often no piped hot water, so the wash happens in buckets and basins, by hand, with whatever soap is available, and the drying happens on a line in the sun. The physics is unchanged, because organisms are still removed by mechanical action, by detergent, by heat, and by drying, but every one of those parameters is now uncontrolled and depends on the judgment of the person doing the work. What the field variant gives up is certainty, since it cannot prove a temperature, a contact time, or a concentration, and what it demands instead is discipline, because a hand wash done carelessly produces linen that looks clean and carries organisms.

Hand washing is organized so that the water itself does not become the contaminant. The practical arrangement is three containers: the first holds warm soapy water for the wash, the second holds clean water for the first rinse, and the third holds clean water with a disinfectant or the hottest water available for the final rinse. The items are worked from the least soiled to the most soiled, so that the wash water is not loaded with blood before the sheets arrive, and each item is agitated, rubbed, and scrubbed against itself or against a board rather than merely dipped. The soap does the chemistry, because its molecules lift oil and soil from the fiber and hold them in suspension so that the rinse can carry them away, but the mechanical work does most of the physical removal. The common operator error is to keep adding soap to water that has already turned grey, which raises the load of suspended soil and redeposits it on everything in the basin.

Water quantity and water quality are the two limiting resources, and both must be managed deliberately. A single basin used for a whole ward's linen becomes a suspension of organisms after the first few items, and each later item is dipped in that suspension rather than cleaned. The rule is to change the wash water when it becomes visibly soiled, to keep the final rinse water clear, and to dry the items in a way that does not return them to the ground. Where water is scarce, the clinic may prioritize the items that touch a wound or a newborn and use a smaller volume with a longer scrub, and that choice must be confirmed against the local protocol for the clinic before it is adopted. The last rinse should be clean enough to drink if the item is to be used on broken skin, which is a standard that most field laundries cannot meet and must therefore acknowledge.

Disinfection in the field is improvised from heat or from chemicals, and each route has a cost. Boiling an item in water for 10 minutes kills most organisms, and the time and the temperature must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the method is relied upon, because a pot that is not fully boiling is not boiling at all. A pressure cooker used as a small autoclave is a genuine sterilizer for drapes and gowns when it reaches its working pressure for the required time, and that pressure and time must likewise be confirmed before use, and the operator must remember that a folded, tightly packed load may not be penetrated by steam. A chlorine-releasing solution is the common chemical route, and its concentration and contact time must be confirmed before use, and the solution must be made fresh because chlorine loses its strength in light and in warm storage. The honest limit of chemical disinfection in the field is that a visibly soiled cloth consumes the chemical and comes out looking bleached rather than made safe.

Drying in the sun is the field method that is most often trusted and least often understood. Sunlight contains ultraviolet radiation, which damages the genetic material of organisms, and the sun also supplies heat and desiccation, which together reduce the load on the surface of a cloth. The effect, however, is confined to the surfaces that the light reaches, so an item hung in a thick fold, dried in the shade of a wall, or left through a humid overcast week loses the benefit while it still looks dry. The cloth must be spread open, turned after a time, and hung where air moves on both sides, and the line must be high enough that animals and dust cannot reach it. Ironing with a hot iron, where an iron and power exist, adds direct heat and pressure to the same surface and is a valuable second step. The operator must therefore treat sun drying as a real but partial intervention, useful exactly in proportion to how much of the cloth the sun actually touched.

What soap and sun do to an organism must be stated honestly, because the field worker will make decisions on the answer. Soap does not sterilize a cloth, and it does not reliably kill the organisms it removes, so its contribution is the removal of soil and the physical carriage of organisms into the rinse water. Sunlight does not sterilize a cloth either, and it acts only where the light strikes, so it cannot be relied upon for a folded bundle or for a cloudy day. The combination of washing, rinsing, thorough drying, and heat reduces the number of organisms greatly and is adequate for bed linen, towels, and gowns on a general ward. It is not adequate for a drape that will lie in an open wound or for a pack that will enter a body cavity, and the clinic that needs those items must obtain them sterile from a central service or produce them in a pressure cooker cycle with a validated load. The truthful field statement is therefore that hand laundering makes linen clean and safe for ordinary contact, and that sterile textiles remain a separate supply problem.

Field storage and transport are governed by the same separation as the hospital, even though the containers are simpler. Clean linen is kept in a closed cupboard or a cloth bag hung from a beam, away from the floor, the wall, and the cooking fire, and soiled linen is kept in a separate bag that is emptied and washed rather than reused damp. Where plastic sheeting replaces a mattress cover, it is wiped and dried between patients and inspected for tears, and where an underpad is unavailable it may be improvised from several layers of cloth placed over a plastic sheet, with the cloth layer changed as soon as it is wet. Disposable theatre textiles are the field supply that cannot be improvised, so their stock is counted and reserved for the procedures that genuinely need a barrier. The field checks before use are the same as the hospital checks, which are to hold the item to the light, to feel for dampness, to pull at the seams, to look for a hole, and to look for a sharp.

To summarize, textiles are equipment made of thread, and their behavior follows from the gaps between the fibers, which decide whether a liquid passes, wicks, or stops. The hospital runs a laundry that separates soiled from clean along a one-way route, disinfects by heat at a held temperature or by a chemical at a measured concentration, doses detergent against the hardness of its water, rinses away every residue, and dries and stores the result in a place that stays dry. The ward keeps a par level for each textile, counts what it uses, and accepts that theft, wear, and contamination will consume the stock whether or not anyone admits it. The field clinic replaces the plant with buckets, soap, a scrubbing motion, a line in the sun, and where possible a boiling pot or a pressure cooker, and it gains mobility and loses certainty. In both settings the operator is the last barrier, and the checks that matter are the same ones, which are to look for the hole, feel for the damp, smell the residue, and never trust a cloth that has not been checked against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.
