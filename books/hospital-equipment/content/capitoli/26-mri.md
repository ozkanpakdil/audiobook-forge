# Chapter 26 — MRI

Magnetic resonance imaging, called MRI, begins with the nucleus of ordinary hydrogen, which is a single proton and behaves like a very small spinning magnet. In tissue at rest those protons point in every direction, so their tiny fields cancel and the body is magnetically silent. Inside a strong static magnetic field a small excess of protons aligns with the field, and this faint imbalance is the only source of everything the scanner later shows. Each aligned proton precesses around the direction of the field like a tilted spinning top, at a frequency fixed by the strength of that field. A burst of radiofrequency energy at that frequency tips the protons away from their resting direction, and as they relax back they re-emit radiofrequency energy that a coil can detect. The machine exists to create the field, to disturb the protons in one chosen place at a time, and to listen to the answer, and the finished picture is a map of how quickly those protons give their energy back.

The static field comes from a superconducting electromagnet, in which a coil of alloy wire is cooled until its resistance falls to nothing, so a current launched into it circulates for years without any power supply. A vessel of liquid helium around the coil, wrapped in vacuum and radiation shields, holds the winding at about 4 kelvin, close to absolute zero, and because the current persists the magnet is never switched off and cannot simply be unplugged. That one fact governs the safety of the whole department. Field strength is measured in tesla, and a clinical scanner commonly runs between 0.5 tesla and 3 tesla, so the contour at which the field falls to 5 gauss, still strong enough to pull a ferromagnetic object from a hand, is called the five gauss line and forms the practical edge of the danger zone. That line does not respect walls, floors, or ceilings, and a scanner on an upper floor projects its field into the room below. The field strength, the position of the five gauss line, and every related warning threshold must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Spatial information comes from three sets of gradient coils, one for each direction in space, which superimpose a small deliberate variation on the main field. Where the field is slightly stronger the protons precess slightly faster and where it is weaker they precess slightly slower, so the frequency of the returning signal encodes the position of the protons that produced it, and switching the gradients in a planned order labels every volume element. Each rapid switch makes the coil assembly hammer against its mountings, and the knocking or buzzing is not a fault but a consequence of the physics. The noise rises with the strength and speed of the sequence and can reach levels loud enough to damage hearing, so every patient must wear protection that fits and is rated for the noise the scanner produces. Earplugs go in before the patient enters the room, headphones are added on top, and the patient is told that the sound is expected so that it does not provoke movement or panic. The gradient strength, the slew rate, the noise level, and the attenuation rating of the protection must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The radiofrequency system has two halves, a transmit path that delivers the tipping pulse and a receive path that listens to the answer. Transmission is usually performed by a large coil built into the scanner, because it must create a uniform field across the whole region examined, and the energy deposited in the patient by that transmission is called the specific absorption rate. Too much deposited energy heats tissue, and the tissues most at risk are those with poor blood flow. The receive side is where the art of the examination lies, because the coil that listens must lie as close to the anatomy as possible, so a head examination uses a snug head coil, a knee uses a wrap or a rigid knee coil, and a wrist or an ankle uses a small surface coil. A coil that is switched off, unplugged, badly seated, or damaged produces a dark band, a region of noise, or no image at all in the part it was meant to cover. The coil and its position, the permissible specific absorption rate, and every displayed energy limit must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the commonest operator error here is to choose a demanding sequence without reading the display that warns of it.

When the tipping pulse ends the protons return to their resting alignment, and the time constant of that recovery along the main field is the longitudinal relaxation time while the time constant of the decay across the field is the transverse relaxation time. Fat, muscle, cerebrospinal fluid, and edema differ in these values, so they look different from one another even though all are mostly water, and by choosing how long to wait and how long to listen the operator weights the image toward one relaxation time, the other, or the density of protons. The patterns of pulses and listening intervals are called sequences. A spin echo sequence uses a second pulse to undo the loss of signal from an uneven field and gives robust contrast at the price of a long scan, while a gradient echo sequence is faster but more sensitive to field unevenness. An inversion recovery sequence nulls the signal from one tissue, which is how fat is made to disappear and how fluid is made to stand out. The timing values, the sequence library, and the weighting chosen for each clinical question must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The signal that leaves the patient is not an image but a set of measurements of frequency and phase across the whole field of view, stored in a mathematical data space filled line by line. Reconstruction converts that space into the familiar grid of picture elements, and the same data can be reprocessed with different filters or shown in different planes. A scan interrupted before enough lines are collected cannot be reconstructed at all, which is why sudden movement at the end of a long sequence wastes the whole sequence. Every examination is a negotiation among contrast, resolution, scan time, and noise, and no setting improves all four at once, because finer resolution means smaller volume elements and less signal from each of them. A scanner asked to do too much at low field will always be beaten by a modest protocol performed well at the field it has. The resolution, the number of signal averages, and every acquisition time displayed must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The hospital machine lives in a room that is part of the device, and the room is kept free of anything that could become a projectile. The magnet has a cylindrical bore, commonly between 60 and 70 centimeters across, and the patient is carried into it on a table whose movement is measured in millimeters so that the region of interest ends up at the center of the field. The table has a weight limit often lower than a hospital bed, so a heavy patient must be weighed or moved with a transfer device rather than tested against the limit. Positioning is a discipline of stillness, because a patient who is uncomfortable will move and a patient who moves will spoil the study, so the body is made straight, the region of interest is centered on the coil, and padding is placed so that nothing presses over a long scan. Arms are kept clear of the bore wall and clear of the coil, because a limb touching either can pick up energy and become warm. The bore diameter, the table weight limit, the patient's position, the padding, and the coil placement must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Access to a magnetic resonance suite is organized into four zones, and knowing which zone you are in is the first safety skill. Zone 1 is the public area outside the suite, where no special control applies, and zone 2 is the reception and screening area, where the questionnaire is completed and checked. Zone 3 is the control room, where the scanner is operated and where a trained person can still intervene, and it is a restricted area. Zone 4 is the magnet room itself, entered only by staff who are trained and formally authorized, by a patient who has been screened, and by nobody else. The locked doors, the warning signs, and the list of authorized persons must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A person who does not know which zone they are standing in has already defeated the model.

The screening questionnaire is the most important object in the department, and it is never skipped, never delegated to an untrained clerk, and never completed on the patient's behalf. It asks about surgery, implanted devices, foreign bodies, metal in the eyes from grinding or welding, tattoos, pregnancy, and anything the patient remembers being put inside the body, and a patient who cannot answer reliably is screened with the help of a relative or a written record, or is not scanned. Certain implanted devices make the scanner dangerous rather than merely unsuitable, because a cardiac pacemaker or an implanted defibrillator may be damaged or may deliver inappropriate therapy, and an intracranial aneurysm clip from an older material can twist or dislodge. Cochlear implants, neurostimulators, medication pumps, and some dental work each carry their own rules, and even a device described as safe for MRI must be verified as safe for the specific field strength and examination. An intraocular foreign body can move inside the eye and cause blindness, so any history of metal in the eye requires imaging or an ophthalmic review before the patient approaches the magnet. Every implant must be identified by name, material, and documentation, and its condition confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The projectile hazard is the one that kills, because a ferromagnetic object near the magnet is accelerated toward the bore with a force that grows as it approaches. Any object containing iron, nickel, or cobalt, or certain stainless steels, may fly, including oxygen cylinders, scissors, keys, tools, and mop buckets, and such an object crosses the room in a fraction of a second and cannot be caught. The published accidents include a patient struck by an oxygen cylinder carried into the room and staff pinned by furniture, and these are why the rules are written in blood. The commonest operator error is not the dramatic one but the ordinary one, a person who steps into the magnet room with a forgotten object because the door was open, and a second error is to assume that a device labeled safe for magnetic resonance is safe at any field strength and with any sequence. The answer is a written check at the door by a trained person, backed by a handheld ferromagnetic detector whose characteristic alarm sounds when metal is present, and the correct response is to stop, remove the object, and re-screen. The use of the detector, its sensitivity, and the objects it may miss must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The magnet is kept cold by liquid helium, and a quench is the sudden loss of that cooling, when the superconducting coil becomes resistive and its enormous stored energy turns the liquid instantly into gas that must leave through a vent pipe. A quench may happen spontaneously or may be triggered by the quench button, which exists for the single emergency in which a person is pinned against the magnet, and that button must never be pressed for a fire, for a distressed patient, or to end a scan. The helium released is lighter than air and odorless and does not announce itself to a person breathing it, because as it expands it pushes oxygen out of the room and a person can lose consciousness without any warning sensation of suffocation, so an oxygen monitor in the magnet room and in any space through which the vent passes is a required device whose alarm threshold must be set and verified. The cryogenic system has its own alarms, in which a low cryogen level warns that a refill is needed, a cold head or compressor alarm warns that the refrigerator has stopped, and a magnet rundown alarm means a quench has occurred and the room must be evacuated. The commonest operator failure here is to silence a warning and keep scanning, which converts a planned refill into an emergency. The quench procedure, the button locations, the oxygen alarm threshold, and the response time for each alarm must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Contrast in MRI changes the magnetic behavior of nearby water protons rather than absorbing X-rays, so the agents used are a different family of molecules. A gadolinium containing agent works because the gadolinium ion is strongly paramagnetic and shortens the relaxation time of the water around it, brightening the tissue where it accumulates, and the free ion is toxic so it is held inside a chelating molecule whose stability determines the risk. Agents differ in whether they remain in the blood vessels or spread into the space between cells, and the choice depends on the question asked. The most serious risk is in a patient whose kidneys cannot clear the agent, because a prolonged dwell time allows the chelate to break down and the released ion to deposit in tissue, producing an illness called nephrogenic systemic fibrosis that thickens the skin and can involve internal organs. A patient with known kidney disease or an uncertain kidney history must have kidney function measured before an agent is given, while immediate reactions such as hives and nausea are managed with observation and a true anaphylactic reaction requires the same emergency response as any other. The agent, its concentration, the injected dose in millimoles per kilogram of body weight, and the emergency drugs must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Claustrophobia is a genuine barrier to care rather than a character flaw, and a substantial minority of patients find the bore intolerable. The patient is told beforehand what will happen, how long each sequence takes, that the noise is normal, and how to call for help, and a panic button that works inside the magnet is placed in the hand before the scan begins. An open or short bore magnet, a mirror that lets the patient see out, a familiar person in the room, and a calm voice through the intercom solve most cases, and the choice to scan a frightened patient by force is never correct. Children need a different plan because they cannot lie still for a long sequence, and a child who is prepared, shown the room, and scanned with a short protocol may succeed without any drug. When sedation is required, the anesthetic equipment and the monitor must be compatible with the magnetic field, and the anesthetist must understand that a ferromagnetic cylinder cannot enter the room. The sedation protocol, the compatible equipment, and the pediatric dose of any agent must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Monitoring a patient inside the magnet is a specialized problem, because ordinary monitors contain iron and ordinary leads can act as an antenna. A patient who needs continuous observation requires equipment formally rated as safe or conditional for the magnetic field, used at the field strength and in the position the rating permits. An electrocardiogram, called ECG, is possible with compatible equipment, though the trace is often distorted by the flow of blood in the field, and pulse oximetry, blood pressure, and capnography each have a maximum cable length and a routing rule. Radiofrequency energy can also burn skin, and the burn is almost always caused by a conductive loop, because a cable that touches the patient and then the bore, or that crosses another cable, concentrates energy at the point of contact. A coil cable, an ECG lead, or a monitoring wire laid carelessly across the skin can produce a serious burn in a patient who cannot feel it. The rule is that no cable touches the skin unless it must, that cables run without crossing the body, and that padding separates the patient from the bore wall, and the compatibility rating, the routing, and the skin inspection after each scan must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A magnetic resonance room is a radiofrequency fortress, because the signal it listens to is weaker than the radio and television broadcasts that fill the air, so the walls, floor, ceiling, and door are lined with a continuous conductive layer, usually copper, whose seams are bonded so that no gap can leak. Every penetration for a pipe or a cable is fitted with a waveguide that lets the service through while blocking the frequencies of interest, and a break in the shield appears as a stripe or a shower of noise across every image. The magnet also needs services most hospital rooms were never designed to provide, including chilled water and a compressor for the cold head, a stable electrical supply sized for the gradients, and an uninterruptible power supply for the control console and the cryogenic monitoring. It needs helium delivered by road on a schedule, with a storage vessel and a transfer line, because a magnet that runs out of helium quenches, and that supply chain is the most fragile part of the installation. The floor must carry the concentrated weight of the magnet, and the room needs ventilation for released gases and for heat. The shield integrity, the chilled water temperature, the electrical load, the floor loading, and the helium delivery interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The consumables of a magnetic resonance department are modest in appearance and absolute in function, and a missing item can cancel a scheduled scan. Every patient needs hearing protection in the correct size, an intravenous cannula and tubing for contrast, a skin preparation and dressing for the puncture, and pads, straps, and wedges to hold the position, while the contrast agent is stored according to its label, checked for clarity and expiry, and drawn up as a single dose for a single patient. Compatible monitoring electrodes and cables, a working call button, and a charged and tested ferromagnetic detector belong to the same list. Cleaning a magnetic resonance room has one rule that overrides every other consideration, which is that no cleaning equipment containing ferromagnetic metal may ever be taken past the zone 3 door, because a mop with a metal bucket and a floor polisher are projectiles. The table, the coil surfaces, the bore liner, and the patient handling aids are wiped between patients with a disinfectant the maker of the equipment allows, and coils are handled gently because their conductors and tuning components are fragile. The expiry date, the storage temperature, the dose of every agent, the concentration and contact time of each disinfectant, and the list of approved cleaning equipment must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Maintenance of a magnetic resonance scanner is mostly the maintenance of its cold chain and its coils, and it is performed by engineers with equipment that never enters the magnet room. The helium level is recorded and the vessel refilled on a schedule, the cold head and compressor are serviced, the water cooling loop is checked for flow and temperature, and the magnetic field is checked for homogeneity with a phantom and measured against a baseline so that a distance in the picture matches a distance in the patient. Every coil is tested individually, because one failing element in an array degrades part of every examination performed with it. The failure modes announce themselves first as changes in the image, so a motion artifact appears as blur or ghosting that repeats in the direction of the movement, a zipper artifact is a line of noise caused by a radiofrequency leak from the door or a cable, and a dark band that appears with one coil and disappears with another proves the coil is at fault. A cold head that becomes unusually loud, a compressor that runs continuously, or a rising helium boil off rate are early warnings of cryogenic failure rather than of image quality. The refill interval, the field homogeneity tolerance, the signal to noise baseline, and the escalation procedure for a cryogenic alarm must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The operator's start of shift check is short, deliberate, and recorded, and it is the habit that prevents most accidents. The zone doors are confirmed locked, the warning signs and lights are working, the ferromagnetic detector is charged and tested against a known object, and the screening forms are stocked in every required language. The quench button is confirmed present and unobstructed, the oxygen monitor is reading and its alarm is tested, the emergency contacts for the cryogenic system are posted, and the helium level, compressor status, and coils are checked. The patient list is reviewed in advance so that implants, contrast, sedation, and monitoring needs are identified before the patient arrives. An emergency inside the magnet room is unlike any other emergency in the hospital, because the first instinct of a rescuer may be the thing that kills, so a patient who collapses in the bore must be brought out before resuscitation begins and no ferromagnetic defibrillator, oxygen cylinder, or stretcher may cross the five gauss line. If a person is pinned by a ferromagnetic object and cannot be freed, the quench button is pressed as a last resort, accepting the loss of the magnet to save that person, and the evacuation route, the approved resuscitation equipment, and the quench decision criteria must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Staff who work in the suite are screened like patients, because a technologist with a pacemaker or a ferromagnetic implant must not enter zone 4, so each person authorized to enter signs a screening declaration and reports any new implant. Training covers the zones, the questionnaire, the projectile hazard, the quench, the oxygen alarm, the emergency procedure, and the recognition of a failing image, and it is repeated on a defined interval, while a visitor, a cleaner, or an engineer must be escorted and screened before crossing the line. The finished examination is a set of images plus the information that makes them interpretable, so the images travel to the picture archiving and communication system, called PACS, along with the sequence parameters, the field strength, the coil used, the contrast agent and its dose if any, and any adverse event. The technologist records the screening result, the sedation given, the repeat, and the clinical question, because a reader who does not know what was being looked for may miss the finding that answers it. The last and most important piece of equipment is the trained person, because the examination is safe only as long as someone is thinking about safety at the door. The training content, the re-screening interval for staff, the escort rule, the data fields required in the record, and the retention period must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

In a mobile or frontier clinic the magnetic resonance device is usually a low field scanner, and its physics is the same while its compromises are large. Such a scanner may use a permanent magnet or a resistive electromagnet rather than a superconducting one, so it needs no liquid helium, no quench pipe, and no compressor, and it can be sited in a room a hospital scanner could never use. Field strength is commonly in the range of 0.2 tesla to 0.5 tesla, and the lower field reduces the signal available for every image. A permanent magnet cannot be turned off, so the safety perimeter and the screening rules survive in full even though the field is weaker, and the same questionnaire is used without exception. A dedicated extremity scanner is the other field relevant variant, built to image a hand, a wrist, or a knee rather than a whole body, and its magnet is small and often open, so the patient sits beside it and places the limb into the gap. That arrangement is closer to a radiographic examination than to a tunnel, so claustrophobia nearly disappears and the fringe field is small enough that the room needs less control. The field strength, the limb to be examined, and the safety perimeter must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

What the low field variant gives up is signal to noise before anything else, and almost every other loss follows from that one. Finer resolution and thin slices become difficult, so small structures and subtle lesions may not be visible at all, and fat suppression becomes less reliable when so many examinations depend on making fat dark so that fluid or a lesion stands out. Sequences that require many rapid switches of the gradients are slow and limited by heating, so functional work, angiography, and cardiac imaging may be impossible. What it demands instead is time, patience, and discipline, because scan times are longer, motion is punished more severely when the signal is weak, and coil placement is even more critical. Quality assurance in the field is a written record and a simple phantom rather than a service contract, so a standard test object is scanned at fixed settings on a regular schedule and compared with the previous set. Where no scanner exists at all, the honest work is to answer the question with the equipment in hand and to refer the rest, and the referral decision turns on the question, the urgency, and whether the answer will change management. Every benchmark, interval, acquisition time, and safety rule used in the field must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

To summarize, magnetic resonance imaging places the patient in a strong static field, tips the hydrogen protons with a pulse of radiofrequency energy, locates them with gradients, and listens with a coil to the signal they return, so the picture is a map of relaxation rather than a shadow of absorption. The static field in tesla, the gradient coils and their noise, the coils chosen by body part, and the sequence that weights the image are the working parts of the hospital machine, while the four zone model, the screening questionnaire, the five gauss line, the quench button, and the oxygen monitor are the working parts of its safety. A ferromagnetic object in the wrong hands becomes a projectile, a gadolinium agent in a failing kidney becomes a poisoning, and a cable looped across the skin becomes a burn, so the hazard, the alarm, and the operator error are named before the patient is placed in the bore. The field variant trades signal, speed, and fine detail for a low field magnet that needs no helium, and in exchange it demands longer scans, stricter positioning, and the same absolute screening discipline. In every setting the rule is the same: no one enters zone 4 unscreened, nothing ferromagnetic crosses the line, and every setting, alarm threshold, and agent dose is confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before a patient is scanned.
