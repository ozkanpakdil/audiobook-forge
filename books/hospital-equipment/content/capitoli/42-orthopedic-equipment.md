# Chapter 42 — Orthopedic Equipment

Orthopedic surgery is the surgery of bone, and bone is unlike any other tissue that the instruments in this book are asked to cut. It is a composite of a flexible collagen scaffold and a hard mineral phase, and the two behave differently according to the direction of loading. Cortical bone, the dense outer shell, is strong along its length and much weaker across it, while cancellous bone, the spongy interior, crushes progressively rather than breaking cleanly. Every tool in this chapter exists because bone must be cut, drilled, reamed, held, or replaced. Each action is a contest between a hard brittle material and the soft living tissue around it, and a listener who grasps that contest will understand why these instruments are heavy, cooled, and unforgiving of haste.

The central problem of cutting bone is heat, because bone is alive and its cells die at temperatures an ordinary drill reaches in seconds. The energy of the rotating cutting edge becomes friction at the interface, and without cooling it raises the bone far above body temperature. Osteocytes, the cells that maintain the bone matrix, begin to die when the local temperature stays near 47 degrees Celsius for about one minute, so the bone is replaced by weaker tissue and an implant may loosen months later. The drill clears chips and heat only when it is sharp, when it turns fast enough to cut rather than rub, and when the surgeon uses light pressure. Irrigation with cool sterile fluid removes the remaining heat, and the common error is to lean on the drill and pause at one spot instead of withdrawing to clear the chips, so the temperature threshold and the irrigation routine must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Orthopedic power tools are grouped by how they make rotation. An electric tool turns a motor with current and gives steady torque from mains power, but it ties the surgeon to a cable. A battery tool carries its own chemical energy and trades power for freedom from the cord, which matters where the supply is unreliable. A pneumatic tool runs on compressed gas and is light and robust because it has no electric motor, but it needs clean dry air and its exhaust is cold and noisy. The saws add oscillation: the blade sweeps through a small arc many times each second, so its teeth engage rigid bone, plaster, or metal and retreat while soft tissue is pushed rather than cut, and typical saw speeds of 10,000 to 20,000 oscillations each minute must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The cutting end of the tool is a consumable with a short life. Drill bits for bone are fluted, with helical grooves that carry debris out of the hole, and they are made of stainless steel or tungsten carbide, which holds an edge longer but is brittle. Burs are small rotating cutters for shaping, and their round, acorn, and tapered shapes decide whether they cut a hollow, a flat, or a groove. A modern quick coupling locks the shank with a ring, while an older keyed chuck needs a key that is easily lost. The hazards here are variations on one accident, in which a glove, a drape, a gown, a cable, or a strand of suture wraps around a spinning shank and pulls the surgeon's hand toward the bone, and the common error is to leave a handpiece on a drape with the trigger locked on.

Reaming enlarges a canal so that an implant of known size will fit, and it is done in steps rather than in one pass. The medullary canal is an irregular cavity lined with soft tissue, and a reamer is a rotating cylinder with spiral blades that shaves the wall as it advances over a guide wire. Each reamer is slightly larger than the last, and orthopedic sets are made in increments of 0.5 millimeters for many applications, a step that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A reamer pushed off the line of the canal thins one wall until the cortex cracks, and long reaming of a femur can drive marrow fat into the circulation and produce a fat embolism. The common error is to force a blunt reamer with heavy pressure, which produces heat, eccentricity, and a canal larger than the planned implant.

Traction applies a steady pull to a limb or to part of the skeleton, and its principle is that a continuous force balanced by countertraction can hold a fracture in alignment while it heals. The force comes from a weight on a cord over a pulley, and the countertraction is usually the weight of the patient's body against the bed. Skin traction is generally limited to about 5 kilograms, while skeletal traction carried by a pin through bone can reach 15 kilograms or more, and both limits must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A balanced suspension frame adds cords and pulleys so the limb can be lifted for care without losing its pull, and the pulleys must turn freely because friction destroys the calibration. The characteristic alarm is the clang of a weight reaching the floor, and the common error is to rest the weights on the bed for care, which silently removes the traction.

Skeletal traction begins with a pin, and the pin site is a decision about anatomy. A pin through the lower femur is used for fractures of the femur, a pin through the tibial tuberosity is used when the knee joint must be avoided, and a pin through the heel is used for fractures of the tibia. A threaded wire grips the bone better but is weaker at the thread, and a smooth pin can slide and loosen earlier, so the pin is drilled at low speed with irrigation, because a pin drilled hot leaves a ring of dead bone that becomes the entry point for infection. Both ends are held in a curved bow that spreads the load so the wire does not bend in the middle. Pin sites must be cleaned and dressed according to the local protocol, and any redness, discharge, or loosening must be reported at once, because a pin tract infection can track to the bone.

An external fixator turns the principle of traction into a rigid frame. Pins or wires are placed into the bone above and below the fracture, and rods and clamps connect them into a frame that holds the fragments from outside the skin and carries the load the broken bone cannot. A unilateral frame places pins on one side, a bilateral frame on both sides, and a circular frame uses thin tensioned wires on rings, with a wire tension of about 90 to 130 kilograms of force, a value that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Because the metal never crosses the fracture inside the limb, the wound remains visible and can be inspected and dressed, which is the great advantage in the contaminated or the open fracture. The characteristic problem is the pin site, where a small discharge crusts, a red margin spreads, and a pin loosens. The common errors are to overtighten a clamp until the threads strip and to leave a clamp only hand tight, and both produce a frame that looks solid and moves under load.

Internal fixation moves the holding force inside the body, and its two families are the plate and the screw. A plate is a flat metal bar with holes that lies on the surface of the bone and holds the fracture by friction, by compression, or by bridging a comminuted zone, while a locking plate grips the screw heads so the assembly behaves as one rigid frame. A cortical screw has a fine thread for dense bone and is commonly 3.5 millimeters in diameter, and a cancellous screw has a coarse thread for spongy bone and is commonly 6.5 millimeters in diameter, so the exact diameters and lengths must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A screw is chosen so that its tip ends just beyond the far cortex, because a screw that protrudes can injure a vessel, a nerve, or a tendon. The common error is to use a worn screwdriver, which rounds off the head so the screw can neither be tightened nor removed, and the second is to mix metals from different makers, because two alloys in one wound corrode each other.

An intramedullary nail is a long metal rod inserted into the hollow center of a long bone, and it works because a splint along the axis shares load far better than a plate on the surface. The nail is introduced through an entry point at one end, passed across the fracture, and fixed with locking screws placed through holes near each end. The locking screws control rotation and prevent the fragments from sliding along the nail, and the pattern of locking decides how much load passes through bone and how much through metal. A nail can be inserted into a reamed canal, which allows a thicker nail but adds heat and marrow embolization, or unreamed, which is faster and gentler but limits the size of the nail. Wires are the thinnest implants, and the most feared complication of a Kirschner wire is migration, because one that is not bent or anchored can travel through tissue and has reached the heart and the great vessels, so the diameters and insertion techniques must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

All of these implants are placed with an instrumentation set, and the completeness of that set is a patient safety issue. A typical set holds the drill guide, the depth gauge, the tap, the countersink, the screwdriver, the torque-limiting handle, the reduction forceps, the bone holding forceps, and the mallet. The drill guide controls the angle and protects the soft tissue, the depth gauge measures the hole so the correct screw length can be chosen, and the torque-limiting handle stops the surgeon before the screw strips or the plate deforms. Implants arrive in sterile packages and must never be resterilized, never reused once opened, and must be recorded by the package label so the exact device and its lot can be traced later, and every screw and implant is counted at the end of the case because a retained implant is a never event. The common error is to substitute an instrument from another set, which fits loosely, slips, and damages the implant and the bone, and the second error is to leave the instrumentation in a tray of blood and bone for hours, which hardens until cleaning is impossible.

A tourniquet is a cuff wrapped around a limb and inflated until the pressure inside it stops the flow of blood in the arteries beneath. When that pressure exceeds the systolic pressure of the patient with a margin to spare, the artery is compressed and the limb becomes bloodless, which makes surgery possible and makes the tourniquet dangerous. A common upper limb pressure is about 250 millimeters of mercury and a common lower limb pressure is about 300 to 350 millimeters of mercury, figures that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A common safe limit is 60 to 90 minutes for the upper limb and 120 to 150 minutes for the lower limb before the cuff is released for reperfusion, times that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The characteristic alarm is the pressure loss or leak alarm of the regulator, and the common error is to ignore it, because a cuff that has lost pressure no longer controls the field, so the operator must record the inflation time and must never allow an alcohol-based skin preparation to pool beneath the cuff and produce a chemical burn.

Not every tourniquet is a surgical instrument, and the difference matters when one is improvised in an emergency. A tourniquet used to stop catastrophic bleeding is a rescue device, and it must be wide enough to compress the artery without cutting into the skin and the nerve beneath it. The pressure under a band is the force divided by the area over which it is applied, so a narrow cord or a thin strap concentrates the same force into a tiny area and can injure the nerve even when it stops the bleeding. A field tourniquet should therefore be at least 5 centimeters wide, a width that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and it is tightened with a windlass or a ratchet until the bleeding stops and no pulse can be felt beyond it. The common error is to apply a venous tourniquet, tight enough to block the return of blood but not the inflow, and the second error is to loosen the device periodically, because that releases the clot and restarts the bleeding.

A cast is a rigid shell that holds a limb still while bone or soft tissue heals, and its chemistry decides how it behaves in the hand. Plaster of Paris begins as calcium sulfate hemihydrate, a powder that becomes a paste when mixed with water, and the paste sets because the hemihydrate takes up water and recrystallizes into interlocking crystals of the dihydrate. The reaction releases heat, and that heat is the first hazard of casting, because a thick cast mixed with warm water can raise the skin temperature beneath it into a burn. The setting time depends on the temperature of the water, so lukewarm water of about 20 to 30 degrees Celsius gives several minutes of working time, a range that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and a wet plaster cast takes about 24 to 48 hours to dry fully, a period that must also be confirmed. The padding is part of the device, with a stockinette next to the skin, layers of wadding over it, and extra felt over the heel, the ankle, the knee, and the elbow, and the mold made while the cast sets produces the three-point fixation that resists the deforming force.

Synthetic casting material replaces the plaster crystals with a knitted fabric of fiberglass or polyester impregnated with a polyurethane resin, and the resin cures when it absorbs moisture. The synthetic cast is lighter than plaster of the same strength, it is radiolucent so a radiograph can see the bone through it, it tolerates water once cured, and it sets in minutes rather than in a day. A typical synthetic cast sets in about 5 to 20 minutes, a range that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and because it gives less working time it must be applied by someone who has already planned the mold. The synthetic material is less forgiving of a poor mold, it can irritate the skin if the edges are rough, and it is harder to remove than plaster because a dull blade will not cut it cleanly. A splint is the same material laid along one or two sides of the limb over padding, and its great advantage is that it does not close the circumference, so the limb can swell without being strangled and a fresh injury is usually splinted rather than cast.

Removing a cast depends on one elegant principle. The cast saw has a stiff blade driven back and forth through a very small arc, so the teeth move fast but travel only a little in each direction. A hard, brittle material such as plaster or cured resin cannot follow that rapid reversal, so the teeth chip into it and cut a groove, while soft skin yields and moves with the blade instead of resisting it, so the blade does not cut it. The same principle makes the saw dangerous, because the blade still becomes hot from friction, and the shoe that rides on the skin can burn if the blade is dull or the operator presses too hard. The operator must use a sharp blade, must keep it perpendicular to the surface, and must stop whenever the patient reports heat, and the common error is to cut through the padding in one deep pass instead of lifting the cast away in stages, after which the limb is examined for skin damage and the neurovascular status is checked.

Arthroscopy looks into a joint through two small openings, and it works because a joint can be turned into a cavity. A rigid telescope built from glass lenses and a bundle of light-carrying fibers is passed through a portal into the joint, and sterile fluid is pumped in to distend the joint capsule so that there is space to see and to work. A typical irrigation pressure lies between about 40 and 60 millimeters of mercury, a value that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and a pressure that is too high drives fluid into the soft tissues and can produce a compartment syndrome. The second portal admits a shaver, which trims torn tissue, or a radiofrequency probe, which seals tissue with heat, or a hand instrument such as a probe. The characteristic alarm is the pressure or occlusion alarm of the pump, and the common error is to raise the pressure instead of checking whether the inflow cannula has left the joint, while the arthroscope, the camera head, the light cable, and the shaver handpiece are heat sensitive and must be sterilized by a low-temperature process whose cycle parameters must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The power tools and the arthroscopes are the most maintenance-hungry items in the orthopedic department, and most of their failures can be predicted. A battery pack is a set of cells whose chemistry decides its habits: an older nickel-cadmium pack loses capacity if it is repeatedly recharged before it is discharged, a nickel-metal hydride pack has less of that memory but loses charge while it sits, and a lithium-ion pack holds its charge well but must never be charged while it is hot from the sterilizer. A common hospital cycle for a wrapped handpiece is 134 degrees Celsius for 4 minutes in saturated steam, a cycle that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and a battery must never be put through it, because steam destroys the cells and the sealed case. The chuck must be cleaned of bone dust, the flutes of the drill and the reamer must be flushed, and the moving parts must receive only the lubricant the maker specifies, because a general-purpose oil traps debris and gums the mechanism, so every failure must be recorded and the tool removed from service.

Cleaning decides whether sterilization will work, and orthopedic instruments are the hardest things in the hospital to clean. Bone, blood, fat, and cement harden inside cannulations, threads, ratchets, and chucks, and a sterilizer cannot penetrate a plug of dried tissue, so the instrument must be opened, dismantled where the maker allows, and flushed through every channel. Cannulated drills, cannulated screws, reamers, and suction tips are flushed with water in both directions, and the fine channels are cleaned with a brush of the correct size, all according to the local reprocessing protocol. The instruments then go through the cleaning and sterilization pathway of the Central Sterile Services Department, or CSSD, where the washer-disinfector and steam sterilizer cycles are validated and recorded, and the cycle parameters must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Burs, saw blades, drill bits, reamers, drapes, and tubing are consumables with a stated life, and a cutting edge that has lost its bite is replaced rather than sharpened. Bone cement is a two-part powder and liquid that polymerizes in the wound, and the reaction is strongly exothermic, with a peak temperature that can exceed 70 degrees Celsius, a value that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, while the toxic monomer can cause a fall in blood pressure, so the anesthetist is warned before it is inserted.

Before any orthopedic case, the operator and the team run through the same list, and most of it targets the failure that would be worst at the worst moment. The power supply and the battery charge are checked, and at least one charged spare battery is on the trolley, because a drill that stops in the middle of a nail is a serious problem. The blades, burs, drill bits, reamers, and saw blades are inspected for sharpness, cracks, missing teeth, and correct fit in the chuck. The irrigation fluid, the suction, and the tourniquet are prepared and tested, with the cuff checked for leaks and the intended inflation time recorded on the anesthetic record. The implant set is matched against the planned procedure, the implants are checked for size, side, and package integrity, and the image intensifier is positioned with the lead apron, the thyroid shield, and the dosimeter worn by everyone in the room, because the radiation dose to the team is cumulative.

In a mobile or frontier clinic the same operations are done with far less, and the substitution changes the character of the work. The power drill may be replaced by a hand drill, a cranked or T-handled device that turns the bit by muscle alone, and by a mallet, which drives a pin through bone by impact rather than rotation. A hand drill gives up speed, controlled torque, and the irrigation pump that cools the bone, so the surgeon turns slowly and pauses often, and the drill is withdrawn at intervals to let the chips clear and the heat escape. The external fixator becomes the workhorse of the field, because it can hold almost any long bone fracture with pins and rods light enough to carry, and because it can be applied through a small incision under local anesthesia. The internal plate and the intramedullary nail give up their place to the external frame and to simple wires, because they demand a full implant set, a power drill, and an image intensifier that the field clinic does not have. The plaster becomes the universal immobilizer, and a carpenter's saw with a fine blade becomes the cast saw, which has no oscillation and no guard, so the operator must cut slowly with a finger between the blade and the skin, and sterility is kept with the smallest possible kit and instruments boiled or chemically disinfected according to the local protocol.

The field clinic also has its own safety hazards, and they are the hazards of the environment as much as of the device. A hand drill can slip and drive the bit into the surgeon's hand or into a vessel, so the limb must be held firmly on a stable surface and the line of the drill kept away from the body. A mallet strike that misses the pin strikes the hand, and a pin driven too fast can crack the bone, so the assistant must hold the pin steady and the surgeon must strike with control. Improvised traction using stones, sandbags, or water containers works by the same physics but cannot be calibrated, so the force is estimated, the skin is checked more often, and the local protocol decides how the weight is secured. An external fixator in the field is exposed to dust, mud, and rain, so the pin sites are cleaned at every opportunity and the frame is protected with a clean dressing. An open fracture in a field clinic carries a high risk of infection, so the wound is cleaned and debrided and antibiotics are given according to the local protocol, and where no image intensifier exists the reduction is judged by feel, by alignment, and by plain radiographs.

The failures of orthopedic equipment are rarely sudden and rarely mysterious, and a technician who knows the machine can often hear them coming. A battery that is losing capacity shows itself as a tool that slows under load before it stops, and a drill that is overheating smells hot at the housing. A blade that is dull requires more pressure and produces more dust and more heat, and a blunt reamer is the common cause of a canal that is too hot. A chuck that is worn slips on the shank and rounds it, so the bit no longer runs true and the hole is oversized. A tourniquet cuff that is failing leaks slowly, so its alarm sounds late in the case, and its tubing must be checked for cracks and its bladder for folds before every use. A cast saw with a dull blade burns the patient, and a cement stored too warm sets in the mixing bowl, so every failure must be recorded, because a device that has failed once will fail again.

To summarize, orthopedic equipment is a family of tools built around one hard, living material, and every design decision follows from that fact. Bone must be cut with sharp edges, at controlled speeds, under irrigation, because the heat of cutting kills the bone and a dead surface is a loose implant waiting to happen. Traction, splints, casts, and external fixators hold a fracture from outside, while plates, screws, nails, and wires hold it from inside, and each method trades one kind of control for another. The tourniquet buys a bloodless field with a measured pressure and a measured time. The cast saw cuts hard material while sparing soft tissue, and the power tools demand batteries that are never autoclaved. In the field the principles do not change but the apparatus does, and the hand drill, the mallet, the external fixator, and the plaster slab ask for skill, patience, and hygiene in place of power and imaging, so the operator must know the physical principle, must check the device before the case, must confirm every setting, pressure, temperature, and cycle against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and must recognize the failure before it reaches the patient.
