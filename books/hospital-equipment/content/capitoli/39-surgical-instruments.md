# Chapter 39 — Surgical Instruments

A surgical instrument is a hand tool that concentrates the force of an arm onto a few square millimeters of tissue, and almost every instrument on a theater tray is one of a few simple machines. A scalpel is a wedge, a scissors is two wedges on a first-class lever, a hemostatic clamp is a spring lever with a ratchet, a forceps is a pair of pincers, and a retractor is a lever that holds tissue under tension. Each design answers the same questions: where the force enters, where the pivot sits, how the grip is shaped, and what the working end must do to the tissue it meets. A listener who sees instruments as levers, wedges, and springs will understand why a dull blade tears, why a long-handled scissors needs less effort, and why a ratchet that has lost its bite fails suddenly.

Every hinged instrument reads from the hand outward, with a handle or rings, a shank, a joint, and a jaw or blade. The handle is shaped for the glove and sets how much torque the wrist can deliver, and its flat sides stop it turning when it is wet. The shank sets the length of the lever, so a long shank gives reach into a deep cavity but also a long bending moment, which is why the longest instruments bend first at the joint. The joint, called the box lock, is the pivot and the weakest point, and it is where blood, saline, and cleaning fluid collect and where corrosion begins. The jaw or blade is the working end, and its shape is a compromise between grip and injury, sharpness and strength, and delicacy and durability. Fatigue and corrosion both start at the box lock and the tip, so the operator must inspect both before use, because a crack at the box lock can open under load and leave half of a clamp inside a wound.

The metal decides how an instrument behaves, and most surgical steel is a compromise between hardness and corrosion resistance. Stainless steel resists rust because chromium forms a thin oxide layer that seals the surface, and that layer repairs itself only when the metal is clean and exposed to air. Harder grades hold a keener edge but contain less chromium and corrode more easily, while softer grades resist staining but lose their edge sooner. Instruments are commonly made from a hardenable grade for cutting edges and a more corrosion-resistant grade for clamps and handles, and the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) designation is the reliable way to know what has been supplied. Titanium is lighter, nonmagnetic, and highly corrosion resistant, which is why it is chosen for neurosurgical and ophthalmic work and for patients with a nickel allergy, but it is softer at the edge and easily scratched. Tungsten carbide inserts give a hard, long-lasting jaw, but the insert is bonded into the steel and can loosen or fall out, so a loose insert must be found before the wound is closed and every sterilization temperature and cycle named in this chapter must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The scalpel is the sharpest tool on the tray, and its physics is the physics of pressure, because cutting requires a pressure greater than the tissue's own strength and pressure equals force divided by area, so a blade ground to a very thin edge needs a light touch while a dull blade needs a heavy push. The handle is numbered by size and blade family, and the blade is numbered by shape, so the operator chooses a shape for the task rather than the largest blade available, with a small handle held like a pen for fine work and a large handle in the palm for long incisions. The blade is fitted with a needle holder and never with the fingers, must seat until it clicks, and must be checked with a gentle tug, because the loading motion is the commonest cause of a scalpel injury to the operator. The characteristic warning of a scalpel is not an alarm but a change in feel, since the blade begins to skate, the incision becomes ragged, and more force is required. The common error is to pass the instrument or discard it with a used blade still on the handle, and the safety rule is that blades are loaded and removed with a holder and dropped immediately into a sharps container.

Scissors cut by shear, and the two blades act as opposing wedges that slide past each other rather than meeting edge to edge. The length of the handles relative to the blades sets the mechanical advantage, so short blades on long handles multiply the force of the hand and give fine control, while long blades on short handles cut thick tissue in fewer strokes. A curved blade follows a plane of dissection without lifting the tissue, a straight blade cuts on a flat surface, and the tips are sharp on both blades, sharp on one and blunt on the other, or blunt on both. A suture scissors has short blades, one blunt or notched tip, and a shape designed to catch a thread without cutting the tissue beneath it, while a tissue scissors has longer, sharper blades and should never be used on suture. Cutting suture with tissue scissors notches the edge, and cutting tissue with suture scissors crushes rather than divides it, so the two instruments must not be substituted for each other. The characteristic warning of a scissors that is failing is that it chews, because the blades no longer meet along their whole length, and the common error is to force it through material heavier than it was designed for, which springs the blades apart permanently.

Forceps are pincers whose physics is the first-class lever, with the pivot at the box lock and the hand closing the shanks toward each other. A non-toothed forceps has ridges, a matte finish, or fenestrations that grip by friction over a broad area and is called atraumatic because the tissue it holds is not punctured, while a toothed forceps has small interlocking points that bite into tissue and prevent slipping. Toothed patterns are described by the arrangement of the teeth, so a 2 by 3 pattern and a 1 by 2 pattern are different instruments with different grip and different injury, and they are used on skin and fascia where grip matters more than gentleness. A vessel, a nerve, a bowel wall, or a delicate organ held in a toothed forceps is likely to be crushed or perforated, and the injury may not be obvious when it is made. The characteristic warning of tissue injury is a white or dusky mark that does not refill when the forceps are released, and the common error is to grip harder when tissue slips, when the answer is a better instrument rather than a stronger hand.

A hemostatic clamp is a spring lever with a ratchet, and the ratchet turns a squeeze of the hand into a lock that holds by itself. The shanks act as springs, the box lock is the pivot, and the jaws close as the ratchet catches one notch after another, so the number and spacing of the teeth set how tightly the jaws close. The familiar names describe different jobs: the mosquito is a very fine clamp for the smallest vessels, the Kelly and Crile carry grooves on the distal and the whole jaw, and the Rochester-Carmalt is a heavy clamp with long grooves for large vessels. A straight clamp is used on a surface, while a curved clamp reaches around a vessel or into a deep cavity where the tip must follow a curve. The Kocher carries teeth at the tip that penetrate and hold tissue, the Allis carries interlocking teeth that grasp a mass of tissue firmly, and the Babcock carries smooth fenestrated jaws that hold bowel or a tube without crushing it. A towel clip holds drapes either by piercing or without piercing, so the choice must match the surface beneath. The characteristic warning of a failing clamp is the click that no longer locks or a jaw that no longer meets at the tip, and the common errors are to use a penetrating clamp on bowel and to leave a clamp on a pedicle until the tissue beneath dies.

A retractor holds tissue away from the field, and its physics is tension and the distribution of pressure, because the pull on the handle is spread over the area of the blade. A broad blade therefore produces less pressure on any one point than a narrow blade carrying the same pull, which is why blade shape matters as much as the strength of the arm. Hand-held retractors are simple levers, a blade on a handle, sometimes with a lip or a rake of teeth to stop it slipping, and a malleable retractor can be bent by hand to fit a cavity. Self-retaining retractors turn tension into a frame, using a screw, a ratchet, or a spring to hold the blades apart without an assistant, in patterns for the abdomen, chest, spine, neck, and limb, and they free a pair of hands and give steady exposure. They apply force for as long as they remain in place, so prolonged retraction of the shoulder can stretch the brachial plexus and prolonged retraction of the abdomen can impair breathing and reduce the return of blood to the heart. The characteristic warning is a pale tissue, a changed pulse in the limb, or a change on the monitor as the retractor tightens, and the common error is to retract harder to see better instead of repositioning or releasing the blade.

The needle holder is a lever that grips a needle and transmits the turning force of the wrist to it, and it often decides whether a suture is placed cleanly. Its jaws are cross-hatched or fitted with tungsten carbide inserts to grip a smooth steel needle, and its ratchet or spring holds the needle while the surgeon regrips, with a ratcheted holder locking at one of several tensions and a spring-handled holder releasing as soon as the hand relaxes. The needle should be grasped at the junction of its shaft and curve, or across its middle, so that the curve follows the rotation of the wrist and the needle enters at right angles and leaves by the same arc. Grasping a needle at its point dulls the point, and grasping it at the very end of the curve gives no leverage and bends it with the first push. A needle holder is not a cutter or a hammer, and using it for those jobs burrs the jaws and ruins the grip on which every future suture depends. The characteristic warning of a failing holder is the needle that turns inside the jaws or springs free at the moment of the push, and the common error is to force a needle through tissue tougher than the needle is designed for.

A suction tip removes blood, irrigation, and smoke by negative pressure, and its behavior is set by the physics of flow through a tube. Flow rises steeply as the bore widens, because resistance falls with the fourth power of the radius, so a small increase in tip diameter gives a large increase in what it can remove, while a partial blockage by clot reduces the flow far more than it appears to. A large single-opening tip moves fluid fast where the field is wet, and a thumb hole lets the operator break the vacuum without lifting the tip from tissue. A fenestrated tip draws through many small holes behind a shield so that soft tissue cannot be pulled into one opening, and it is the safer choice around bowel or a graft, while a very fine tip blocks easily. The hazards are the tissue drawn into the opening and injured, the graft or nerve sucked against the tip, and the blood removed faster than it can be replaced. The characteristic warning of a suction problem is a change in its sound, a sudden silence, a hiss at a loose connection, or a canister reaching its limit, and the common error is to use the tip as a retractor or a probe.

Beside the cutting, grasping, and holding instruments is a group of quiet tools whose purpose is to feel, to part, and to guide. A probe is a rigid rod that transmits the sense of touch from tip to hand, so the operator can feel a stone, a tract, or a mass without seeing it, and a probe too flexible or too thick destroys the information it carries. A grooved director is a probe with a channel that guides a knife or scissors into a stricture so the cut follows the groove, and a dissector, a periosteal elevator, and a curette are variations on a shaped tip that follows a plane or scrapes a surface without cutting across it. A skin hook uses a very small sharp point and tension to hold skin without crushing it. The characteristic warning of a probe is the false passage, because a tip pushed with force can create the very channel it was meant to find, and the common error is to push instead of letting it follow the natural line of the tissue.

Surgical instruments are organized into sets, and the set is a device in its own right because it decides what the team can do without leaving the room. A basic set holds a scalpel handle and blades, a needle holder, a scissors, toothed and non-toothed forceps, several hemostatic clamps, and towel clips, while a major abdominal set adds long clamps, deep retractors, large scissors, suction tips, and a range of needle holders. Each specialty adds its own: vascular fine clamps and scissors, orthopedic holding and bone-cutting tools, obstetric long curved clamps and cord scissors, and ophthalmic, neurologic, and pediatric sets scaled to the tissue and the patient. Set contents are standardized and the tray is laid out in the same order every time so a missing item is noticed at a glance, and heavy instruments are never stored on top of fine ones because they bend them. The characteristic alarm of a surgical set is not a sound but a number, the instrument count, taken before the case, before a cavity is closed, before the skin is closed, and at the end, and a count that does not match is treated as a retained instrument until it is proved otherwise. The common error is to add an instrument to the field without telling the person who keeps the count, and every count threshold must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Inspection is the operator's own check, and it must be done by the person who will use the instrument rather than assumed from the packaging. The first check is alignment, because the jaws of a clamp must meet along their whole length, a scissors must close smoothly to its tips, and a needle holder must grip a needle without the needle turning. The second check is the ratchet, which must lock at every tooth and release without sticking, because a ratchet that has lost a notch will open under the tension of a pedicle. The third check is for corrosion, and rust shows as a brown or black stain and pitting as small dark craters, either of which weakens the metal and shelters organisms from cleaning, so a stained instrument is removed from service rather than merely wiped. The fourth check is the box lock and screw, which must be tight but not frozen, because a loose screw lets the jaws wobble and a frozen joint hides debris, and the fifth is the cutting edge, tested on suture rather than on a finger. The characteristic warning of a hidden defect is an instrument that feels different, clicks at the wrong point, or shows rust inside the box lock, and the common error is to accept an instrument without looking at it, because packaging says nothing about alignment, sharpness, or a crack.

Lubrication and storage are the habits that decide how long a set lasts, and both are about water and friction. A film of the approved lubricant displaces water from the box lock and screw and lowers the friction of the joint, and the maker's instructions decide which lubricant may be used and which sterilization methods it can survive, while an unapproved oil attracts debris, may soften plastic, and can carry contamination into the joint. Instruments are stored dry, with ratchets open, on racks or trays that let air reach every surface, because an instrument closed on its own ratchet traps moisture and blood in the box lock. Heavy instruments are stored below fine ones, and fine instruments rest on a soft mat rather than bare metal, because the vibration of handling is enough to blunt a delicate edge. Every sterilization cycle, temperature, and holding time referred to in this chapter must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, because a lubricant, a mat, or a packaging material can invalidate a cycle that looks correct, and the common error is to lubricate a stiff joint heavily when the correct remedy is cleaning and repair.

Reprocessing begins at the point of use, because blood and saline that dry on an instrument form a film that protects organisms and corrodes the steel. The instrument is wiped or rinsed of gross soil as soon as it leaves the field, kept moist during transport, and never left to soak in saline, because chloride ions pit stainless steel and the pits deepen with every cycle. In the reprocessing area, which in a larger hospital is the central sterile services department (CSSD), instruments are opened, disassembled where possible, and cleaned in an enzymatic or alkaline detergent chosen for surgical instruments, and the box lock, ratchet, and lumen of a hollow instrument need a brush because fluid alone does not reach them. An ultrasonic cleaner does the work a brush cannot, because sound waves create tiny bubbles that collapse against the surface and lift debris from joints and serrations, and it works only when the instruments are open and the solution is fresh, after which the instruments are rinsed with soft or deionized water because tap water leaves mineral deposits that stain the surface. Any instrument that fails inspection is set aside for repair or disposal before it is packed, since a defect that is packed becomes a defect that is used, and every cycle parameter must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The common error is to soak instruments in a chlorine solution that is not compatible with stainless steel, producing pitting that appears weeks later on a tray that was thought to be clean.

The failures of surgical instruments fall into a few families, and naming them makes them recognizable before they reach a patient. Corrosion, from blood, saline, a chlorine soak, or a humid store, weakens the metal and shelters organisms, showing first as staining and then as pitting and cracking. Fatigue is the slow cracking of metal under repeated stress, and it begins where stress concentrates, at the box lock and at any scratch, so a clamp used thousands of times can fail with no visible warning. Deformation is the bending or springing of a shank or jaw by a force greater than the design allows, and a sprung instrument stays out of alignment even if it is straightened. Wear is the loss of the edge or the grip, and it shows as a scissors that chews, a needle holder that lets the needle turn, and a forceps whose teeth no longer interlock, and the consequences are a fragment left in the wound, a vessel that bleeds again, a needle dropped in the field, or tissue crushed beyond its design. The characteristic warning is a small click and a change in the feel of the tool, and the common error is to keep a favorite instrument in service long after it has failed its inspection.

The field version begins with a short list, because a mobile clinic cannot carry a theater's worth of trays and does not need them. A workable field set is a scalpel handle with a small stock of blades, 1 or 2 needle holders, 4 to 6 hemostatic clamps in straight and curved patterns, a toothed forceps, a non-toothed forceps, one scissors for tissue and one for suture, 2 or 3 retractors, a grooved director or probe, a suction tip if suction is available, and a handful of towel clips. This list covers the incision, the control of bleeding, the retraction of tissue, the placement of a suture, and the removal of a specimen, with fewer choices rather than fewer possibilities. What the field set gives up is the specialty tray, the self-retaining retractor that holds exposure without hands, the tungsten carbide insert that keeps an edge for years, the powered suction that clears a deep field, and the count system that catches a missing item before the wound is closed. What it demands instead is that the operator inspect every instrument before every case, sharpen or replace what is dull, and clean and sterilize the whole set without a central department, and it also demands disciplined improvisation, since a clean metal spoon can retract, sterile tubing can drain, a bent wire can probe, and strong clean thread can suture, but only when the substitute can be cleaned and will not break or leave a fragment. The characteristic warning in the field is unchanged, a clamp that does not lock and a scissors that chews, but there is no spare tray to reach for, and the common error is to begin with an instrument known to be doubtful because the patient is already asleep.

Sterilization in the field is a cycle that must be measured rather than believed, and every boiling time, pressure cooker temperature, and holding time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Boiling kills most organisms when the instruments are fully submerged and the water has boiled for the full time the protocol requires, and a lid keeps the heat in while a suitable additive may raise the temperature only if the protocol allows it. A pressure cooker sterilizes by steam under pressure above the boiling point of water, and its time begins only when the pressure is reached and the air has been driven out, because trapped air keeps the temperature below the target even though the gauge looks right. A hospital steam autoclave commonly runs at 134 degrees Celsius for about 3 minutes or at 121 degrees Celsius for about 15 minutes, and a field pressure cooker reaches about 121 degrees Celsius in roughly 15 to 20 minutes, values that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Drying matters as much as sterilization in a humid climate, because a wet instrument in a closed box rusts overnight, so the set is dried and given a thin film of approved lubricant, and scissors are sharpened only along the bevel on a fine stone because sharpening the flat side destroys the two wedges that must slide past each other. The common error is to shorten the boiling time when the queue of cases is long, because the number of organisms on a used instrument is not reduced by the hurry.

The comparison between the two versions is the heart of the matter, and the field set is not a lesser hospital set but a different instrument with different rules. The hospital version offers specialty trays, the best available alloys, self-retaining retractors, powered suction, a central reprocessing department with validated cycles, and a count checked several times around every operation. The field version gives up that depth of choice, that quality of steel, that freedom of the surgeon's hands, and that systematic accounting, and it accepts a set small enough to be carried in a bag and reprocessed in a pot. What it demands in return is an operator who knows why a clamp locks, why a scissors chews, and why a needle turns in a worn jaw, and who inspects the set with the attention a hospital gives to a printed cycle record, and whether the available instruments are good enough for the operation proposed. The measure of safety is not the number of instruments on the tray but the condition of each one, the discipline of the count, and the operator's willingness to reject a tool that is not fit.

To summarize, a surgical instrument is a simple machine, a wedge, a lever, a spring, or a pair of pincers, shaped so that the force of a hand becomes a controlled effect on tissue. Scalpels cut by concentrated pressure, scissors shear, forceps grip by friction or teeth, clamps lock with a ratchet, retractors hold tissue under tension, needle holders turn a needle through an arc, and suction tips move fluid by negative pressure through a bore whose flow depends steeply on its width. The metal matters as much as the shape, because hardness and corrosion resistance pull in opposite directions, and because fatigue and corrosion begin at the box lock and the tip and end an instrument's life without a visible warning. The safety of the tray depends on inspection for alignment, ratchet function, corrosion, and sharpness, on cleaning that reaches the joints and lumens, on drying and lubrication before storage, and on a count repeated until it matches. Every temperature, cycle, holding time, and count threshold named in this chapter must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, because an instrument is only as safe as the process that prepared it. In the field, a short set covers most surgery and gives up the specialty tray, the fine alloy, and the mechanical retractor for portability and independence, and what it demands instead is an operator who inspects every tool before every case, sterilizes by a measured cycle, and refuses to operate with an instrument that is not fit.
