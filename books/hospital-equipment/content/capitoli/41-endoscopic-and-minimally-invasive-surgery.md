# Chapter 41 — Endoscopic and Minimally Invasive Surgery

Minimally invasive surgery is surgery performed through a small opening rather than a long incision, and every device in this chapter exists to make that substitution possible. The clinical promise is easy to state, because less of the body wall is divided there is less pain, less bleeding, a shorter hospital stay, and a faster return to ordinary activity. The cost is equally easy to state, because the surgeon no longer looks directly at the tissue and no longer touches it directly either. The eye is replaced by a camera on a cable, the hand by a shaft tens of centimeters long, and the working space by a cavity that must be created and held open with gas. Three substitutions, and everything that follows is a consequence of them.

The telescope is the eye, and it works by relaying an image along a tube. Light from the tissue enters a small objective lens at the tip, which brings it to a focus inside the instrument. In the oldest design a series of small lenses carried that image to the eyepiece, losing brightness at every glass surface. The modern rigid telescope replaces the air spaces with solid glass rods separated by narrow gaps, so most of the path is glass and the image crosses fewer air-to-glass boundaries. Each rod acts as a light pipe, and because the light strikes the glass wall at a shallow angle it is reflected back inside rather than escaping, a behavior called total internal reflection. The result is a bright image delivered to a socket at the back of the telescope, where a camera head or an eyepiece can be attached.

The camera head converts that optical image into an electrical signal. At its center is a solid-state image sensor, a silicon chip covered with a grid of tiny light-sensitive cells called pixels. Each cell accumulates an electrical charge in proportion to the number of photons that fall on it, and the chip reads the charge out row by row many times each second to build a moving picture. A single-sensor camera uses one chip under a mosaic color filter, while a three-sensor camera splits the light with a prism and sends red, green, and blue to separate chips for cleaner color. The camera is white balanced before use by pointing it at a white surface and telling the electronics that this is white, so the colors of everything else can be corrected. The camera head and its cable are not immersible unless the maker states otherwise, and the permitted disinfection or sterilization method must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Illumination comes from a light source on the trolley that sends light down a cable of glass fibers to the telescope. An older source used a halogen bulb, a later one a xenon arc lamp, and the newest a light emitting diode, or LED, array. The lamp is not merely bright but concentrated, and the end of the cable can reach a temperature high enough to burn tissue or ignite a drape if it is laid on the patient while the lamp is on. The cable is unplugged from the source, or the source placed in standby, whenever it is not in use, and its end is never left lying on the drapes. The lamp hour meter, the intensity setting, and the over-temperature and lamp failure alarms must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The laparoscopy tower is the rack that binds these parts into one machine. It holds the light source, the camera control unit, the monitor, the recorder, and often the insufflator on a wheeled trolley with a power strip and hooks for cable management. On a well-designed tower the monitor height is adjustable so that the surgeon's gaze and the axis of the instrument line up, because a screen placed too low or too far to one side causes neck and shoulder strain over a long case. The trolley also carries a spare lamp, a spare camera cable, and a spare light cable, because a dark screen during a dissection is an emergency. The recorder captures what the camera sees for teaching, audit, and defense, and it documents what was found and what was done. A high-definition camera feeding an older standard-definition screen wastes its resolution, and a long or damaged cable leaves shadow bars, color drift, or intermittent noise. The screen is cleaned only with a solution the maker permits, because alcohol and ammonia attack some surfaces and anti-glare coatings. The electrical safety of the tower, the viewing distance, the retention of recordings, and the permitted cleaning agents must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Making a working space is the central physical problem of laparoscopic surgery. The abdominal wall is a flexible tent over the organs, and unless it is lifted the cavity collapses and the camera sees nothing. The insufflator solves this by pushing gas into the abdomen through a needle or a port and holding a set pressure while a valve admits more gas to replace what leaks. Carbon dioxide is the gas of choice for three reasons, because it is highly soluble in blood, it does not support combustion, and it is cheap and widely available. Pressure is measured in millimeters of mercury and flow in liters per minute, and the insufflator reports both continuously. The target pressure, the maximum pressure, the flow rate, and the alarm thresholds must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A pneumoperitoneum is not a neutral act, and its consequences follow from simple mechanics. Pressure inside the abdomen pushes the diaphragm upward, reduces the volume the lungs can expand, and raises the airway pressure the ventilator must generate. The same pressure compresses the large veins that return blood to the heart, so venous return falls and blood pressure becomes sensitive to position and fluid status. Carbon dioxide is absorbed across the peritoneum and must be removed by breathing, so its level in the blood tends to rise and the ventilator is usually adjusted to remove more of it. The head-down position used to let the bowel fall away adds its own effects on the heart, the lungs, the eyes, and the brain, and a patient with heart or lung disease tolerates all of this poorly. These effects are why insufflation pressure is kept as low as the surgery allows, and the pressure and ventilation settings for each patient must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The insufflator has a small vocabulary of alarms, and each one points to a different cause. A high pressure alarm sounds when the set limit is exceeded, and the usual causes are a blocked port, a kink in the tubing, a patient coughing or straining, or an over-enthusiastic attempt to create space quickly. A low flow or flow failure alarm suggests a leak, an empty cylinder, a closed stopcock, or a disconnected tube, and the first response is to look for the leak rather than to raise the pressure. Some insufflators warm the gas before it enters, which reduces fogging of the telescope, and that heater has its own over-temperature alarm. The common operator error is to silence an alarm and continue, and the pattern that must never be dismissed is a sudden rise in pressure with a fall in flow, because the tip of the needle or port may have entered a structure. The pressure limits, flow limits, heater temperature, and alarm behavior must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The most feared consequence of insufflation is gas embolism, in which carbon dioxide enters a vein or an organ and travels to the heart and lungs. It appears as a sudden fall in blood pressure, a fall in the carbon dioxide measured at the end of exhalation, a change in heart rhythm, and a mill wheel murmur that is rarely heard in time. The response is to stop the gas, release the pressure, give oxygen, and position the patient so that gas is kept away from the heart, and the exact steps belong in the local protocol. Subcutaneous emphysema, in which gas tracks into the skin of the chest and neck, usually follows a port that has slipped out of the cavity or a pressure that is too high. A capnothorax can follow a defect in the diaphragm, and the anesthetist is usually first to notice because airway pressures rise and oxygen saturation falls.

A trocar is the door through which everything else enters. It has three parts, a tip that opens the abdominal wall, a hollow sleeve that stays behind, and a seal at the top that closes around whatever passes through it. Disposable trocars usually carry a spring-loaded shield over a blade so that the blade retracts once the wall is crossed, and reusable trocars rely on a blunt obturator or a dilating tip that separates muscle fibers rather than cutting them. The seal is a slit valve, a duckbill, or a combination, and it has a side stopcock for gas. A port with a worn seal leaks continuously and makes the insufflator work harder, and a port that has lost its shield can injure the bowel. The insertion technique, the port size, and the condition of the seal must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Most serious injuries of laparoscopy happen at the first entry, because that entry is made without seeing where the tip is going. The classical error is to push a sharp trocar too hard in a patient whose bowel lies against the abdominal wall, and the injury may not be recognized until the abdomen is examined from inside. The alternative is an open entry in which a small incision is made layer by layer and the port is placed under direct vision, and this route is often chosen in patients who have had previous surgery and are likely to have adhesions. A port site later becomes a weak point, and a port larger than 10 millimeters is usually closed at its fascial layer to prevent a hernia. The first entry technique, the incision size, and the closure of each port site must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Telescopes are described by two numbers that the listener should be able to picture. The first is the angle of view, which is the direction in which the lens faces, so a telescope at 0 degrees looks straight ahead and one at 30 degrees looks forward and to one side, letting the surgeon sweep the view around the cavity by rotating the shaft. The second is the diameter, where a 10 millimeter telescope admits more light and gives a brighter picture and a 5 millimeter telescope fits a smaller port at the cost of brightness. A telescope is a delicate optical instrument inside a metal tube, and a bend of only a few degrees cracks the rod lenses inside, leaving a dark crescent or a blurred edge that no cleaning will remove. It is never used as a retractor, never dropped into a tray, and never left where an instrument can fall across it, and checking the image before every case is the cheapest quality test in the room. The angle and the diameter chosen for each procedure, and the inspection of the image, must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The instruments of laparoscopy are recognized by function rather than shape, and each is a small hand at the end of a long shaft. Graspers hold tissue, dissecting forceps spread it, scissors cut it, hooks lift it, and needle holders drive a suture through it. A suction and irrigation probe clears blood and smoke, a clip applier places a metal or polymer clip across a vessel, and a specimen bag receives tissue that must be removed without spilling its contents. Electrosurgical probes, ultrasonic shears, and vessel-sealing devices have their own chapter, but their handles and shafts are made long for this route. The length of the shaft is a compromise, because a longer instrument reaches deeper but magnifies every tremor of the surgeon's hand and applies less force.

The shaft also hides a maintenance problem. Insulation covers the metal to protect the patient from current, and a pinhole in that insulation lets current escape at the point of contact and burn tissue nobody can see. Insulated instruments are tested with a dedicated insulation tester at a defined interval, and any instrument with a visible crack, a burned spot, or a loose connection is removed from service rather than repaired in the operating room. The moving parts of a reusable instrument need a lubricant that survives sterilization, and a stiff or gritty action warns that the instrument may fail during the case.

Staplers bring the closure technology of open surgery through a narrow port. A linear stapler lays two or three rows of staples and then cuts between them, which is how a length of bowel or a large vessel is divided without bleeding. A circular stapler creates a ring of staples and removes the tissue inside the ring, which is how two hollow organs are joined end to end. The staples must form closed loops rather than partly open ones, and a malformed staple leaks, so the fired line is inspected before the instrument is withdrawn. Each stapler accepts a reload of a specific length and staple height, and matching the reload to the thickness of the tissue is the difference between a secure closure and a leak. The staple height, the tissue thickness, and the number of firings allowed must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Taking tissue out through a small hole is its own problem. A specimen too large to pass through a port is placed in a retrieval bag, a thin plastic sack with a drawstring that is opened inside the abdomen, filled, closed, and pulled out through the port or a slightly enlarged incision. If the specimen is not bagged and it is malignant or infected, its cells or bacteria can seed the port site and the wound. A morcellator solves the size problem by cutting tissue into strips drawn out through the shaft, and it carries the specific hazard of spreading an unrecognized tumor through the abdomen.

Robot-assisted surgery is master-slave telemanipulation rather than an independent machine. The surgeon sits at a console, looks into a stereo viewer that shows two slightly different images for depth, and moves two master handles. A computer translates those movements into the motion of instrument arms docked to the patient, and it can scale a large hand movement into a small instrument movement, filter out tremor, and articulate an instrument wrist far beyond what a straight shaft allows. The system decides nothing and does not restore the sense of touch, so the surgeon works from vision alone. The instrument life, the arm alignment, and the console settings must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The cost of ownership is the least visible part of robotics and the hardest to justify, and it must be understood before a hospital buys one. The console and the arms are expensive, the instruments are consumables with a limited number of uses, and the training and the service contract continue to cost after the purchase. The failure modes are characteristic, and a communication fault between the console and the arms freezes the slave, so the immediate response is to remove the instruments from the patient while a second team stands ready to convert to open surgery. Docking is slow, the patient must be positioned rigidly, and a patient who moves can be injured against an arm that cannot feel them. The instrument use limits, the fault alarms, the emergency undocking procedure, and the conversion plan must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Cleaning is a physical process that must happen before sterilization, and its purpose is to remove the organic material that shelters microbes and blocks sterilants. Blood, fat, and protein dry into a film that water alone will not lift, so instruments are kept moist after use, pre-cleaned where they are used, and transported in a closed container to the reprocessing area. There the instrument is disassembled as far as it will go, because a box lock, a ratchet, or a screw joint hides soil that no steam will reach. Long shafts and hollow channels are brushed with a brush of the correct diameter, flushed with a detergent solution, and cleaned in an ultrasonic bath, where bubbles that form and collapse on the surface lift debris from the crevices. The detergent concentration, the water temperature, the ultrasonic cycle, and the rinse quality must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Telescopes and flexible instruments cannot be judged by the eye alone, and the leak test is the check that protects them. A leaking flexible endoscope takes fluid into its optical bundle and its electronics, and the damage appears as a foggy image or a dead section weeks later. The tester is a small hand pump that slightly pressurizes the sealed shaft, and the instrument is then immersed and watched for bubbles, which mark a hole in the outer sheath that must be repaired before the next use. Rigid telescopes are inspected by holding them to a light and looking along the barrel for a cracked lens, a loose lens, or moisture. Any loss of brightness, a color cast, a dark crescent, or a hazy spot means the telescope goes for repair.

Sterilization destroys the microorganisms that remain after cleaning, and the method must be matched to what the device can survive. Rigid stainless steel instruments and most rigid telescopes tolerate steam under pressure, with a typical cycle at 134 degrees Celsius for a few minutes at the autoclave's stated pressure, and that cycle must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, because exceeding the temperature or the time damages seals, cements, and lens mounts. Heat-sensitive instruments, camera heads, light cables, and flexible endoscopes require a low-temperature process such as hydrogen peroxide gas plasma, ethylene oxide, or a liquid peracetic acid system. A sterilized set goes into a container or a peel pouch with a chemical indicator and a process label, and the pouch is inspected for holes, dampness, and a broken seal before it is opened. The choice of sterilant, the cycle parameters, the aeration time, and the storage limit must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Everything that enters the abdomen is either sterile or it is a source of infection, and the consumables of laparoscopy are counted before the case and again after it. Trocars, clip appliers, stapler reloads, specimen bags, and suture on long needles all leave the sterile field and must be accounted for, and the count is reconciled before the patient leaves the room. Reusable instruments are stored dry and protected from one another in a way that lets air circulate, because instruments dropped together into a drawer are how a telescope is cracked. Single-use items are checked for the expiry date, the integrity of the package, and the correct size before the case, and a package that is damp or torn is not used.

The tower rewards a short scheduled inspection more than it rewards repair. The light cable is examined for broken fibers, which appear as dark spots that reduce illumination and as heat that concentrates at the damaged end. The camera cable is checked for kinks at the connector, the insufflator is checked against a calibrated pressure gauge, and the gas supply is checked for the correct cylinder and a full contents gauge before the list begins. The electrical safety of the trolley, including its protective earth and its insulation, is measured at a stated interval, and the battery of any cordless component is tested for capacity rather than voltage alone. The image chain is checked with a test target, so that a dimming lamp or a drifting white balance is found before it is discovered midway through a case. The inspection interval, the calibration tolerance, and the electrical safety limits must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

What the operator must check before the first case is a short list that prevents most disasters. The tower is powered, the lamp hours are within the maker's limit, the white balance is performed, and the image is confirmed sharp on the monitor. The insufflator shows the expected cylinder pressure, the tubing is connected the right way around, and the pressure and flow limits match the plan for this patient. The telescopes are inspected, the instruments are laid out in the order they will be needed, the ports are checked for intact seals, and the stapler reloads and clip appliers are matched to the planned steps. The diathermy return pad is on the patient, the alarm limits of the anesthetic monitor are set, and the team has spoken the patient's name, the procedure, and the side aloud.

In a mobile clinic or a district hospital without a laparoscopy tower, the same clinical problem is met with a fraction of the equipment. The light source becomes a battery-powered LED headlamp or a small battery light box attached to the telescope, and the monitor becomes a laptop or a small portable screen. The gas supply may not exist, so the surgeon works with a gasless technique or with a mechanical lifting device that raises the abdominal wall with a fan-shaped retractor held on an external frame. The ports and instruments are reusable and reprocessed on site, which shifts the burden from a supply chain to a cleaning and sterilization discipline. Each substitution changes what the operation can safely do, and the honest limit must be understood before the first incision.

Gasless laparoscopy is not a lesser version of the same operation but a different physical situation. Lifting the wall mechanically creates a smaller and more irregular space, and the surgeon must use counter-traction against the lifting device rather than relying on the even pressure of gas. The advantages are real where carbon dioxide is scarce, where anesthesia is light, or where the patient's heart and lungs cannot tolerate a pneumoperitoneum, because there is no gas to absorb and no pressure on the veins. Single-port surgery, in which the telescope and instruments pass through one opening, is possible with ordinary instruments, but the instruments cross one another, the camera and the hands compete for the same space, and triangulation is reduced. Both approaches demand more time and more patience, and both should be converted to an open operation without hesitation when the view is poor.

The field kit that works is small and unglamorous. It carries a rigid telescope with both a straight and an angled lens if both can be afforded, a battery light source with spare cells, a portable screen, 2 or 3 reusable ports, a basic set of graspers and scissors, a suction and irrigation syringe, a bipolar forceps unit, and a supply of suture and ligatures. Sterilization on site usually means steam under pressure if an autoclave is available, and if it is not, the honest option is to refer the patient rather than operate with instruments that cannot be made safe. The lack of a reliable sterilizer, the lack of smoke evacuation, and the lack of a second trained operator are the three limits that most often make a planned laparoscopic procedure unsafe. The available sterilization method, the instrument set, and the criteria for referral must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The last requirement of this chapter is not a device at all but a system of competence. Minimally invasive surgery is harder to learn than open surgery because the eye and the hand are separated, so the surgeon practices the new coordination on a simulator or a box trainer before testing it on a patient. The team rehearses the failures, including a lost image, a bleeding vessel that cannot be seen, a gas embolism, a stapler that misfires, and the decision to convert to open, and every member knows their role in each. Cases are reviewed for complications, conversion, port-site infection, and instrument failure, and the findings are fed back into training rather than filed away. The credentialing requirements, the simulator practice hours, and the complication review interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

To summarize, minimally invasive surgery replaces a long incision with a small port, and every device in the chapter exists to serve that single idea. A telescope relays an image by total internal reflection to a camera that turns light into an electrical signal, and a monitor returns that signal to the surgeon's eye. A light source illuminates the cavity, an insufflator holds it open with carbon dioxide at a pressure that must be kept as low as the operation allows, and the physiology of the pneumoperitoneum dictates much of what the anesthetist must do. Trocars, long-shafted instruments, staplers, and clip appliers do the work of the hand, while robotic arms add articulation and steadiness at a cost of ownership that must be planned for years in advance. Cleaning, leak testing, sterilization, insulation testing, and instrument counting are not housekeeping but the conditions that make the operation safe. Where no tower exists, a battery light, a portable screen, reusable ports, and a gasless lift can still serve, provided the team accepts the narrower view and refers early when the honest limits are reached.
