# Chapter 46 — Pediatrics

Pediatrics takes every device in this book and asks one question, which is whether it is the right size for this body. A child is not a miniature adult made by uniform shrinking. The head of a newborn is large and heavy, so the neck flexes easily, and the airway is narrow everywhere and shortest at the larynx. The chest wall is soft, so it draws inward rather than expanding, and the work of breathing rises with every extra resistance. A child has a large surface for its mass, so heat escapes quickly and the small body cools in minutes. Size is therefore a physiological variable as powerful as any drug.

The first principle behind the pediatric airway set is that airflow resists narrowing with extraordinary force. Resistance rises steeply as the radius falls, in proportion to the fourth power, so halving the diameter multiplies resistance about 16 times. A newborn trachea is about the width of a drinking straw, and a swelling of 1 millimeter can halve the opening and multiply the work of every breath. The child then retracts the ribs, tires quickly, and stops feeding. This is why the operator chooses the largest device the child tolerates, not the smallest that fits, and why a small leak around a tube is often accepted as a margin against swelling. Every size, cuff pressure, and leak value named here must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The pediatric airway set is one family of devices graded by size. Face masks are chosen by rim diameter so the rim seals over the nose and chin without covering the eyes, and an adult mask will not seal on an infant face. Self-inflating bags come in about 250 milliliters, 500 milliliters, and 1600 milliliters, volumes that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Laryngoscope blades are straight or curved, and the straight blade is preferred in infants because it lifts the floppy epiglottis directly. Supraglottic devices sit over the laryngeal opening and help when the cords cannot be seen. Endotracheal tubes run from about 2.5 millimeters internal diameter in a premature infant to about 7 millimeters in an adolescent, a range that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The bedside formula for an uncuffed tube equals the age in years divided by 4 plus 4, with a cuffed tube one half size smaller, and the result must also be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Once a tube is chosen, the problems move to the patient. A cuff seals the trachea with a small balloon that protects the lungs from stomach contents, but the balloon presses on the mucosal blood supply. A common target is about 20 to 25 centimeters of water, checked with a manometer, a range that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A small child often needs no cuff, because the ring of cartilage below the cords forms a natural seal. The tube is fixed at a depth commonly about 3 times its internal diameter in millimeters, a rule that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The characteristic alarms are the high pressure and low exhaled volume alarms, and the common errors are to advance the tube into one bronchus, to fix it too loosely, and to let the tubing kink under the chin.

Vascular access in a child is a contest between a small target and a device that must not move inside it. Gauges rise as diameter falls, so a 24 gauge cannula is thinner than a 22 gauge cannula, and the smallest sizes used in newborns are 24 gauge and 26 gauge, a range that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The veins of an infant are short, superficial, and easily blown, and the tourniquet is a finger or a soft band. The transilluminator makes these veins visible because hemoglobin absorbs red and infrared light far more strongly than fat and skin do, so a bright cold light pressed to the skin passes through the tissue. The blood-filled vein then appears as a dark line on a glowing background, and a hot lamp left on the skin burns a newborn in seconds. The common errors are to probe after the vein is punctured, to release the tourniquet too late, and to leave a cannula that is no longer needed.

When veins cannot be found, the marrow cavity becomes the route. The medullary cavity of a long bone is a sponge of marrow and vessels that does not collapse when the circulation fails, so it behaves as a vein that never closes. Fluid delivered there reaches the central circulation at a speed comparable to a peripheral vein. The needle is short and stout, about 15 to 18 gauge and 15 to 25 millimeters long, figures that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. It enters the flat upper surface of the tibia below the knee, the lower femur, or the upper arm bone in older children. The hazard is a needle through the far wall, so fluid runs into the soft tissue and produces a compartment syndrome, which is why placement is confirmed by aspirating marrow, by free flow, and by watching the limb for swelling.

Fluids given to a child are measured rather than dripped, because the margin between enough and too much is narrow. The blood volume of an infant is only about 80 to 90 milliliters per kilogram, a figure that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A common starting bolus for shock is 10 to 20 milliliters per kilogram, and a common maintenance rule gives 4 milliliters per kilogram each hour for the first 10 kilograms, 2 for the next 10, and 1 thereafter, all of which must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The device that makes these volumes safe is the graduated burette, a chamber of 100 to 150 milliliters that holds only the intended amount, a range that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. An infusion pump adds control and raises alarms for occlusion, air, and end of infusion. The common errors are to hang a full bag on a gravity line, to leave a roller clamp open, and to override an occlusion alarm without looking at the limb.

The length-based resuscitation tape turns one measurement into a whole plan. In children older than a few months, body length predicts weight far better than age does, so a tape marked in length intervals can assign the child to a weight zone with useful accuracy. The tape is laid from the crown of the head to the heel, and each zone is printed in a color that repeats on the drawers of a resuscitation trolley. The correct tube, blade, bag, cannula, drug dose, and defibrillation energy are then found together. Defibrillation commonly begins at about 2 joules per kilogram, a value that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The common errors are to ignore the zone when it disagrees with a measured weight and to keep only one tape in the department.

Monitoring a child uses the same measurements as monitoring an adult, delivered through devices sized to a smaller body. The bladder of a blood pressure cuff should be about 40 per cent of the arm circumference and cover about 80 per cent of it, so a cuff that is too narrow reads falsely high and one that is too wide reads falsely low, and both figures must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The oxygen saturation probe passes light of two wavelengths through a translucent part of the body and compares how much of each is absorbed, because oxygen-rich and oxygen-poor blood differ in that absorption. A child needs a small soft pad for the finger, the toe, or the edge of the hand, and the characteristic alarm is the low saturation alarm, commonly set below about 90 per cent, a threshold that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The common errors are a probe that is too tight, a probe on poorly perfused skin, and a reading accepted while the child moves. Cardiac electrodes are smaller and closer together than adult electrodes.

Temperature is the pediatric variable that kills quietly, and the physics of a small body explains why. Heat leaves by four routes at once, which are evaporation from skin and breath, conduction into a cold surface, convection into moving air, and radiation to cooler objects. A newborn loses heat by all four faster than an adult because surface area is large relative to the mass that produces heat. A newborn also cannot shiver effectively and has little insulating fat, so it burns brown fat, which consumes oxygen and glucose and fails quickly in a sick infant. Cold stress leads to a fall in temperature, a rise in oxygen consumption, a fall in blood glucose, and a metabolic acidosis that makes everything worse. The neutral thermal environment is the range in which the child holds a normal temperature with the least oxygen consumption, and for a small unclothed infant it commonly lies near 32 to 34 degrees Celsius of air with a skin temperature of about 36.5 degrees Celsius, values that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

An infant warmer and an incubator are two answers to the same problem. A radiant warmer hangs a heated element above an open cot and warms the skin by infrared radiation without touching it, which allows full access but increases water loss. An incubator encloses the child in a chamber of heated circulating air, often with a double wall and a humidifier, which reduces evaporation but delays access and harbors bacteria in a poorly cleaned humidifier. Both devices in automatic mode are controlled by a skin probe containing a thermistor, a resistor whose resistance falls as its temperature rises. The characteristic alarms are the over-temperature, probe failure, and low temperature alarms, and each threshold must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The most dangerous common error is a probe that has come loose and now measures the air, because the controller then drives the heater hotter while the alarm may stay silent.

Jaundice in a newborn is visible because bilirubin, the pigment released when red blood cells are broken down, accumulates in the skin and the whites of the eyes. Bilirubin absorbs blue and green light strongly, with a peak near 450 to 460 nanometers, a value that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A transcutaneous meter flashes light into the skin and compares absorption at a wavelength bilirubin absorbs with one it does not. The device measures skin and not blood, so it is a screening tool affected by skin maturity, by the site, by the darkness of the skin, and by whether phototherapy is running. The hazard is a reassuring reading while the blood level rises, so a value near a treatment threshold must be confirmed with a laboratory measurement of a blood sample. The common errors are to use the meter during phototherapy, to measure over a bruised area, and to treat the number as a diagnosis.

An infant scale and a length board look simple, and they are the devices whose error propagates into every drug dose. Most modern scales use a load cell, a metal bar that bends very slightly under a load, with strain gauges whose electrical resistance changes as the metal stretches. An infant scale commonly reads to the nearest 5 or 10 grams with a capacity of about 20 kilograms, figures that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A length board has a fixed headpiece, a sliding footpiece, and a scale in millimeters along its edge. Length is measured lying down until about 2 years of age and standing after that, and the two are not interchangeable because a standing child is shorter. The common errors are to weigh a child with a wet diaper without subtracting it, to record the wrong unit, and to plot a single point instead of the trend.

Nasal prong continuous positive airway pressure, or CPAP, keeps the small airway open by holding a positive pressure in it throughout the breath. The infant breathes through short prongs just inside the nostrils, and the expiratory limb is dipped into sterile water to a measured depth. The pressure needed to push a bubble out equals the depth of the tube below the surface, so 1 centimeter of depth gives about 1 centimeter of water pressure. A common setting is 4 to 6 centimeters of water with a flow of 6 to 8 liters per minute and oxygen adjusted by a blender, all of which must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The pressure holds the pharynx and small alveoli open and reduces the work of breathing, but it can also distend the stomach or rupture an alveolus and produce a pneumothorax. The common errors are a large mouth leak that destroys the pressure, prongs that are too large and press on the nasal septum, and an interface left too long in one position.

A nebulizer turns a liquid medicine into a mist that a child can inhale, and the common jet type uses a stream of compressed gas. The gas is forced through a narrow opening, and the drop in pressure as it accelerates draws the liquid up a tube and tears it into droplets, which strike baffles that knock the large droplets back. The useful droplet size for the lower airway is roughly 1 to 5 micrometers, and the flow is commonly 6 to 8 liters per minute, figures that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A child is treated with a small face mask rather than a mouthpiece, because a crying child deposits most of the drug in the nose and throat. A spacer, or holding chamber, is the simpler alternative for an inhaler, a plastic chamber that holds the puff as a cloud so the child can take several quiet breaths. The common errors are to let the mask sit away from the face, to forget to shake the inhaler, and to leave the cup unwashed.

Immobilization in a child means matching a rigid device to a soft and growing body. A cervical collar transfers the load of the head from the neck to the shoulders and chest, so its size is chosen by neck circumference and chin height. A collar that is too large lets the head fall forward, and one that is too small presses on the throat. Pediatric collars follow the shorter distance from chin to shoulder, and a hospital keeps a range from the smallest infant size upward, a range that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A pediatric backboard carries extra padding and a head support so that the large head does not force the neck into flexion, because a rigid collar or a tight strap can obstruct the airway or raise the pressure inside the skull. The common errors are to place a folded towel behind the shoulders without checking that the neck is neutral and to move a child with a suspected spinal injury without a coordinated log roll.

Cleaning and reprocessing in pediatrics is harder than in adult practice because the devices are smaller and their lumens narrower. Sterilization kills but does not remove, so a device that reaches the sterilizer with dried blood in a channel comes out sterile and still carrying the material that shields the next patient. Pediatric circuits, masks, prongs, blades, suction catheters, and cannulae are therefore taken apart as far as the maker permits, rinsed at the bedside, and cleaned with a soft brush of the correct diameter. A common steam cycle for wrapped pediatric instruments is 134 degrees Celsius for 4 minutes, a cycle that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and many plastic circuits and soft masks cannot survive it and need the low temperature method their maker specifies. The consumables a pediatric ward must never run short of are the small gauge cannulae, the small masks and blades, the correct tubes, the small suction catheters, the oxygen tubing and prongs, the electrodes, the oral rehydration solution, and the smallest gloves. The common errors are to reuse a single-use item because it is the only one left and to mix parts from different size sets.

Preventive maintenance in a pediatric department is mostly about instruments that sense, because a device that measures wrongly is more dangerous than one that fails to switch on. Temperature probes, load cells, pressure transducers, oxygen sensors, and pulse oximeter probes all drift with age and abuse, and each has a stated interval for calibration or replacement that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A rechargeable battery loses capacity gradually, so a transport monitor or a syringe pump that once ran for hours begins to fail during a transfer. Tubing cracks at the connector, seals harden, filters block with dust, and an incubator humidifier grows a biofilm that rinsing will not remove once it has formed. The most insidious failure is a silent alarm, because a failed speaker or a limit that has been switched off lets the device sit quietly while the child deteriorates. The operator must test every alarm at the start of every shift and record every fault, because a monitor that has failed once will fail again.

Before any child is attached to a device, the operator runs a check that is shorter than the adult version but stricter, and the first item is size. The intended tube, mask, blade, bag, cannula, collar, and blood pressure cuff are laid out and compared with the child's measured length and weight, and a length-based tape is opened beside the bed. The oxygen source and tubing are checked for flow and connection, the suction is tested, and the self-inflating bag is squeezed to confirm that it refills and its valve works. Every monitor is switched on and allowed to complete its self-test, its alarm limits are set to the child's age rather than left at adult defaults, and the probe and electrodes are checked for a clean signal. The weight and length are recorded and the drug doses are calculated before they are needed, and the environment is prepared with a warm surface and a hat. Every setting, alarm limit, pressure, temperature, and drug volume used on a child must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

In a mobile or frontier clinic the pediatric equipment list is rebuilt from the diseases that kill children. The hospital department is organized around diagnosis and surveillance, while the field clinic is organized around the few interventions that change the outcome, which are warmth, oxygen, oral rehydration, the first dose of an antibiotic, the treatment of malnutrition, and the recognition of a child who must be referred. The devices that survive are the simple ones, because a device with a battery, a sensor, and a circuit board cannot be repaired at a health post. The field version keeps the self-inflating bag with small masks, a manual suction device, a thermometer, a timer, and a scale, and it lets go of the servo-controlled incubator, the infusion pump, and the electronic monitor. What it gives up is precision and continuous surveillance, and what it demands instead is the trained hand and eye of a worker who measures, counts, and looks.

The mid-upper arm circumference tape is the single most useful piece of pediatric equipment in a field clinic. Its principle is that the circumference of the arm changes remarkably little between the ages of about 6 months and 5 years as long as the child is well nourished. Muscle and fat in the upper arm respond early to a shortage of food, so the tape measures the reserve, and the measurement is quick and repeatable in trained hands. The tape is marked in centimeters with colored bands, and cutoffs commonly used are 11.5 centimeters for severe acute malnutrition and 12.5 centimeters for moderate acute malnutrition, thresholds that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The measurement is taken on the left arm, midway between the tip of the shoulder and the tip of the elbow, and the tape is pulled snug without compressing the skin. The common errors are to read at the wrong point, to pull too tight, and to record the color band without the centimeter value, and a swollen arm from edema gives a falsely large reading.

A weighing scale in the field is usually a hanging spring scale, and its principle is that the extension of a spring is proportional to the force that stretches it. The child is placed in a sling suspended from the hook, and the pointer moves along a marked scale, and a scale of this kind commonly holds up to about 25 kilograms with graduations of 100 grams, values that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Its virtue is that it has no battery, and its weakness is that the spring can fatigue, the zero can drift, and the pointer can stick, so the worker must check the zero and hang a known mass from the hook. The hazards are a sling that tears, a struggling child, and a reading taken while the scale swings, and each produces a false weight that becomes a false drug dose. The common errors are to forget to subtract the sling and clothing, to read at an angle, and to record a single weight without plotting it.

Ready-to-use therapeutic food is the equipment of severe malnutrition, and it is a manufactured device with a specification, a package, a storage condition, and a failure mode. It is a dense paste of groundnut, milk powder, sugar, vegetable oil, and a vitamin and mineral premix sealed in a foil sachet. Its energy density of roughly 500 kilocalories per 100 grams means a small volume carries enough energy to rebuild a child who cannot eat a large meal, a figure that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A common sachet holds about 92 grams and is given in a scheduled number of sachets each day according to weight and protocol, an amount that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The hazards are contamination when the paste is shared, spoilage when the sachet is stored in heat, and the dangerous use of the food at home for a child with infection, poor appetite, or edema, who needs inpatient care. The oral rehydration solution is the companion equipment of diarrhea, a measured mixture of salts and sugar that uses sodium and glucose transport in the gut to pull water back into the body, and the common errors are to mix it too strong, to mix it too weak, and to use a dirty container.

The field version of the pediatric airway is a self-inflating bag in the smallest size with a set of masks, a manual suction bulb or foot-operated pump, and a straight blade if the worker is trained. There is no ventilator, so every breath is delivered by hand and the worker watches the chest rise and counts the rate. There is no infusion pump, so fluid is given by gravity through a chamber that counts drops, and the worker must know how many drops make a milliliter for that set. There is no electronic monitor, so the respiratory rate is counted for a full minute, the work of breathing is judged by rib recession and grunting, and the temperature is taken with whatever thermometer the clinic has. Warmth comes from the oldest device in pediatrics, the skin of the mother, where the naked child is placed upright between the breasts, covered, and held day and night, which is called kangaroo care. The field kit gives up servo control, continuous measurement, and the alarm, and it demands a trained hand, a second helper, a schedule, and the honesty to refer a child who is not improving.

The hazards of pediatric equipment in the field are mostly the hazards of improvisation and of the environment. An improvised spinal board is a plank or a door with cloth padding, and it works only if the head and neck are held in line and the child is strapped without pressure on the chest. Small instruments are usually sterilized by boiling in clean water for the time stated in the local protocol, which kills most organisms but does not destroy spores and does not remove protein. The failure modes of the field kit are predictable, because a bag valve sticks and does not refill, a mask rim hardens, a thermometer reads low as its battery fades, a scale zero drifts, and therapeutic food stored in the sun turns its oil. The operator must check each of these before the clinic opens and must know the referral criteria by heart, because the most important equipment in a frontier clinic is the vehicle and the decision to use it.

To summarize, pediatrics is the discipline in which size becomes a clinical decision, and almost every device in this chapter matches a machine to a body that is small, growing, and changing in proportion. The airway set is graded by diameter because resistance rises so steeply as a tube narrows, the tubes and cuffs are checked by measured values, and the length-based tape converts one measurement into a complete plan of sizes and doses. Vascular access uses the smallest cannulae and, when the veins fail, the marrow cavity, and every fluid is measured in milliliters per kilogram against a narrow margin. Blood pressure cuffs, saturation probes, and electrodes all carry a size that changes the reading, while the bilirubin meter and the transilluminator turn light into information about skin and veins. Warmth is a device in its own right, delivered by a warmer, an incubator, or the skin of a mother, and the scales and length boards that look trivial are the source of every dose. Whatever the setting, the operator must understand the physical principle, choose every item by size against the measured child, test the alarms before relying on them, and confirm every setting, pressure, temperature, cycle, and threshold against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.
