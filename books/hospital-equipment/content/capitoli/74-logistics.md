# Chapter 74 — Logistics

Logistics is the movement of things, and inside a hospital it is the movement of everything that is not the patient. The principle is the plainest in this book, because matter is conserved and nothing appears at the bedside by itself. A sterile dressing, a bag of fluid, and a cylinder of oxygen each begin outside the building and must be carried, counted, stored, found, and placed in a clinician's hand at the moment of need. A hospital is therefore a machine with 2 outputs, the care it gives and the waste it makes, and the first is limited at every moment by a supply chain that is almost invisible. An empty store room does not announce itself, and the consequence is not inconvenience but delay, a cancelled operation, or a death later called unavoidable, because a missing item is a clinical event. Every storage temperature, shelf life, and alarm threshold named here must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Central stores is the room where an institution holds its reserve, and its purpose is to break the link between the arrival of goods and the use of goods. Deliveries arrive in bulk on a schedule the hospital does not control, while wards consume on a schedule the patient controls, and the store is the buffer that lets 2 such rhythms coexist. The room is arranged in zones: a receiving area where vehicles unload, a quarantine area where new stock waits for checking, bulk racking, a picking area, a cold room, and a locked cage for controlled substances. Shelves are loaded so the oldest stock sits nearest the door, because stock is consumed by date and not by convenience. The storage conditions, the racking load rating, the quarantine period, and the rotation rule must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Distribution is the daily act of moving stock from the store to the place where it is used, and it runs on a document called a requisition. A requisition carries an item, a quantity, a destination, and an author, and the store answers by picking, packing, and sending the items on a fixed round. The alternative is the exchange system, in which a department is not asked what it wants but is simply topped up to a fixed quantity, and the number that drives it is the par level. A par level is a target built from how much the department uses in a day, how many days pass between deliveries, and how much extra is needed to survive a delay. Set too low, the ward runs out between rounds, and set too high the stock expires on the shelf. The par levels, the delivery rounds, and the review interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Consumables decide whether the equipment in every other chapter can be used at all, and they obey rules of their own. Each item carries a batch or lot number, an expiry date, and a storage instruction, and those 3 facts are the whole of its identity on the shelf. A sterile item is sterile only while its package is intact, so a torn wrapper, a damp patch, or a broken seal turns a sterile device into a contaminated one with no change a hurried hand would notice. Some items must be kept cold, some must never be frozen, and some must be protected from light, which is why the cold chain is a logistics problem before it is a clinical one. The storage temperature, the humidity limit, the expiry policy, and the package inspection routine must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The pneumatic tube system is the most visible piece of hospital logistics, and its principle is the one used by a bicycle pump. A blower moves air into a network of pipes and creates a difference in pressure between 2 ends, and a carrier with a soft seal becomes a piston, driven from the station that sends to the station that receives. Diverters switch the carrier from branch to branch, so 1 blower room serves many destinations, and the station is a cabinet with a door, a keypad, and a port. The carrier is a plastic cylinder with a lid and a gasket, and its contents must be light, sealed, and unable to shift, because anything loose becomes a projectile when the carrier decelerates. Some items may never travel this way, and the list is absolute: blood for transfusion, because acceleration damages red cells; hazardous and cytotoxic medicines; tissue; anything that can leak; batteries; and any object that can jam the pipe. The system pressure, the carrier speed, the station list, and the prohibited item policy must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The characteristic alarm of the tube system is a carrier that has stopped, and it appears as a message at the station and the control panel while the pipe becomes a blocked artery. A jam is usually caused by an overloaded carrier, a lid not fully seated, a gasket worn flat, hardened spilled liquid, or a piece of equipment that should never have been sent. The system also fails quietly, because a station door whose seal has hardened leaks air, the sending pressure falls, and the carrier arrives too late to be useful. The safety hazards are a hand caught in a closing door, a spill of body fluid inside a carrier, and the infection that travels with a carrier that has never been cleaned. The commonest operator error is the unsealed or overfilled carrier, and the second is sending a prohibited item because the sender never learned the list. What the operator checks before each shift is the door seal, the gaskets, the cleanliness of the carriers, and a test send to the furthest station, and the maintenance interval and the pressure limits must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Automated guided vehicles are self-propelled carts that carry loads along a fixed route without a driver, and their principle is navigation by a signal the vehicle can follow. The earliest followed a wire buried in the floor, and the wire carried an alternating current that generated a magnetic field the vehicle sensed with a coil, so the path survived only as long as the floor did. Modern versions read floor markers, count wheel rotations, and measure distance to walls with a rotating laser, which lets the path be changed in software rather than by a builder. The vehicle opens doors, calls a lift, docks at a station, and returns to a charger when its battery falls below a set level, and its blind spots make it dangerous among people, so it carries bumpers and proximity sensors. The characteristic alarm is a stopped vehicle with a flashing light in a corridor, which usually means a damaged marker or a doorway that no longer matches the map. The route plan, the speed limit, the sensor test interval, and the battery schedule must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Vertical transport is the part of logistics no corridor plan can avoid, because a hospital stacks its departments and its heavy loads must rise and fall. An elevator is a car suspended on cables and balanced by a counterweight of roughly its own mass, so the motor overcomes only the difference between the load and that counterweight, which is why a modern lift moves smoothly and why its brakes are the part that matters when the power fails. A hospital needs more than 1 lift because traffic separates by kind: patients on beds use a bed lift large enough for the bed and its attendants, clean supplies use a clean route, soiled linen and waste use a dirty route, and staff and visitors use the public cars. A power failure can trap a patient between floors, so the lift depends on emergency power and on an entrapment alarm that is tested rather than assumed. The emergency power arrangement, the entrapment alarm test, and the lift inspection interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The rule that clean and dirty traffic must not cross follows directly from how contamination moves. Microorganisms travel on wheels, on hands, on cart surfaces, and through the air, so every object leaving a dirty area is a carrier until it has been cleaned, and every object entering a clean area must arrive with no history attached. The solution is one-way flow, in which clean supplies move from the store toward the patient while soiled material moves from the patient toward disposal or reprocessing, so the 2 streams advance in opposite directions and meet only at the bedside. In practice this means separate doors, separate lifts, separate carts, and separate holding rooms, especially around the operating theatre and the sterilization department. Linen shows the rule clearly, because soiled linen leaves the ward in a closed bag on a covered cart while clean linen arrives wrapped, and waste separates by category, since ordinary waste, infectious waste, and sharps each have their own container and route. The color coding, the container specification, the route plan, and the collection interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A cart is a store that has learned to move, and its physics is the wheel and the axle. A wheel converts sliding friction into rolling friction, so a load a person cannot drag becomes one that can be pushed, and the effort required depends on wheel diameter, floor hardness, load weight, and the condition of the bearings. Casters are small wheels on pivots, and their arrangement decides whether a cart turns in a corridor, climbs a threshold, or stops safely on a slope, which is why the brake and the swivel fail first. Hospitals use carts of named kinds: the supply cart, the medication cart, the emergency cart that must be sealed and checked, the linen cart, the waste cart, and the surgical case cart for instruments and drapes. Each has a load limit and a cleaning requirement, and an overloaded cart damages its wheels, its bearings, and the back of the person pushing it. The load limit, the caster specification, the brake test, and the cleaning interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The exchange system is where the arithmetic of logistics becomes visible, and the operator is the person who must check it. A par level is normally built from 3 quantities added together: the amount consumed in a normal day, the amount consumed during the days before the next delivery, and a safety quantity covering a delay or a lost order. When the par is too low the ward borrows from another ward, and borrowing hides the shortage by moving it, so the next round is planned from false numbers. When the par is too high the ward hoards, and the hoard occupies cupboard space and expires in place. The safety hazard here is the personal stash kept in a drawer so that no one is ever caught short, because a private reserve makes the official record understate use and keeps the par artificially low. The consumption records, the lead times, the review interval, and the emergency quantity must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The surgical case cart deserves its own description because it is where logistics and surgery meet, and it is assembled against a list rather than from memory. The list comes from the procedure booked and the surgeon's preference card, the record of the instruments and consumables this operation has been found to need. A technician picks each item, checks it against the list, and loads the cart in a fixed order so the contents can be counted without unloading them, then covers it and moves it along the clean route. The instruments inside have been reprocessed by the central sterile services department, called CSSD, and returned in a sealed container, so the assembled cart is a sterile store whose limited life begins when it is opened. The safety hazard is a missing instrument, which is worse than a missing dressing because the gap is discovered with the patient already anesthetized and the cavity already open, so the count against the pick list is the entire protection. The pick list, the count procedure, the covering rule, and the shelf life of the assembled cart must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A missing item is not a shortage but a clinical event, and the moment it happens is worth describing. The clinician reaches for a device that is not there, the search expands to the next cupboard, the plan changes to a less appropriate alternative, and the delay is measured in minutes the patient may not have. In an operating theatre the same sequence ends in a cancelled case, because the sterile field cannot be opened twice and the team cannot improvise an instrument that was never delivered. The characteristic alarm of this failure is silence, since no machine sounds when a shelf is empty, which is why an empty shelf must be found by a routine count rather than by a complaint. The commonest operator error is the deferred order, in which a person notices the low stock, intends to report it after finishing a task, and the task leads straight into another. The count interval, the stock record, the minimum quantity, and the shortage escalation route must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Receiving is the gate of the institution, and the moment of delivery is the best chance to catch a problem. A delivery arrives with a packing slip that lists what the supplier believes was sent, against a purchase order that lists what was requested, and the receiver compares the 2 with the boxes before signing anything. The inspection covers the outside first: the count, the packaging, the temperature indicator on a cold item, the shock indicator on a fragile machine, the expiry dates, and the manuals the contract promised. Capital equipment is then given an asset number and entered in the equipment register, while consumables are entered in the store ledger and held in quarantine until accepted, because a box opened and put straight on the shelf has escaped every check. The safety hazard is the item accepted in good faith and found later to be damaged, expired, counterfeit, or incompatible with the power or gas of the building, and the paperwork is what makes that later discovery traceable. The commonest operator error is signing for a delivery without counting it, and the acceptance criteria and the required documentation must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The equipment of logistics needs maintenance like any other equipment, and because it is unglamorous it is the maintenance most often postponed. A tube system needs its blower filters changed, its gaskets and seals replaced, its diverters rebuilt, and its pipe joints checked for leakage, and a system never serviced sends carriers late long before it stops at all. Carts need casters cleared of hair and thread, bearings checked, brakes tested, and surfaces disinfected between loads, since a cart wheel is a rolling collection of everything the cart has crossed. Refrigerators and freezers need door gaskets checked, condensers cleaned, internal temperature verified against a separate thermometer, and alarms tested, because a cold room that fails quietly destroys stock that still looks perfect. Vehicles, lifts, shelving, and the heating, ventilation and air conditioning system that serves the sterile store each carry their own inspection interval, and the person who uses an item is the first line of its maintenance. The preventive maintenance schedule, the temperature verification method, the load rating, and the alarm test interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Now the chapter leaves the building and travels with the load, and the field version of this whole system is 1 pickup truck, 1 cold box, and 1 driver. Matter is still conserved and a box still has to travel from a warehouse to a patient, but the distance grows, the road becomes a variable, and the time between ordering and receiving stretches from hours to weeks. The truck is the mobile central store, and everything the hospital keeps in a room must here survive heat, dust, vibration, rain, and a road that is sometimes not a road. What this variant gives up is the entire apparatus of the institution: no pneumatic tube, no automated vehicle, no clean lift, no cold room, no backup power for a refrigerator, and no second delivery if the first is lost. What it demands instead is fuel, a spare tire, a driver who can repair what he drives, and a decision made in advance about what to leave behind when the load is too heavy. The load limit, the strapping method, the fuel reserve, and the vehicle inspection interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The cold box is the field substitute for the whole cold chain, and its physics is insulation and thermal mass. A well insulated box slows the flow of heat from the hot air outside to the cold contents inside, and a phase change pack holds a steady temperature while it melts, so the box keeps its load within a temperature band for a stated number of hours rather than making it cold. The pack must be conditioned before use, because a pack taken straight from a freezer is colder than the band and can freeze a vaccine that must never freeze, while a pack that has already melted is a warm weight with no reserve left. The load should be packed so the packs surround it, and the lid should be latched between openings, because every opening exchanges cold air for outside air. A temperature indicator or a recorder travels with the load so the history of the journey can be read at the far end. The temperature band, the conditioning time, the pack arrangement, and the maximum transit time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The driver in this system is not a courier but a member of the clinical team, and the argument of this chapter depends on that claim. The driver knows the roads, the river crossings, and the villages, so the driver is the only person who can give a realistic arrival time, and that time is a clinical fact because it decides whether a specimen is still valid. The driver carries the requisition, the stock card, and the return load, which may be a specimen, a broken device for repair, an empty cylinder, or a patient, and the handover of that load matters as much as the handover of a drug. The characteristic alarm in this half of the chapter is a telephone call that does not connect, because the vehicle has left network coverage and the clinic waiting for a cold box cannot know whether it is 1 hour or 1 day away. The commonest operator error is treating the trip as a transport task rather than a clinical one, so the cold box rides in the open bed under the sun and the delay is never reported. The route plan, the communication schedule, the custody record, and the cold chain log must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

At the frontier clinic the receiving dock is a doorway, the store is a cupboard, and the ledger is a stock card written by hand. The principles still hold, and the small scale sharpens each of them: the count must be done at the handover, the package must be inspected for tears, damp, and expiry, and the temperature indicator on a cold item must be read before the box is accepted. Storage space is the binding constraint, so the clinic cannot hold a large buffer and must hold a small quantity chosen for the diseases it actually treats, which makes the par level an act of clinical judgment. A solar powered refrigerator keeps the cold chain without fuel, but it depends on a battery, a controller, and a panel that must be kept clean, and its failure is silent until the temperature is checked. The storage temperature, the refrigerator alarm setting, the stock review interval, and the lock and key procedure must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The characteristic failure of field logistics is a cold chain that breaks without anyone noticing, and its anatomy is worth following. The vehicle breaks down, or the road floods, or the fuel runs out, and the box sits for 6 extra hours in air that is 38 degrees Celsius or hotter. The packs melt, the internal temperature climbs, and the load arrives looking exactly as it did when it left, which is why the indicator or the recorder is not optional equipment. The decision that follows is the hardest part of the chapter, because the vaccine, the insulin, or the blood product may still be usable or may be a danger, and the decision must come from the recorded temperature, the product instruction, and the local protocol rather than from hope. The commonest operator error is opening the box repeatedly along the route to check on it, since every opening shortens the life of the load, and the second is adding warm packs at the last stop. The temperature trace, the excursion rule, the discard procedure, and the reporting route must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The field variant gives up the machine and demands the human body, and the substitutes are older than the hospital. A 2 wheeled hand cart, a wheelbarrow, a bicycle with a trailer, a motorcycle with a box, a boat, a pack animal, and a person carrying a shoulder bag are all legitimate transport where no vehicle can pass, and each imposes its own limit on weight and fragility. A long lead time changes the par level completely, because a clinic resupplied every 12 weeks must hold enough for 12 weeks plus the delay, and it must also decide in advance which items it will share with a neighboring clinic when one of them runs short. The safety hazards are the ones that run through this chapter, which are the sharps in the wrong bag, the cold box in the sun, and the cart pushed past its load limit. The load limit, the packaging rule, the sharing agreement, and the review interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The return direction of field logistics carries specimens, and a specimen is a load with a clock attached. A sample of blood, sputum, or water leaves the clinic in a container that must not leak and must not become a hazard to the person carrying it, so it travels inside a primary container, a secondary container with absorbent material, and a rigid outer box. The requisition with it must identify the patient, the test, and the time of collection, because a specimen that loses its identity must be repeated, and the characteristic failure is a box that arrives with a broken tube and a soaked label. The field system also needs its own maintenance, and the toolkit is small and specific: a spare wheel, a jack, a pump, wire, fuses, hand tools, tape, and a printed manual in the local language, because a repair that depends on a telephone call waits for coverage. The packaging specification, the temperature range, the maximum transit time, and the spare parts list must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The operator's part in logistics is smaller than in most chapters of this book, but it is not zero, and it can be stated as a short round. At the start of a shift the responsible person checks the par level on the shelf against the stock card, checks the expiry dates of the items nearest the front, checks that the emergency cart is sealed and that its seal number matches the record, and checks that the sharps container is below its fill line. During the shift every issue is written on the card at the moment it is made, because a movement remembered later is a movement lost. The person reports the fault before it becomes a failure, which in logistics means reporting the empty shelf, the damaged cart, the leaking tube carrier, and the warm cold box while there is still time to correct them. The par level, the expiry check, the seal number, the fill line, and the temperature band must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

To summarize, logistics is the movement of things, and it is a clinical system rather than a housekeeping one. The hospital holds a central store because arrivals and consumption follow different rhythms, and it distributes stock by requisition or by topping each department up to a par level that must be calculated rather than guessed. Pneumatic tubes move a sealed carrier on air pressure, automated vehicles follow a path on the floor, elevators separate clean from dirty traffic, and carts carry stores on wheels, while clean and dirty routes never cross. Receiving and inspection are the gate, the stock card is the memory, and maintenance keeps the unglamorous machinery of movement alive. In the field the whole apparatus collapses into a truck, a cold box, and a driver who belongs to the clinical team, and what the field variant gives up in machinery it demands back in fuel, spare parts, honest paper records, and judgment about a broken road. Every chapter of this book depends on this one, because a device with no consumable, no spare, and no delivery is a device that does not exist, and the patient who pays for that failure is never the person who ordered the stock.
