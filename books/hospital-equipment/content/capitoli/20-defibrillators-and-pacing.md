# Chapter 20 — Defibrillators and Pacing

The heart is an electrical organ before it is a pump, and defibrillation overwhelms a chaotic rhythm with one brief, organized wave of current. In normal conduction, depolarization sweeps through the muscle in order, each cell contracting after its neighbor, so the chambers empty and fill usefully. In ventricular fibrillation that order collapses into many small re-entrant circuits wandering at random, so the tissue quivers rather than squeezes. The only escape is to depolarize a critical mass of muscle at the same instant, which extinguishes every circuit at once and lets the heart's own pacemaker tissue restart. What the machine controls is not voltage but current density inside the chest, and the current that reaches the heart depends on the resistance of the path, called transthoracic impedance. That impedance is set by the chest wall, the lungs, the heart, the size and position of the electrodes, the pressure applied, and above all by how well skin and electrode conduct. A listener who understands that the dial controls stored energy while the heart receives current will see why the same setting succeeds in one patient and fails in the next.

The electrodes are part of the electrical circuit, and their size, position, and contact decide how much current the heart sees. A large electrode spreads the current over a wider area, lowering the impedance and reducing the risk of a skin burn, while a small one concentrates the current and raises the impedance. Firm pressure improves contact, which is why the paddles are pressed down hard, and a hairy or wet chest with poor adhesion behaves like a small electrode. One electrode below the right collarbone and the other at the left side below the armpit sends the current roughly along the long axis of the heart, while a front and back placement sends it from another direction. Both positions work, but the operator must never allow the 2 electrodes to touch, because contact shorts the current across the skin. The hazard of a misplaced or heavily gelled electrode is a burn or a shock that misses the heart, and the common error is to press the paddles together or let gel bridge the gap between them.

A defibrillator is essentially a capacitor, charged over several seconds from a battery or from the mains and then discharged across the chest in a few thousandths of a second. The energy stored is described in joules, and the energy that reaches the heart is only a fraction of it, because some is lost inside the machine and much depends on impedance. Nominal first shock energies for an adult commonly lie between 120 and 200 joules for a biphasic device and up to 360 joules for a monophasic one, and every one of those figures must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The shape of the discharge in time is the waveform, and the oldest form is monophasic, in which current flows one way and stops, usually requiring high energies. The modern form is biphasic, in which the current reverses partway through the pulse, lowering the threshold and allowing success with less energy and less injury. Many machines also measure the impedance and adjust the duration or the voltage to compensate, so a thick chest may receive a longer pulse than a thin one. The common misunderstanding is that a higher number on the dial always means a stronger shock, when a well shaped lower energy pulse may succeed where a poorly shaped higher one fails.

The manual defibrillator is the hospital-grade instrument, and it usually combines a monitor showing an electrocardiogram, a defibrillator, and often a pacing unit. The front carries the electrocardiogram trace, the energy controls, a charge control, and either 2 paddles or a pair of adhesive pads on cables. The paddles are pressed firmly onto the chest, and the operator holds clear while calling a warning and checking that nobody is touching the patient, the bed, or a wet surface. The stored energy is lethal if it crosses a person's heart, so every member of the team must be clear before the discharge control is pressed, and the operator must be able to see every pair of hands. A thin layer of conductive gel must lie between electrode and skin, because dry contact burns and concentrates the current, while excess gel can bridge the electrodes and divert the current across the chest. The characteristic alarm is the rising charge-ready tone, and its absence after charging is a machine fault that must be treated as a failure rather than as a slow patient. The common error is to charge before the team is ready, which wastes battery and creates a charged device in a crowded room, and the second is to discharge while a colleague still touches the patient.

Synchronized cardioversion times the discharge to the patient's own electrical cycle rather than delivering it at random. The machine reads the electrocardiogram, marks each tall R wave, and delays the discharge by a few thousandths of a second so that it falls outside the vulnerable part of the cycle, where a shock can provoke ventricular fibrillation. The synchronizer is used for rhythms that still produce a pulse, such as atrial fibrillation and unstable organized rapid rhythms, and never for ventricular fibrillation, where there is no R wave to sense. Sedation and analgesia are needed whenever the patient is conscious, and the drugs and their doses must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The starting energy for cardioversion is usually lower than the energy used for defibrillation, and that energy and any step up must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The common error is to leave the synchronizer switched on after a cardioversion, because the machine will then refuse to discharge during a later arrest while it waits for a marker that never comes. The alarm that matters is the message that no marker has been detected, and the operator must check the sync indicator before every shock rather than trust memory.

Children change both the physics and the settings, because a small chest has a lower impedance and less muscle to depolarize. Manual machines offer smaller pediatric paddles or pads, and some use an attenuator that reduces the delivered energy to a fraction of the selected value, so the attenuator must be fitted correctly. Automatic machines often ship with a pediatric attenuator, a key, or a switch, and the age or the weight at which it is used must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The nominal child energy is chosen by weight, and that weight-based dose and the maximum permitted energy must both be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Placement in a small chest may be front and back rather than front and side, so that the 2 electrodes do not touch and short the current across the skin. The hazard is contact between the pads, and the common error is to use adult pads on a small child without an attenuator, delivering far more energy than was intended.

The automated external defibrillator removes the decision from the operator's hands, using an algorithm that analyzes the electrical signal for a shockable rhythm. The operator attaches 2 adhesive pads, one below the right collarbone and one at the left side, switches the device on, and follows voice prompts ordering everyone to stand clear while the rhythm is analyzed. The algorithm looks for the coarse, irregular activity of ventricular fibrillation and the fast, wide complexes of pulseless ventricular tachycardia, and advises a shock only for those 2 patterns, leaving every other rhythm to be treated by cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR). Movement, compressions, and a loose electrode corrupt the analysis, which is why the device orders hands off the patient, and a team that ignores that order may cause a shockable rhythm to be misread as a normal one. After a shock the device prompts for a fixed period of compressions and then analyzes again, and that compression interval and the whole sequence must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The characteristic alarm is the spoken order to stand clear followed by the shock advisory, while a problem with the pads is announced as a message to check them. The common error is to interrupt compressions for too long while the pads are found, because every second without circulation lowers the chance that the next shock will succeed.

Every automated external defibrillator runs a self-test on a schedule, checking its circuitry, its battery, and its pads, and displays a readiness symbol. Some units test daily, some weekly, and some run a partial test daily and a fuller test at a longer interval, and the schedule and its meaning must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The battery is a sealed pack rated for years of standby, and the pads are sealed in foil with an expiry date, so readiness depends on both of them as much as on the electronics. A device with out of date pads may still show readiness on some models and will still fail to shock, which is why the dates must be read by a person rather than inferred from a light. Some units store a record of their self-tests for later interrogation, and that record is the only proof that the checks actually happened. The common failure in a well-run hospital is a battery taken for another device and never returned, and the common failure in a neglected cupboard is a readiness symbol that was assumed rather than seen.

The consumables of defibrillation are few, cheap, and fatal to omit, and the first is the pad or the gel that makes the electrical contact. Adhesive pads carry their own gel, are sealed in foil, and bear an expiry date, and an expired pad loses moisture, adheres poorly, and raises impedance so that less current reaches the heart. Bottled gel for paddles dries, thickens, and becomes contaminated once it is opened, and a bottle whose contents have separated must be discarded rather than stirred back into use. Monitoring electrodes are needed at the same time as the pads, because the machine must show a rhythm before and after the shock. A single-use razor belongs in the kit, because hair under a pad adds impedance and weakens adhesion, and gauze and scissors are equally ordinary and equally necessary. Thermal printer paper, a spare battery, a test load for the technician, a pediatric attenuator, and pacing cables complete the list, and the stock level and replacement interval of each must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The used pads and packaging are contaminated with the patient's blood and sweat and belong in the clinical waste stream, and the daily check must confirm the pad dates, the gel, the battery, and the readiness symbol.

In the hospital the defibrillator is one part of an organized system, and the system is what makes it usable within seconds. The device lives on a crash cart or in a wall bracket in every area where a patient may arrest, including the emergency department, the intensive care unit, the catheterization laboratory, and every general ward. The cart carries the pads, the gel, the electrodes, the razors, the suction, the airway equipment, and the drugs, and it is sealed with a numbered tag so that a missing item is visible before the arrest rather than during it. The defibrillator is kept connected to the mains so that its battery stays charged, and many hospitals put that socket on an emergency power circuit, an arrangement that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The team is organized as well, with defined roles for compressions, airway, drugs, and the defibrillator, and the person on the defibrillator calls the shocks aloud so that the others can time their work around them. The machine records the rhythm, the shocks, and the times, and that record is filed with the patient's notes and often into the electronic health record. The characteristic failure of the system is a cart whose seal was broken days ago and never replaced, so that the next arrest begins with a search for a pad.

The daily check of a hospital defibrillator is a short ritual that a nurse can perform, and its purpose is to find the failure before the arrest. The operator looks at the readiness indicator, switches the device on, verifies that a rhythm appears on the screen, checks the date and time, confirms the pads and the gel, and confirms that the battery is charged and correctly seated. The biomedical technician performs deeper tests on a schedule with a defibrillator analyzer that measures delivered energy, pulse duration, peak current, and the impedance of a simulated patient. The delivered energy must fall within the tolerance set by the maker, and that tolerance and the test interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The technician also verifies the synchronizer with a simulated electrocardiogram, tests the pacing output into a load, and checks the leakage current of the patient circuit. The battery fails most often, because it ages silently whether or not the machine is used, and a pack charged daily for years still holds less and less. The written record of these tests is the only proof that the device was verified, and an untested machine that looks clean is not a tested machine.

The maintenance failure discovered during a resuscitation is almost always a flat battery, a dried pad, a loose electrode, or a cable cracked inside its insulation. Each is invisible in a cupboard and obvious in an arrest, and each can be prevented by a check that takes less than a minute. A flat battery is the most common, because a device left on its charger in a warm cupboard may still lose capacity, and a spare pack never rotated may be as old as the machine. A dried pad comes next, because the foil was opened and imperfectly resealed, or the pad was stored in heat and the gel that should conduct became a dry film. A loose electrode is third, because the trace depends on skin contact, and a patient being moved, compressed, and shocked will shed electrodes. The cracked cable is the most treacherous, because it passes every test while it lies still and fails the moment it is pulled across a chest. The defense is the daily check, the documented functional test, and a spare device or a spare set of consumables within reach.

Cleaning after use is quick but must be done properly, because the same machine may be needed within minutes and one patient's residue must not become the next patient's hazard. The paddles are wiped free of gel, dried, and inspected for a dried film or a brown pit on the electrode surface, either of which reduces contact. The housing is wiped with the approved disinfectant, and liquid must never be poured onto the machine or allowed to run into the vents, because fluid in the high-voltage section can cause a short or a shock to the next operator. The cables are wiped along their length, uncoiled, and inspected for cracks and exposed conductors, because a cable that fails only when it is bent will pass every test and fail every arrest. Abrasive agents, solvents, and excessive moisture must be avoided as the maker directs, with the cleaning agent and the contact time confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The used pads are discarded, the gel bottle is closed, and the machine is returned to its charger, and if it was used on a patient with a transmissible infection the local decontamination rules apply, confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Transcutaneous pacing uses the same large adhesive pads but delivers a much smaller current, repeated at a set rate, to capture the ventricles when the heart's own conduction has failed. The machine generates pulses of a few tens of milliamperes, each lasting a few thousandths of a second, large enough to depolarize muscle through the chest wall and small enough to avoid injury. The operator sets a rate and an output, attaches the pads, and increases the output until each pulse produces a wide complex on the screen and a pulse that can be felt at the wrist. That moment is capture, and it is the only proof that the machine is truly pacing rather than merely stimulating the chest wall. Typical outputs range from about 40 to 70 milliamperes and typical rates from 60 to 80 per minute, and both figures, together with the method of confirming capture and the sedation needed, must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The discomfort is severe, because the current contracts every skeletal muscle in the chest and the diaphragm with every pulse, so analgesia and sedation are essential and the patient must be warned. The characteristic alarm is the message that capture has been lost, and the common error is to trust the pacing spike on the screen as proof of capture, when the spike proves only that the machine discharged.

An implanted pacemaker is a small sealed generator under the skin of the chest, connected by insulated leads that pass through a vein into the right atrium, the right ventricle, or both. The generator senses the heart's own electrical activity and delivers a tiny pulse only when a beat is missing, which is called demand pacing, and it can also pace at a fixed rate when it is programmed to ignore what it senses. The device is programmed before implantation and can be reprogrammed later by a programmer, a dedicated computer with a wand or a cable that communicates with the generator through the skin. The programmer also reads the stored history, including the rate, the recorded episodes, and the remaining battery voltage, and interrogation is the only way to know what an implanted device is doing. A magnet placed over the generator closes a switch inside it and forces a fixed, asynchronous rate, and the effect of the magnet and the rate it produces must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Electrosurgery, magnetic resonance imaging, large magnets, and strong radio transmitters can all interfere with an implanted device, because the circuit may mistake them for the heart's own signal or may reset itself. The interference may make the device withhold a beat, pace too fast, or change to a default mode, so the return pad of an electrosurgical unit is kept far from the chest and the device is interrogated after any procedure that could have disturbed it.

An implanted cardioverter defibrillator is a pacemaker with a wider mandate, because it senses the same signals but is permitted to treat dangerous rhythms rather than only to support a slow one. When it recognizes a rapid rhythm it may first try a burst of fast pacing called antitachycardia pacing, which is painless, and only then deliver an internal shock. The internal shock is far smaller than an external one because the current passes directly between the lead and the generator, but the patient feels it as a blow to the chest that may be repeated several times. Interrogation reveals how many episodes occurred, what therapy was delivered, and whether any shock was appropriate, and that record guides every later decision about drugs and programming. A magnet over the device usually suspends the shock function while leaving pacing intact, and that behavior must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. If external defibrillation is needed, the adhesive pads are placed front and back so that the current does not pass through the generator, and they are never placed directly over it. After any resuscitation or electrosurgery the implanted device must be interrogated, because a shock or an electromagnetic field can change its programming and leave the patient unprotected without anyone noticing.

In a mobile or frontier clinic the defibrillator is almost always an automated external defibrillator, because it is the only version that a small team can use safely without a physician at the bedside. The field device gives up the screen, the manual energy selection, the synchronized cardioversion, and the transcutaneous pacing of the hospital machine, and in exchange it offers a sealed case, a long shelf life, and a voice that tells an untrained rescuer what to do. Its pads are chosen for long life and a wide temperature range, its battery is rated for years of standby rather than for many shocks, and its self-test runs without anyone thinking about it. The advantage is decisive, because a nurse or a clinical officer who has never interpreted a rhythm can still deliver a shock within the first minutes. The price is that the device cannot treat a fast rhythm that still produces a pulse, cannot pace a slow heart, and cannot show the team the rhythm, so a patient who needs cardioversion or pacing must be moved to a hospital that holds the manual machine. The field unit depends entirely on consumables that must be replaced on schedule, and the expiry dates of the pads and the battery are the single most important information written on the case. Heat, humidity, dust, and the vibration of a vehicle all age the device, and the rule in the field is that it is checked at the start of every shift and after every journey, with the check written down.

Where a trained operator does exist, a manual defibrillator belongs in the field kit as well, because it can do what the automated device cannot and the clinical difference is real. The field manual device is usually a compact battery-powered monitor with paddles or pads, a small screen, a recorder, and sometimes pacing, and it gives up the large display, the mains supply, and the printer of the hospital unit. It demands a trained operator on every shift, a disciplined battery rotation, a stock of pads and gel that does not expire unnoticed, and the self-control to discharge only when the whole team is clear. It also demands a working electrocardiogram, because a manual shock without a rhythm is a blind shock, and the electrodes, the cables, and the screen are therefore part of the defibrillator rather than optional accessories. The team must know in advance which device is primary, where the pads are stored, how the battery is charged from a vehicle or a solar panel, and who is responsible for the daily check when the usual nurse is away. A device that depends on one trained person is fragile, so at least 2 members of the team should be able to use it and the skill must be refreshed on a fixed schedule. The honest field position is that an automated defibrillator with fresh pads and a charged battery saves more lives in most remote settings than a manual machine that nobody present has been trained to use.

The maintenance of a field defibrillator is mostly the work of the people who carry it, because the biomedical technician may visit once a year or never. The operator must check the readiness symbol, the expiry dates of the pads and the battery, the case and its seal, the gel if paddles are used, and every cable, and must record the check with a name and a date. The self-test is the device's own maintenance system, and its result must be read rather than assumed, because a machine that blinks a readiness light while its pads are 2 years out of date is ready to monitor and unable to shock. The characteristic failures in the field are a battery that has lost capacity after repeated partial charging, pads whose gel has dried in the heat, a screen that cannot be read in direct sunlight, and a cable crushed in a vehicle door. When the self-test fails, the device must be withdrawn from service and labeled, and the failure must be reported rather than hidden, because a machine quietly left on the shelf is a machine that will fail at the worst possible moment. The team must also plan for the case in which the device works but the operator does not, so practice with the actual machine, the actual pads, and the actual battery is part of maintenance in the fullest sense.

To summarize, a defibrillator stores electrical energy in a capacitor and releases it in a few thousandths of a second to depolarize a critical mass of heart muscle at once, and almost everything else about the device follows from that single fact. The hospital manual defibrillator adds a monitor, a synchronizer for cardioversion, pediatric energy control, and transcutaneous pacing, and it needs mains power, a biomedical service schedule, and a trained operator who can read the rhythm on the screen. The automated external defibrillator gives up the screen and the manual controls, and in exchange it lets a small team in a remote clinic deliver a shock within the first minutes, which is the interval that decides survival. The hazards are the charged capacitor and the wet or gel-bridged chest, the operator errors are charging too early, shocking into a vulnerable part of the cycle, leaving the synchronizer on, and mistaking a pacing spike for capture, and the alarms are the charge-ready tone, the stand clear prompt, the loss of capture message, and the low battery warning. The consumables are pads, gel, monitoring electrodes, batteries, printer paper, and razors, and their expiry dates and stock levels decide whether any of the rest of it works. The maintenance failure found during a resuscitation is almost always a flat battery, a dried pad, a loose electrode, or a cracked cable, and the daily check exists to find it the day before instead. If the reader remembers only one instruction, let it be that no setting, energy, rate, threshold, or interval in this chapter may be used from memory, and that each must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.
