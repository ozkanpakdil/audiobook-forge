# Appendix C — Crash Cart and Department Checklists

A checklist is a device made of words, and its physical principle is that it moves a sequence out of a tired human memory onto a surface that does not fatigue. Working memory holds only a handful of items at once, and under the noise and urgency of a resuscitation it holds fewer, dropping the quiet items such as the second dose, the battery, and the suction. A written or spoken list turns recall into recognition, so the person checking confirms what the list names instead of remembering what to look for. A list also fixes an order, and order matters because suction precedes ventilation and ventilation precedes drugs. The list is not a substitute for judgment but a floor beneath it. Every number in every list here must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The crash cart is a box on wheels designed to be found, opened, and emptied in a defined sequence. It is a cabinet with a flat top, several drawers, and four casters, one or two of which lock, and the top carries a defibrillator and a surface for drawing drugs. Each drawer is dedicated to one phase of resuscitation so that the hand goes to a known place without searching, and a numbered seal passes through the handles with its number written on a log. That seal is the cart's memory, because a broken seal means the cart must be checked before it is trusted, while a matching number means only that no one has opened it. The characteristic failure is the sealed cart that is empty inside, restocked with the wrong sizes or holding drugs that expired quietly in the dark. The daily check exists because a seal never proves the contents are correct, and the check interval and inventory list must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The cart is opened in the order the resuscitation opens the patient: airway, breathing, circulation. The top drawer holds airway equipment, meaning a laryngoscope with working batteries and a spare set, blades, endotracheal tubes, a stylet, a supraglottic airway, a self-inflating resuscitation bag with several mask sizes, oral and nasal airways, and a carbon dioxide detector. The second drawer holds oxygen tubing and masks, a nasal cannula, a manual suction unit with catheters, and spare canisters and tubing. The third holds drugs in the order they are likely to be drawn, usually a first-line cardiac arrest drug, a vasopressor, an antiarrhythmic, atropine, a benzodiazepine, dextrose, and naloxone. The fourth holds intravenous catheters, intraosseous needles, tourniquets, antiseptic swabs, syringes from 1 to 20 milliliters, needles, and tape, while the fifth holds larger items such as a difficult airway kit, a chest drain set, and obstetric supplies. The pediatric cart differs in scale rather than shape, with smaller blades, tubes, masks, and bags, weight-based drug cards on the lid, and a length-based tape for estimating weight, and every size, dose, gas flow, and energy setting here must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The daily check and the shift check are two rituals with two different jobs. The daily check is the deep one: it verifies each drawer against its inventory, reads every expiry date, runs the defibrillator self-test, confirms battery charge and a spare battery, checks oxygen cylinder pressure and suction function, and compares the seal number with the log. The shift check is the fast one, confirming only that the seal is intact, the cart is in its place, the defibrillator shows ready, and the log is signed. The seal system assigns a number and a signature to a moment, so the next person can tell whether the cart has been opened since it was verified. The common operator error is to break the seal, remove one item, and reseal the cart without restocking, or to sign the log without touching the cart, which transfers blame to the next person. A cart whose seal number does not match the log must be checked completely, and the check interval, seal numbering convention, and self-test threshold must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The code checklist runs from the first compression to the debrief. The first step is to confirm unresponsiveness and absent or abnormal breathing, call for help, and begin chest compressions at the rate and depth the protocol states, because compressions generate the pressure that keeps the brain and heart perfused while the rhythm is corrected. The second is to attach the monitor and identify the rhythm with an electrocardiogram, called an ECG after this first mention. The third is to defibrillate when indicated at the energy the protocol states and resume compressions at once, and the fourth is to establish intravenous or intraosseous access and give drugs on the protocol schedule while recording every dose. The fifth is to search for reversible causes while the compressor rotates every two minutes. The characteristic sounds are the defibrillator charge and shock-ready tones, the monitor lead-off and poor-signal alarms, and the low-battery tone; a charge alarm without a delivered shock, or a monitor reading asystole because a lead fell off, is itself a hazard. The debrief follows within minutes and asks what happened, what went well, and what will change, and compression rate, depth, drug intervals, and defibrillation energy must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The operating theatre checklist is built around two moments. The first is the surgical safety pause before the incision, when the team confirms the patient's identity, the site, the procedure, the allergies, the airway plan, the anticipated blood loss, and the antibiotics already given; the pause works because it makes the surgeon, the anesthesia provider, and the nurse speak one plan aloud. The second is the count, performed at the opening, at the start of wound closure, and at skin closure, when instruments, sponges, needles, and small items are counted aloud by two people independently. The physical principle is that a retained object causes infection, obstruction, or erosion while remaining invisible from the surface, so only a count, and where it disagrees an intraoperative X-ray, can exclude it. The count fails when the theatre is noisy or when one person counts while the other writes, and the common operator error is to count from memory at the end instead of keeping a running ledger. A discrepancy is an emergency stop rather than a debate, resolved before the patient leaves the room, and the pause items, count sequence, and X-ray threshold must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The intensive care unit, called the ICU after this first mention, is checked as a place where a patient can be kept alive without interruption, and the check moves from the wall outward. At the wall, gas and vacuum outlets are confirmed to deliver, sockets are confirmed live, and the uninterruptible power supply, shortened to UPS after this first mention, is confirmed online with a charged battery. At the bed, the ventilator passes its self-test and holds a leak-free circuit, a set tidal volume, tested alarm limits, and a charged internal battery, while the monitor has working leads and a tested alarm. Suction, oxygen, an emergency bag, and the resuscitation drugs are present at the bedside with a call bell and a clear route to the door. The physical principle is redundancy, since every critical function needs a second source. The characteristic alarm is the ventilator low-pressure or disconnection alarm, followed by the monitor low-limit and pump occlusion alarms, and the common operator error is to silence an alarm at the bedside and walk away. Alarm limits, tidal volumes, oxygen concentrations, and battery runtimes must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and recording the check in the electronic health record, shortened to EHR after this first mention, leaves a trace a paper sheet never does.

The ambulance check is a bed space check that must survive motion. The vehicle is checked for fuel or charge, tires, brakes, lights, and a working siren. The equipment is checked whether the vehicle moves or not: oxygen with a full cylinder and a spare, suction with a charged battery, a defibrillator with tested pads and charge, a monitor with the right leads, and a self-inflating bag with adult and pediatric masks. Vibration and temperature cycling loosen every connection, so the check repeats at the start of every shift rather than weekly, and the drugs are checked for expiry and for protection from heat and cold. The characteristic alarms are the defibrillator low-battery tone and the monitor lead-off alarm. The common operator error is to check the equipment in the station and never again after the first call, so the second patient meets an empty cylinder or a depleted battery. Restocking after every call rather than at the end of the day is the only reliable rule, and cylinder pressures, battery runtimes, and transport drug lists must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The delivery room check is written for two patients at once, the mother and the newborn. The room is checked for a radiant warmer switched on long enough to hold its temperature, and neonatal equipment sits at the warmer: a small self-inflating bag, masks in newborn and premature sizes, a suction device with a small catheter, oxygen with a blender, and a pulse oximeter with a newborn sensor. The physical principle of a warmer is that a newborn loses heat rapidly by evaporation, radiation, and conduction, and a cold newborn burns oxygen and glucose to stay warm instead of using them to breathe, so warmth is a resuscitation drug. The mother's side carries an electrocardiogram, a blood pressure device, intravenous access and fluids, an infusion pump, and drugs for hemorrhage and high blood pressure. The characteristic alarms are the warmer high-temperature alarm, the pulse oximeter poor-signal alarm, and the pump occlusion alarm, and a warmer alarm reset without checking the probe is a burn hazard. The common operator error is to prepare a perfect adult tray and find at the moment of delivery that the newborn airway supplies are missing, and the warmer temperature, oxygen concentration, suction pressure, and newborn bag size must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

An emergency tray is the smaller cousin of the crash cart, made for a defined procedure rather than a whole resuscitation. It is a shallow sealed and labelled tray holding a laryngoscope, a range of blades and tubes, a stylet, a supraglottic airway, syringes, suction catheters, and the few drugs the procedure may require. The physical principle is proximity, because the tray exists so the equipment arrives with the patient instead of being fetched from a cupboard while the airway closes. The tray is checked after every use and at a stated interval even if it has never been opened, since seals age, batteries corrode, and drugs expire inside a closed box. A tray carrying an airway must also carry a means of ventilation and suction, because an airway without those is a tube that cannot be used. The characteristic failure is the laryngoscope whose light is dim or dead, discovered only when the blade is in the mouth, so the light is switched on during the check. The common operator error is to draw a drug and return the tray unsealed, and the contents list, seal interval, and blade and tube sizes must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The refrigerator and freezer check is a temperature check in a place where temperature is a drug. A pharmaceutical refrigerator holds vaccines, some drugs, and blood products, and the physical principle is that a vaccine or a protein loses potency as a function of heat and time, so a short excursion upward can waste a whole shelf with no visible change. The check reads the thermometer and the independent data logger, writes the value on a log with the time and initials, and confirms that the door seal is intact and the refrigerator is not overfilled. The usual pharmaceutical range is 2 to 8 degrees Celsius and the usual freezer range is minus 20 degrees Celsius or colder, and these numbers must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the refrigerator is used, because the correct range differs by product. The characteristic alarm is the high-temperature alarm. The common operator error is to keep food and drink in a pharmaceutical refrigerator, which raises the temperature and invites the door to be opened constantly. An excursion must be documented, the stock quarantined, and the decision to use or discard taken by a named person, and the alarm threshold, logging interval, acceptable excursion time, and quarantine procedure must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The field variant of these lists is a set of answers for the mobile clinic, the health post, and the team carrying its hospital in a rucksack. The crash cart becomes a soft bag or a hard case divided into pouches that follow the same airway, breathing, circulation order, and it is rechecked at the same moments because a bag has no seal and no lock. What the field setup gives up is redundancy: one defibrillator instead of two, one suction unit, one oxygen source, and a pharmaceutical stock measured in doses rather than shelves. What it demands instead is personal discipline, because the same person who used the last vial of epinephrine must write it down and replace it before sleeping. The field check is written on a laminated card and is signed and dated after every use and every morning. Every battery is charged from the vehicle or a solar panel and every charger is counted as part of the kit, and the stock level, charging routine, and pack contents must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

In the field the checklist is often spoken rather than written, and a spoken list is performed by two people who ask each other the questions in order. The team leader names the items and the second person confirms each by touch and by sight. Where no crash cart exists, a plastic tackle box, a bicycle pannier, or a wooden cupboard with labelled compartments can be governed by the same list, and the list is attached to the container so that it cannot be separated from it. The team must also plan for the absence of a defibrillator, a blood bank, and a surgeon, so the checklist includes the trigger for referral and the time at which referral must begin, because a decision to transfer is itself an intervention that benefits from being written down. The physical principle of that trigger is that distance costs time and time costs perfusion, so it is set early enough that the patient can still survive the journey. The characteristic failure is the hospital list used unchanged in a clinic, full of items that cannot be performed and empty of the ones that can, and every trigger, transport time, and stock level in a field checklist must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Behind every checklist stands the unglamorous work that keeps the listed items usable. The cart and tray surfaces are wiped with the detergent and disinfectant the protocol names, and never with a dripping cloth that pushes fluid into the electronics. Batteries are the quiet failure of every emergency device, and one that passes a self-test may still fail under load, so test and replacement intervals belong on the maintenance calendar. Laryngoscope blades are checked for a working light and a clean lens, cylinders are weighed or their gauge read, and suction is tested by occluding the tube and watching pressure build. Consumables are rotated so the oldest stock is used first, expiry dates are read at every check rather than trusted to memory, and nothing expired is left in a cart because a cart is opened under stress and no one reads labels then. The operator error that defeats all of this is the cart used as a general store and stripped of its own list. Cleaning agents, disinfection contact times, battery replacement intervals, and cylinder pressure thresholds must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The central sterile services department, or CSSD, partners with every checklist holding reusable instruments, and an instrument that never reached it is a hazard rather than a tool.

A checklist is only as strong as the institution that audits it. The written record, whether on paper or in the electronic health record, must show who checked, when, what was found, and what was corrected, and an audit reads those records for patterns a single check cannot see, such as a drug that expires repeatedly because it is stocked beyond its demand. Every resuscitation and every near miss should be reviewed, and the list should be revised when the equipment, the protocol, or the patient population changes. The physical principle behind revision is that a checklist is a model of a task, and a model that no longer matches the task will be abandoned by the people who know better. A list nobody follows is worse than no list, because it manufactures recorded compliance without changing behavior and hides the gap from the next auditor. The antidote is ownership, since the people who use the cart should write its list, sign it, and decide what counts as ready. The final authority on any list remains the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol, which must be consulted before the list is adopted or changed.

To summarize, a checklist is a memory written down at a calm moment, and its physical principle is that recognition is more reliable than recall. The crash cart is opened in the order the patient is treated, airway then breathing then circulation, and its seal and log record whether anyone has touched it since it was verified. The daily check reads expiry dates and tests the defibrillator, the shift check confirms the seal and the ready state, and the code checklist runs from the first compression through drugs and reversible causes to the debrief. The theatre pause and the count protect against a plan misunderstood and an object retained, while the bed space, ambulance, delivery room, and tray checks place the right equipment within reach. The field variant keeps the same order in a bag or a box, and demands personal discipline and a laminated card. Behind all of it sits the unglamorous work of cleaning, battery testing, rotating stock, and auditing the record, because a checklist is a habit performed by people who know the equipment must be ready before the patient arrives. Every number and threshold in every list in this appendix must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.
