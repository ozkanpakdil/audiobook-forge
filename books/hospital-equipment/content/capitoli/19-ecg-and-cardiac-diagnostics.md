# Chapter 19 — ECG and Cardiac Diagnostics

The heart is a moving battery, and the electrocardiogram, ECG for short, is what that battery looks like from the surface of the body. Each heartbeat begins as a wave of activation that sweeps through the atria and ventricles, and because the body is a volume conductor of salt water, the resulting voltage changes spread outward to the skin. Two electrodes at different points therefore sit at slightly different voltages at the same instant, and the instrument measures only that difference, amplifies it many thousands of times, and plots it against time. The signal at the skin is tiny, on the order of 1 millivolt, and the usual gain makes 1 millivolt of input produce a deflection of 10 millimeters on the record, values that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Direction matters as much as size, because activation moving toward an electrode gives an upward deflection while activation moving away gives a downward one.

The recording chain is a series of links, and each link can spoil the result on its own. At the patient end sits the electrode, a conductive pad that turns the ionic current of the skin into the electronic current of the wire. Above it are the adhesive and the conductive gel, which hold it in place and bridge the dry outer skin. A lead wire then carries the tiny voltage to a differential amplifier that compares each pair of inputs and rejects the interference common to both, which is why a clean trace is possible in a room full of lights and pumps. The amplifier passes only the frequencies of interest, commonly a diagnostic range of 0.05 to 150 hertz with a notch filter at 50 or 60 hertz, and every bandwidth, filter setting, and gain value must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The machine drives a thermal printer or fills a screen, and it should emit a calibration pulse of exactly 1 millivolt before and after a recording, a reference that must also be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The standard diagnostic recording uses 10 electrodes to produce 12 views. Four electrodes sit on the limbs, and the right leg electrode serves mainly as a neutral reference. From those 4 points the machine calculates 6 frontal views, in which lead 1 compares the right arm with the left arm, lead 2 compares the right arm with the left leg, and lead 3 compares the left arm with the left leg. Three augmented leads reweight the same inputs into the augmented right arm lead, the augmented left arm lead, and the augmented left leg lead. Six further electrodes cross the chest, from chest lead 1 beside the breastbone to chest lead 6 in the mid axillary line, and each looks at the heart from a different angle. Lead 2, lead 3, and the augmented left leg lead face the inferior wall, the chest leads face the anterior and lateral walls, and lead 1 with the augmented left arm lead faces the high lateral wall, so the pattern of abnormality is itself a map of where the trouble lies.

Placement is not a formality, because a tracing made 2 centimeters too high or with 2 wires exchanged can imitate a serious disease that is not present. The limb electrodes go on soft tissue rather than bone, and the chest electrodes follow the spaces between the ribs at the positions the machine expects. The classic operator error is exchanging the left and right arm wires, which inverts lead 1 and can suggest a lateral problem while concealing a real one, and the second is placing all 6 chest electrodes too low on the abdomen, which flattens the anterior views. The machine cannot detect either mistake, because the electrodes are connected and the trace looks plausible, so the operator must read the positions from the machine's diagram and inspect the patient rather than the screen. When the standard 12 views are not enough, electrodes are added deliberately, mirrored onto the right side of the chest for the right ventricle or placed posteriorly for the back wall. More leads mean more wires and a greater chance of a loose connection, and the positions and their number must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The ECG machine is deceptively safe because it only listens, but a patient connected to it is part of an electrical circuit and the connection is a real hazard. Every electrode and lead wire brings the patient into contact with a mains powered instrument, so the machine must be properly grounded, its cables must be undamaged, and its leakage current must be tested at the intervals its maker specifies. A fault leaking current into a patient with a catheter inside the heart can provoke a fatal rhythm disturbance. The patient must never be connected to a machine whose case is cracked, whose cable is frayed, or whose plug bypasses the earth connection, and electrodes must not be placed over broken skin or a rash. The characteristic alarms are the lead off message, the low battery warning, the paper out message, and the rhythm alarms of a monitor with a heart rate limit, and every threshold and message must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The common operator error is to silence a lead off alarm, tape the cable, and continue recording, because the resulting flat line can be read as a pause in the heart rather than a loose wire.

Electrode quality decides more tracings than any electronic setting, and the electrode is the consumable that must never be improvised. A disposable electrode is a pad carrying a metal stud, a conductive adhesive, and a gel that may be wet, semi solid, or solid. A wet gel conducts well but dries out once the package is opened, while a solid gel lasts longer and grips the skin less strongly. The gel must be moist across the whole contact area, the adhesive must be pressed firmly to the skin, and the electrode must be within its expiry date, because an expired electrode may already have lost its moisture and will produce a wandering baseline that looks like disease. Single use electrodes are discarded after one patient, and reusable suction electrodes or limb plates are cleaned and fitted with fresh gel or a fresh strap for every recording. Chest hair, sweat, cream, and powder all separate the gel from the skin, so the site may be clipped rather than shaved, wiped with an alcohol pad, and dried, and the local skin care protocol must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Artifact is any mark on the tracing that did not come from the heart, and recognizing it is the difference between a clinician and a machine operator. Baseline wander is a slow undulation caused by breathing, a loose electrode, or a shivering patient, and it can hide the elevated or depressed segment between the S wave and the T wave that matters most. Muscle tremor is a dense fuzz from a tense patient, and it is reduced by warming the room, resting the hands, and asking the patient to relax rather than by turning up a filter. Interference from the mains appears as a fine ripple at 50 or 60 hertz and is usually caused by a dried electrode, a coiled lead wire, or a cable lying across a power supply. Motion from a cough or a mobile telephone can imitate a dangerous rhythm, and a poor contact can produce a flat line that mimics a pause. The operator must correct the cause at the patient, because a filter that hides tremor can also flatten the wave the clinician is looking for, and the filter settings and acceptable noise limits must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A poor tracing does not announce itself; it becomes a wrong decision with the confidence of a real recording. A wandering baseline from a dry electrode can be read as an injury of the inferior wall and lead to a transfer or a treatment that was never needed, while a flat line from a disconnected wire can be read as a stopped heart. A muscle tremor can be mistaken for a fast irregular rhythm, and an inverted lead from exchanged arm wires can be mistaken for an old scar. The operator must therefore look at every tracing before it leaves the room, check that the calibration pulse is square and of the expected height, check that the baseline is steady in every view, and repeat the recording if any part is uninterpretable. A repeat costs minutes while a wrong decision costs a great deal, and the repeat should be documented with the reason it was made. A tracing sent for a remote opinion must be complete and legible, with the patient's identifier, the date, the time, and the recording settings visible on it, and the local retention rules must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Maintenance of an ECG machine is mostly cleanliness, inspection, and a battery that is always ready. The housing, the keyboard, and the screen are wiped with the disinfectant the maker approves, and no liquid is allowed to enter the connectors or the printer, because the lead wire socket is the part most easily damaged by fluid. Lead wires are inspected along their whole length for cracks and loose connectors, since a single broken strand can produce an intermittent trace that appears only when the patient moves. The battery of a portable unit is charged before every shift, its contacts are cleaned, and a spare battery is carried wherever the unit is used away from a socket. Thermal paper is stored away from heat and sunlight, because it darkens with age and a faded strip cannot be read. A calibration pulse and a short test recording are made at the start of every day, and the intervals for electrical safety testing, calibration verification, and battery replacement must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Most ECG failures are gradual, and the operator is the only detector. The first group is contact failure, in which electrodes dry out, gel migrates, adhesive lifts, or a strap loosens, and it appears as a wandering baseline, a lead off alarm, or a trace that changes when the patient is touched. The second is conductor failure, in which a lead wire breaks inside its insulation, a connector corrodes, or a socket loosens, and it appears as one view that fails while the others remain clean. The third is instrument failure, in which the amplifier drifts, the printer darkens the paper unevenly, or the software freezes, and it produces a tracing that is internally consistent but wrong. A fourth group belongs to the user and includes the wrong filter, the wrong gain, the wrong paper speed, and the wrong patient identification. Any unit that fails a check is labeled, removed from service, and repaired, and an intermittent fault is written down with the conditions in which it appeared.

When the heart produces its evidence over hours rather than seconds, the recording must move with the patient, and the ambulatory recorder does exactly that. A Holter recorder is a small battery powered unit worn on a belt or a shoulder strap, connected to the chest by 3 to 7 electrodes that give 2 or 3 simultaneous views, and it stores every beat of a full day or more in solid state memory rather than on paper. The patient keeps a diary of symptoms, meals, exercise, and medication times, and that diary is what turns a mark on the record into a clinical event. The value of the recording is decided in the first 10 minutes, when the operator prepares the skin, avoids muscle that will move, secures the wires with loops of tape, tests every channel, and records the exact start time. The common operator error is to send a patient home with a channel that is already noisy, because the whole day will be wasted. The characteristic alarms are the lead off alert, the low battery warning, and the memory full message, with the recording duration and every alarm threshold confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Not every symptom happens within a single day, and the event recorder is the answer to the symptom that appears once a week. An external event monitor stores a short strip from before and after the patient presses a button, capturing the heart's activity at the moment of the symptom. A wire free patch recorder is worn continuously for up to 2 weeks and combines the long wear of an ambulatory recorder with an event button that marks the moment. An implantable loop recorder placed under the skin of the chest can watch for 3 years or more, which makes it the tool for the rare fainting episode no external recorder catches. Each device trades the completeness of the continuous recording for the length of the observation, and the recording duration and the event detection settings must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Exercise makes the heart reveal a shortage that rest conceals, and the exercise stress test is the deliberate production of that situation. When the patient works against a treadmill or a cycle, the muscles demand more oxygen and the heart rate and blood pressure rise. A narrowed coronary artery that was adequate at rest can no longer supply the region it feeds, which shows itself as a change in the electrical pattern before the patient feels pain. The workload is increased in stages, each lasting a set number of minutes, and the protocol, the increments, and the stage duration must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A cycle ergometer keeps the upper body stable and measures the work in watts, while a treadmill reproduces daily exertion more faithfully. Throughout the test the operator records a full 12 view ECG continuously, samples the blood pressure at fixed intervals, and watches the patient's face, gait, and speech. The usual endpoint is a target heart rate calculated from the patient's age, which must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, or a stopping sign such as chest pain, a dangerous rhythm, or a fall in blood pressure.

Some patients cannot exercise, and the stress laboratory must be able to provoke the heart by drug instead. A pharmacological test infuses an agent that raises the heart rate and the force of contraction, or that dilates the coronary arteries and reveals a difference in blood flow between regions. The agent, the dose, the infusion rate, and the duration must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. An agent that increases the work of the heart can provoke an injury, a rhythm disturbance, or chest pain, and a dilating agent can cause flushing, breathlessness, and a fall in blood pressure, so monitoring matches that of exercise. The drug is given through a secure intravenous line, an antidote for the dilating agents must be immediately available, and a physician trained in resuscitation must remain in the room. A stress laboratory is an emergency room that usually has no emergency, so a defibrillator, a resuscitation bag with masks, suction, oxygen, a crash cart, and a working telephone are checked before the first patient of the day. The characteristic alarm is the monitor's rhythm alarm with its heart rate limits and its low saturation alarm, the hazard is the exercise induced heart attack, and the common operator error is to let the patient grip the treadmill handrails.

Echocardiography answers the questions the electrical tracing cannot, because it shows muscle, valves, and the movement of blood rather than the timing of activation. The instrument works like a depth sounder, because a crystal that changes shape when a voltage is applied is made to vibrate at millions of cycles per second and to produce a voltage when it is squeezed. The pulse of sound it sends into the chest is partly reflected at every boundary where the stiffness of the tissue changes, and the time taken for the echo to return gives the depth of the boundary. The same crystal listens between pulses, so rapid repetition produces a moving image the eye reads as real time motion. The frequency of the sound decides the central trade off, because a higher frequency gives a finer image but penetrates less deeply while a lower frequency reaches the far side of the heart with less detail. The operator therefore chooses a probe and a frequency for the patient's size and the depth of interest, and the frequency, the depth, and the image settings must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The echocardiography machine offers several ways of using the same echoes. In motion mode a single line of the image is followed over time and the movement of each structure is plotted as a wavy line, which measures the motion of a valve leaflet or a chamber wall. In the cross sectional mode many lines are combined into a fan shaped image that the operator sweeps through the heart, and this is the mode used to judge chamber size, muscle thickness, valve shape, and the squeezing of the ventricles. Doppler modes add the motion of the blood itself, using the fact that sound reflected from moving cells changes its frequency by an amount proportional to the speed of the movement. Pulsed wave Doppler measures flow at one chosen depth up to a speed limit set by the pulse rate, continuous wave Doppler measures the highest speeds without that limit but cannot say at what depth they occurred, and color Doppler paints the same information across the image in shades of red and blue, with every mode setting, angle, and gain value confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A cardiac ultrasound examination is only as good as the window the operator finds and the probe that fits there. The standard transthoracic probe is a small phased array crystal steered electronically to sweep the beam through a sector, and it is pressed between the ribs beside the breastbone, below the breastbone, and at the apex of the heart, because bone blocks sound and air scatters it. A coupling gel fills the microscopic gap between probe and skin, and without it almost all of the sound is reflected at the first boundary and the screen stays black, so the gel is a consumable of the same importance as the electrode. The transesophageal probe is mounted on a flexible gastroscope and looks at the heart from inside the esophagus, where no rib or lung intervenes, at the cost of sedation and of a demanding cleaning routine. Every examination should be documented with a set of standard views, the measurements taken, the reason for the study, and the operator's impression. The images and report are stored in the picture archiving and communication system, PACS, which accepts the standard format of the digital imaging and communications in medicine, DICOM, with the naming and retention rules confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Stress echocardiography joins the exercise or the drug to the image, and it looks for a segment of muscle that stops moving when the heart is asked to work harder. The same views are recorded before the stress, at each stage, and immediately afterward, and the comparison is made between a segment that contracts well at rest and the same segment under load, because a region supplied by a narrowed artery loses its movement before it is permanently damaged. The useful window closes within a minute or 2 of stopping as the heart rate falls, so the images are acquired in a fixed order and compared honestly. A contrast agent may be used when the plain image is poor, and the agent is a suspension of tiny gas bubbles with a thin shell that scatters sound far more strongly than blood or tissue. Those bubbles are destroyed by the sound beam and by the lungs, so they are given intravenously as a slow injection and imaged within minutes, and the dose, the injection rate, and the imaging settings must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The hazard is an allergic reaction that can be severe, so the patient is screened for previous reactions and resuscitation equipment is at hand, while the common operator error is to compare images taken from different positions or depths.

The echocardiography probe is the most fragile expensive object in the room. The face of the probe is a delicate lens that must never be dropped, scraped, or rested on a hard surface, and the cable must be supported so that its weight does not hang from the connector. Cleaning begins at the bedside with removal of the gel, using a soft cloth and the approved disinfectant, and the probe is wiped without being immersed beyond the line marked on its shaft, since fluid entering the handle destroys the electronics. Transesophageal probes must be leak tested before every immersion, cleaned of all biological material, and then given high level disinfection or sterilization, with the concentration, the contact time, the temperature, and the rinsing steps confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Probes are stored hanging vertically so that they are not coiled or crushed, and a probe that shows a broken element, a dark band in the image, or a failed leak test is removed from service at once. The characteristic alarms include a probe over temperature warning and a system or image fault message, and the temperature limit and every alarm threshold must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

In a mobile clinic or a frontier hospital the full diagnostic machine is usually absent, and the ECG contracts to a rhythm monitor that a single trained operator can carry and trust. The field instrument is commonly a small battery powered monitor with 3 electrodes that produce 1 or 2 views, a screen that can be read in daylight, and a rhythm alarm with a heart rate limit. Its purpose is narrower than diagnosis: it answers whether the rhythm is fast, slow, regular, or dangerously chaotic during a treatment or a transfer. Its electrodes are the same disposables used in the hospital, but heat, dust, and repeated use of one site make them fail sooner, so the operator carries twice as many as the count suggests. Its battery may be the only power for the day, so the unit is charged whenever a socket or a solar panel is available, and the charging state is recorded rather than assumed. Because the screen is small and the trace is often noisy, the operator places the electrodes carefully, keeps the patient still for the initial recording, and stores a baseline strip of that patient's normal rhythm, with the alarm thresholds set to the patient and every limit confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The smartphone has become a genuine field instrument, and it earns that place through 2 mechanisms that must not be confused with one another. A small handheld unit with 2 metal contacts records a single view when the patient touches it with the fingers of both hands or when it is laid against the chest, and a miniature recorder with 2 or 3 adhesive electrodes sends a continuous rhythm to the telephone by a short range radio link. Both methods record a real electrical signal that can be printed, stored, and sent for an opinion, and their tracings are adequate for rhythm but not for fine detail, which still requires a proper 12 view recording. The single most valuable habit in field cardiac diagnosis is the repeat tracing, because a first tracing made in a hot, crowded room with a nervous patient is often uninterpretable. The repeat is made after the patient has rested, after the skin has been prepared again and the electrodes changed, and after any obvious interference has been moved away. If a rhythm is fast, the repeat shows whether it is sustained, and if a tracing suggests an injury, the repeat shows whether the finding is stable rather than a single artifact, with every device limit and alarm threshold confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The field kit lives or dies by its consumables, so electrodes, gel, alcohol pads, clippers, tape, spare lead wires, a charged battery, and a means of storing images are all checked before the clinic. Heat dries the gel, softens the adhesive, and drains the batteries, so the kit is stored out of direct sun and the oldest electrodes are used first. Without a cardiologist and without a full machine, the field clinician must decide what can be answered locally and what must be referred. A single view can show a rate, a regularity, the width of the Q, R, and S spike, and the small wave that precedes each beat, enough to recognize many dangerous rhythms and to choose a treatment or a transfer. What it cannot do is localize an injury, measure an interval that needs several simultaneous views, or detect a change that appears in only one of 12 positions, and the clinician must say so plainly rather than guess. The absence of a number is a real loss that must be admitted in the notes, because a confident diagnosis made from an uninterpretable strip is more dangerous than an honest uncertainty, and the service intervals and tolerance limits of every field device must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

To summarize, the ECG records the small voltage that a moving wave of cardiac activation produces at the skin, and every lead is a viewpoint rather than a picture. The hospital machine depends on good electrodes, prepared skin, intact lead wires, a verified calibration pulse, and correct filter and gain settings, and most of its failures are contact and conductor faults the operator must find. Artifact imitates disease, and a poor tracing accepted without question leads to a wrong decision, so every tracing is inspected, repeated when unclear, and documented with the conditions in which it was made. The recording extends in time through ambulatory and event recorders, and it extends in provocation through exercise and drug stress testing, which must be conducted with the resuscitation readiness of an emergency room. Echocardiography adds structure and flow through reflected sound, and its modes, probes, contrast agents, and demanding cleaning routine each carry their own hazards and operator errors. In the field the ECG contracts to a single view on a small monitor or a telephone, and the discipline that replaces the missing machine is skin preparation, a stored baseline, and the habit of the repeat tracing, with every setting, threshold, dose, cycle, and temperature named in this chapter confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.
