# Chapter 5 — Hospital Beds and Patient Handling

The hospital bed is not furniture but a machine, and every part of it exists to solve a problem of force. A body lying still still presses downward with its whole weight, and that weight passes through the skin, the soft tissue, and the bone onto whatever surface supports it. A flat, hard surface concentrates that load on the few places where the skeleton is close to the skin, while a soft surface spreads it over a wider area but lets the body sink into a hollow. The frame carries the load, the moving sections change its direction, the mattress distributes it, and the side rails and brakes keep the whole assembly where it was put. Every adjustment described in this chapter is a setting, and every setting must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before it is used on a patient.

A manual bed is the purest expression of the mechanism. Its frame is a rectangular steel or aluminum chassis on 4 casters, with a head section, a seat section, and a leg section hinged to each other. Cranks at the foot of the bed turn shafts that drive worm gears or screw jacks, and each turn of the handle raises or lowers a section by a fixed distance. A ratchet holds the section at the height chosen, and the patient's weight is what the gear train must overcome, which is why a bed loaded with a heavy patient is far harder to crank than an empty bed. The common operator error is to leave the crank handle protruding into the walkway, where it catches a passing hip or a swinging door and bends the shaft or breaks the handle. The characteristic sign of a failing manual bed is a crank that turns freely without moving the section, which usually means a stripped gear or a sheared pin rather than a problem the user can repair. The angle each section reaches, whether 30 degrees for a backrest or 45 degrees for a knee break, must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, because the graduations on an old bed may no longer be honest.

An electric bed replaces the crank with a motor and a linear actuator, a threaded rod that extends and retracts inside a tube when the motor turns it. The characteristic alarm is a warning tone or a flashing indicator when the actuator is overloaded, when the handset is disconnected, or when the battery is too weak to answer the button. The common operator error is to keep pressing a button against a mechanical stop, which stalls the motor and heats the windings rather than protecting them. The height range, the backrest angle, and the battery capacity printed in the manual are settings that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The intensive care bed is the same machine with more of everything. It is usually longer and wider, rated for a much heavier patient, and built so that a radiograph cassette can slide beneath the mattress without lifting the patient. Many intensive care beds carry a built-in scale, which works by measuring the load at each corner of the frame and summing the 4 readings, and this is why the bed must be level and the patient still before a weight is trusted. The characteristic alarm is the scale fault or the bed exit alert, the latter triggered by a sensor pad or by a load change when the patient tries to climb out. The weight limit of such a bed may be 250 kilograms or more, and that figure must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the bed is used, because the limit includes the mattress, the rails, and every pump hung on the frame.

Side rails look like a simple safety feature and are in fact among the most serious hazards in the ward. They are barriers, but a barrier with gaps, and a patient whose body is small, confused, or frail can slide through the gap between the rail and the mattress, or between 2 sections of rail, and become trapped by the neck or the chest. The danger is greatest with a mattress that is too thin for the rail, with a rail that is not locked in its raised position, or with a rail raised only along a single edge so that the patient tries to climb over the other. The correct response to a patient who keeps climbing out is not more rails but a proper assessment and, where needed, a low bed, a mat on the floor, or a bed exit alarm. The common operator error is to treat raised rails as a restraint and to leave them up for a patient who is fully mobile, which increases the fall from a greater height when he climbs over. Any gap measurement quoted for a rail and mattress combination must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, since a rail matched to the wrong mattress is the failure that causes the injury.

The brakes and casters decide whether the bed stays where it is put. A caster is a small wheel in a swiveling fork, and a brake is usually a cam or a lever that presses a pad against the tread, or a lock that drops a pin into the wheel. Braking a single wheel of the 4 is not the same as braking the bed, because the frame can still pivot around the locked wheel, which is why the rule is to apply the brakes on all 4 casters, and on most beds to press the pedal at the head end and the foot end in turn. A bed on a polished floor with wet wheels can travel several meters from a single push, and a patient stepping onto an unbraked bed is a classic cause of a fall and of a staff back injury. The common operator error is to leave the brake pedal in the mid position, where it looks applied but holds nothing, or to brake only the wheels along a single edge. The braking mechanism on a bed that has been dragged through detergent and disinfectant for years may need adjustment, and the inspection interval for that adjustment must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol.

Position is the reason the bed has sections at all, and each position is a controlled application of gravity. Raising the backrest shifts the patient's weight onto the sacrum and the heels, and raising the knee break keeps him from sliding down the bed, but the pair together create shear, a force that pulls the skin sideways across the mattress and damages it from within. The Trendelenburg position tilts the whole bed head down, which uses gravity to drain the airway or to shift the abdominal contents upward, while the reverse Trendelenburg tilts the foot down and is used to relieve pressure on the diaphragm in a patient who cannot breathe lying flat. A tilt of 15 to 30 degrees is the range usually quoted for these positions, and that angle must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The hazard of a steep head-down tilt is that the patient slides toward the head of the bed, that the diaphragm is pushed up, and that a full stomach can reflux into the airway. The common operator error is to reach for the Trendelenburg position as a reflex in every collapse, when in many cases it delays compressions and does nothing for the cause.

The mattress is where the body actually rests, and its job is to keep the interface pressure between skin and surface below the pressure at which capillaries close. A foam mattress distributes load by compressing where the body is heaviest and springing back where it is light, and its quality is measured by density and by the way it recovers rather than by its softness in the hand. A mattress that is too thin or too soft lets the sacrum, the heels, and the shoulder blades press almost directly onto the bed frame, and it is at those 4 or 5 points that pressure injury begins. A cover that is waterproof keeps fluids out but also keeps air in, so a mattress with a torn or wrinkled cover traps moisture and heat against the skin and multiplies the damage. The thickness and the load rating of the mattress must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the mattress must be matched to the bed frame and to the side rails, because the 3 are designed as a single system.

Pressure-relieving surfaces go beyond foam by changing the pressure under the patient over time. A static surface uses a material that flows, such as viscoelastic foam, gel, or air cells linked to each other, to spread the load over a larger area. An alternating pressure mattress is built from parallel air cells that inflate and deflate in cycles, usually of several minutes, so that any single patch of skin is loaded and then released; the physical principle is that even a short period of unloading restores blood flow to the tissue. A low-air-loss system is different: it keeps the cells inflated but forces air through small perforations to evaporate moisture from the skin, which addresses maceration rather than pressure alone. The inflation pressure, the cycle duration, and the alarm threshold on such a pump must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, because an under-inflated cell that touches the frame transfers the patient's weight straight to it.

The pump of a powered mattress is a small blower or compressor, and it is the part that fails first. Its filter clogs with lint and dust, its hose splits at the connector, and its alarm sounds when the pressure in the circuit falls below the set point, which usually means a leak, a disconnected cell, or a patient who has slid off the surface onto the frame. The characteristic alarm is therefore a low pressure tone, sometimes with a flashing light, and the correct response is to look for the leak rather than to silence the pump. The common operator error is to lay the mattress hose across the floor where it is stepped on, or to set the pump on the bed frame where it falls, and both produce the same low pressure alarm within hours. The filter change interval and the pressure setting must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A powered surface that has been running without its filter for a year is not a pressure-relieving device; it is a noisy mattress.

Stretchers and trolleys are beds designed around movement rather than rest. Their frames are lighter, their wheels are larger and often of different sizes at the 2 ends so that the trolley can be steered, and most carry a brake and a steering lock on separate pedals. They add a head-end section that raises for transport, side rails that fold flat, and a mattress that is thinner because it must be light enough for 2 people to lift. The common operator error is to push a loaded trolley at a run while a single staff member steers and nobody watches the patient's arms, which are the first thing to strike a doorframe. The safe working load of the trolley and the pressure of its hydraulic system must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A wheelchair is a chair on 2 large wheels and 2 small ones, and its physics are dominated by where the weight sits. The large wheels carry the occupant, the small casters steer, and the rear axle position decides how much effort the user needs to push and how easily the chair tips backward. Its brakes are not brakes in the vehicle sense; they are parking locks that press on the tire, and they do nothing to slow a moving chair, which is why a wheelchair on a slope must be controlled by hand or by an attendant. An examination couch is a fixed or adjustable table, often with a backrest and a leg rest, and its height is chosen so that the examiner can work at a comfortable angle, commonly between 70 and 90 centimeters, a dimension that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The common operator error with both devices is to ignore the weight limit, which on a standard wheelchair may be around 120 kilograms, a figure that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and to overload it without noticing the frame flex.

Weight limits are engineering statements, not suggestions, and the label on the frame is the place to find them. The rating refers to the whole load: the patient, the mattress, the pumps, the monitors, the traction frame, and anything else hung on the bed. A bed rated at 150 kilograms that carries a 130 kilogram patient, a 15 kilogram mattress, and 2 infusion pumps is already at or beyond its rating, and the failure that follows is a bent frame, a cracked weld, or a collapsing leg. The common operator error is to assume that a bed which has held a heavy patient before will hold another, and to skip the label because the bed looks substantial. The rating and the labeling convention for a given frame must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and where the label is missing, the bed should be treated as unrated until the manufacturer or a biomedical engineer can say otherwise.

Moving a patient is a problem in friction and leverage. The load is the patient's body, the lever is the staff member's spine, and the force needed rises steeply as the load is held farther from the body; that is why reaching across a bed to lift is far more damaging than lifting the same weight close to the chest. Friction between the patient and the sheet is what makes a slide so hard, and the whole family of transfer aids exists to reduce that friction or to change the direction of the effort. A sliding sheet, made of a smooth low friction fabric, lets 2 people move a patient who could not be lifted by 4, and a transfer board bridges the gap between bed and chair so that the patient slides rather than is carried. The common operator error is to pull a patient by the arms or by the shoulders, which loads a joint that is not designed to carry the body, instead of using the sheet and a coordinated count. The safe working load of every transfer aid must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A hoist changes the direction of the effort from horizontal to vertical, so that a motor and a sling carry the patient instead of the staff member's back. A mobile hoist is a wheeled frame with a mast, a boom, and a spreader bar from which the sling hangs, and a ceiling lift is the same mechanism fixed to a track above the bed, which removes the need to push a heavy frame across the floor. The sling is the critical part: it must match the patient's size and the lift's attachment points, and a sling that is too small, wrongly threaded, or worn at the seams can release its load without warning. The characteristic alarm is an overload tone or a low battery warning.. The common operator error is to leave a patient suspended in a sling while attending to something else, or to use a damaged sling because a replacement is not in the cupboard. The lifting capacity, the battery charging routine, and the sling inspection interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Because a bed is shared by a succession of patients, its cleaning is a clinical procedure and not a housekeeping afterthought. The frame, the rails, the handset, the crank handles, and the casters are touched constantly and carry organisms from the previous occupant, and the mattress cover is the surface with which a broken or moist skin is in contact for hours. Cleaning usually means detergent first to remove organic soil and then a disinfectant rated for the organisms of concern, applied for the contact time on its label, and the contact time itself must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The common operator error is to spray disinfectant onto the handset and the motor housing, where liquid enters the switches and causes corrosion or an electrical fault, instead of wiping a dampened cloth over a closed surface. A mattress with a compromised cover, or a foam core that has been wet, is a reservoir that no amount of surface cleaning will make safe.

Preventive maintenance for beds and handling equipment is mostly inspection, lubrication, and replacement of small parts. The biomedical technician or the maintenance officer checks the welds and the frame for cracks, tests each caster and brake, tightens the bolts that hold the sections and the rails, inspects the handset cable for cuts, and loads the actuator or the hydraulic ram to see whether it holds. Consumables include mattress covers, foam cores, air cells, pump filters and hoses, hoist slings, sliding sheets, transfer boards, caster wheels, brake pads, and the batteries in handsets and hoists. The maintenance interval is set by the manufacturer and by the intensity of use, and it must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol rather than assumed from the last workshop visit. The common operator error is to report a fault only when the bed stops working, so that a brake that needed adjustment for months becomes a fall, and a hose that needed a new filter becomes a burned out pump.

Beds fail in a small number of characteristic ways, and each has a signature. A manual bed whose backrest sinks under the patient has a worn ratchet or a slipping clutch. An electric bed that moves in a single direction has a failed limit switch or a broken wire in the handset. A bed that drifts on the floor has a brake that is not engaging, and a bed that veers sideways has a caster that has seized. A mattress that bottoms out under the sacrum has lost its foam resilience or its air pressure, and a pump that runs continuously without inflating the cells has a leak or a blocked filter. A hoist that descends slowly under load has a worn battery or a damaged actuator, and a hoist that will not lift at all may simply be on its emergency lowering function. Any pressure, angle, or load value used to diagnose or adjust these faults must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device returns to a patient.

Before a bed is given to a patient, the operator must walk around it and test what the patient will depend on. The brakes are applied on all 4 casters and the bed does not move when pushed. The side rails rise, lock, and descend smoothly, and the gap between rail and mattress is small enough that no body part can pass through. The mattress is the right size for the frame, its cover is intact, and the foam or the air cells have not collapsed. The bed is at the correct height for the patient to get in and out with his feet on the floor, and the call bell, the light, and the table are within reach. Any height, angle, or gap figure the check relies on must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and any fault found must be reported and the bed taken out of service rather than handed over with a warning.

In a mobile or frontier clinic the bed becomes an object that must be carried, improvised, or done without. The classic field stretcher is a canvas sheet stretched between 2 poles, with a second sheet or a blanket laid over it, and its whole value is that it is light, repairable with a needle, and can be carried by 2 people through a door or a forest track. A locally welded frame takes the opposite approach: a workshop copies the geometry of a hospital bed from steel tube, adds a hinged backrest and holes for the pins, and produces a device that is strong and cheap but has no standardization, no weight rating, and no manual. Its welds, its pin positions, and its tube thickness are the entire safety case, and they must be verified against the local protocol by a competent technician before it takes a patient. The characteristic failure is a welded joint that cracks at the point where the backrest hinge meets the frame, which happens suddenly and without any alarm to warn the staff. Where no frame exists, the rope bed is a woven platform of cord or webbing on a timber frame, and it is only as good as the knots and the timber that hold it.

The ground is the final field variant, and it must be described honestly, because a patient on a mat on the floor is receiving care on the oldest and least capable of all surfaces. A thin mat over earth or concrete concentrates pressure at the sacrum, the heels, and the hips within hours, and the lack of airflow and the inability to turn the patient easily make moisture and shear worse. There is no backrest, so the head cannot be raised for eating, breathing, or conversation, and there are no rails, so a confused patient rolls into whatever is beside him. There is also a matter of dignity: the height of a bed places a patient at the level of the caregiver's hands and eyes, and the floor places him below everyone who comes to help, which changes how he is spoken to and how often he is checked. Where the ground must be used, a thick mat or a stack of blankets, a regular turning schedule written on the wall, and a low platform of boards or bricks are the minimum that converts a floor space into something resembling a bed. Any turning interval or mat thickness chosen in the field must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and where neither exists, it must be set by the most experienced clinician present and recorded.

The field variant gives up precision, alarms, and the assumption of power, and it demands skill, inspection, and improvisation in return. Without motors there are no overload alarms, so the staff must judge the load by eye and by feel, and a frame that creaks under a heavy patient is a warning that only a person can hear. Without adjustable air cells there is no way to unload a pressure point except by turning the patient, so the turning schedule becomes the pressure-relieving device and the person who keeps it becomes the pump. The field kit that answers this is small: a canvas stretcher, a set of sliding sheets, a transfer board, and a rope or a set of straps for lifting. What the field configuration requires above all is that someone inspects it before every use, because nothing in it will announce its own failure.

Protecting the mover is as much a part of patient handling as protecting the patient. The injury that ends a career is rarely a single dramatic lift; it is a disc or a muscle damaged by years of small, awkward transfers, and it appears as back pain that the staff member works through until he cannot. A handling program therefore reduces the number of manual lifts by design: it puts a hoist or a ceiling lift within reach of every bed that needs it, it keeps sliding sheets and boards where they are used, and it trains staff in the specific moves their ward actually performs rather than in a general lecture. The common failure of such a program is equipment that is present but unusable, a hoist with a flat battery, a sling in the wrong size, or a board stored in a locked room, and staff who then lift by hand because it is faster. The load limits and the training requirements of any handling program must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

To summarize, the hospital bed is a machine for carrying a body, and every part of it answers a force. The frame and the casters carry the load and keep it still, the sections redirect gravity into the positions the patient needs, the mattress spreads the load below the pressure that closes capillaries, and the rails, the brakes, and the hoists stand between the patient and a fall. The electric bed adds motors, handsets, and batteries, and with them the alarms that say when an actuator is overloaded or a handset is unplugged, while the manual bed keeps the same geometry in a form that fails in ways a user can sometimes see. The powered mattress adds movement and airflow, and it brings a pump, a filter, and a low pressure alarm that must never be silenced without finding the leak. The field variants, the canvas stretcher, the welded frame, the rope bed, and the ground, do the same job with fewer parts and no alarms at all, which is why they demand more inspection and more turning, not less. In every setting the rule is the same: check the device before the patient depends on it, keep the weight inside the label, and treat every setting, angle, pressure, and load limit as a number to be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.
