# Chapter 45 — Obstetrics and Neonatology

Obstetric and newborn equipment answers three questions, and each rests on a different physical principle. The first is how the unborn child tolerates labor, answered with reflected sound, and the second is how the uterus contracts and how birth progresses, answered by pressure against a belt or by a trained hand. The third is whether the newborn can breathe and stay warm, answered by pressure, radiant heat, convection, and light. A listener who understands these principles recognizes a failing machine before it stops working.

The handheld Doppler holds a crystal that vibrates at 2 to 3 million cycles per second, to confirm against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Moving blood and moving valves reflect that sound at a slightly different frequency, so the device produces an audible whoosh and a rate. The probe is angled with gel to exclude air, and a flat battery is the characteristic failure, while the common error is to mistake the mother's pulse for the fetal heart. The cardiotocograph records the fetal heart rate and the contraction pattern together, so it is two instruments in one housing. One channel turns the Doppler signal into a rate, and the other uses a tocodynamometer, a strain gauge pressed against the fundus by a belt so that the changing curve of the abdomen becomes an electrical signal. The characteristic alarm is a loss of signal from a slipped belt or a dried probe, and the common error is to read a flat trace from slack belts as reassuring.

The trace is read as a baseline, a variability, accelerations, and decelerations. The baseline rate normally lies between 110 and 160 beats per minute, and variability of 5 to 25 beats per minute is reassuring, ranges to confirm against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Early decelerations mirror the contraction, variable decelerations fall and recover abruptly, and late decelerations begin after the peak and suggest a placenta short of oxygen. The characteristic alarm is a sustained rate below 100 beats per minute, a threshold to confirm in the same way, and the common error is to treat one number as a diagnosis. The external tocodynamometer measures only the changing shape of the abdominal wall, so it shows when a contraction happens but not how strong it is. An internal pressure catheter is a fluid-filled tube whose tip lies in the amniotic cavity, and it turns the pressure of that column into millimeters of mercury, though it can be used only after the membranes rupture and it carries the hazards of infection, bleeding, and perforation.

Fetal monitors are cleaned on the parts that touch the patient. The belts are wiped and hung to dry, because a damp belt becomes a reservoir of organisms, and each transducer face is cleaned with the agent the maker approves but never immersed or autoclaved, because water and heat destroy the crystal. Transducers, belts, cables, and leg plates are consumables whose replacement intervals must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The common error is to coil a cable tightly around the housing, which breaks the fine wires and produces an intermittent trace that looks like a problem with the fetus. A delivery bed is mechanics rather than electronics. The mattress sits on a frame raised and lowered by a hydraulic pump or an electric motor, and the backrest, head section, and leg supports adjust separately so that the mother can sit, lie back, or be placed with her legs in stirrups. A removable section at the foot opens a gap where the baby is received, and the hand grips and side rails support her between contractions, while the hydraulic pedal and the electric control must lower the patient quickly in an emergency.

The hazards of the delivery bed are mechanical. A bed whose brakes are off can roll while a woman pushes, and a frame that drops when a hydraulic seal fails can injure mother or attendant, while hooks, mattress gaps, and unlocked side rails are entrapment points. The schedule covers the seals or the motor and battery, the locking pins, and the brakes, with intervals to confirm against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The common error is to treat the bed as a storage shelf, which damages the mechanism and delays the next delivery. Forceps are two crossed levers whose fulcrum lies between the blades. Each blade curves to follow the head and the birth canal, and the shanks cross so that a pull on the handles becomes a controlled pull on the head, while an outlet forceps has a gentle pelvic curve, a mid-cavity instrument is longer, and a rotation forceps has a sliding lock for turning a head that faces the wrong way. The hazards are maternal tearing, nerve injury, and skull fracture, and the common error is to apply the blades blindly and then pull hard.

The vacuum extractor replaces grip with suction, and its physics is the difference between the pressure inside the cup and the pressure of the atmosphere. The cup is pressed against the scalp and a pump removes air beneath it, so the pressure difference holds the scalp and the skull, and a metal cup can rotate the head while a soft cup is kinder to the scalp but slips more easily. A typical working pressure is about 0.8 bar, roughly 600 millimeters of mercury below atmospheric pressure, to confirm against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Traction is applied only during a contraction and along the axis of the canal, and the characteristic sign of failure is the cup detaching with a pop, while the common error is to continue after a detachment. Bruising or blood beneath the scalp warns of a subgaleal hemorrhage, which can hide a large loss of blood inside a newborn and is a reason to stop. A perished seal or a cracked cup holds on the gauge but not on the head, and the common error is to use either forceps or vacuum as a substitute for a decision that should have been made earlier.

An episiotomy is a controlled cut, and the instruments must be sharp and complete. The scissors are blunt-tipped and angled, so the blade can be introduced without cutting the baby or the rectum, and the repair set holds a needle holder, toothed forceps, tissue forceps, scissors, and a retractor. The repair is a knot, because the suture brings tissue together without strangling it, and deeper layers close first, while the hazards are a needle stick, a retained needle or swab, and a repair drawn too tight. The common error is to count needles only at the end. Delivery instruments are reprocessed in the central sterile services department (CSSD) or in a small unit following the same logic, which begins with cleaning rather than sterilization. Blood, tissue, and dried secretion are removed first, because no sterilizing agent reaches a covered surface, so instruments are opened, disassembled, and washed before packing. The sterilizer then uses steam under pressure, and a typical cycle for wrapped instruments is 134 degrees Celsius for 4 minutes, to confirm against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, while the common error is to release a load that has not passed its tests.

Suction clears the airway of a newborn by pressure difference. A bulb syringe produces a weak suction by hand, a trap collects material in a bottle while wall suction works, and a mechanical unit may run from a foot pedal or a battery, while the pressure must be limited because the lining of a newborn airway is delicate. A typical limit is 80 to 100 millimeters of mercury, to confirm against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the hazards are a fall in heart rate, bleeding, and a collapsed lung, while the common error is to suction deeply and repeatedly after a normal birth. A radiant warmer heats a newborn by radiation. A heated element at the top emits infrared energy that crosses the air and is absorbed by the first surface it meets, the skin of the baby. The warmer is controlled by hand or automatically by a skin probe on the abdomen, and a typical servo setting holds the skin at about 36.5 degrees Celsius, to confirm in the same way, while its alarms announce a probe that has fallen off, a skin temperature that is too high, and a heater that does not respond. The common error is to leave a baby under it with the probe detached.

The bag and mask displace a known volume of gas. A self-inflating bag refills because its elasticity pulls it open, so it works even when no gas supply is connected, and a relief valve releases gas above a set pressure, typically 30 to 40 centimeters of water, to confirm against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A newborn bag holds roughly 240 milliliters, and the mask is chosen by size so that it covers mouth and nose without covering the eyes. The characteristic sign of a poor seal is no chest movement and no rise in heart rate, and the common errors are an adult bag and a forgotten oxygen supply. An oxygen blender mixes air and oxygen to any concentration between about 21 and 100 per cent, to confirm in the same way. Too much oxygen narrows the vessels of the immature eye and damages the lung. Typical saturation targets are about 60 to 65 per cent at one minute and about 85 to 95 per cent by ten minutes, to confirm in the same way, and the common error is to leave the blender at its default while a probe on the right hand watches the oxygen.

The resuscitation area is checked before every birth, because a device found empty has already failed. The check covers the warmth of the platform, the gas supply and blender setting, the suction with its pressure limit, the bag and masks in the sizes needed, and a bag that refills briskly when squeezed. The airway set holds a laryngoscope with a straight blade, tubes of 2.5, 3.0, and 3.5 millimeters of internal diameter, a stylet, a carbon dioxide detector, and suction catheters. Expired consumables, discharged batteries, and a missing probe are the failures a checklist prevents, and the common error is to assume that someone else will carry the equipment in. An incubator makes a warm, humid, quiet, oxygen-enriched environment for a baby who cannot hold its own temperature. Its physics is convection and conduction, because a fan moves air over a heating element and through a water reservoir, and the warmed air circulates inside a double wall that reduces loss to the room. Typical settings are an air temperature near 34 degrees Celsius and a relative humidity near 60 per cent, to confirm against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, while its alarms announce a high temperature, a low temperature, a probe failure, and a power failure. The common error is to leave the hood ajar, which destroys the environment the machine has built.

Incubator maintenance fights water, dust, and organisms. The reservoir is emptied and refilled with sterile water, because a standing pool of warm water grows bacteria and can cause pneumonia in the infant, and the humidifier and tubing are cleaned or replaced at the maker's interval. The air filter is washed or changed, the fan is listened to for noise, the skin probe is checked against a reference thermometer, and the heater and thermostat are verified, while the mattress, porthole sleeves, probes, and tubing are consumables whose replacement intervals must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The common error is to set the alarm limits so wide that they never sound. Phototherapy treats jaundice by turning bilirubin into a form that can be excreted, and its physics is the absorption of light by a molecule. Bilirubin in the skin absorbs light most strongly between about 460 and 490 nanometers, which is blue-green, to confirm against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the absorbed energy converts the pigment into water-soluble products that liver and kidney can clear. The source may be fluorescent tubes, a panel of light emitting diodes (LED), a halogen lamp, or a fiberoptic blanket, and the useful measure is not apparent brightness but irradiance, the power delivered per unit area in the blue part of the spectrum. A typical therapeutic irradiance is 30 microwatts per square centimeter per nanometer, to confirm in the same way, and the common error is to judge treatment by the color of the light.

Two things must accompany phototherapy. The eyes are covered with opaque shields fixed so that they cannot slip over the nose and obstruct breathing, and the shields come off at every feed and examination so that the eyes can be inspected. The baby loses water through the skin under the lamp, so feeding is increased, weight and urine output are followed, and the temperature is checked, while the lamps age and a tube that has run for its rated hours produces far less light than its label promises. An irradiance meter is the only honest check, and the common error is to hang the lamp at a convenient height without measuring the dose at the skin. Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) keeps a newborn's lungs open between breaths, and its physics is a pressure held in the airway that prevents the small air sacs from collapsing at every expiration. A gas is made to escape against a known resistance, and the pressure that builds behind it does the work. In the bubble system the expiratory limb sits under a measured depth of water, so a tube held 5 centimeters below the surface produces about 5 centimeters of water of pressure, to confirm against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A typical starting pressure is 5 to 6 centimeters of water, to confirm in the same way, and the common errors are a leaking mouth, oversized prongs, and a pressure raised to chase a low saturation.

Nasal continuous positive airway pressure depends on its interface and humidifier. The prongs are sized to fill the nostril without blanching it, and the cap and straps hold the interface without pulling the nose out of shape, while the position is changed and the skin inspected commonly every 4 hours, to confirm against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The nasal septum is the structure at risk, because steady pressure on the columella produces necrosis that heals slowly. The characteristic alarm is a low or high pressure signal from the driver, and the common error is to accept a large leak and raise the flow instead of refitting the interface. Weight and length are recorded at birth, and the instruments that measure them are easily fooled. A digital infant scale uses a load cell whose resistance changes as it bends under a weight, and it is zeroed before each use and checked with a known weight at an interval to confirm in the same way. A spring scale is more robust in the field and less precise, and both types must stand on a hard level surface, while a measuring board has a fixed head piece and a sliding foot piece and the baby lies with the legs straightened. The common error is to weigh a baby in a wet diaper and a heavy blanket without subtracting the tare.

A newborn needs a thermometer accurate at the low end of the scale. A thermistor thermometer measures the change in resistance of a small bead as it warms, and it is the reference method in the axilla, where it stays long enough for the reading to stop rising, while an infrared thermometer estimates temperature from the radiation emitted by skin and is fast but misled by a cold surface, a draft, or a probe held too far away. A low-reading thermometer confirms hypothermia, because an ordinary clinical thermometer may not register below 35 degrees Celsius, to confirm against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the common error is to accept a number that is still climbing. Keeping a newborn warm is a chain of small measures, and every link matters. The delivery room is warmed before the birth, the baby is dried at once with a warm towel while the wet towel is discarded, and a hat covers the head, the largest surface for heat loss. A skin-to-skin position on the mother's chest uses her body as the heater, and a typical delivery room temperature is 25 to 26 degrees Celsius, to confirm in the same way, while the characteristic warning is a temperature below 36.5 degrees Celsius, to confirm in the same way. The common error is to delay drying while attending to the mother.

The records are part of the equipment, and the obstetric record is where trace, drugs, times, and interventions meet. A partograph plots the dilation of the cervix, the descent of the head, the contractions, and the fetal heart rate against time, so that a labor which has stopped progressing appears as a shape on paper. An electronic health record (EHR) can display the same information continuously, but it brings its own failure modes, including a monitor that is not networked and a clock set wrongly. Every device here produces a number that a person must interpret, and the common error is to treat documentation as an administrative task to be finished after the clinical work. The field version begins with the clean delivery kit, assembled so that a birth can happen without a hospital. It contains a square of plastic sheeting, a bar of soap, two clean cloths, a sterile blade, two lengths of clean cord tie, and a pair of gloves. Its principle is hygienic rather than electronic, because the sheet separates the mother from the ground, soap and gloves break the chain of contact, and the blade and cloths keep the cord and the baby clean, while the common error is to open the kit days before the birth and store it in a damp place.

Where no kit exists, the same principles are assembled from what can be cleaned. A clean cloth or a piece of plastic serves as the surface, water that has been boiled and cooled is the cleanest fluid available, and a new razor blade that has been boiled cuts the cord. The attendant wears gloves if they exist and washes hands before and after if they do not, and the stump is kept dry and uncovered because traditional dressings are a common route to infection in a newborn. The common error is to reuse a blade or a needle, which passes blood-borne infection in both directions, and anything which cuts skin is used once and then disposed of safely. Kangaroo mother care is the field answer to the incubator, and its physics is the transfer of heat from one body to another by direct contact. A mother's chest is a warmer with a skin temperature near 37 degrees Celsius, to confirm against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and its feedback loop is a living nervous system rather than a thermostat. A baby held upright between the breasts, naked except for a hat and a diaper, loses far less heat than a baby in a cot, and the position steadies breathing and encourages breastfeeding. The limits are a mother who is ill or unavailable and a baby who needs suction or oxygen or light, and the common error is to interrupt the contact for every weighing and examination.

Fetal monitoring in the field uses a trained hand and ear. A Pinard stethoscope is a wooden or plastic trumpet pressed against the abdomen, and it carries the fetal heart directly to the ear without electronics, so it never needs a battery and never fails silently. A handheld Doppler with a charged battery extends the range, and a fetoscope with a headband frees both hands, while the trace of the cardiotocograph is given up entirely, so the field counts the rate before, during, and after a contraction commonly every 15 to 30 minutes in the active phase, to confirm against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The common error is to listen for a few seconds and report a rate that was never counted. The field vacuum extractor is a hand-pumped device with a reusable cup, and it gives up the electric pump and the pressure gauge of the hospital model. Because the pressure is felt rather than displayed, the discipline is to build it slowly and test the hold with a gentle pull before any real traction. Traction is used only with a contraction and only until the cup detaches or a set number of pulls is reached, to confirm in the same way. Manual vacuum aspiration works from the same negative pressure made by a hand pump, and the common error is to use a cup that is too small or a pump whose seal has hardened.

Field newborn resuscitation is built around warmth, airway, and a bag, in that order. A self-inflating bag with a newborn mask and no gas supply delivers room air, which is an acceptable starting gas for most newborns, while oxygen from a concentrator is added when it is available. A bulb syringe or a mucus trap provides suction, a cloth dried in the sun provides the towel, and the mother's chest provides the warmer, so resuscitation can be conducted with the baby on the mother. The common errors are to forget to dry and cover the baby while ventilating, to use a bag meant for an adult, and to keep squeezing without a second person checking the heart rate. Jaundice in a place with no phototherapy unit is treated with sunlight, and the physics is the same absorption of blue light, though the dose is uncontrolled. The baby is placed in indirect sunlight near a window, lightly dressed so that as much skin as possible is exposed, and turned at intervals so that all sides receive light, while direct midday sun is avoided because the skin burns and the baby overheats. The eyes are shielded with the same care as under a lamp, and a baby who becomes lethargic or refuses to feed is referred rather than exposed longer. A fiberoptic blanket running from a battery or a small solar panel is a better field answer where it is available, and the common error is to believe that a few minutes of sun equal a night under a therapeutic lamp.

Continuous positive airway pressure in the field uses a bubble circuit and an oxygen concentrator. The circuit is assembled from a nasal interface, a length of tubing, a bottle of sterile water, and a gas source, and the depth of the expiratory tube below the surface sets the pressure, typically 5 centimeters of water, to confirm against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The concentrator delivers a limited flow, so the blender that would control oxygen concentration is usually absent, and the only guide is a battery pulse oximeter with a newborn probe. The hazards are a pressure that drifts as the bottle is knocked or the water evaporates, a nose damaged by an ill-fitting interface, and a collapsed lung that is not recognized, and the common error is to leave the circuit for hours without measuring the depth. Reprocessing in the field is a choice among boiling, chemical disinfection, and single use. Boiling in clean water for a full 5 minutes kills most organisms but does not sterilize, because some spores survive, to confirm against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A chlorine solution is used for surfaces and for items that cannot be boiled, and its strength and contact time must be measured rather than guessed, while blades, needles, suture, and cord clamps are never reused. The common error is to add more chlorine to a solution that has lost its strength, because a disinfectant that still smells strong may be spent.

Field equipment survives when it is simple, repairable, and fed with the right consumables. A handheld Doppler needs a charged battery, a supply of gel, and a probe that is not dropped, while a suction unit needs clean tubing, a trap that is emptied and washed, and a filter that is changed. A radiant warmer needs a working probe, a spare element, and a power source that can carry its load. The spare parts worth carrying are those that stop a device completely, which are fuses, batteries, tubing, masks in two sizes, prong sets, probe covers, and a spare thermometer, and the common error is to stock a device without its consumables. The comparison comes down to what is measured and what is merely observed. The hospital has continuous cardiotocography, a servocontrolled warmer, a closed incubator with humidity, a metered phototherapy unit, a driven continuous positive airway pressure circuit, and a sterilization service, and each turns a clinical impression into a number. The field gives up all of that, and in exchange it gains portability, independence from mains electricity and piped gas, and the freedom to work in a house rather than a ward. What it demands instead is a trained attendant who counts the fetal heart, dries and covers the baby, keeps the cord clean, measures the water depth, and recognizes the signs that mean referral.

To summarize, the equipment of obstetrics and neonatology is a small family of instruments built on a few physical principles, which are reflected sound for the fetal heart, pressure for contractions and suction, levers and negative pressure for assisted birth, radiant heat and convection for warmth, and the absorption of blue light for jaundice. The hospital versions measure continuously and are supported by consumables, calibration, cleaning, and a sterile service, and they surround two patients with numbers that a trained person must still interpret. The field versions give up the monitor, the incubator, the metered lamp, and the driven circuit, and they demand instead clean hands, a warm cloth, skin-to-skin contact, a counted fetal heart, and a plan for referral. Every number named in this chapter, whether a pressure, a temperature, a wavelength, an irradiance, a sterilization cycle, or an alarm threshold, must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The safety of the mother and the newborn rests less on the machines than on the operator who checks them, cleans them, understands why they work, and notices the alarm, the leak, and the error before harm is done.
