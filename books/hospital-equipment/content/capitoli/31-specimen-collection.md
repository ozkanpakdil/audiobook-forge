# Chapter 31 — Specimen Collection

Specimen collection is the equipment and technique of the pre analytical phase, the part of the laboratory process that happens before any machine touches the sample. It is where most laboratory errors are born, because a result can be measured perfectly and still be wrong. The fault may lie in the tube, the volume of blood, the mixing, the warmth of the journey, or the label. This chapter treats those tools as physical objects, from the evacuated tube and the winged needle to the culture bottle, the swab, the specimen jar, and the insulated transport box. Every setting, volume, temperature, and time limit named here must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A specimen is not a fixed photograph of the patient but a living chemical system that keeps reacting after it leaves the body. Cells in a tube of blood consume glucose, and once the blood clots the serum no longer represents the plasma that circulated. Bacteria in urine multiply, potassium leaks out of cells and rises in the plasma, and clotting factors decay unless the calcium that drives clotting is bound. Warmth and delay accelerate every one of these changes, so the container, the additive, the fill volume, the storage temperature, and the transport time are part of the test itself. All of these must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The ordinary blood tube is an evacuated cylinder closed by a rubber stopper, and its working principle is a pressure difference. Most of the air is removed at manufacture, so when a needle already inside a vein pierces the stopper, atmospheric pressure drives blood into the lower pressure inside. That vacuum sets the volume the tube will accept, which is why it fills to a printed line and stops, and why a tube that has lost its vacuum through a cracked stopper or a hot store draws slowly or not at all. The stopper color is a code naming the additive inside, and the additive is the real difference between one tube and another. The vacuum, the additive expiry, and the fill line must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The additives are chemical tools chosen to preserve one property of the blood at the expense of others. A red stopper carries no additive or a fine clot activator powder and the blood clots so that serum can be tested, while a gold stopper adds a gel that traps cells below the serum when the tube is spun. A green stopper carries heparin, which blocks the cascade that builds a fibrin mesh, and a light blue stopper carries sodium citrate, which binds the calcium that clotting requires. A lavender or pink stopper carries ethylenediamine tetraacetic acid, which holds calcium more tightly still and preserves cell shape for counting, and a gray stopper carries fluoride and oxalate, which stop cells from consuming glucose. The additive and its concentration in each tube must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Because a needle passes through several stoppers, a trace of one additive can be carried into the next tube, and that trace is enough to corrupt a result. The order of draw prevents this cross contamination, and the tubes whose tests are most easily disturbed are filled first. Blood culture bottles come first, then citrate, then plain serum, then heparin, then ethylenediamine tetraacetic acid, and finally fluoride oxalate. The fill volume matters just as much, because a citrate tube is made for nine parts blood to one part citrate, so a half filled tube is over diluted and its clotting times are falsely prolonged. The order of draw, the fill volumes, and the acceptable tolerance must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The needle is a short steel tube with a slanted tip, and its behavior follows from fluid mechanics, because flow through a narrow tube falls steeply as the diameter shrinks. A fine needle fills a tube slowly, and pulling harder on a syringe across it tears red cells and releases their contents into the plasma. That change, called hemolysis, makes potassium appear high and makes many chemistry results unreliable. Diameter is stated in gauge, and a 21 gauge needle is common for routine adult collection, while a 23 gauge needle serves fragile veins at the cost of a slower draw. The holder grips needle and tube as one rigid assembly, and the gauge, the length, and the safety mechanism must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A winged set replaces the rigid assembly with flexible tubing, a small needle with two plastic wings, and a connector for a holder or a syringe. The wings let the collector steer the needle into a shallow or rolling vein, and the tubing can be taped flat so the needle stays still while tubes are changed. The price is dead space, because the blood filling the tubing never reaches the tube, and the first tube may be diluted by fluid already in the line. A small needle on a winged set attached to a vacuum tube can also hemolyze the sample, and the hazard is the needlestick, which loose tubing makes more likely. The gauge and the tubing volume must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Capillary collection is used when the patient is an infant, a small child, or an adult whose veins will not yield. A lancet makes a small incision in a bed of capillaries, and the drop that appears is a mixture of capillary blood with a little interstitial fluid and, if the site is squeezed, with fluid from the cells. Warmth brings blood to the surface, and the heel or the side of a finger is chosen to avoid bone and the dense nerve supply of the fingertip pad. The first drop is wiped away, and later drops are touched to the lip of a small tube rather than scooped, because scooping adds air and starts clotting. The lancet depth, the site, and the number of drops required must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The technique of the draw is part of the equipment, because a good tube handled badly produces a bad specimen. The tourniquet distends the vein and must be released as soon as blood flows, since one left in place for minutes concentrates cells and raises potassium. The site is cleaned and allowed to dry, and the vein is not touched afterward with a bare finger, which is the commonest source of skin contamination. The patient's identity is checked before the needle is uncovered, by asking the patient to state a name and comparing it with the wristband and the request. The tourniquet time, the number of attempts, and the identification routine must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A blood culture bottle is a sealed vessel of nutrient broth with a headspace of gas, and it is designed to be an incubator for whatever microorganisms the blood contains. It holds an anticoagulant, a medium that supports growth, and often a resin or charcoal material that absorbs the antibiotics the patient is receiving. The monitoring instrument watches for the physical signs of growth and raises a positive alert when it suspects that organisms are multiplying. The most important variable is the volume of blood placed in the bottle, because the number of organisms in adult blood is often extremely small, and the blood volume and the number of bottles in a set must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Skin preparation for a blood culture is more demanding than for an ordinary draw, because the culture will grow whatever enters the bottle, including the bacteria that live harmlessly on the skin. The site is cleaned with friction in a spiral from the center outward, and the agent is allowed to dry completely, because a wet site carries disinfectant into the bottle and a site wiped dry carries skin organisms back to the surface. The commonest operator error is to draw the culture through an existing intravenous line rather than a fresh site, which produces a false positive result. The second commonest is to take only one set, when two sets from separate sites are needed to separate true infection from contamination. The preparation agents, the drying time, and the number of sets must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A swab is a sampler rather than a container, and its job is to lift material from a surface and keep the organisms in it alive until they reach the laboratory. The transport medium is the part most often misunderstood, because a bacterial medium holds salts, a buffer, and sometimes charcoal to absorb substances that would kill the organisms, while a viral medium holds salts, protein, and antibiotics that suppress bacteria but keep viruses intact for the molecular test. A swab sent dry, in the wrong medium, or kept warm for a day delivers a dead or overgrown sample. The medium, the storage temperature, and the contact time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The urine container is a sterile screw topped jar, and much of what makes a urine result trustworthy happens before the jar is closed. A clean catch midstream specimen is the standard, because the first part of the stream washes the urethra and the middle part best reflects the bladder. The preservative depends on the test, since boric acid holds the bacterial population steady while an acid preservative stops chemical and hormonal change during a day of collection. The patient washes the genital area, begins urinating into the toilet, passes the container into the stream without touching the inside, and closes it before the stream ends. The preservative, the volume, and the maximum delay must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A stool container is a clean wide mouthed jar with a tight lid, and the parasitology pot is a smaller version that may contain a fixative. The physical state of the specimen matters here more than in almost any other test, because the motile forms of some parasites lose their shape within a short time, so a fresh warm specimen examined promptly is worth more than a preserved one examined late. The fixative, usually formalin, hardens the organisms so their form survives the journey and the concentration step. Stool must not be collected from toilet water or mixed with urine, and it must travel in a container that does not leak, because it carries a heavy load of infectious organisms. The fixative, the volume, and the maximum delay before examination must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A tissue pot is a wide mouthed container of formalin, and its purpose is to fix a fragment of tissue so that its structure survives the journey to the pathology laboratory. Formalin is a solution of formaldehyde gas in water, and it links the proteins of the tissue to one another, which stiffens the tissue and stops the enzymes that would otherwise digest it. The volume of formalin must be generous, because the fixative must diffuse from the surface to the center of the specimen, and a crowded pot leaves the middle unfixed and rotting. The container is filled only partly and closed firmly, because formalin vapor irritates the eyes and airways, and the handler wears gloves and eye protection and works with ventilation. The formalin concentration, the ratio of fixative to tissue, and the fixation time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A sputum container is a wide mouthed sterile jar with a screw cap, and its value depends entirely on whether the material inside is sputum from the airways or saliva from the mouth. Sputum is thick, coughed up from deep in the chest after several deep breaths, and carries the organisms and cells the test is looking for, while saliva is thin, watery, and full of mouth flora, so a specimen of saliva can give a result that means nothing. When a patient cannot produce sputum, a trained operator may induce it with a fine mist of salty water breathed from a nebulizer for a few minutes, though that produces a cloud of infectious droplets. It is therefore done in a well ventilated room or outdoors and away from other patients, and the operator wears a mask, eye protection, and a gown. The nebulizer concentration, its duration, and the required volume must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.


The transport box is the part of the chain easiest to neglect and hardest to improvise, and its physics are those of insulation and phase change. A rigid insulated box slows the movement of heat in both directions, and a cold pack holds the interior near a chosen temperature by absorbing heat as its contents change phase. A pack that has melted no longer holds that temperature, and a pack straight from a freezer can freeze the specimen against the wall, which bursts red cells and destroys the cells a count requires. A temperature logger or a simple maximum and minimum thermometer inside the box is the only honest record of the journey. The temperature range, the number of cold packs, and the monitoring method must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Every analyte has a stability, and that is why the laboratory writes a time limit beside each specimen type. Glucose in a tube without preservative falls steadily as the cells consume it, so a sample delayed for hours can suggest hypoglycemia in a patient whose blood sugar is normal. Potassium moves the other way, leaking out of cells so that a delayed sample can suggest hyperkalemia, and clotting factors decay so that a delayed coagulation sample can suggest a bleeding tendency. Bacteria in urine multiply and can turn a small colony count into a large one. The time limits are part of the measurement, and the acceptable interval between collection and analysis must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Labeling is where the specimen stops being a biological sample and becomes information about a named person, and the rule is that the label is applied at the bedside, in the presence of the patient, at the moment of collection. The label carries at least two independent identifiers, such as the full name and the date of birth or the hospital number, with the date, the time, and the collector's identity, written in waterproof ink or printed from the hospital system. The commonest operator error in the whole chapter is to write labels in advance or carry a batch of pre labeled tubes to the bedside, which inverts the logic of identification. A specimen that arrives unlabeled, mismatched, or labeled after the collector left the bedside must be discarded and a new specimen obtained, however precious it is. The identifier fields, the labeling materials, and the discard rule must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The request form or its electronic equivalent carries the clinical question, and without it a laboratory can report only numbers without meaning. In a hospital with a laboratory information system, the request is entered once and a barcode is printed that the analyzer reads directly, which removes the transcription step and a whole class of error. The characteristic alarm here is the barcode that will not read at the analyzer, which stops the specimen on the track until a person decides what it is, and a second is the rejection flag for a short or clotted tube. Before dispatch the collector confirms the fill line, the gentle mixing, the label, the matching request, and the absence of clots or hemolysis, and all of these criteria must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Specimen collection is one of the most hazardous tasks in the hospital, because it places a sharp instrument into a patient who may carry a blood borne infection and then places the collector's hands near that instrument. Gloves are worn for every draw, with an apron and eye protection when a splash is possible, and the defenses are backed by vaccination against hepatitis B and a written procedure for after a needlestick. The sharp is never recapped by hand, bent, or left on a tray, but dropped into a puncture resistant container placed within reach before the needle is uncovered. The container is closed and replaced before it reaches its marked fill line. The fill line, the protective equipment, and the post exposure procedure must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Cleaning is part of specimen collection, because the same trays, tourniquets, and work surfaces pass from one patient to another. The tourniquet is the object most often forgotten, since it touches the skin of every arm and is rarely washed, and it should be cleaned between patients or dedicated to one patient when an infection is present. Trays and holders are wiped with a compatible disinfectant, and blood spills are covered with absorbent material, flooded with fresh bleach solution, left for the stated contact time, and removed as clinical waste. The hands are washed or rubbed with an alcohol preparation before and after every patient contact, and gloves do not replace that step. The cleaning agents, their contact times, and the surfaces that tolerate them must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Maintenance of collection equipment is mostly a matter of time and temperature, and it is unglamorous work that prevents a great deal of waste. The refrigerator that holds specimens and reagents is checked against a thermometer twice a day and the reading is written down, and the characteristic alarm of such a unit is a temperature alarm that must be answered rather than silenced. The bench centrifuge is leveled, its buckets are loaded in balance, and its lid interlock is checked, because an unbalanced centrifuge vibrates, walks across the bench, and can release a cloud of aerosol. The stock of tubes and media is rotated so the oldest is used first, and the vacuum in each box is sampled occasionally, because a tube that has lost its vacuum fails at the bedside. The thermometer check interval, the temperature range, and the centrifuge service schedule must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The consumables of this chapter are cheap individually and expensive collectively, and one missing item can stop a clinic. They include the tubes of every additive and size, the needles in their several gauges, the holders, winged sets, lancets, and capillary tubes, and the blood culture bottles with their broth. There are the swabs and transport media, the sterile urine jars with and without preservative, the stool and parasitology pots with fixative, the tissue pots and formalin, and the sputum jars. There are the cleansing agents, alcohol wipes, iodine or chlorhexidine applicators, gauze, tape, bandages, and gloves. There are the labels, barcode stock, request forms, transport bags, cold packs, insulated boxes, temperature loggers, sharps containers, and the spill kit, and each has an expiry date or shelf life that must be checked before use.

In a mobile or frontier clinic the pre analytical phase is the part of the laboratory that survives, because it needs no electricity, no analyzer, and no specialist. All the chemistry of the tubes, the sterility of the containers, and the arithmetic of the fill volumes still apply, but the supporting infrastructure is missing, since there may be no refrigerator, no centrifuge, no incubator, and no courier other than a person on a motorcycle. A specimen carried on a motorbike can still save a life, and the same specimen can arrive warm, hemolyzed, or mislabeled and waste the only chance the patient had. What the field gives up is redundancy and certainty, and what it demands instead is discipline, planning, and a written record of every step. The storage conditions, transport times, and substituted containers used in the field must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The commonest field collection device is the ordinary syringe, because it works without vacuum and can be controlled by hand on a vein that a vacuum tube would collapse. The operator draws slowly, watching the barrel, and stops before the syringe is full, because pulling hard on the plunger across a small needle bursts red cells and releases potassium into the plasma. The blood is then transferred into a tube, and that step loses the most, because pushing blood through the drawing needle hemolyzes it, so the safer method is to remove the needle, cap the syringe, and use a transfer device or a gentle gravity transfer. If the tube contains an additive, the operator must fill it to the proportion the laboratory expects, because a tube filled by eye underfills the anticoagulant tube and distorts a clotting time. The syringe size, needle gauge, transfer method, and fill proportions must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Where the laboratory is days away and no cold chain exists, one useful field format is the dried blood spot, in which a few drops of blood are soaked into a card of absorbent filter paper and allowed to dry in the air. The physical principle is that the cells dry onto the fibers and the proteins and genetic material of parasites and viruses stay bound to the paper, so a laboratory far away can later punch out a small disc and elute the material into a liquid. The card must be filled with free flowing drops that soak through to the far side, must not be touched in the printed area, and must dry horizontally away from sunlight and insects before it is bagged with a desiccant pack. The drying time, the storage temperature, and the permissible interval before the card reaches the laboratory must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The field cold chain is built from a vaccine carrier or a similar insulated box, a set of phase change packs, and a thermometer, and it succeeds or fails on the temperature inside rather than the appearance of the container. The thermometer is read at every handover and written in the log, so the receiving laboratory knows the temperature history and not merely the temperature at arrival. The hazard of the field journey is the long delay and the rough road, and the commonest operator error is to pack too little insulation or too many specimens for the packs to hold. The number of packs, the box loading, and the temperature log must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

What the field gives up is the backup specimen, the printed label, and the refrigerator, and what it demands instead is a set of habits a hospital can leave to the system. Since there is no second tube, the draw must be right the first time, and the collector decides before the needle is uncovered which tests matter most and which tube is filled first. Since there is no label printer, the label is written by hand at the bedside in waterproof ink with two identifiers, the time, and the collector's name, and the same details go into a bound specimen register. The label fields, the register contents, and the transport plan must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

In the field the safety hazards of collection are sharper because the defenses are thinner, and naming them is part of using the equipment. A needlestick in a clinic without a nearby supply of post exposure treatment is a life changing event, so the sharp is handled in one direction only, into a puncture resistant box improvised from a rigid sealed container if no commercial one exists. The characteristic alarms of this chapter are the temperature alarm of a refrigerator or transport logger, the imbalance alarm of a centrifuge, the positive alert of a blood culture instrument, and the rejection flag of an analyzer for a short or clotted tube. The protective equipment available, the post exposure procedure, and the alarm responses must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

To summarize, specimen collection is the pre analytical equipment of the laboratory, and its governing idea is that a specimen continues to change from the moment it leaves the patient, so the tube, the additive, the fill volume, the temperature, and the time are all part of the measurement. The evacuated tube works by pressure difference and is coded by stopper color, the additives preserve one property at the expense of others, the order of draw prevents cross contamination, and the fill line protects the ratio of blood to anticoagulant. Blood culture bottles are incubators whose sensitivity rests on the volume of blood and the cleanliness of the skin, swabs are samplers that must reach the right surface and travel in the right medium, and urine, stool, sputum, and tissue each demand their own container, preservative, and time limit. The label is applied at the bedside with two identifiers and an unlabeled specimen is discarded, while the transport box with its cold packs and its temperature log is the last defense of the result. The field substitutes a syringe, a hand labeled tube, a dried blood spot card, and a motorbike for that infrastructure, giving up redundancy and refrigeration and demanding a planned journey, a written register, and a draw that is right the first time. Every volume, temperature, time limit, and alarm threshold named here must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.
