# Chapter 11 — Syringes, Needles, and Injection Devices

A syringe is a pump worked by the human hand, and a needle is the hole through which its output escapes. Together they are the smallest delivery system in the hospital and the one used most often, because almost every drug that cannot be swallowed must be pushed through the skin or into a vein. This chapter describes the physical act of injection before the clinical habits that surround it, then takes the hospital-grade syringe, needle, and injection device apart piece by piece. It then turns to the field clinic, where the same act is performed with fewer devices, more reuse, and a much narrower margin for error, and where a single contaminated needle can undo a year of careful work. The safety hazard, the common operator error, and the characteristic warning sign are named with each device rather than gathered at the end.

Pressure applied to a liquid that fills a closed space is transmitted through it in every direction without appreciable loss, a principle named for Blaise Pascal. A syringe exploits this by giving the hand a large flat plunger to push and giving the fluid a very small hole to escape through. The force of the thumb is spread over the area of the plunger, producing a modest pressure in the barrel, and that same pressure acts on every square millimeter of the needle channel. The volume that leaves the needle is exactly the volume the plunger displaces as it travels down the barrel, which is why the graduation printed on the barrel can be trusted as a measure rather than a guess. Nothing about the injection is mysterious, because the device converts a small force over a large area into a large force over a small area, and the hand that understands this pushes steadily instead of jerking. Any pressure, volume, or dose setting named in this chapter must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The rate at which fluid leaves a needle is governed ferociously by the width of its channel. Flow rises with the fourth power of the internal radius, so doubling the diameter of the channel multiplies the flow for the same pressure by roughly 16, and halving it divides the flow by the same enormous factor. Length works the other way, because a longer needle resists flow in direct proportion to its length, and the thickness of the fluid matters as well, which is why a thick oily preparation is far harder to push than salt water. In practical terms this explains why a 21 gauge needle empties a syringe in a few seconds while a 27 gauge needle of the same length demands patience and a firmer thumb. It also explains the danger of forcing a plunger, because when the needle is blocked or buried in tissue the pressure rises inside the barrel, the hub may separate from the tip, and the joint fails under the hand. Needle gauge, needle length, and the fluid being given must all be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The syringe itself is a clear barrel of known cross-section, a plunger with a soft elastomer seal at its tip, a flange at the top for the fingers, a graduated scale along the side, and a tip at the bottom that receives the needle. The seal is what makes the device a pump, because it must slide easily and still resist the pressure of injection, and a seal that has dried, swollen, or been lubricated with the wrong substance will leak at the moment of use. The tip comes in 2 main forms. A luer slip is a smooth taper that grips the needle hub by friction alone, and a luer lock adds a threaded collar that screws the hub down so the joint cannot separate under pressure. The taper is a precision fit made to a fixed angle, and mixing parts from different makers defeats it, so the pairing of a syringe and a needle must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Volumes run from 1 milliliter for a fine dose through 2, 3, 5, 10, and 20 milliliters to 50 milliliters for irrigation and aspiration. The rule of thumb is to choose the smallest syringe that holds the dose, because a wide barrel magnifies every error of the thumb.

A syringe never delivers quite everything it holds, because a small volume remains in the tip and in the hub of the needle after the plunger reaches the end of its travel. This dead space is a real amount of drug, on the order of a few hundredths of a milliliter in a conventional device and much less in a low dead space design that narrows the tip and fills part of the hub. The difference matters for expensive medicines, for vaccines where every dose must count, and above all for the small volumes given to infants, where the retained amount can be a visible fraction of the intended dose. A prefilled syringe avoids the problem differently, because the manufacturer fills and seals the barrel, and the operator has only to expel the air and inject. Air in the barrel should be cleared before injection by holding the device upright and advancing the plunger until a bead of liquid appears at the bevel, and the volume of that clearing step must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A needle is a hollow steel tube cut at an angle at one end and fixed into a molded hub at the other. The cut end, the bevel, is ground in facets so that the tip is sharp and the opening faces the tissue being entered, and the sharpness of that geometry, not the diameter of the shaft, decides how much force the skin demands. The hub carries a color that follows an international convention keyed to gauge, and the convention is useful for sorting a drawer but must never replace reading the label, because makers differ and a color remembered wrongly becomes the wrong needle. Gauge is a measure of the outside diameter of the shaft, and the numbers run backwards, so a 30 gauge needle is far finer than a 14 gauge needle. The finest gauges, from 30 down to 27, belong to intradermal and subcutaneous work, including insulin; the middle range, from 25 to 21 gauge, suits most intramuscular and intravenous injections; and the wide bores, from 20 down to 14 gauge, are reserved for rapid fluid, blood, and the aspiration of thick material. Every gauge and length mentioned here must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Length is chosen as carefully as gauge, because the needle must reach its target layer without passing through it. Subcutaneous injections use short needles of roughly 6 to 16 millimeters, intramuscular injections in an adult use 25 to 40 millimeters, and a patient with thick subcutaneous fat may need a longer needle than the standard one to reach muscle at all. The bevel must face upward, toward the surface of the skin, because that orientation lets the point enter cleanly and the opening glide under the skin rather than tear it. Turning the bevel downward is one of the most common operator errors, and it produces a painful injection, a nick in the tissue, and sometimes a failed puncture. The needle must also be inspected before use for a bent shaft, a burr on the bevel, or a loose hub, since a bent needle tracks a curved path and a burred point tears as it enters. Needle length, insertion angle, and the choice of bevel orientation must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Several needles exist for work that never touches a patient. A filter needle contains a small mesh inside its hub that traps glass fragments when a drug is drawn from a glass ampoule, and it is used for the drawing step only, because the filter would blunt the therapeutic dose if the same needle were used to inject. A blunt fill needle has no cutting bevel and is used to draw from a rubber-stoppered vial, where a sharp point would slice a core of rubber that then floats in the solution. That coring is a genuine hazard, and its characteristic sign is a small dark speck in the fluid or a fragment lying against the wall of the syringe, so the solution must be inspected again after drawing. A vented needle or a vented vial adapter lets air into the vial as fluid leaves, which equalizes the pressure and prevents the frothing and the spray that otherwise soak the stopper. Reconstitution devices and vial adapters go further by joining 2 vials, or a vial and a diluent container, so that the transfer needs no exposed needle at all. Filter pore size, adapter compatibility, and the technique for each device must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Safety-engineered needles exist because the moment of greatest danger is the moment between the injection and the disposal. Their designs differ but their principle is the same, because after the injection the sharp is either drawn back inside the barrel, covered by a sleeve that slides forward, or folded behind a hinged shield, so that no hand ever approaches the point. A needleless system removes the sharp altogether by placing a smooth valve or a blunt connector on the line, allowing drugs to be drawn and injected through a closed pathway. Protection is routinely defeated by habit. Operators still recap needles two-handed, they leave the safety device unactivated because it slows the case, they push the shield only partway so that the point remains exposed, or they remove a needle from a syringe before disposal so that the sharp travels loose. The characteristic sign is the absence of the audible click or the visible lock that tells the operator the shield has engaged, and any device that does not click has protected no one. Activation technique and disposal requirements for each safety device must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A needlestick injury transfers a small volume of blood directly into the tissues of the worker, which is why it is treated as a serious exposure rather than a minor cut, and why human immunodeficiency virus, hepatitis B, and hepatitis C are the infections that shape practice around it. The immediate response is to wash the wound, report it, and follow the local exposure protocol, but the better answer is that the injury should not happen at all. The sharps container is the device that prevents it, and its essential property is that it sits at the point of use, within arm's reach of the injection, so that no one carries an exposed needle across a room. The container is puncture resistant, leak proof, closable, and marked with a fill line, and it must be replaced when it reaches about three quarters full rather than pressed down or shaken to make more space. Nothing is ever retrieved from a sharps container by hand, and a container that is overfilled or left open is a hazard in its own right. Fill levels, closure requirements, and the disposal chain must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The insulin pen is a syringe made convenient, and it is a good example of how a device succeeds by rearranging the same physics. A spring-loaded or screw-driven piston in the pen advances a cartridge that holds several hundred units of insulin, and a dose dial on the end clicks as it is turned so that the patient can hear and feel the amount selected. The needle is a short, very fine one, usually in the 4 to 8 millimeter range, and it screws onto a threaded collar over the rubber septum of the cartridge. It is changed for every injection, because a reused pen needle is dull, contaminated, and a path for infection. The pen fails in particular ways. A blocked or bent needle leaves the dose inside the cartridge while the dial still clicks, and the characteristic warning sign is the absence of a bead of insulin when the operator performs the priming step before injection. An air bubble, a partly used cartridge, or a dial turned the wrong way can produce a dose that is small or absent. Unopened cartridges are stored cold, usually between 2 and 8 degrees Celsius, while a pen in use is kept at room temperature for a limited number of days, and both the storage temperature and the in-use period must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. One pen belongs to one patient, because blood can travel backward into the cartridge.

An autoinjector is a spring-loaded syringe that fires its needle and delivers its dose in one action, and it exists for the emergency in which the patient or a bystander cannot manage a conventional injection. The device holds a prefilled syringe, a compressed spring, and a trigger, and pressing the device against the thigh releases the spring so that the needle enters and the drug is pushed through automatically. Its characteristic sounds are a click at the moment of firing and often a second signal when the injection is complete, and some devices also show a color change in a window that confirms the dose has left. The common operator errors are to fire the device into the wrong site, to fire it through clothing when the design requires bare skin, to remove it immediately instead of holding it in place for the full dwell time, and to place the thumb over the trigger end of a device held backwards. Dwell time, injection site, and storage temperature must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. An autoinjector that has been frozen or overheated may fail silently at the exact moment it is needed, so its storage and its expiry deserve the same attention as the drug inside it.

The syringes made for pumps are the same object refined to a tolerance that a hand does not need. A pump drives the plunger with a motor that knows the exact cross-section of the barrel, so it can compute a volume from the distance it travels, and for that calculation to be true the barrel must be straight, the plunger must move with even friction, and the device must recognize the syringe size loaded into it. A syringe that is too small, too large, or of a type the pump does not recognize will produce a false rate, and a modern pump displays a syringe recognition error rather than proceed. The characteristic alarms of a syringe pump are the occlusion alarm, which sounds when the pressure in the line exceeds a set limit, and the air-in-line or near-empty alarm, which sounds as the syringe empties. The common operator error is to silence an occlusion alarm and increase the rate instead of looking for the clamped tube or the displaced cannula that caused it. Occlusion pressure limits, alarm thresholds, and the accepted syringe list must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The reuse of a needle or a syringe is a practice that survives wherever supply is uncertain, and it fails for physical reasons that no amount of care can remove. A needle is sharpened to a fine bevel that dulls with a single passage through skin, and a resharpened point cannot reproduce the original facets, so a reused needle demands more force, causes more pain, and tears rather than cuts. The lumen of a fine needle is a long narrow channel that cannot be brushed, so blood and tissue remain inside it even after soaking, and the plastic hub and the barrel have crevices that no rinse reaches. Heat does the rest of the damage, because the temperatures that kill organisms also deform plastic, soften the seal, and cloud the graduation. Sterilization therefore offers a device that is neither clean nor accurate, and prions and some resistant organisms survive cycles that would ordinarily be adequate. The older reusable glass syringe with its ground glass plunger and metal needle is still found in some settings, and its parts can indeed be autoclaved, but the ground joint wears with every cycle until the fit leaks and the needle lumen remains as unreachable as ever. The correct rule is that a needle and a syringe are single-use devices, and any decision to reprocess one, together with the sterilization cycle, must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The consumable side of this chapter is a discipline of checking rather than repairing. Before an injection the operator confirms that the packaging is intact and dry, that the sterile indicator has changed as it should, that the expiry date has not passed, and that the drug in the syringe matches the order in both name and dose. The syringe is inspected for a cracked barrel, a plunger that sticks or moves too freely, a legible scale, and a tip that holds the needle without wobble. The needle is inspected for a straight shaft, an unbent bevel, and a hub that seats fully, and the injection site is inspected for the skin condition that decides the technique. The parts that touch the patient only indirectly, the pen body, the pump cradle, and the connector valve, are wiped between patients with the disinfectant named in the local protocol. Stock is stored dry, out of sunlight, and within a temperature range the manufacturer defines, and it is rotated so that the oldest batch is used first. Sharps containers are checked on every shift for fill level and closure, and any container that is cracked is replaced rather than taped. Storage temperature, expiry intervals, and batch rotation rules must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The failure modes of the injection set are quiet, and most of them are visible to a careful eye. A plunger seal that has lost its lubrication drags and then releases in a jump, delivering a bolus instead of a steady push. A barrel that has been cracked by freezing leaks at the flange, and the leak is discovered only by the wet glove. A luer joint that has been cross-threaded holds until the pressure rises and then separates, spraying the contents. A needle pushed through a stopper without a fill needle carries a rubber core into the tissue. A syringe loaded upside down in a pump is recognized as the wrong size or delivers at the wrong rate. The characteristic alarms of the powered devices, the pump occlusion tone and the silent failure of a pen to prime, are the sounds and the silences that tell the operator something has gone wrong. Any pressure limit, alarm threshold, or rate setting associated with these failures must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

In a field clinic the disposable syringe is the whole delivery system, and it arrives through a supply chain that may fail for months at a time. The result is that the same device is asked to do more, and the discipline that keeps it safe is stated as one needle, one injection, one disposal. That sentence is not a slogan but a chain of custody, because a needle is taken from a sealed package, used once, and placed immediately into a container, with no step in which it is set down on a tray, tucked into a pocket, or handed to another person. Where immunization programs cannot rely on this discipline, they use an auto-disable syringe, whose plunger locks at the end of the stroke so that the device cannot be used a second time even by accident. The World Health Organization recommends such devices for immunization for exactly this reason. The supply, the storage, and the disposal of every one of these devices in a field setting must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Where disposable devices run out, the field does what it can, and most of what it can do is not sterilization. Boiling a needle in water at 100 degrees Celsius kills many organisms but leaves the lumen uncleaned, dulls the point, and does nothing about the blood inside the hub. Passing a needle through a flame chars the residue and destroys the temper of the steel, so that the point bends on the next insertion. Soaking in bleach corrodes the metal and leaves a residue that is painful in tissue, while the cleaning that must precede disinfection is exactly the step that a narrow needle makes impossible. Sharpening a needle on a stone can restore a point that a finger can feel, but it cannot restore the shape of the bevel, and the resharpened instrument tears rather than cuts. The improvised sharps container of a field clinic is often a sturdy plastic bottle or a sealed metal tin, and its value depends entirely on its being kept closed, kept away from children, and carried to a safe disposal site when full. Any boiling time, chemical concentration, or reprocessing method considered in the field must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Storage in the field is the part of the injection chain that fails first, because heat and freezing destroy the drug before the device ever reaches the patient. Insulin and most vaccines must be held cold, and a vaccine that has been frozen by an ice pack pressed against the vial may look normal and be useless, which is why a freeze indicator or a thermometer in the carrier matters more than the container itself. Multi-dose vials exist for economy in mass campaigns, and they are safe only when each entry uses a new sterile needle, the septum is disinfected before every puncture, and the vial is discarded at the interval the manufacturer sets. The shared vial and the shared needle are the 2 ways in which a campaign spreads infection rather than preventing it. Where large numbers must be injected quickly and supplies are scarce, needle-free jet injectors push a fine stream of liquid through the skin at high pressure, which removes the sharp but introduces the problem of the reusable nozzle and the contamination it can carry between patients. Cold chain temperature, multi-dose vial expiry after opening, and nozzle disinfection must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The operator checks in the field are the same as in the hospital, with the safety net of a supervisor and a maintenance department removed. The drug is read aloud against the order, and the label is read a second time by whoever is present, because the vial that looks like another vial is the classic error of a busy clinic. The dose is drawn in front of the patient wherever the patient can see it, the needle is shown to be new before the skin is prepared, and the sharp goes into the container before anything else is touched. The site is chosen with the same care as in a hospital, and the patient is watched for a few minutes afterward, because a reaction to an injection can begin within seconds and the field clinic may be far from help. Where an autoinjector is the emergency device on the shelf, its expiry and its storage temperature are checked as often as the drugs beside it, since a device that has been cooked in a vehicle will not fire as designed. Every dose, expiry date, and observation period named here must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

To summarize, the syringe is a hand pump that turns a small force into a precise volume, and the needle is a precision channel whose diameter, length, and bevel decide how the dose enters the body. The hospital-grade set adds engineered safety, color-coded gauges, filter and fill needles, vial adapters, pens, autoinjectors, and pump syringes, each with its own alarm or characteristic sign, and each depending on consumables that must be in stock before the patient needs them. The field set gives up the engineered safety and the redundancy of supply, and demands in their place an unbroken discipline of one needle, one injection, one disposal, backed by cold storage, honest record keeping, and a sharps container that is never filled past its line. The hazards are the same everywhere, the needlestick, the reused sharp, the wrong dose, and the injection into a blocked or unsterile site, and the defense is the same as well, because the operator who understands the physics, inspects the device, reads the label, and confirms every setting against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use is the one who prevents them.
