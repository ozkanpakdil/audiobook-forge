# Chapter 76 — Regulation, Standards, and Quality

A device reaches a patient only after passing through rules that decide whether it may be sold, what it must prove, and who may repair it. Those rules exist because a leakage current, a burst pressure, and a sterilization temperature are measurable facts. A regulation is the agreement that somebody measures those facts before a patient is exposed. Every setting and threshold named in this chapter must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before it is relied upon.

A regulation is law, published by a government or by a delegated body, and it carries a penalty that can reach the withdrawal of a product. The authority registers devices and their makers, inspects factories, samples products, and can order a recall. A directive sets a required result and leaves each country to write its own law. A standard is a voluntary technical document that becomes binding only when a regulation refers to it.

Approval rests on the principle that a safety claim is a measurable quantity. Insulation is tested by raising the voltage across it until breakdown, and earthing by measuring the resistance of the earth path. A housing is dropped, a seal pressurized, a tube pulled apart, and a package held at a controlled temperature and humidity. Each test yields a number, and the approval file is the record of those numbers.

Classification is the regulator's first sorting decision, because the evidence demanded rises with the risk. A tongue depressor may need only registration and a truthful label. An infusion pump or a dialysis machine usually needs examination by an independent conformity assessment body. The highest class covers devices that sustain life, that are implanted, or that can kill when they fail.

Approval follows one of several pathways, each giving the object a different degree of scrutiny. A low-risk device may be self-declared by its maker. An intermediate one may be cleared by showing equivalence to a device already marketed. The strictest one needs clinical data, a factory inspection, and a renewable certificate, and its class and pathway must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before purchase.

For the hospital, the practical output is a bundle of paper that the buyer should demand and keep. It includes the certificate, the declaration of conformity, instructions in a language the staff read, the service manual, and the name of the local representative. A device that arrives without this bundle is not a bargain, because the hospital inherits obligations nobody else will meet. The documents and the retention period must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before acceptance.

Traceability is the thread that runs from the factory to the patient and back again. A maker assigns a lot number to a production run and a unique identifier to many items, and the hospital records that number at the point of use. For a consumable the log sheet is what allows a contaminated batch to be found before it reaches anyone else. For an implant the record goes into the patient's file, so a failure found years later is traced to the exact device.

The characteristic failure of traceability is a missing number rather than an alarm. When the lot is not recorded, a recall cannot find the patient who received the batch. The common operator error is to leave the lot field blank because the ward was busy. The same omission hides a failed sterilization cycle and fluid stored too warm.

Standards are easier to hold in mind when they are grouped into families. The first is electrical safety, which governs insulation, earthing, leakage current, and alarms. The second is gas and medical supply, which governs cylinders, valves, connectors, regulators, and the identity of the gas. The third is sterility and biocompatibility, and the fourth is quality management, with risk management and usability supplying the reasoning the others assume.

The electrical safety family is written so that a device can be tested on a bench. The general standard for medical electrical equipment requires protection against electric shock and separation between the mains circuit and any part a patient can touch. Collateral standards cover electromagnetic disturbance, radiation, and alarms, while particular standards add tests for each device type. The test values and the retest interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before a device is passed.

The gas and medical supply family exists because a cylinder is a pressure vessel and a connector is a safety device. Its standards fix the shape of cylinder valves and the pin index arrangement that prevents an oxygen regulator from fitting a nitrous oxide cylinder. They also fix the color and label of a cylinder, the pressure rating of a pipeline, and the terminal unit at the bedside. The valve types, colors, pressure ratings, and flow ranges must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before a cylinder is connected.

The sterility family asks whether a device can be reliably freed of living organisms. The answer is proved by validating a process against a known bioburden and expressing the result as a probability of a surviving organism, called the sterility assurance level. That validation is physical, because the maker measures temperature, pressure, humidity, and duration at the coldest point of the load. The parameters and the sterility assurance level must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before a process is adopted.

The biocompatibility part of the family asks whether materials that touch the body cause harm. It is tested by extracting substances from the material and exposing cells and living tissue to them. The search is for toxicity, irritation, and sensitization, and in blood-contacting devices for effects on clotting and red cells. A material that passes one test can still fail another, which is why the intended contact matters.

The quality management family governs the maker rather than the object. The central standard requires a documented system covering design, purchasing, production, inspection, storage, servicing, complaints, and corrective action. Its purpose is not paperwork for its own sake, but the ability to reproduce a safe device and to find the cause when one fails. A factory that cannot trace its components cannot investigate its defects, and the procedures and audit interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the system is trusted.

A separate standard governs risk management and supplies the reasoning the other families assume. The process identifies every foreseeable hazard, including electric shock, mechanical injury, contamination, radiation, and use error, and estimates the severity and probability of each. Controls are applied in a fixed order, which is to remove the hazard by design, then to guard against it, then to warn, and only last to rely on training. A warning is the weakest control invented, and the acceptability criteria must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the risk file is accepted.

Standards also govern how a person and a device meet, and this family explains more accidents than any other. Usability standards require the maker to find the steps where a reasonable user is likely to slip, and to design the interface so that the correct action is the easy one. They govern the shape of connectors, the position of a button, the wording of a label, and the priority of an alarm. A flow control that turns the wrong way or a gas connector that fits the wrong gas is a hazard built into the product.

The bodies that write these documents are international, regional, and national. The International Organization for Standardization and the International Electrotechnical Commission write the international standards, with the electrotechnical commission taking the electrical field. National member bodies such as the American National Standards Institute and the British Standards Institution adopt and sometimes strengthen the text. The World Health Organization publishes guidance and lists of essential equipment that carry particular weight where no national framework exists.

A standard is only paper until somebody independent confirms that a maker can meet it, and that confirmation is called accreditation. Accreditation is granted to the laboratories and certification bodies that perform the tests. A test report is meaningful only when the laboratory can show that its instruments trace back through a chain of comparisons to a national measurement standard. That chain is why a calibration certificate names the reference and the uncertainty, and the traceability must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before a report is relied upon.

Where a national framework exists, the hospital operates inside it and its duties follow from that. It must buy devices that are lawfully on the market, store and use them as instructed, and keep records that allow an item to be found again. It must report adverse events within the required time, act on a recall, maintain and calibrate as the maker requires, and train everyone who operates the device. The duties and the reporting deadlines must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before any change is made.

The hospital must also avoid the quiet modifications that void an approval. A homemade adapter, a substituted fuse, a repaired circuit board, a bypassed interlock, or a software setting changed beyond its permitted range can remove the device from the protection of its certificate. Such a change is usually made to keep a machine working during a shortage, and it converts an approved device into an unapproved one. The permitted modifications and the parts that must be original must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before any work is performed.

Adverse event reporting, often called vigilance, is the system by which a failure in the field reaches the people who can correct it. A reportable event is usually a death, a serious injury, or a malfunction that could have caused either if it recurred. The maker must investigate the cause and report to the national authority, which may then circulate the finding to every user. The system depends on the person closest to the event, because a device that failed on a night shift leaves the ward unexamined unless somebody writes it down.

The weakness of every vigilance system is under-reporting. The person who noticed is busy, the form is long, and the device seems to work again after a restart. A report that is not made allows the same fault to reach the next patient, and a culture that works punishes nobody for reporting. The common operator error is to describe the problem in conversation rather than in the system, which informs three colleagues and nobody who can fix it.

A recall is the action taken when a device already in use must be corrected or removed, and recalls arrive in degrees that reflect the danger. The most serious concerns a device that will probably cause serious harm or death and demands immediate action, including stopping use and contacting patients. The intermediate degree concerns a device that may cause temporary or reversible harm. The least urgent concerns a violation unlikely to cause harm that must still be corrected.

The notice is usually a field safety notice from the maker, and the clinic's procedure should be fixed in advance. Affected items are identified by serial or lot number, quarantined in a locked and clearly labeled place, and removed from use. Everyone who might use them is informed, and they are returned or corrected as instructed. The quarantine location and the removal deadline must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the notice arrives.

The characteristic alarm of this chapter is not a sound but the arrival of a notice that nobody reads. The recall letter in an unopened envelope, the message filtered into a spam folder, and the telephone message taken by someone who has since left are all the same failure. Each of them has kept a known-dangerous device in use. The defense is a named person who receives notices, a second who covers that absence, and one log in which every notice is entered and closed.

Post-market surveillance is the continuing collection of information after approval, and it is how a slow failure is discovered at all. The maker must gather complaints, service records, literature, and registry data, and periodically judge whether the balance of benefit and risk has changed. An artificial joint or a heart valve may fail only after several years and several thousand patients. Implant registries link the device to the outcome, and an implant card in the patient's hand lets a clinic find the person when a design is found to fail early.

The quality system inside a hospital mirrors the maker's system, and it begins with written procedures. A standard operating procedure states who does what, in what order, with what equipment, and what record is produced. Document control keeps only the current version in circulation, because a ward following an outdated procedure is following no procedure at all. The content, the version control, and the review interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the system is called complete.

An internal audit checks the system against its own rules, while an external accreditation survey checks it against an outside standard. A good surveyor follows a real patient through the building rather than reading a folder in an office. The purpose of all this paper is to protect the patient rather than the institution, and the test of a record is whether it would help the next patient. The common failure is the retrospective entry, written days later to fill a gap in a log, which turns a record into a fiction and hides the drift it was meant to reveal.

Quality control is the daily proof that a process still works, and it differs from quality assurance, which is the design of the system that makes the process reliable. A laboratory runs a control sample of known value before patient samples, plots the result on a chart, and stops the analyzer when the control falls out of range. A sterilizer proves each cycle with a chemical indicator and, at a defined interval, with a biological indicator containing harmless spores. The control limits, the acceptance rules, and the incubation conditions must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before results are released.

The characteristic alarm of quality control is the failed control, and the common operator error is to repeat the control until it passes. That does not repair the process, but selects the lucky result and hides the drift that caused the failure. The correct response is to stop and look for the physical cause, which may be an expired reagent, a dirty probe, an aged lamp, or a shifted temperature. Results that stay inside the limits while drifting steadily one way are also a warning, and the trend rules must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before a result is reported.

Calibration and its traceability are the bridge between the hospital and the national measurement system. To calibrate is to adjust a device until its reading agrees with a reference, while to verify is to check that the reading already agrees. The reference used must itself be traceable, which means it has been compared, through an unbroken chain, to a national standard. The hazard is the device that reads confidently and wrongly, and the calibration interval and tolerance must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before a result is trusted.

Maintenance is a regulatory obligation and not merely a service preference. The schedule the maker publishes is part of the conditions under which the device was approved, and skipping it can shift liability to the hospital when a patient is harmed. The work must be recorded with the date, the person, the parts used, and the measurements taken. The common failure is a repair that restores function without restoring safety, such as a fuse replaced with a higher rating or an earth wire left disconnected inside a closed housing.

Consumables carry their own regulatory burden, and it is often heavier than the burden on the machine. A single-use device is marked as such because the maker has shown that it is safe once, not that it can be cleaned, sterilized, and used again. Where reprocessing is permitted, it is permitted only through a validated process with documented cleaning, inspection, sterilization, and traceability, and the liability moves to whoever reprocessed it. Expiry dates are part of the approval, and the reuse status and expiry rules must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before any item is reused.

Compatibility is a physical property that a purchasing decision can quietly destroy. A maker validates its machine with particular consumables, and a third-party set may look identical while differing in internal diameter, wall stiffness, filter pore size, or the material that touches the fluid. Any of those differences can change the delivered dose or the behavior of an alarm. The substitute often appears to work for a hundred uses before it fails, and the compatibilities must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before a substitute is introduced.

Cleaning and disinfection are regulated because a device that is not clean is not the device that was tested. Reprocessing instructions are part of the approval file, and they specify the cleaning agent, its concentration, the contact time, the temperature, the rinsing, and the drying. The physical failure is often invisible, because a channel that is not brushed keeps a biofilm and a connector that is not dried grows organisms in the moisture. The concentrations and contact times must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before any device is reprocessed.

The hazard of poor cleaning is greatest in devices with narrow lumens, hinges, and rough surfaces, which is why flexible endoscopes and jointed instruments are the classic examples. The characteristic sign is the positive culture or the failed adenosine triphosphate test that arrives days after the procedure. A detergent that is too dilute does not lift the soil that protects the organisms, and a disinfectant that attacks the housing destroys the device it was meant to protect. Cleaning is therefore a device specification as much as a housekeeping task.

Complaints and failure modes are the raw material of quality, and a hospital that reviews them learns what no audit will teach. A single report of a cracked housing is an anecdote, while twelve reports of the same crack in the same model are a pattern. The pattern is what justifies a repair campaign, a change in training, or the withdrawal of a device. The investigation should ask what the device, the user, and the environment each did, because a fault attributed to the operator is often a design fault that made the error easy.

The common mistake is to record the complaint as a work order and close it when the device is returned, losing the trend that would have revealed the defect. The person at the bedside is the last and cheapest layer of the whole system. Before a device is used, the operator should confirm that it is the correct device, that its label and serial number match the register, and that the consumables are within expiry and of the approved type. During use the operator should watch for a warm plug, a new noise, a drifting reading, and an alarm that appears more often than it used to.

In a well-resourced hospital this whole structure is carried by people and by the consumables that the quality system itself requires. A quality unit keeps the procedures and the audit calendar, a biomedical engineering department keeps the register and the calibration schedule, and an infection prevention team validates the cleaning. Calibration certificates, reference standards, log sheets, labels, indicator strips, control samples, and training records are all consumables of the system. Service contracts are the usual place where the regulatory promise is broken, and a contract should name the response time, the parts included, and the documentation to be left behind.

Beyond the reach of this structure, the same clinical need is met by equipment that arrives with none of it, and regulation becomes a local practice rather than a national system. The field clinic usually receives its devices by donation, by ministry allocation, or by purchase in a market with no enforcement. The paperwork a hospital takes for granted is often absent, incomplete, in a language nobody reads, or written for a different model. What the field clinic gives up is the independent check on the design, the guarantee of spare parts, and the certainty that consumables will exist next year.

What it demands instead is judgment exercised before acceptance and the willingness to refuse. The device may be built for a voltage and frequency the local supply does not deliver. It may need a gas whose fittings do not exist locally, or water of a purity the clinic cannot produce. Its consumables may be proprietary and unavailable, and its service manual may never have been sent.

The question a receiving clinic must ask before accepting anything should be asked before the truck is unloaded. Can the device run from the power that actually exists here, and does it tolerate the swings and the outages? Can its consumables be obtained for the next several years, and at a price the clinic can pay? Is there a manual in a language the staff read, and a service manual for the technician? Are spare parts available, and is there someone within reach who can fit them?

When the device is accepted, the clinic must build the traceability the maker did not provide, and the substitute is a paper register kept honestly. Each item receives a local asset number painted or engraved on the case, and a line in a bound book records its origin, arrival date, serial number, condition, and responsible person. A photograph of the maker's plate and of the plug is a cheap copy of the information a lost manual would have carried. For consumables the lot number and the expiry are written on the log sheet at the point of use.

Vigilance in the field means using whatever channel exists rather than waiting for a formal one. The district health office, the regional referral hospital, the visiting technician, and the professional network are all part of the reporting route. A telephone call written down the same day is a valid report where no form exists. The field recall is the hardest case, because the notice must travel a road the device already travelled in the opposite direction.

A recall may arrive by radio, by a message to the district office, by a courier on a motorcycle, or by a colleague who heard about it at a meeting. The procedure that works must be decided in advance, with a quarantine shelf that is physically separate and clearly labeled. One person checks every notice against the register, and a recalled item leaves use on the day it is identified. The common failure is the notice filed with the intention of acting on it later, and the item used in the meantime.

The field quality system is small enough to be held by one person and must be written down anyway. One trained staff member keeps the equipment register, the log sheets, the maintenance calendar, and the file of instructions, and is given the time to do it. The daily checks match a hospital's, with the refrigerator temperature read and written, the sterilizer cycle recorded, the concentrator output checked, and expiry dates scanned. A short written procedure in the local language turns a habit into a standard that survives the departure of the person who wrote it.

Calibration and maintenance in the field rest on comparison, on the toolkit, and on the technician, and the honest statement is that the traceability chain is short. A device can be verified against a second device known to be good, a thermometer against an ice point and a boiling point, and a scale against a known mass. The physical principle is the same as in the hospital, because a measurement is only as good as the reference it is compared against. The common error is to adjust a device that is only slightly out rather than to record the deviation and watch for drift.

Cleaning, consumables, and failure modes in the field obey the same rules with fewer safeguards behind them. A chlorine solution mixed by hand must be measured, because a solution that is too weak does not disinfect and one that is too strong corrodes metal. A pressure cooker used as a sterilizer can be effective only when the cycle is timed, the temperature is reached, the air is removed, and an indicator proves the result. The consumable supply is the true constraint on every device, and the characteristic failure in the field is silence, because a device with no battery monitor has no low battery alarm.

The final field obligation is the same as the first, which is to decide before accepting rather than to repair afterwards. A receiving clinic that asks the hard questions, refuses what it cannot sustain, writes down what it owns, and reports what it sees has built a quality system out of paper and habit. That system protects patients as surely as an accreditation certificate does. The certificate is not the safety, because the safety is the physical test, the written record, and the person who reads the record.

To summarize, a regulation is the law that decides whether a device may be sold, a directive sets the required result and leaves the law to each country, and a standard becomes binding when a regulation refers to it. The standards that matter cover electrical safety, gas supply, sterility, quality management, risk, and usability. Approval is only the beginning, because traceability, adverse event reporting, recalls, calibration, maintenance, and cleaning continue for the whole life of the device. A hospital carries that structure with departments, contracts, and records, while a field clinic replaces it with a register, a log sheet, a trained person, and the discipline to refuse what it cannot sustain.
