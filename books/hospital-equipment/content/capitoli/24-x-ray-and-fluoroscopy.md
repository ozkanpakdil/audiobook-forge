# Chapter 24 — X-Ray and Fluoroscopy

An X-ray image begins with a stream of electrons stopped abruptly in a metal target, because most of the electron energy becomes heat and a small fraction becomes electromagnetic radiation of very short wavelength. The continuous part of that radiation comes from the deceleration of electrons in the field of the target nuclei, while sharp characteristic lines come from the ejection of inner orbital electrons and depend on the metal used. The beam leaves the tube in a cone, and as it crosses the patient some photons are absorbed and some pass unchanged, so the pattern reaching the detector is a shadow map of how much material stood in the way. Absorption rises steeply with tissue density and with the atomic number of the elements present, which is why calcium-rich bone attenuates far more than fat or air and why a metal implant appears almost white. Contrast therefore comes from differential attenuation rather than from any lens, and sharpness comes from the small size of the spot where the electrons landed.

The tube is a sealed glass or metal envelope under vacuum, with a coiled tungsten filament as the cathode. A low current heats the filament until electrons boil off its surface, a process called thermionic emission, and the negative charge on the filament drives them toward the positive anode. The anode is a tungsten disc set at an angle so that the spot where the electrons land appears small when viewed from the patient's side, and a rotating anode spreads the heat over a large circular track instead of burning one point. That focal spot is the origin of the geometric sharpness of the image, because a large spot casts a blurred shadow just as a large lamp does, so the smallest spot is chosen whenever the technique allows it. Nearly all the energy arrives as heat, so the tube sits in insulating oil inside a lead-lined housing cooled by a fan or a heat exchanger. A tube temperature or overload warning is the characteristic alarm, and the correct response is to let the tube cool rather than repeat the exposure, because a tube forced while hot will arc and fail; the heat rating and the cooling intervals must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The generator converts the alternating mains supply into the direct high voltage the tube needs, by stepping the voltage up through a transformer and rectifying it. The kilovoltage sets the penetrating power of the beam and therefore the contrast, because more energetic photons pass through bone more readily and flatten the difference between tissues. The milliamperage sets the number of electrons and the quantity of radiation, the exposure time sets how long that current flows, and the product of the two, measured in milliampere seconds, is the usual measure of dose to the patient. A three phase or high frequency generator holds a nearly constant voltage and delivers a more predictable dose for the same settings, which is why a technique chart from one machine does not transfer to another. The automatic exposure control is a set of chambers behind the detector that stop the exposure when a chosen amount of radiation has arrived, and a generator or chamber that has drifted silently changes every dose in the room; the kilovoltage, current, time, and chamber selection must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Between the tube and the patient sits the collimator, a box of movable lead shutters with a lamp and a mirror that projects a visible light field to show where the radiation will fall. That light field is the operator's contract with the patient, and the shutters are set so the illuminated rectangle is the only part of the body exposed, which reduces dose, improves contrast, and limits scatter. The filter in the same housing, usually a thin sheet of aluminum, absorbs the lowest energy photons before they reach the skin, because those photons would be absorbed in the patient without contributing to the image. Below the patient lies the grid, a wafer of fine lead strips separated by layers of low density material, and it stops scattered radiation that has changed direction inside the body. Scatter carries no useful position information and veils the image in a flat fog, and the grid removes much of it at the cost of a higher dose, because the primary beam must be increased to compensate. The commonest operator error in the room is a collimator light that no longer matches the radiation field, so the patient is irradiated outside the visible rectangle; the light field alignment and the grid ratio must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The detector turns the invisible shadow into something the eye can read, and three generations coexist in most hospitals. The oldest is the film and screen cassette, in which a phosphor screen absorbs radiation and emits visible light that darkens a silver halide emulsion, which is then developed, fixed, washed, and dried in a processor. The second is computed radiography, in which a plate coated with a photostimulable phosphor stores the image as trapped electrons, is scanned by a laser that releases the energy as light, and is then erased by a bright lamp so the plate can be used again. The third is the flat panel detector, in which a scintillator or photoconductor sits directly on a thin film transistor array, so the charge from each pixel is read electronically within seconds and appears on a screen without any chemical step. Digital detectors buy speed, a wider tolerance for exposure error, and direct transfer into the picture archiving and communication system, which is called PACS, but they also hide overexposure, because a detector receiving too much radiation simply produces a clean looking noisy image; every exposure index and flag threshold must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A fixed radiographic room is built around the geometry of the examination rather than around the machine. The table has a floating top that slides with the patient on it so a weak or injured person need not be lifted, and beneath the top sits a tray that carries the detector or cassette, known as the Bucky assembly. A vertical stand holds a detector for standing chest images, and the tube hangs from a ceiling crane or rides on a column so the source can be aimed at either receptor from the correct distance. The console sits behind lead glass, and the walls, door, and floor may be lined with lead or barium plaster so the beam stops inside the room, which is why the door interlock and the warning light above the door must be tested rather than assumed. The source to detector distance, the table height, and the alignment of the tube to the receptor must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The consumables of a radiographic room are modest in price and absolute in effect, because a missing cassette or an unmarked image can waste an examination. Each cassette needs a clean intact envelope, and the phosphor plates of computed radiography need a fresh sleeve for every patient, because bodily fluids ruin a plate and the sleeve is the only barrier. Positioning aids include radiolucent sponges, sandbags, straps, and foam blocks that hold a limb still without appearing on the image, and right and left markers placed on the cassette before the exposure so the finished image proves which side was examined. Gonad shielding is a shaped piece of leaded rubber placed over the reproductive organs of a patient who is still growing, while lead aprons, thyroid shields, and leaded glasses protect the staff who must remain in the room. A room used for contrast studies also holds iodinated contrast media, intravenous needles and cannulae, pressure injectors with their syringes and tubing, and a resuscitation kit, because a contrast reaction can begin within seconds of the injection. Cleaning supplies, hand hygiene materials, and single use gloves and other personal protective equipment belong on the same list, and the concentration, volume, and flow rate of any contrast medium must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A mobile or portable unit is a radiographic machine on wheels, built for a patient who cannot come to the department, and its physics is unchanged while its compromises are many. It carries a smaller generator, a lighter tube on an articulated arm, a short collimator, and either a long mains cable or a battery bank holding enough charge for a limited number of exposures. Because the arm is short, the source to detector distance is often no more than 100 to 150 centimeters, a distance that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and a shorter distance means a larger apparent focal spot and a less sharp image. A grid is often absent because alignment is imperfect, which is one reason a bedside chest image is usually inferior to one made in a dedicated room, and the hazards are the cable crossing a darkened intensive care unit floor, the tube head striking a monitor or a ventilator, and the beam passing into a neighboring bed. The characteristic alarm is a low battery or charge ready tone, and the commonest operator error is holding the cassette by hand, which puts the operator's fingers in the primary beam; the exposure factors must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Fluoroscopy is radiography watched while it happens, and the detector must produce a moving image at a low dose rather than a single still image at a high dose. The classical device is the image intensifier, a vacuum tube with a large input phosphor that converts radiation into light, a photocathode that converts that light into electrons, electrodes that accelerate and focus the electrons onto a much smaller output phosphor, and a lens or camera that views the bright result. The gain comes from minification, because squeezing the same number of electrons onto a smaller area makes the image many times brighter, and also from the acceleration of the electrons by the applied voltage. The automatic brightness control adjusts the kilovoltage and current so the monitor picture stays readable as the beam passes through thicker or thinner parts of the body. The hazard specific to fluoroscopy is that the beam stays on the same patch of skin, so dose accumulates in one place while the operator watches the screen and a skin injury can begin before anyone notices; the pulse rate and brightness control settings must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The C-arm is a fluoroscopic machine shaped like the letter C, with the tube at one end and the detector at the other, which allows the beam to be aimed from almost any angle around a patient lying on an operating table. It rotates around its arc, angles from side to side, and slides along the patient, and the image appears on a monitor while the operator's hands work inside the field. Modern C-arms use the same flat panel detectors as radiographic rooms, and the older image intensifier survives mainly in equipment not yet replaced. Two features cut dose dramatically and are routinely neglected: pulsed fluoroscopy, which produces a short burst several times a second instead of a continuous beam, and last image hold, which keeps the final frame after the radiation stops so a question can be answered without more exposure. Magnification is a trap, because zooming concentrates the same dose into less skin, and the characteristic alarm is a dose or fluoroscopy time warning whose threshold and pulsed settings must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Contrast studies add a substance that attenuates radiation far more than soft tissue, so vessels, the gut, and the urinary tract appear as dark branching shapes rather than a uniform shadow. The usual agent contains iodine, injected intravenously or through a catheter, and it works because iodine has a high atomic number and absorbs the beam strongly. A pressure injector delivers a measured volume at a set flow rate for angiographic work, while a hand syringe suffices for a swallow, and the timing of the exposure relative to the injection determines whether the vessel is seen filled or empty. Digital subtraction removes a mask image acquired before the contrast arrives from the images acquired after it, so only the opacified vessels remain, which allows a smaller volume of contrast and a smaller dose than a plain series. The hazards are an allergic or anaphylactic reaction, kidney injury in a patient whose kidneys are already weak, and extravasation into the tissues when the needle has left the vein; the concentration, volume, flow rate, and delay must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Radiation protection rests on three quantities the operator controls, and the physics of each is simple. Distance matters most, because the intensity of a beam from a small source falls with the square of the distance, so doubling the distance from 1 meter to 2 meters reduces the dose rate to one quarter, and the protective distances used in a room must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Time matters next, because the dose received is the dose rate multiplied by the minutes spent in the beam, so the shortest possible screening time is itself a protective act. Shielding is the third, and a lead apron absorbs the low energy scatter that dominates in a fluoroscopy room, while a thyroid shield protects an organ that is unusually sensitive and unusually exposed at the neck. Aprons are rated by lead equivalent, commonly 0.5 millimeter of lead, a value that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and they must be inspected and imaged at intervals for cracks, because a folded apron becomes a leaking shield. A dosimeter worn on the chest or collar records the accumulated dose, and the wearer must read it and understand it rather than hang it on a wall at the end of the shift.

The pregnancy question is asked before the examination and not after, and of every patient of childbearing age, because the answer changes the plan. A developing fetus is more sensitive to radiation than an adult, particularly in the early weeks when the organs are forming, so a necessary examination is still performed but the technique is adjusted, the field collimated tightly, and an alternative such as ultrasound chosen whenever it can answer the same question. A patient who is pregnant and one who might be pregnant are treated the same way, because the ten day rule, which confines elective examinations of the abdomen and pelvis to the first ten days of the cycle, is not reliable for every woman. Staff who are pregnant must declare it, because the occupational limit for a declared pregnant worker is lower than for other workers, and the employer must then review her duties and dosimeter readings with her. The numerical limits, declaration procedure, and shielding arrangements must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the decision to expose a pregnant patient belongs to the clinician who needs the information rather than the person pressing the button.

Image quality is a balance of four properties, and a change that improves one usually damages another. Contrast is the difference in darkness between tissues and rises as the kilovoltage falls, so a low kilovoltage gives a dramatic bone image at the price of a high patient dose. Spatial resolution is the ability to separate two small objects and depends on the focal spot, the geometry, and the detector, so a magnified view made with a small spot resolves detail that a large spot would blur. Noise is the grain that appears when too few photons reach the detector, and the only honest cure is more radiation rather than more brightness on the monitor, while motion is defeated by a short exposure time and by asking the patient to hold still. The repeat is the central fact of a radiographic department, because an image taken again doubles the dose to the patient and wastes the machine's time, so every rejected image should be counted and its cause named; the exposure factors and rejection thresholds used in review must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Maintenance and quality assurance separate a machine that is trustworthy from one that merely switches on. The tube needs a warm-up routine after a long idle period, in which a series of low current exposures builds anode heat gradually, because a cold anode struck by a full load can crack. The generator is checked for accurate kilovoltage, time, and output, and the automatic exposure control is tested with a uniform phantom so the same thickness always produces the same density. The collimator is checked for alignment between the light field and the radiation field, and that alignment is measured rather than eyeballed, because a small offset becomes a large error at the skin. The detector is checked for dead pixels, flat field uniformity, and gain calibration, while lead aprons and thyroid shields are examined for cracks by hand and at intervals by imaging them, each apron carrying an identification number and a test date. The intervals for warm-up, calibration, detector flat field, and apron inspection must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Cleaning is a clinical procedure in an imaging room, because the equipment is touched by every patient and by every hand that positions them. The table top, control handle, detector surface, cassette, and positioning aids are wiped between patients with a disinfectant that the maker of the device allows, since some solvents cloud a phosphor plate or craze a detector window. Cassettes and computed radiography plates need particular care, because a scratch, a fingerprint, or a droplet of disinfectant on a plate becomes an artifact on every image made with it, so the plate must be cleaned and dried before it returns to its sleeve. The darkroom or reader area is a clean, dry, dust free space, and a processor must be replenished and cleaned on a schedule rather than at the moment the films begin to look wrong, while blood and body fluid spills are cleaned immediately and the sharps and contaminated waste of contrast procedures follow the usual segregation rules. The concentration, contact time, and compatibility of every disinfectant used on a detector, cassette, or table must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The failure modes of radiographic equipment are few and repetitive, and each announces itself in a characteristic way. A tube nearing the end of its life arcs, heard as a sharp crack and seen as a fault code, and a tube that arcs twice will soon fail completely, while an anode that has begun to wobble produces a blurred image at every technique. A generator fault makes the exposure terminate early or not at all, and a drifted automatic exposure control produces images consistently too dark or too light across a range of patient sizes. A flat panel detector losing pixels shows fixed bright or dark dots in the same place on every image, while a scratched phosphor plate shows a line that follows the plate from room to room, so the fault travels with the plate and not the machine. A grid that is upside down or misaligned leaves grid lines across the image, and a cassette with a light leak fogs one corner of every film; the fault codes, tube rating, and alarm thresholds must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The operator's start of shift check is short, and it prevents most of the day's failures. The tube is warmed up, the collimator light is switched on and its field compared with the shutters, the exposure switch and emergency stop are tested, and the console is read for any fault message left by the last shift. The apron and thyroid shield are lifted and felt for cracks, the dosimeter is worn on the outside of the apron at the collar, and its previous reading is examined rather than merely filed. The cassettes or detector are checked for cleanliness and correct identification, the markers and positioning aids are confirmed present, and the processor or reader is confirmed ready. The operator also confirms that the previous patient's images reached the picture archiving and communication system, because a study that was never sent must be repeated; the warm-up sequence and daily check list must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Every exposure should be justified by a question the image can answer, and the equipment is only one part of that decision. A chest radiograph is among the lowest dose examinations in medicine, while a fluoroscopic study of the gut or an interventional procedure can deliver a dose hundreds of times larger, so the choice of examination is itself a form of radiation protection. A clinician who orders an image to reassure rather than to decide adds dose without information, and a second view that repeats an established finding adds the same again. The commonest organizational error is to accept a request that states no clinical question, because the radiographer then cannot choose the projection or exposure that would answer it. Communicating the radiation risk to the patient in plain language, and recording that the discussion took place, is part of the procedure; the reference doses, protocols, and referral criteria must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Children deserve their own technique, because their tissues are more radiosensitive, their bodies are smaller, and the remaining lifetime in which a radiation effect could appear is longer. The smaller body means the beam must be collimated more tightly and the exposure factors must fall sharply with size, so a technique chart not indexed to thickness will overexpose a small child. Immobilization is a genuine problem, because a moving child produces a blurred image that must be repeated, and the answer is a dedicated positioning aid, a properly shielded parent, or a short exposure rather than a faster film. Gonad shielding is most valuable in this group, and the shield must be placed correctly or it will obscure the very region under examination and force a repeat. Fluoroscopy in a child uses the lowest pulse rate and tightest collimation that still answer the question, and the pediatric exposure factors, shielding placement, and pulse settings must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

In a mobile or frontier clinic the radiographic variant is the portable unit carried in a vehicle, and the room is whatever space can be cleared, often with mud walls, a wooden floor, and no lead anywhere. The machine is battery powered with a small generator and a fixed or folding arm, and the detector may be a reusable phosphor plate read in a lightproof bag by a laptop driven reader, or a film cassette developed by hand in a darkroom that is no more than a cupboard with a red lamp. Because there is no shielding, the operator replaces the architecture with the distance rule and with direction, standing as far from the tube and the patient as the space allows and never in the line of the primary beam. The beam is aimed toward an outside wall, the ground, or an unoccupied area, and everyone who is not the patient leaves the room or stands behind a thick wall during the exposure. The most useful protective objects are a well fitting lead apron with a thyroid shield and a collimator that works, because the smallest field is the cheapest protection, and the minimum distance and beam direction must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The field unit gives up the grid, the automatic exposure control, the lead lined room, the lead glass window, the automatic processor, the picture archiving and communication system, and the dose monitoring a hospital takes for granted, and each loss must be replaced by a habit. Without a grid the image is veiled by scatter, so the field radiographer compensates with tighter collimation, the body part close to the detector, and a larger source to detector distance whenever the room allows one. Without automatic exposure control there is no chamber to stop the exposure, so the operator works from a written technique chart based on measured thickness, and that chart must be rebuilt for each machine rather than copied from a hospital manual. Without a processor the film is developed in a tank whose temperature and time decide the density, so the developer is kept at a measured temperature, the timer used honestly, and the chemicals replenished on a written schedule instead of when the images look pale. Without a picture archiving and communication system the image is a physical object, labeled with the patient's name, date, and side marker before the exposure and stored dry and flat, because a lost image means a second dose; the developer temperature, developing time, replenishment interval, and technique chart values must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Quality in the field is proved with a simple phantom and a daily record rather than a service contract, because there is no engineer within reach. A step wedge or small test object is exposed at a fixed setting once a day, and the image is compared with the one from the day before, so a drifting developer, a failing tube, or a dying battery announces itself before a patient is exposed. The output is checked against a written benchmark, the collimator light is aligned by hand, the plate or cassette is inspected for scratches, and the lead apron is examined for cracks in good light. The darkroom is kept truly dark, because a small light leak fogs a film and the fog is then blamed on the developer for weeks. When the needed examination cannot be done safely with the equipment at hand, the correct decision is to stabilize, document, and refer to a facility with a shielded room, because the distance rule protects the operator and not the patient's skin; every benchmark, temperature, and safety distance must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Sending the image to someone who can read it is the last step, and in the field it is usually the weakest link. A film can be photographed on a light box with a telephone, and a plate can be read into a laptop and sent over a mobile data link where one exists, but both degrade the image and carry the patient's identity across an unsecured channel. The honest position is that a transmitted photograph is a triage aid rather than a diagnostic image, used to decide whether the patient must travel rather than to rule out a fracture at home, and the picture archiving and communication system standards that govern hospital images cannot be met by a telephone photograph. The last piece of equipment is the trained person, because positioning decides whether the anatomy is projected without overlap and no detector can recover a view in which the wrong side was placed against the plate. In a remote setting the same person may be clinician, radiographer, and technician, which makes training and refresher practice more valuable than any single device, and a library of good and rejected images reviewed together is the best measure of skill through the repeat rate. The image handling rules of the local protocol and the competence and retraining requirements for each operator must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

To summarize, an X-ray image is a shadow cast by a very small focal spot, and every part of the machine exists to make that shadow accurate at the lowest dose that answers the clinical question. The tube boils electrons from a filament and stops them in a rotating anode, the generator sets the kilovoltage, current, and time, the collimator and filter shape the beam, the grid removes scatter, and the detector, whether film, phosphor plate, or flat panel, turns the pattern into a picture. Fluoroscopy watches the same shadow in motion, and its image intensifier or flat panel detector, its pulsed mode, last image hold, and accumulated dose warning keep a moving study from becoming a skin injury. Protection is distance, time, and shielding, with a lead apron, thyroid shield, and dosimeter, and with the pregnancy question asked before the examination, while quality is a balance of contrast, resolution, noise, and motion in which every repeat costs the patient a second dose. Maintenance, quality assurance, cleaning, and a short start of shift check keep a room trustworthy, while the field variant trades the grid, the automatic exposure control, the lead lined room, and the picture archiving and communication system for a technique chart, a measured darkroom, a long distance, and a disciplined habit of referral. In every setting the rule is the same: justify the exposure, collimate to the question, stand back, and confirm every setting against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before a patient is placed in the beam.
