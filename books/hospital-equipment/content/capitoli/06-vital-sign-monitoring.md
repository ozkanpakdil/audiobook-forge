# Chapter 6 — Vital Sign Monitoring

Vital signs are the small set of numbers that tell a clinician whether a body is governing itself: temperature, blood pressure, pulse and oxygen saturation, respiratory rate, and carbon dioxide in the breath of a sedated or seriously ill patient. Each number comes from a transducer that converts a physical quantity into a readable signal, accurate in one range and misleading outside it. This chapter takes the instruments one at a time, principle before clinical use, then the hospital-grade device with its infrastructure and consumables, then the field variant and what it gives up. Hazards, characteristic alarms, and habitual operator errors are named where each device is described.

Body temperature is a balance between heat produced by metabolism and heat lost to the environment, and the core stays in a narrow band while skin and limbs vary widely. A thermometer reads its own sensor at equilibrium with the tissue, or the infrared energy the tissue radiates, never the body directly. Clinical thermometry uses three families: thermal expansion of a liquid in a narrow bore, change in electrical resistance as a material warms, and emission of infrared radiation from a surface. Each has a different response time, site, and way of being wrong, so the dwell time or temperature threshold used must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Mercury expands as it warms and is forced up a narrow bore beside a printed scale, and a constriction below the bulb holds the column so that the highest temperature is retained. Its accuracy is excellent, but mercury is a neurotoxin, a broken tube scatters droplets that evaporate, and glass in the mouth of a confused patient invites injury. The common errors are reading too early, failing to shake the column down, and reading the meniscus at the wrong angle. There is no alarm, only the silent error of a premature reading, so the required dwell time of several minutes must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The digital probe replaces the column with a thermistor whose electrical resistance falls predictably as it warms, and many devices predict the final temperature from the rate of change without waiting for equilibrium. Sites are the mouth, the armpit, and the rectum, each with an offset from core temperature that the operator must apply. The consumables are single-use probe covers that form the barrier and must not be doubled, because two layers slow the response and bias the reading. A low battery or out-of-range alarm means the reading cannot be trusted, and site offsets and concern thresholds must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The tympanic thermometer measures infrared energy radiated by the tympanic membrane, which shares its blood supply with the hypothalamus and tracks core temperature closely. A thermopile converts received energy into a temperature once emissivity and probe temperature are known, so the device is fast and needs no mucosal contact. Accuracy depends on pulling the pinna back to straighten the canal, aiming at the membrane, and keeping the canal free of wax, and the common errors are aiming at the canal wall and omitting the single-use cover. The fever threshold used to act must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The temporal artery thermometer reads infrared emission along a swept path over the forehead and temple, where the superficial temporal artery runs close to the surface. Because the artery carries blood near core temperature, a correct sweep from the forehead to behind the ear approximates the core and depends far less on probe angle. The technique is one continuous stroke with the hair parted and the skin dry, and stopping the sweep, pressing too hard, and scanning over hair are the habitual errors. Sweat or a cold forehead after outdoor exposure produces a low reading that may hide fever, and any treatment threshold must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The chemical dot thermometer is a strip of heat-sensitive material, often a matrix of squares that each change color at a slightly different temperature, so it reports a band rather than a number. It is cheap, powerless, unbreakable, and useful for screening many people or for sending a mother home with a means of checking her child. Its accuracy is poor near the thresholds that matter, it is single-use, and its calibration drifts if it is stored hot, so a box left in a parked vehicle may be useless. Common errors are reading in dim light, using the wrong dwell time, and using a strip past its expiry date, and storage temperature and the action threshold must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

In the field, every thermometer is a contest between dust, heat, and the battery, since glass breaks, a digital probe needs covers and power, an infrared device needs a clean lens, and chemical dots need shade and a cool box. The field substitutes are a hand on the abdomen, a careful axillary reading taken the same way every time, and the picture of a child who is hot, lethargic, and not feeding. What the field gives up is precision, and what it demands instead is consistency of site, dwell time, and device. Every temperature threshold that leads to treatment must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Blood pressure measurement begins with a physical act, because a cuff is inflated until it exceeds the pressure inside the artery and the artery is occluded. As the cuff deflates, the turbulent jet of returning blood is heard through a stethoscope as sounds that appear, soften, and finally disappear, with the first appearance read as systolic and the disappearance as diastolic. Three instruments measure the cuff pressure itself: a mercury column by the height of liquid metal pushed up a tube, an aneroid gauge by the deformation of a sealed metal element, and an automated device by the small oscillations the artery transmits into the cuff. The diagnostic and treatment thresholds used must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The mercury sphygmomanometer is a vertical tube, a reservoir, a bulb, a valve, and a cuff joined by tubing, read by eye against a scale. Its virtue is that it reports a length of mercury, a quantity that cannot drift, so a clean and correctly zeroed instrument is a reference for checking others. Its hazards are the roughly one kilogram of toxic mercury it contains and the fragility of its glass, and it must be checked for a broken column, bubbles, oxidized mercury, and leaks in the tube and valve. Common errors are deflating too quickly to hear the sounds, letting the mercury rest below the zero mark, and using a cuff too small for the arm, and any pressure used to diagnose or to treat must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The aneroid sphygmomanometer replaces the column with a circular gauge in which a curved, sealed metal element straightens as pressure enters and drives a needle across a dial. It is light, cannot spill mercury, and is the device most often found in a clinic bag, but its mechanism drifts with age, drops, and heat, usually reading low. Common errors are a gauge that does not rest at zero, a bent needle or cracked glass, and a cuff left folded for years so that the bladder perishes. A gauge should be checked against a known good reference at zero and across its range, and calibration intervals and the acceptable error must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The automated oscillometric device pumps the cuff, then releases the pressure in steps while a sensor watches the small pulsations that each heartbeat adds to the cuff. Those oscillations grow to a maximum and then fade, and the pressure at the maximum corresponds closely to mean arterial pressure, from which an algorithm estimates the systolic and diastolic values. The method removes the observer's ear but also the observer's judgment, and it is less reliable with an irregular rhythm, in shock with low flow, in certain hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, and in a moving patient. Common errors are measuring through clothing, an arm hanging below heart level, talking during the measurement, and kinked tubing, and inflation and alarm limits must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The cuff is not an accessory but part of the instrument, and its size determines the result, so the bladder should encircle about 40 per cent of the limb circumference and wrap about 80 per cent of the way around. Too narrow a cuff for a large arm reads falsely high, because more pressure is needed over a smaller contact area, and too wide a cuff for a small arm reads falsely low, because pressure is spread along a longer segment of artery. Manufacturers mark cuffs with a range of limb circumferences and often a color code, and common errors are an adult cuff on a child, a child cuff on an adult because it fits more comfortably, and not measuring the arm at all. Cuff size ranges and the correction they imply must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Even with the right cuff and a calibrated device, technique changes the number, so the patient should rest for 5 minutes, sit with the back supported and the feet flat, and keep the arm supported at heart level. The bladder belongs over the brachial artery with its lower edge about 2 to 3 centimeters above the crease of the elbow, and deflation for an auscultatory reading should run about 2 to 3 millimeters of mercury per second. An auscultatory gap, a silent interval between the sounds, causes the systolic value to be underestimated if the cuff is inflated only slightly above the expected pressure. Talking, an unsupported back, crossed legs, a full bladder, and a cold room all raise the pressure. The rest period, deflation rate, repeat interval, and thresholds used must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Maintenance of blood pressure equipment is mostly a search for leaks and drift, because cuff, tubing, and valve are rubber and fabric that perish, and a leak makes the pressure fall faster than intended. A simple leak test is to inflate the cuff, close the valve, and watch whether the pressure holds, and the cuff should be inspected for a cracked bladder, a frayed closure, and perished tubing at the connectors. An aneroid gauge should be checked against a reference at more than one pressure, and an automated device for the accuracy of its cuff and the integrity of its hose and connector. Devices in daily use belong on a calibration schedule with a written log, and calibration intervals and the acceptable error must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

In the field, mercury is too fragile and heavy and an automated device needs batteries and an intact cuff, so an aneroid gauge or a battery-operated oscillometric device is usual. Neither can be sent away for calibration every year, so the field demands a local reference, meaning a second device kept clean and handled carefully, cross-checked against the first, with a written note whenever the two disagree. The palpation method, in which the operator inflates the cuff and feels the pulse return at the wrist, gives a systolic value only, but it needs no stethoscope and no quiet room and it beats no measurement at all. What the field gives up is traceability, and what it demands is the same technique, limb, and cuff for the same patient, and the action thresholds derived from those readings must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The pulse oximeter rests on an optical fact, because oxygenated and deoxygenated hemoglobin absorb red and infrared light differently. It shines two wavelengths through a translucent part of the body, one near 660 nanometers that deoxygenated blood absorbs more strongly and another near 940 nanometers that oxygenated blood absorbs more strongly, and measures how much light reaches a detector on the other side. The signal has a steady part from skin, bone, and non-pulsatile blood and a small pulsatile part that rises and falls with each heartbeat, and the device uses only the pulsatile part, which is why a good oximeter shows a pulse waveform. The ratio of the wavelengths becomes a saturation value through an empirical curve built from healthy volunteers, and that value says nothing about how much hemoglobin is present or how much oxygen reaches the tissues, and alarm limits and the values used to act must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A transmission probe places the light source and the detector on opposite sides of a finger or an earlobe, while a reflectance probe places both on the same surface of the forehead or the chest, and infants wear a wrap around a foot or a toe. Reusable probes are robust but must be cleaned between patients, while single-use adhesive probes reduce cross-contamination and stay in place on a moving patient but cost money and cannot be reused. The safety hazard is pressure, because a probe left too long or applied too tightly can injure skin and underlying tissue, especially in a small child or a patient with poor circulation, so the site must be inspected and rotated. Common errors are a probe too large or too small for the digit, a cable trapped under the patient, and cleaning the sensor with a solvent that damages its optical surfaces, and rotation intervals and cleaning agents must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The pulse oximeter is the vital sign instrument most likely to report a confident wrong number. Movement makes the pulsatile signal unstable, cold causes vasoconstriction and a poor signal even in a well oxygenated patient, and nail polish and artificial nails absorb the wavelengths used. Poor perfusion from shock, low cardiac output, or severe anemia reduces the signal, venous congestion from a tight probe confuses the algorithm, and ambient light or electrical interference adds noise. Carboxyhemoglobin and methemoglobin are misread as normal saturation, so carbon monoxide poisoning may show a reassuring number, and the common operator error is to trust the number without looking at the waveform and the perfusion at the site. Alarm thresholds and the response to a poor trace must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The characteristic alarms of a pulse oximeter are a low saturation alarm, a pulse rate alarm, and a probe-off or low-perfusion alarm, and each asks the operator to look at the patient rather than the screen. A saturation threshold is often set near 90 per cent for an adult, but the correct value depends on the patient and the setting and must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Common errors are silencing an alarm without examining the patient, setting limits so wide that they never fire, and setting them so narrow that they fire constantly and train the staff to ignore them. When a reading is doubtful, the operator should check the waveform, warm the limb, move the probe to another site, and compare the result with the patient's breathing, color, and conscious state, because a pulse oximeter monitors rather than diagnoses.

In the field the oximeter is often the only continuous monitor available and also the most fragile, since a handheld battery device survives dust and a drop but its probe is the weak point, with a cable that breaks at the connector and optical surfaces that scratch. A reusable clip probe needs careful cleaning and a spare, and a supply of adhesive wraps is worth carrying because they keep a child's foot still. Solar charging and a stock of batteries are part of the equipment, since a device that cannot be charged will fail at night, and cold hands make warming the limb a routine step. What the field gives up is the hospital's continuous multi-parameter display, and what it demands is a disciplined spot check with the waveform inspected and the result written down, and the limits used for action must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Capnography measures carbon dioxide in the breath and belongs beside the other vital signs because it shows whether the airway is patent and whether air is reaching the alveoli. Carbon dioxide absorbs infrared light strongly at a wavelength near 4.3 micrometers, so a mainstream device measures across a chamber in the breathing circuit while a sidestream device pulls a sample through a thin tube to a bench instrument protected by a water trap and a filter. The output is the capnogram, which rises as the patient exhales, plateaus as alveolar gas passes the sensor, and falls sharply as the next breath begins. The value at the end of the plateau, the end-tidal carbon dioxide, approximates alveolar and therefore arterial carbon dioxide, and alarm limits and interpretation thresholds must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The practical difficulty of capnography is water and secretions, because a sidestream sampling line is narrow and saliva, mucus, or condensed water can block it, producing a flat line that looks exactly like apnea. The water trap is a consumable that must be emptied or changed as its design requires, and the sampling line is single-use and should be replaced when contaminated or when the waveform degrades. A nasal cannula with a sampling port monitors a sedated patient who is breathing spontaneously, while an airway adapter with a mainstream sensor monitors a ventilated patient. Common errors are treating a blocked line as a clinical event, skipping zeroing where the design requires it, and leaving the sensor where it is heated or soiled, and replacement intervals and alarm thresholds must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The characteristic alarms of a capnograph are apnea, a low end-tidal value, and a high end-tidal value, with a low alarm often set near 25 millimeters of mercury and a high alarm near 50 millimeters of mercury. Both values must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use because they differ with the patient and the setting. A sudden loss of waveform during airway management usually means the tube has moved, but it can also mean a disconnected circuit or a blocked line, so the operator must check the patient first and the tubing second. A rising value suggests hypoventilation or rebreathing, a falling value suggests hyperventilation or reduced cardiac output, and a sudden rise during chest compressions is a recognized sign that circulation has returned, so the waveform and not the single number carries the information.

In the field, continuous capnography is rare because it needs a battery, a supply of sampling lines, and a device that survives being carried, so the substitute is the colorimetric detector. That detector is a single-use device between the circuit and the patient, containing an acid-sensitive chemical that changes color with carbon dioxide, and the color swings with each breath so that the change over several breaths confirms the airway is in the trachea. It reports a range rather than a number, has a limited life once opened, and is sensitive to heat and expiry, so it must be stored within its stated range and checked before use. A handheld electronic capnograph with disposable lines is the middle option, and what the field gives up is the continuous trend while what it demands is the discipline to confirm placement and ventilation at the moment that matters, and storage temperature, expiry, and response time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The respiratory rate is the vital sign that changes first in a deteriorating patient and the one most often estimated rather than measured. It should be counted for a full 60 seconds while the patient remains unaware, because people breathe differently when observed, and counting for 15 seconds and multiplying by four multiplies the error. A newborn breathes much faster than an adult and the normal range narrows with age, so the same number means different things in different patients. The common operator error is to copy the number from the last chart entry, which is serious because the respiratory rate triggers escalation in most early warning systems, and escalation thresholds and normal ranges must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Several devices count the respiratory rate automatically, including impedance pneumography through two electrocardiogram electrodes that detects the change in chest impedance, a capnograph that derives the rate from the carbon dioxide waveform, a strain gauge belt that measures the change in length as the body moves, and acoustic or radar devices that listen without touching the patient. All are defeated by movement, coughing, poor electrode contact, and the irregular breathing of a partly obstructed patient. The common error is to accept a displayed rate when the underlying waveform is noisy. Alarm limits and the apnea interval used must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

In the field the respiratory rate is measured by a watch and a hand, because the health worker places a hand on the chest or the abdomen, watches the rise and fall, and counts for a full minute. There is no device to fail and no battery to die, but there is also no alarm to call attention to a patient deteriorating between visits, so a small notebook and the observation chart record the value and the time. A pediatric counting aid or a simple timer supports the count, and a pulse oximeter with a respiratory rate display can supplement it when it works. What the field gives up is automation, and what it demands is the discipline to count every time and compare the number with the previous one, and escalation thresholds must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Every instrument in this chapter touches the patient, and every one can carry organisms from one person to the next, so reusable cuffs, thermometer probes, oximeter probes, and device surfaces must be cleaned between patients with an agent compatible with the materials. Single-use items, including probe covers, adhesive oximeter wraps, capnography sampling lines, water traps, and chemical dot thermometers, are used once and discarded, and a clinic that reuses them trades a small saving for an infection. Consumables must be stored within their stated temperature range, rotated so that the oldest are used first, and checked for expiry before they reach the patient, because a thermometer without a cover or an oximeter without a probe is out of service. Storage conditions, cleaning agents, contact times, and the compatibility of each product with each device must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Maintenance of monitoring equipment is mostly batteries, calibration, and inspection, and it is cheap compared with the cost of a missed deterioration. Batteries must be charged on a schedule, tested at the start of a shift, and replaced before they fail, because a monitor that dies during a transport is worse than no monitor. Devices with a calibration requirement, including automated blood pressure monitors, capnographs, and some infrared thermometers, belong on a documented schedule with results written in a log. The failure modes to hunt are the silent drift of a gauge, a cracked cuff, an intermittent cable, a blocked sampling line, and a flat battery, and calibration intervals, acceptable error, and the self-test procedure must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Before any device in this chapter is used on a patient, the operator must check a short list, because the cuff must match the limb, the device must rest at zero or pass its self-test, the probe must be clean and the right size, covers and wraps must be in place, and the battery must be charged. Alarm limits must be set to the patient rather than left at the factory setting, the patient must be identified, and the technique must be the one the device assumes. The reading must be compared with the patient and with the previous reading rather than copied onto the chart, and any value that contradicts the clinical picture must be repeated with a different device or site before it is acted on. Every threshold, interval, and limit named in this chapter is a starting point that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

To summarize, the instruments of vital sign monitoring are physical transducers first and clinical tools second, and a listener who understands the expansion of mercury, the deformation of a metal element, the oscillations inside a cuff, the absorption of two wavelengths of light, and the absorption of infrared by carbon dioxide can predict how each one will fail. The hospital-grade versions assume calibration services, clean power, a stock of covers and sampling lines, and staff trained to set alarm limits deliberately, while the field versions assume none of that and demand consistency, spare batteries, careful technique, and the honesty to record an approximate value as approximate. Every device carries a hazard, a characteristic alarm, and a habitual operator error, and safe use begins with the operator looking at the patient rather than at the number. The trend and the combination of signs are more informative than any single measurement, so no setting, pressure, temperature, or alarm threshold should be taken from memory, because each must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.
