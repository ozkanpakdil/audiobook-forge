# Chapter 53 — Rehabilitation and Physiotherapy

Rehabilitation equipment restores function by rearranging force rather than by killing a microbe or replacing a molecule. A cane, a brace, and a prosthetic foot take a load that a damaged limb can no longer carry and pass it to a structure that can. A therapy unit adds energy to tissue as an electrical current or a mechanical vibration, and the tissue answers with a sensation, a contraction, or a warming. The therapist is a technician of leverage, alignment, and dose, and the equipment makes a correct dose repeatable. This chapter covers walking aids, wheeled chairs, orthoses, prostheses, therapy units, exercise frames, and measuring tools, first in a hospital and then as they are made and repaired far from a workshop.

Every walking aid begins with the relationship between the base of support and the center of gravity. A standing person is stable when a vertical line dropped from the center of mass falls inside the area bounded by the feet. Walking is a controlled fall caught by the next foot before balance is lost, and a damaged limb, a painful joint, or a weak muscle reduces the force available to catch it. An aid enlarges the base of support, adds a point of contact that shares the load, or carries force to the floor through a rigid column instead of through the leg. The height of that column and the condition of its tip decide whether the device helps the patient or betrays the patient.

The cane is the simplest column, a single shaft with a handle and a rubber tip. Its handle usually sits at the wrist crease with the arm relaxed, leaving the elbow bent about 20 to 30 degrees, a range that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Holding the cane on the side of the weak leg is the most common operator error, because the cane belongs in the hand opposite the weak limb where it forms a tripod with the affected foot. The tip is a consumable that hardens, wears flat, and loses its grip on a wet floor, and a worn tip is a fall waiting to happen. An axillary crutch is set so its pad rests about 5 centimeters below the fold of the armpit, a distance that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The safety hazard of that type is pressure in the armpit, where the radial nerve and the axillary vessels pass, and prolonged pressure produces numbness, a weak wrist, and the palsy that carries the crutch's name. A forearm crutch with a metal cuff is lighter but demands more arm strength, and the operator checks the gap, the elbow angle, and the rubber tips before every walk.

The walker converts the patient into a four legged structure, which is why it goes to the person with the poorest balance. The frame widens the base of support in front of the body, so a forward lean is caught by the frame rather than by a single stick. A rollator adds wheels so the frame is pushed instead of lifted, and its height follows the rule of the wrist crease, a setting that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The safety hazards are a wheel that rolls because the brake was not set, a folding hinge that collapses because it was not locked, and worn tips that slide on a polished floor. The characteristic failure is a stretched brake cable, which leaves the lever feeling firm while the wheel turns freely, and the common error is to load the frame before the brakes are set. The operator rocks the frame, tests both brakes, and inspects the tips and locking clips before the patient stands.

The manual wheelchair is a chair on two large rear wheels and two small front casters, and its behavior comes from the wheel and axle. A large wheel climbs a threshold more easily because each turn carries it further, while a small caster accelerates more easily. The seat width is chosen so the hips clear the side frames by about 2 centimeters on each side, and the depth so the front edge stops just behind the knee crease, with both measurements confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Moving the rear axle forward makes the chair easier to push and more likely to tip backward, so almost every frame carries an anti tip device and a pair of wheel locks. The wheel lock is a parking brake, because it holds a chair already at rest and must never be relied on to slow a moving chair on a slope. The common error is to transfer a patient into a chair whose locks are loose or whose armrest is raised, so the check before each transfer is the locks, the anti tip devices, the upholstery, and the footrests.

Maintenance is what keeps a wheelchair rolling, and it is a small and unglamorous routine. Tire pressure is checked with a gauge rather than by thumb, because a soft tire increases rolling resistance and damages the rim, and the correct pressure must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The spokes are squeezed in pairs to find the loose ones, the axles are turned by hand to feel for rough bearings, and the wheel locks are adjusted to bite the tire when engaged. The common errors are to inflate from a compressor without a gauge, to leave the chair in the rain, and to ignore a front caster wobble that grows into a seized bearing. The consumables are tires, inner tubes, bearings, hand rims, armrest pads, footrests, and upholstery, and a workshop that holds them keeps a chair in service for years. The operator confirms the tires and locks each day, and the technician inspects the frame and axles at an interval that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The cushion under the patient is a clinical device, because the physics of pressure injury is pressure multiplied by time. Sitting drives body weight through a small area over the ischial tuberosities, and the soft tissue is compressed between bone and seat until its blood supply closes. A cushion spreads the load over a larger area and reduces shear, the sliding force that stretches and closes small vessels. Foam cushions are cheap, gel cushions stay cooler, air cell cushions float the pelvis on many small pockets, and a contoured cushion keeps the pelvis from sliding forward. The common errors are to substitute an ordinary pillow, to over inflate an air cushion until it feels firm like a ball, and to cut a hole in a cushion in the belief that it removes pressure over a sore. The cover is cleaned between patients, the foam is checked for collapse, and any cleaning agent and its contact time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The powered wheelchair adds motors, a controller, and a battery to the same frame. Electrical energy in the battery becomes torque in the motors, so the controller sends a current proportional to the joystick deflection and the motors answer with a defined force. Most chairs run on a 24 volt battery pack, and the charging voltage and time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The characteristic alarm is the low battery warning, usually a repeated beep with a flashing indicator, and the common error is to ignore it until the chair stops on a slope with the patient aboard. Sealed lead acid batteries release hydrogen while charging and must be charged in a ventilated place away from a flame, while lithium packs carry a fire risk if punctured, overheated, or charged with the wrong charger. The operator checks the battery gauge, the joystick, the motors, the freewheel levers, and the tires before every journey, and the chair is secured with four point tie downs in a vehicle.

An orthosis is a shell that holds a body segment in a chosen position, and its action is explained by three point fixation. A force is applied at the apex of the deformity and two opposing forces on the far side, so the segment is held in a corrected line and the brace replaces the pull of a muscle or a ligament. Materials are chosen for stiffness, weight, and the ability to be shaped, and they include plaster, thermoplastic sheet, polyethylene, polypropylene, carbon fiber, aluminum, steel, leather, canvas, and foam. A custom brace is made over a positive model of the limb, which is poured from a cast taken with the limb corrected, and the heated sheet is vacuum formed over that model before the edges are smoothed and the straps and hinges are fixed. The forming temperature varies with the material and must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The safety hazards are pressure over a bony prominence, which can produce a sore that a patient with impaired sensation does not feel, and a joint held in the wrong position until a contracture sets in. The common error is to apply a brace over a wrinkled sock, because a fold becomes a hard ridge under load.

The orthoses are named for the joints they cross. An ankle foot orthosis controls the ankle so the toes clear the ground during the swing phase of walking, and it is used when the foot drops. A knee ankle foot orthosis extends the shell to the thigh with a knee hinge, and it is used when the knee is unstable or the limb must be protected from weight bearing. A spinal orthosis limits a movement, unloads a vertebra, or reminds the patient to keep the spine in a safe range. The fitting check is the skin at every edge, the circulation beyond the brace, and the position of the joint. The common errors are a shell too tight at the calf, a hinge placed away from the axis of the knee, and a strap tightened to hide a bad fit rather than to hold the shell.

A prosthesis replaces a missing part, and the socket is where the physics becomes clinical. The residual limb is held in a rigid cup, and body weight passes through soft tissue to the socket wall, so the shape of that wall and the distribution of load decide whether walking is comfortable or impossible. A total surface bearing socket spreads the load over the whole limb, while a patellar tendon bearing socket concentrates it on tolerant areas and relieves sensitive ones. Suspension holds the socket on the limb with suction through a one way valve, a pin that locks into a liner, a silicone sleeve, or a belt around the waist. A liner is a soft interface of silicone, gel, or foam, and woven socks are added in thin layers as the limb shrinks after amputation. The common error is to add several socks at once, which lifts the limb and produces a piston motion that damages the skin.

The components above and below the socket decide the quality of the gait. A prosthetic knee may be a single axis joint, a polycentric linkage that folds in a more natural path, or a microprocessor knee that measures load and angle many times each second and adjusts the resistance to bending. A prosthetic foot may be a solid ankle cushion heel design that mimics the roll of the foot, or an energy storing foot of carbon fiber that returns energy at push off. The pylon joins the foot to the knee or socket, and the adapters allow the angle and rotation to be adjusted. Alignment is set on the bench, standing still, and walking, and the therapist reads the gait for a lean, a pelvic drop, a wide step, or a rotation that betrays a wrong angle. The characteristic problem is a limb a few millimeters too long or too short, and every alignment value must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A prosthesis needs daily care, and the skin is the instrument that reports its condition. The liner is washed with a mild soap, rinsed, and dried completely, because sweat and salt left on the surface irritate the skin and degrade the gel. The socket is wiped out and allowed to dry, the suspension sleeve and valve are checked for tears and a proper seal, and the foot and knee are inspected for cracks and loose bolts. The safety hazard is a fall from a loss of suspension or a locked knee, and the common error is to keep walking on a red and painful limb because the prosthesis feels too good to leave off. The volume of the limb changes through the day, so socks are adjusted in thin layers, and the correct thickness and wearing schedule must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Therapeutic electricity is applied through electrodes on the skin, and the physics begins with current and resistance. A current passing through tissue can depolarize a nerve, and the result depends on the amplitude and shape of the pulse rather than on total energy. The gate control theory explains one common use, because comfortable sensory impulses carried by large fibers reach the spinal cord and reduce the transmission of pain signals carried by small fibers, so transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation floods that gate with sensation. A motor level current instead depolarizes the motor nerve and produces a visible contraction that retrains a weak muscle. Typical settings include a pulse width of 50 to 250 microseconds and a frequency of 80 to 120 hertz, and all of these values must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The characteristic alarm is a contact warning when an electrode has lifted and the current density under the remaining pad has risen.

The hazards of electrical therapy are burns, skin irritation, and unexpected muscle contraction, and most come from a poor electrode or an impatient operator. Two electrodes of different sizes concentrate current under the smaller one, a dried out pad raises resistance and heats the skin, and a metal implant, a pacemaker, or an implanted defibrillator can carry current along an unintended path. Electrical stimulation is avoided over the carotid sinus and throat, over the eyes, over the abdomen in pregnancy, over infected skin, and across the chest of a patient with a pacemaker, and each contraindication must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The electrode pad is a consumable, and its adhesive gel dries, collects dirt, and stops conducting evenly, so it is replaced when it no longer sticks. The common errors are to raise the amplitude before the patient is ready, to reuse a pad that has lost its gel, and to leave a connected patient without a clear view of the control.

Ultrasound therapy turns electricity into mechanical vibration, and the conversion happens in a ceramic crystal that changes shape when a voltage is applied to it. That oscillation passes into tissue through a coupling medium, producing a gentle deep warming from absorption plus a mechanical stirring called cavitation that moves fluid and may alter the behavior of cells. The frequency decides how deep the energy reaches, with about 1 megahertz for deeper tissue and about 3 megahertz for a superficial target, and the exact frequency and intensity must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Intensity is prescribed in watts per square centimeter, commonly from 0.5 to 2, and treatment may run from 5 to 10 minutes, with both values requiring confirmation against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The transducer is kept moving in slow overlapping circles, because a stationary head deposits its energy in one place and can burn the periosteum, and the common errors are a head held still and a treatment started with no coupling gel. Ultrasound is avoided over a pregnant uterus, a malignancy, the growth plates of a child, the eyes, the gonads, a pacemaker, and a blood clot, and these exclusions must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Other modalities fill the same role with different physics. Shortwave diathermy uses a radiofrequency field to heat deep tissue by making ions and water molecules rotate, and its hazard is a burn from a cable that touches the skin or from metal in the field. A paraffin bath warms a hand or foot by conduction, with the wax held at about 50 to 54 degrees Celsius, a temperature that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Cryotherapy removes heat with ice or cold packs, and the safety hazard is a cold burn from prolonged contact, so a barrier cloth is always used. Intermittent compression inflates a sleeve around a limb in cycles to move edema, and traction pulls the spine with a measured force, both requiring settings that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The characteristic alarm on a motorized unit is an obstruction or overload signal, and the common error is to leave a patient attached while the therapist leaves the room.

The exercise equipment of a rehabilitation gym is built around controlled weight bearing and controlled movement. Parallel bars are two rails at hand height that let a patient practice standing and stepping while both hands take part of the body weight, and they are set at the wrist crease, a setting that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A standing frame supports a patient upright with straps and a table, allowing weight to pass through the bones as a mechanical stimulus and a rehearsal for standing tolerance. A tilt table raises a patient from lying toward standing in measured steps, letting the circulation adapt gradually for someone who cannot yet stand. The safety hazards are a fall in blood pressure during the tilt, a patient who faints against the straps, and a frame that moves during a transfer, so the patient is strapped at the chest, hips, and knees. The common errors are to leave the tilt table wheels unlocked and to leave a strapped patient unattended, and the rate of tilt and maximum angle must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Measurement is the other half of the department, because a treatment that cannot be measured cannot be justified or corrected. A goniometer is a protractor with two arms, and it measures a joint angle when the fulcrum sits over the joint axis and the arms lie along the fixed and moving segments. A tape measure records limb girth, length, and swelling from a fixed landmark with the same tension each time, so numbers can be compared across visits. A hand dynamometer measures grip strength by compressing a spring or a hydraulic cell, and a volumetric tank measures the water displaced by a swollen hand as the most sensitive test of edema. Outcome measures turn function into a number, such as a timed walk over a set distance or a count of the activities a patient can perform without help. The common errors are to measure from a different landmark each time and to record a number that flatters the patient, and the landmark and testing position must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The consumables of a rehabilitation department are quiet but constant, and a shortage stops treatment as surely as a broken machine. Electrical therapy needs self adhesive electrode pads, lead wires, and conductive gel, and ultrasound needs coupling gel, paper towels, and transducer covers. Brace and prosthetic work consumes plaster bandage, stockinette, thermoplastic sheet, padding foam, leather, rivets, buckles, hook and loop straps, adhesive, and thread. Wheelchairs consume tires, tubes, bearings, brake cables, and upholstery, and walking aids consume rubber tips of several diameters. Cleaning is a clinical act, because the same plinth, exercise bar, and cushion are used by many patients in one day, and shared equipment is a surface that hands, skin, and clothing touch. Hard surfaces are wiped with detergent and then with a disinfectant, and the surface is kept wet for the contact time stated on the label, which must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Fabric is the difficult item, because a cover may be wiped while the foam inside cannot be washed, so a fluid resistant cover and a barrier sheet are used, and electrical equipment is never sprayed or immersed.

Maintenance here is mostly inspection and adjustment rather than repair of electronics, and its greatest enemy is the assumption that a simple mechanical device cannot fail. The daily round covers the rubber tips, brake levers, wheel locks, straps, buckles, hinges, electrode leads, cables, and the battery gauge of every powered item. The scheduled round covers wheel bearings, caster stems, spoke tension, upholstery, frame bolts, the tilt table lock, and the calibration of the therapy timer at an interval that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A workshop holding tires, tubes, bearings, tips, straps, and fuses returns most equipment to service the same day, and every repair is recorded with its date, part, and the person who did it. The safety hazard is a device returned to the ward without a test, and the common error is to tighten every bolt on a wheelchair so hard that the frame cracks at the hole. Any device that has been dropped, soaked, or involved in a fall is taken out of service and inspected before it is used again.

The field version begins with what is absent, because the constraint shapes every choice. Electricity may come only from a solar panel, clean water may be a walk away, plaster of Paris may be the only molding material, and no spare part may lie within a day's travel. The clinician chooses not between a good device and a lesser one, but between a device that can be maintained locally and no device at all. A locally made brace is built from plaster, wood, leather, canvas, metal strip, or heated plastic pipe, and each material has a character the maker must respect. Plaster is cheap and molds directly on the limb, but it is heavy, softens when wet, and cannot be adjusted once set, while leather and canvas are repairable but stretch and trap moisture. A metal strip from a barrel hoop can be bent into a rigid upright, and a plastic pipe heated in water can be flattened into a shell, though the temperature must be judged by eye. The common errors are a brace made too tight because the limb will swell, a rivet or cut edge left where it presses the skin, and a fit done without teaching the patient where to look for redness, and the review interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The wheelchair that survives a village is usually built from bicycle and motorcycle parts, because those parts already have a supply chain. A rough terrain chair may run on three wheels with a bicycle front wheel and two rear wheels so it can be pushed along a dirt path, and a hand powered tricycle may use a chain and crank so the rider propels it with the arms. The advantages are enormous, because a punctured tube, a worn tire, a snapped cable, and a dry bearing can all be repaired by the mechanic who fixes bicycles in the market. What is given up is adjustability, light weight, and sophisticated seating, since the frame is welded to one size and the seat is often a wooden board or a canvas sling without a pressure relieving cushion. What is demanded instead is a stock of bicycle tires, tubes, bearings, and spokes, a pump and a set of cone spanners, and a mechanic taught how the wheel locks and anti tip devices work. The safety hazards are a missing lock, a soft tire, a sharp edge on the seat, and a frame cracked at a weld, and the operator checks all of them before the chair leaves the clinic.

A field prosthesis is a compromise between a good socket and a material that can be shaped without a workshop. The common approach is to take a plaster cast of the residual limb, pour a positive model, and form a socket in polypropylene over that model, which yields a socket fitted to the individual limb with only a heat source and simple tools. Alignment is then set by eye and by watching the patient walk rather than with a measurement jig, so the prosthetist looks for a lean, a pelvic drop, a wide step, and a rotation, and adjusts the angle, height, and rotation of the foot in small steps. What is given up is the precision of a modern alignment system and the durability of a laminated frame, and what is demanded instead is a longer fitting session, repeated visits as the limb changes shape, and a supply of liners, socks, and suspension materials. The common errors are to fit a socket before the swelling has settled, to leave a rough edge inside the socket, and to send the patient home without a written appointment for the first review. The interval between reviews and the sock schedule must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The therapist who improvises is not practicing a lesser medicine but a different logistics, and the principles of dose and safety still govern. When a therapy unit fails or the current is off, treatment becomes exercise, manual therapy, heat from a hot water bottle wrapped in cloth, cold from a river, and a graded program of walking and strengthening with weights made from bottles filled with sand. A parallel bar becomes two lengths of pipe lashed to a frame, a plinth becomes a firm table, and a mirror becomes the visual feedback that a hospital would provide with an electrode and an electromyography, or EMG, display. The safety hazards are a hot water bottle that burns a numb limb, an improvised weight that falls on a foot, and unsupervised walking practice near a step or a drain, so the therapist stays within arm's reach and checks the skin after every session. Transfers of heavy patients without a hoist also injure the therapist's back, so a mechanical lift or a sliding sheet and enough hands are used instead. Every improvised device is recorded with its material, its dimensions, and the date it was checked, and the supervision interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

To summarize, rehabilitation equipment restores function by changing the way force moves through the body, so a cane widens the base of support, a brace applies three point fixation, a socket spreads the load of walking, and a therapy unit delivers a measured dose of electrical or mechanical energy. In a well-resourced hospital these devices come with casting ovens, vacuum forming, microprocessor knees, calibrated ultrasound, and a maintenance workshop, and they depend on a steady supply of pads, gel, straps, tires, bearings, plaster, and thermoplastic sheet. In a field clinic the same principles are met with plaster, leather, heated pipe, bicycle parts, hot water, and the hands of a therapist who improvises, and the price of that improvisation is a longer fitting, a closer follow up, and a constant demand for local repair. Whichever setting applies, the operator checks the tips, the brakes, the straps, the hinges, the electrodes, the gel, and the skin before use, and every setting, pressure, temperature, energy level, treatment time, and alarm threshold named in this chapter must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A device that is inspected, measured, documented, and cleaned keeps a patient walking, while a device assumed to be safe because it is simple will eventually fail under the weight of a person who trusted it.
