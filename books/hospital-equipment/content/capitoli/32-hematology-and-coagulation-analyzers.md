# Chapter 32 — Hematology and Coagulation Analyzers

A hematology analyzer turns a tube of blood into numbers, and a coagulation analyzer times how long that blood takes to clot. Both are counting and timing instruments before they are clinical instruments, and a listener who understands the counting and the timing will recognize a wrong result before it harms a patient. This chapter describes the hospital laboratory versions first, with their reagents, waste, calibration, and maintenance, then the field versions, which are a microscope, a hand centrifuge, a hematocrit reader, and a careful pair of eyes. Every setting, temperature, incubation time, centrifuge speed, alarm threshold, and reagent expiry named here must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Blood is a suspension rather than a solution, and every measurement in this chapter depends on that fact. The cells are denser and less conductive than the plasma, they settle when left standing, and they change as soon as the sample leaves the vein. A tube for a full blood count contains a dried salt that removes calcium and stops clotting, and because that salt is fixed in amount, the tube must be filled to its marked line to keep the proportion correct. A short-filled tube is over-anticoagulated, an overfilled tube may clot, and a clotted tube can block the analyzer and produce a falsely low count, so the anticoagulant type and fill volume must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The commonest counting principle is electrical impedance, which is easiest to picture as a narrow gate in a stream. The blood is diluted in a conducting salt solution and drawn through a small aperture with an electric current passing across it. A cell conducts poorly compared with that solution, so as it passes it displaces conducting fluid and briefly raises the resistance of the path, and that rise appears as a voltage pulse counted as one cell. The pulse height depends on how much fluid the particle displaced, so it is read as a measure of cell volume. The aperture diameter, the current, and the counting thresholds must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Two cells passing the aperture together produce one large pulse instead of two small ones, an error called coincidence, and the sample is wrapped in a sheath of the same salt solution so the cells are forced into single file along the center of the aperture. Platelets are counted in the same pass as the red cells but at a lower size threshold, so cell fragments, debris, or bacteria in the platelet window are counted as platelets. When an aperture begins to clog, the flow slows, the pulses distort, and the count falls, which is the moment to stop and clean rather than repeat the sample. The aperture size, the coincidence limits, and the platelet threshold must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Many modern analyzers identify cells by flow cytometry, in which the diluted sample is squeezed into a single stream that passes one cell at a time through a focused beam of light. Forward scatter increases with the size of the cell, side scatter increases with the complexity of the cell interior, and fluorescence adds a third signal because stains that bind to nucleic acid or to particular proteins glow when the beam strikes them. Combining size, granularity, and fluorescence lets the instrument sort the white cells into their families and print a differential. The laser power, stain volumes, and gating boundaries must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Hemoglobin is not counted but measured by light, in a method called photometry. A measured volume of blood is mixed with a reagent that bursts the red cells and releases the hemoglobin, and the same reagent converts it into a stable colored compound whose intensity is proportional to the amount present. A lamp shines through the mixture at a chosen wavelength and a detector on the far side measures how much light survives the passage, so the darker the color the less light reaches the detector, and a cloudy or lipemic sample can bias the reading unless a blank is measured first. The wavelength, the reagent volume, and the conversion factor must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The analyzer is built around its sampling system, and the aspirating probe is the part the operator touches most. In open mode the probe is lowered into an uncapped tube and draws a measured volume; in closed mode it pierces the cap of a sealed tube, which protects the operator from aerosol but requires the correct cap and depth. The probe tip is wiped clean between samples, because a drop of one patient's blood carried into the next tube gives a wrong result to two patients at once, and the tubing that carries the sample onward must be free of kinks and air. The probe depth, the aspiration volume, and the carryover wash volume must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A full blood count analyzer consumes three or four liquids, and confusing them is a classic way to ruin a run. The diluent is the salt solution in which cells are counted, the lysing reagent destroys the red cells so the white cells and hemoglobin can be measured, the sheath fluid keeps cells in single file inside a flow cytometer, and a cleaning solution removes protein residue from the tubing and aperture. Each has a storage temperature, an expiry date, and a limit on how long it may sit on the instrument, and a warm or expired reagent drifts results without any obvious alarm. Everything it aspirates it must also discard, and that waste is a biohazard disposed of by the local infectious waste route, while some reagents are corrosive or contain cyanide, so the waste may be chemically hazardous as well as infectious. The storage temperatures, expiry rules, and waste handling must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The machine does not simply print numbers; it attaches flags, and the flags are the part that demands judgment. A flag may mean that a value falls outside a reference range, that the instrument could not classify the cells confidently, or that two measurements disagree. A delta check compares today's result with the patient's previous result and flags a change too large to be explained by biology, which is often the first sign that the wrong sample was loaded. Suspect flags for immature cells, abnormal lymphocytes, or fragments are the instrument's request for a stained film, and the operator reads them, decides which need a microscope, and records that decision, with the flag rules confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The commonest operator error with a full blood count analyzer is to trust a result from a bad sample, usually a clotted or short-filled tube that someone decided to run anyway, or to mix the sample by shaking or let it stand for hours so that platelets clump or cells swell. The hazard that follows is a transfusion or a treatment decision made on a false number, and the error is invisible once the tube is discarded. The characteristic alarm is a clog or flow error, and a machine that alarms repeatedly on the same sample should be cleaned and the sample re-collected rather than forced through again. The acceptable sample age, the mixing method, and the action after a clog alarm must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Daily maintenance of a full blood count analyzer is short but not optional, and it is the operator's work rather than the engineer's. The probe is wiped, the instrument is run through a cleaning cycle, and a background count is performed with diluent alone, which should return counts near zero; a rising background is the earliest warning of a dirty aperture or an expiring reagent. The waste is emptied, reagent levels are checked, and the previous run's flags are reviewed before the first patient sample, while at longer intervals the aperture is cleaned or replaced, the peristaltic pump tubing is changed before it flattens, and the instrument is calibrated. A neglected analyzer usually does not stop; it drifts, which is far more dangerous than a failure, and the cleaning cycle, background limits, and parts replacement intervals must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A result is only a number until it is proved, and the proof is a calibrator and a control. A calibrator has values assigned by a reference method, and calibrating anchors the instrument's calculations to a known sample, while a control has a known range and is run every day at a normal level and at least one abnormal level. The control values are plotted on a chart, and a value outside the accepted range, a run of values on one side of the mean, or a sudden shift all mean that patient results cannot be released until the cause is found. Calibration is repeated after a major part change, a change of reagent lot, or a failed control, and the calibrator values, control ranges, and acceptance rules must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

When the analyzer raises a suspect flag, the reference method is still the microscope. A drop of blood is spread along a glass slide in a thin wedge, dried, and stained so that the white cells can be told apart by the color and shape of their nuclei and granules, and a technologist classifies a fixed number of white cells, traditionally 100, while noting the size and shape of the red cells and the platelets. A mechanical differential counter, a row of keys or a rotating dial that tallies each cell type, is still found on many benches because it prevents arithmetic errors. The film also reveals what no counter can, including malarial parasites inside the red cells, which is why the microscope has never been retired, and the stain quality and counting rules must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Before automated counters, every cell count was done in a counting chamber, and the method is still taught because it is the fallback when the machine fails. The chamber is a thick glass slide with a ruled grid of known area and depth, so the volume above each square is fixed, and a measured volume of blood is diluted by a known factor, sometimes in a fluid that also lyses the red cells when only the white cells are wanted. The mixture is drawn under a cover glass, the cells in a defined set of squares are counted under the microscope, and the count is multiplied by the dilution and divided by the volume counted to give a concentration. The errors are almost always in the dilution, the filling, or the distribution of the cells, and an overfilled or dried chamber gives a wrong answer with great confidence, so the chamber depth, dilution factor, and counting rules must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The erythrocyte sedimentation rate is the oldest hematology test still in daily use, and its principle is gravity. A tall narrow tube of fixed bore is filled with anticoagulated blood and stood perfectly vertical, and the distance the red cell column has fallen in 1 hour is the result, measured in millimeters. The rate depends on how readily the red cells stack into rolls, which depends on the proteins coating them, so a rise in fibrinogen or in the globulins makes the cells settle faster while anemia and a low temperature slow them. The test is crude and nonspecific, but it is cheap and sensitive to inflammation, and the tube dimensions, standing time, temperature, and reading method must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Coagulation testing rests on a different physical principle, because the endpoint is not a count but a moment in time. Plasma is separated from the cells by centrifugation, and a reagent is then added that starts the clotting cascade while the instrument watches for the instant fibrin appears. The cascade is a chain of enzymes normally held in check, and each test bypasses part of the chain so that a defect in one region can be isolated; adding calcium restores what the anticoagulant removed, and adding a phospholipid surface or a tissue factor substitute supplies what the reaction needs. The time from the addition of the starting reagent to the formation of fibrin is the result, and the reagent volumes, incubation temperature, and starting point of the timer must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

One way to detect that fibrin has formed is to feel for it, and the mechanical method does exactly that. A small metal ball, a wire loop, or a plastic flag is moved through the plasma mixture, and the instrument senses the change in its motion when the mixture thickens from a liquid to a gel, while the magnetic method instead drives a small bead with a moving field and records the endpoint when fibrin traps it. These methods are robust in the presence of lipemia, jaundice, or slight hemolysis, because they respond to the physical change rather than to the light. Their weakness is that bubbles, a short sample, or a strand of fibrin from a badly collected tube can stop the moving part early and shorten the time, so the bead size, movement amplitude, and detection threshold must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The optical method detects the clot by watching the light instead. A beam passes through the plasma mixture to a detector, and as fibrin strands form the mixture changes from clear to cloudy, so the light reaching the detector falls or the scattered light rises, and the instrument records the time at which that change crosses a set threshold. This method is gentle on the sample and lets several tests run at once from a small volume, but it is easily fooled by anything else that changes the clarity of the plasma. Severe lipemia, deep jaundice, much free hemoglobin, or a very anemic sample can all move the optical signal and produce a wrong time, so the path length, wavelength, and detection threshold must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The prothrombin time measures the tissue factor pathway and is the standard way of monitoring therapy that reduces the activity of the vitamin K dependent clotting factors. A tissue factor preparation and calcium are added to the plasma, and the time to clot is recorded in seconds, so a prolonged time means that one or more of those factors is deficient or that an inhibitor is present. Because tissue factor preparations differ in sensitivity, the result is converted into an international normalized ratio, which compares the patient's time with the mean normal time and corrects for the reagent's sensitivity using a value assigned to that reagent, and this is the number used to adjust the dose of a vitamin K antagonist drug. The assigned sensitivity value, the mean normal time, and the therapeutic range must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The activated partial thromboplastin time measures the contact pathway instead. A contact activator and a phospholipid substitute are added to the plasma, the mixture is incubated, and calcium is added to start the reaction, after which the time to clot is recorded; it screens for defects in the early part of the cascade and monitors therapy that enhances the action of antithrombin. The fibrinogen test measures the amount of the final raw material rather than the time, usually by adding a high concentration of thrombin to diluted plasma so that the time depends almost entirely on how much fibrinogen is present. A thrombin time adds thrombin alone and is prolonged by heparin or by a fibrinogen abnormality, and the reagent concentrations, incubation time, and reference ranges must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Platelet function testing asks a different question, not whether the clotting factors are present but whether the platelets respond. In one method platelets in plasma are stirred and a trigger substance is added, and the instrument watches the mixture become less cloudy as the platelets clump, because clumped platelets scatter less light than single ones; in another the clumping is detected electrically, because platelets sticking to electrodes change the current between them. A simpler screen draws whole blood through a small aperture coated with a trigger and measures how long the platelets take to plug it, which is called the closure time. These tests are sensitive to the age of the sample, to the anticoagulant, and to drugs that block platelets, so the trigger substances, sample age limits, and reference values must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A coagulation analyzer is a small instrument compared with a full blood count analyzer, but it depends even more on its consumables and its temperature. It needs water of good quality to reconstitute frozen or lyophilized reagents, a refrigerator to store them, and a warm station held near body temperature for the reaction, usually 37 degrees Celsius, which must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Reagents are reconstituted in exact volumes, allowed to reach working temperature, and used within a stated time, because a partly degraded reagent lengthens or shortens every time it produces, and a blocked pipette biases every result in the same direction. Calibrators and normal and abnormal controls are run daily, and the control ranges and reconstitution rules must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The commonest operator error in coagulation is to use a sample that is short-filled or collected into the wrong tube, because the ratio of blood to anticoagulant decides the result. An underfilled tube carries too much anticoagulant for the plasma and prolongs the time, while a tube that sat too long or was mixed badly may hold a small clot that consumes fibrinogen and shortens it. Drawing blood from a line that is running heparin, or filling the coagulation tube after a tube with another additive, contaminates the sample and produces a result that reflects the line rather than the patient. The characteristic alarm is a clot detection error or a short sample warning, and the correct response is a new sample drawn properly, with the fill volume, sample age, and collection order confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The centrifuge is the quiet workhorse of both hematology and coagulation, and its principle is the one that makes sediment settle in a river, made faster. Spinning a tube around a central axis subjects its contents to a force much greater than gravity, so denser material moves outward while lighter liquid stays nearer the center, and the force depends on the rotor radius and the square of the speed. Tubes are arranged in balanced pairs opposite one another and of equal weight, because an unbalanced load sets up vibration that can damage the rotor and throw a tube from its bucket. That vibration can crack a tube and release an aerosol of blood into the rotor chamber as the operator leans over it, so the lid is interlocked and must never be defeated. The speed, running time, rotor radius, and rotor life limit must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The consumables of this chapter are unglamorous and decisive. They include blood collection tubes of the correct size and additive, needles and holders, capillary tubes for microhematocrit and manual counts, glass slides and cover glasses, a Romanowsky type stain and its buffers, a counting chamber with its cover glass, cuvettes for coagulation, calibrators and control materials, cleaning solutions, and the disinfectant for the bench. Every one has a storage condition, an expiry date, and a rule about how long it may be used after opening, and stock is rotated so the oldest is used first, with every reagent lot number recorded with the control results so that a bad lot can be traced. The storage temperatures, expiry dates, and open-vial limits must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

In a mobile clinic or a frontier hospital, this whole laboratory may be one wooden box and a bench by a window. The field version is a light microscope with a mirror or a battery lamp, a hand driven or small electric centrifuge, a hematocrit reader card or a ruler, a staining rack, and a supply of slides, capillary tubes, and diluting fluids. It gives up the automated count and everything that comes with it, including the machine differential, the flags, and the speed, so a single full blood count may take 30 minutes or more of trained attention, and what it demands instead is a good stain, a clean microscope, a steady hand, and a person who has counted enough cells to know when a number is plausible. The light source, centrifuge speed, and stain times must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The commonest field measurement is the hematocrit, and it can change a decision within minutes. A plain capillary tube is filled about three quarters full with blood, one end is sealed, and the tube is spun in a microhematocrit centrifuge for a fixed time, usually 5 minutes, which must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The tube is then laid on a reader card with a scale, and the height of the packed red cell column is read against the total column of blood, with the thin pale buffy coat of white cells and platelets ignored but noted when it is unusually thick. The common errors are an incompletely sealed tube, a tube not filled to the right length, too short a spin, and reading the level at an angle, so the fill length, spin time, and reader scale must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

When the field clinic needs a white cell count or a differential, it falls back on the counting chamber and the stained film. The dilution is made with a pipette or a small measured tube, the chamber is charged, and the cells in the ruled squares are counted under the microscope, with the arithmetic done by hand and checked twice. The film is spread, dried, fixed, stained, and examined, and 100 white cells are classified one by one while the red cells are examined for shape, size, and parasites. The limits are real, because a count from one chamber, a film stained in humid air, and a sedimentation tube on a vibrating table all produce numbers that deserve caution, so the dilution, staining times, and counting rules must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Coagulation in the field is harder, because it is a timed test and time is what a busy clinic has least of. The manual method uses a small water bath or a heated block to hold the plasma and reagent near body temperature, a tube, a stopwatch, and a wire loop or wooden stick to lift the mixture and watch for the first thread of fibrin. The reagent is added at a recorded moment, the tube is tilted or the loop lifted repeatedly, and the endpoint is the first time the mixture no longer drips but forms a gel, so the result depends on the operator's eye, on the temperature of the bath, and on how evenly the mixing is done. This is why field coagulation is usually limited to tests that change immediate care, and the bath temperature, reagent warm-up time, and observation interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

What the field version gives up is precision, speed, and the automatic check that catches an impossible result. What it demands is a trained person who can look at a film and see what is there, a reliable light source, clean water for stains and reagents, and a stock of unexpired consumables that survives heat and dust. The safety hazards are sharper in the field than in the hospital, because there is usually no biological safety cabinet, no sharps container at every bench, and no dependable infectious waste route, so blood is handled at arm's length, sharps go into a rigid puncture proof container, and the bench is disinfected after every session. The microscope is the fragile and expensive part, and it must be carried in a padded case, kept dry, and cleaned only with lens tissue, so the disinfectant concentration, sharps disposal, and microscope storage conditions must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

To summarize, hematology and coagulation analyzers are counting and timing instruments whose results rest on the sample, the reagent, and the daily proof that the machine is still correct. The hospital analyzer counts cells by impedance or by light scatter, measures hemoglobin by photometry, flags what it cannot classify, and depends on the operator to clean it, run the controls, and send a film to the microscope when the flags demand it. The coagulation analyzer detects the moment fibrin forms by mechanical, magnetic, or optical means, and it is only as reliable as the tube that was filled correctly and the reagent that was reconstituted correctly. The centrifuge, the microscope, the counting chamber, and the hematocrit reader are the same devices in the hospital and in the field, and in both settings the rule is the same: the number is not the patient, a doubtful result must be confirmed before it changes care, and every setting, temperature, time, and threshold used must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.
