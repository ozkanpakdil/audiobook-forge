# Chapter 23 — Cardiac Surgery and Circulatory Support

Cardiac surgery is where a hospital stops treating the heart as an organ to be measured and starts treating it as a machine it can stop, open, and repair. The heart and lungs cannot be repaired while they work, because stopping them starves the brain of oxygen. Cardiopulmonary bypass borrows their function, draining venous blood, adding oxygen and removing carbon dioxide outside the body, and returning blood to the arterial tree at a flow the tissues accept. The heart-lung machine performs that borrowed circulation, and the balloon pump, the assist device, and the membrane oxygenator used as a support all descend from its logic. Every setting, pressure, flow, temperature, and alarm threshold named here must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The circuit begins with gravity, because venous blood drains without a pump if the reservoir sits low enough. A cannula in the great veins joins wide tubing, and the falling column of blood creates a siphon that empties the venous system as fast as the heart would. Some circuits assist with a gentle vacuum whose level must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. From the reservoir the arterial pump pushes blood through the oxygenator and heat exchanger and returns it through an arterial cannula into the aorta. The reservoir is the safety buffer, because its visible level shows whether drainage and return are balanced. If it empties while the pump turns, air is forced into the aorta, the classic lethal accident of bypass, and the level sensor and its low level alarm exist to prevent it, with the alarm threshold confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Two kinds of pump are used, and they fail differently. The roller pump squeezes flexible polyvinyl chloride (PVC) tubing with 2 or more rollers against a curved track, so each roller pushes a fixed slug of blood forward and output depends on rotation speed and tubing diameter. The centrifugal pump spins a cone or impeller that throws blood outward, so its flow falls as resistance rises, it generates less dangerous pressure, and it damages red cells less. Adult flow is commonly 2.2 to 2.4 liters per minute for every square meter of body surface, or roughly 4 to 6 liters per minute for an adult, and that target must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The characteristic alarm is the low flow or low pressure warning, and the common error is to blame the pump for a kinked or clamped line, so the first check is the tubing between patient and reservoir.

The oxygenator brings blood and gas together across a thin membrane without letting them mix. It holds thousands of hollow fibers finer than a human hair, through which gas flows while blood passes outside, so oxygen diffuses in and carbon dioxide out. A blender sets the oxygen and air mixture, the sweep gas flow controls carbon dioxide removal, and the oxygen fraction controls how much oxygen enters. Because the porous fibers become wet, a plasma film limits working life to hours, and lost gas exchange, rising pressure, or visible clot signals failure, with gas flow, oxygen fraction, and replacement interval confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The heat exchanger keeps blood and water apart with a thin metal wall while heated or cooled water moves heat into or out of the blood. Cooling to 28 to 32 degrees Celsius lowers metabolic demand and buys time for the repair, and that temperature must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Warming toward 37 degrees Celsius restores the body before separation, and that target must equally be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The hazard is heat, because blood warmed above about 42 degrees Celsius can be damaged and dissolved gas can come out of solution in the brain if rewarming is too fast, and that limit must also be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The characteristic alarm is the over temperature or water level warning, and the common error is to set a blood temperature while forgetting to fill and test the water bath.

The tubing circuit is the patient's temporary vasculature, and every connection in it can bleed or admit air. Arterial tubing is narrow and thick walled for pressure, venous tubing is wide and floppy for gravity, and confusing the 2 during assembly is fatal. Blood returns through an arterial cannula in the ascending aorta, commonly 20 to 24 French, while venous blood drains through a two stage cannula in the right atrium reaching the inferior vena cava, or through 2 separate cannulae, commonly 34 to 46 French. Small additional cannulae carry a vent, deliver cardioplegia, or provide retrograde flow, and their sizes, insertion depths, and pressure limits must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A connector left partly seated, an open stopcock, or a forgotten clamp looks like a pump failure and is really a plumbing failure.

Suction is the least glamorous part of the circuit. Spilled blood is collected by a cardiotomy line, filtered, and reinfused, which reduces the need for donated blood but returns the dirtiest blood of the case, carrying fat, bone fragments, and air. A vent line empties the heart so that it does not distend while the aorta is clamped. Entrained air can froth the blood, and if a wrong connection lets it reach the arterial side it can embolize to the brain, so suction always returns to the venous or cardiotomy reservoir and never to the arterial line, and the suction pressure and its filter must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The common error is running cardiotomy suction over a dry field, which pumps air into the reservoir and destroys blood cells for nothing.

Before the circuit is joined to a patient it must be filled with fluid, because an empty circuit connected to a living body would fill itself with the patient's blood. This filling is the prime, usually 1 to 2 liters of balanced crystalloid with a colloid, mannitol, or bicarbonate. The prime dilutes the blood, because more than a liter of clear fluid mixed with a child's whole blood volume lowers the hematocrit sharply, so the perfusionist must decide whether that dilution is tolerable or the prime must contain blood. The composition, volume, acceptable hematocrit, and the decision to add blood products must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The characteristic alarm during priming is the low level warning, and the common error is connecting the arterial line before the bubble detector is tested.

Cardioplegia makes the repair possible by deliberately arresting the heart in a relaxed state. A solution high in potassium, commonly 20 to 30 millimoles per liter, depolarizes the muscle and holds it in diastole, so the heart stops consuming oxygen when its blood supply is taken away, and that concentration must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The solution is usually cold, about 4 degrees Celsius, sometimes mixed with the patient's own blood to carry oxygen, and that temperature must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Delivery is antegrade into the aortic root or coronary openings, retrograde through a cannula in the coronary sinus, or both, because a diseased artery may not carry the solution where it is needed. The initial dose is commonly 10 to 20 milliliters per kilogram, repeated every 15 to 20 minutes, and those figures and the route must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The characteristic failure is a heart that begins to beat before the repair is finished, and a blocked coronary artery can leave a region warm, beating, and ischemic.

The perfusionist operates the machine, and the skill is a specialty because the machine has no closed loop that knows what the patient needs. The console shows pump flow, arterial and venous pressures, reservoir level, blood temperature, and venous oxygen saturation. The electrocardiogram (ECG) is watched for returning electrical activity, the arterial line for the pressure the tissues receive, and the hematocrit, blood gases, electrolytes, and activated clotting time are measured at intervals. The activated clotting time measures how well heparin is working, and a target of greater than 400 to 480 seconds during bypass must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The surgeon, the anesthetist, the perfusionist, and the nurses work from a shared picture of the circuit, and the safety check before bypass is read aloud. The characteristic alarm is a slow drift in venous saturation or a rising arterial pressure, and the common error is to treat the monitor as the patient.

Blood that touches plastic clots, so every circuit demands anticoagulation for as long as it runs. Heparin is given before the arterial cannula is opened, commonly 300 to 400 units per kilogram, and the dose is confirmed by the activated clotting time rather than by calculation alone, because patients vary widely. Some are resistant and need much more, while others with a low platelet count or recent heparin exposure may react badly, and recognition of resistance must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. At the end of the case protamine binds and neutralizes the heparin, commonly about 1 to 1.3 milligrams for every 100 units given, a ratio that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The hazard of reversal is a reaction to protamine itself, which can drop the blood pressure, cause a rash, or narrow the pulmonary vessels catastrophically, so the patient is watched rather than the syringe. The characteristic warning here is not a sound but a falling blood pressure as the protamine begins.

The heart-lung machine is the visible part of a much larger installation, and that installation separates a cardiac center from a hospital that would merely like one. It needs a dedicated theatre with directed airflow, water for the heat exchanger, vacuum for drainage, medical oxygen and compressed air, and power that will not fail while the patient's whole circulation runs through plastic. The console therefore sits on an uninterruptible power supply (UPS) and the essential theatre circuits on a generator, with battery time and generator start delay confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The heating, ventilation and air conditioning (HVAC) system must hold a temperature and humidity that suit the patient and the electronics, confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A blood bank within minutes, a laboratory open at any hour, a sterilization service, and an intensive care unit (ICU) with ventilators and dialysis belong to the same requirement. Spare pump heads, roller assemblies, pressure modules, temperature probes, and level sensors fail on their own schedule, and a program without a stocked spares shelf cancels cases for one cracked part.

The consumables of bypass are almost all single use, and one missing item stops the case. The packaged tubing set, the oxygenator with its heat exchanger, the venous and cardiotomy reservoirs, the arterial line filter, and the cardioplegia set with its delivery cannula come first. Then the cannulae, arterial, venous, retrograde, vent, and suction tips, each in a range of sizes and each wrapped separately. The priming crystalloid, colloid, mannitol, bicarbonate, heparin, cardioplegia concentrate, and potassium chloride are drugs as much as supplies, and the pressure transducer sets, temperature probes, activated clotting time cartridges, blood gas syringes, and sampling tubing are just as necessary. Every item has an expiry date and a storage condition, and expiry, storage temperature, and replacement stock level must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The commonest cause of a cancelled operation is an expired oxygenator found during setup, so the pack is checked against the written list before the patient is anesthetized.

Cleaning the heart-lung machine is mostly a matter of what is discarded and what is carefully wiped. Everything that touched blood is thrown away after the case, including tubing, oxygenator, reservoirs, filters, and cannulae, because no hospital can guarantee removal of protein and clot from the fine channels of an oxygenator. What remains is the console, its pump heads, pressure modules, temperature probes, and water circuit, wiped with the approved disinfectant after every case. The water side of the heat exchanger is drained, flushed, and disinfected on the maker's schedule, because stagnant warm water grows biofilm that can reach the next patient through a pinhole in the membrane. Reusable metal parts such as stylets, clamps, and holders go to the central sterile services department (CSSD) for cleaning, inspection, packing, and steam sterilization at 134 degrees Celsius for the specified cycle, and that cycle, the cleaning agent, its dilution, and its contact time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The common error is wiping the console while leaving the pump raceway, where dried blood hides beneath the rollers, untouched.

Maintenance of a heart-lung machine is scheduled, documented, and done between cases, because there is no safe moment to test it during one. The pump heads are calibrated so displayed flow matches delivered flow, and a roller pump is tested for occlusion, because a roller that no longer compresses the tubing delivers less blood than the screen claims. The battery is tested under load, alarms are provoked deliberately, pressure modules are compared against a reference gauge, and temperature probes are checked in a water bath. The gas blender, level sensor, bubble detector, and servo water heater each have a recorded test, and the test interval and pass limits must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The classic failure modes are a fractured pump boot, a cracked connector, a seized roller bearing, a probe reading 2 degrees high, and a battery holding minutes rather than the hour its display suggests. The most dangerous failure is silent, a bubble detector that no longer detects, because it looks identical to a working one until it is needed, and only the written record proves the checks occurred.

The intra-aortic balloon pump is a different kind of support, and its principle is timing rather than flow. A long, slender balloon sits in the descending thoracic aorta just below the left subclavian artery and is inflated and deflated in step with the cardiac cycle. Inflation in diastole raises aortic pressure as the coronary arteries fill, so the heart muscle receives more blood, while deflation just before systole lowers the pressure and lets the ventricle empty against less resistance. The result is more oxygen delivered to the muscle and less work demanded of it, which is why the device is used in cardiogenic shock and in a heart that cannot leave bypass. The balloon is triggered by the ECG or the arterial waveform, and early or late timing can reduce coronary flow instead of increasing it, while the gas is helium because it is light and flows quickly through narrow tubing. The trigger mode, inflation and deflation timing, and inflation ratio must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The characteristic alarm announces a helium leak or blood in the gas line, and it is a genuine emergency, because a ruptured balloon can release helium into the bloodstream and leave fragments in the aorta. The operator stops the pump and leaves the balloon deflated or removes it as the protocol directs, and that protocol must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Other alarms warn of a lost trigger, a low battery, an autofill failure, or a catheter out of position, and each has its own first action. The ordinary hazards are limb ischemia from the catheter obstructing the femoral artery, bleeding, infection, and a fall in the platelet count, and the common operator error is to leave the patient sitting upright, which kinks the catheter and defeats the timing. Balloon volume, commonly 25 to 50 milliliters, and catheter size, commonly 7 to 8 French, along with the exact limits for the patient, must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Consumables are the catheter, helium cylinder, safety disk, extension tubing, sterile sleeve, and pressure transducer, and the helium cylinder is the item most often forgotten, so its pressure belongs in the daily check.

A ventricular assist device is a pump implanted or worn to take over part of the work of a failing ventricle. The usual implanted form draws blood from the apex of the left ventricle and returns it to the ascending aorta, driven by a small continuous flow pump that spins without pause, so the patient may have no palpable pulse and the blood pressure must be assessed by other means. An external or paracorporeal form leaves the pump outside the body with cannulae crossing the skin, and is used when support is expected to be short or the chest is still open. Every form has a controller worn on a belt or shoulder strap that sets pump speed and manages alarms, and a pair of batteries giving several hours away from the mains, with pump speed, battery life, and the exchange routine confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The driveline, the cable crossing the skin to carry power and control signals, is the permanent weak point, because it is a foreign body in constant contact with the outside world. The characteristic alarms are low flow, low battery, controller fault, and driveline disconnect, and each demands a response rehearsed with the patient and the family.

Driveline infection defines long term assist device care, beginning as redness or discharge at the exit site and progressing along the cable toward the pump. The dressing is changed with sterile technique on a fixed schedule, the site is inspected at every change, and any change is reported at once, with that schedule and the dressing materials confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Anticoagulation is required, usually with an agent measured by the international normalized ratio, and both the target ratio and the testing interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The other hazards are stroke from clot in the pump, bleeding from anticoagulation, right heart failure when the left ventricle is unloaded, and a suction event in which the pump pulls the ventricular wall into its inlet and flow falls. The daily operator checks are the controller log, speed setting, battery charge, driveline dressing, and the patient's weight and symptoms, and the common error is changing a setting without recording it. The patient and the family are trained as operators, because in the community the first person to hear an alarm is not a nurse.

Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation is the heart-lung machine turned into a support that runs for days or weeks rather than hours, and the difference is endurance rather than principle. A centrifugal pump, a large membrane oxygenator, and tubing coated to resist clotting form a circuit managed at the bedside, draining blood from a large vein and returning it to a vein or an artery. The venous to venous form supports the lungs alone when the heart works but the lungs cannot oxygenate, while the venous to arterial form supports lungs and heart together in cardiogenic shock or after cardiac arrest. Peripheral cannulation through the groin avoids opening the chest, but oxygenated blood may be delivered backward up the aorta while the heart ejects poorly oxygenated blood toward the brain, so a saturation monitor on the right hand watches that competition. Anticoagulation is still needed despite the coating, and balancing a clotting circuit against a bleeding patient is the central daily problem, with the target and the monitoring test confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The characteristic alarms are low flow, low saturation, and rising pressure across the oxygenator, and a rising pressure signals clot forming in the fibers.

The complications of extracorporeal membrane oxygenation are the complications of all extracorporeal circulation multiplied by time: bleeding, hemolysis, infection, neurological injury, and failure of the oxygenator itself. The oxygenator has the shortest life, and its replacement is planned rather than improvised, with the replacement interval confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The consumables are the circuit, oxygenator, cannulae, tubing, connectors, anticoagulation monitoring cartridges, and dressing materials, and the circuit is generally discarded rather than reprocessed. Where any component is reused, the cleaning, disinfection, and sterilization cycle must be defined by the maker and confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and a component that cannot be validated is not reused. The common operator error is to clamp a line during a change without checking which line it is, because clamping the arterial return even briefly can raise pressure enough to rupture the circuit and can stop the patient's only circulation.

It must be said plainly: none of this equipment travels, and a frontier clinic cannot perform cardiac surgery. The heart-lung machine weighs hundreds of kilograms, needs clean water, medical gases, mains power, a blood bank, a laboratory, and a sterile processing service, and is useless without a surgeon, an anesthetist, and a perfusionist beside it. The intra-aortic balloon pump console and the assist device controller demand a supply chain of helium, batteries, catheters, and disposables that no mobile clinic can carry. Even extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, the most portable of these technologies, needs a trained team, reliable oxygen, anticoagulation monitoring, and a receiving intensive care unit before it helps anyone. The correct field position is not to improvise a heart-lung machine but to recognize the cardiac emergency, buy time with simple measures, and move the patient to where the machine exists. A book that pretended otherwise would send a clinical officer looking for a pump that is not there while the patient dies of a problem that could have been referred.

What the field clinic does instead is stabilization and referral, and the measures are ordinary even though the disease is not. Oxygen by mask or nasal cannula, the sitting position for a breathless patient, a diuretic given intravenously for pulmonary edema, morphine for pain and anxiety, and nitrates for ischemia are the tools used anywhere, and every dose must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Anemia is corrected with transfusion where blood is safe and available, infection is treated with antibiotics, and recurrences of rheumatic fever are prevented, because rheumatic heart disease remains a common reason for a young patient to need a valve operation. A handheld ultrasound probe or a portable ECG can show the problem, but neither changes the fact that definitive treatment is surgical. The patient is kept warm, watched for the signs of shock, and moved as soon as transport can be arranged.

The referral pathway is a piece of equipment in its own right, and it works only if it is built before it is needed. The clinic must know which hospital takes cardiac patients, how to reach the on call team, what information that team needs, and what the patient must bring, all written where the night staff can find it. The referral note carries the history, examination, ECG, any imaging, the drugs already given, and the patient's weight, and the receiving team decides whether the patient travels by road, by air, or not at all. A patient in cardiogenic shock may need a clinician on the ambulance, a defibrillator, oxygen, and drugs for the journey, and that equipment belongs to the transport service rather than the clinic. In a few systems a mobile extracorporeal team travels to the referring hospital, places the circuit there, and carries the patient back on support, which is the only form in which a heart-lung machine truly travels. That service needs a call made early, a place to land, a power supply, and a receiving bed, and the call is the part the field clinic controls.

What the field demands instead of the machine is training, protocol, and an honest decision made early. The staff must recognize the difference between heart failure that can be managed and heart failure that needs surgery, read a rhythm strip, place an intravenous line safely, and say out loud that this patient must go. The clinic must keep oxygen, suction, a defibrillator, drugs, and referral documents ready and test them on the hospital's schedule, with checks recorded and intervals confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A simple pericardial drainage set and a trained operator can relieve a tamponade that would otherwise kill a patient before transport, and that is the one cardiac procedure a district hospital may reasonably attempt. The hardest skill is not technical: it is the willingness to refer a patient whose family expected treatment to happen here.

To summarize, cardiac surgery and circulatory support rest on one borrowed idea, that blood can be drained from the body, oxygenated outside it, and returned at a flow the tissues accept, and every machine in this chapter is a variation on that idea. The heart-lung machine is the complete version, with its roller or centrifugal pumps, membrane oxygenator, heat exchanger, tubing, cannulae, and prime, and it is only as safe as the perfusionist, the anticoagulation, and the reservoir level that sustain it. The intra-aortic balloon pump augments the patient's own heart by timing, the ventricular assist device replaces part of it with a continuous flow pump and a driveline that must never be allowed to infect, and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation stretches the same circuit from hours to weeks of support. All of them demand clean water, medical gases, reliable power, sterile consumables, a laboratory that never closes, and trained staff, and none is portable in any sense a frontier clinic can use. The field clinic's equipment for these diseases is oxygen, a diuretic, an antibiotic, an ultrasound probe, a referral note, and the discipline to send the patient early.
