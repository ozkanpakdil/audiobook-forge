# Chapter 14 — Oxygen Systems

Oxygen is a drug, and in most hospitals it arrives in a heavy steel cylinder rather than a paper packet, so the person who gives it must also understand a machine. The physical principle is a gradient, because oxygen crosses from the alveolus into the blood only when its partial pressure there is higher than in the blood returning to the lungs. Room air contains about 21 per cent oxygen, which is enough for a healthy person at rest but not for a lung that is blocked, flooded, stiff, or poorly perfused, so the patient breathes faster to compensate. Adding oxygen to the inspired gas raises the alveolar partial pressure, restores the gradient, and buys time while the underlying problem is treated. Every device here, from the bulk tank to the smallest nasal prong, exists only to control that fraction and to deliver it at a flow the patient can tolerate.

The gas must be obtained, stored, and moved, and pressure is what makes this possible. Oxygen is held as a compressed gas in steel or aluminum cylinders, as a cryogenic liquid in an insulated vessel, in a piped supply generated centrally, or in a concentrated stream made at the bedside from room air. A compressed cylinder is filled to roughly 2000 pounds per square inch, a value that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A gas follows a simple relationship, because pressure multiplied by volume stays constant when the temperature does not change, so gas squeezed into one tenth of its volume exerts ten times the pressure. When the valve opens the gas expands toward atmospheric pressure and rushes out, and the regulator tames that rush into a steady, measured flow.

Cylinders come in a family of sizes, chosen by how the gas will be used rather than by clinical preference. The smallest hold a few hundred liters and are carried by hand for resuscitation or transport, the middle sizes sit on a trolley, and the largest stand nearly a meter tall and are chained to a wall. Manufacturers and national systems name them differently, sometimes by a letter and sometimes by the water capacity in liters, and that capacity is the multiplier that converts pressure into usable gas. Every cylinder must carry its own identity, which means the gas name, the concentration, the batch, the filling pressure, and the date of the last hydraulic pressure test. Color coding of the shoulder and body is a useful first glance, but conventions differ between systems and are no substitute for reading the label.

The top of the cylinder carries the valve, and this is where most handling errors begin. The valve is a metal block screwed into the neck, with a stem that opens and closes the gas path, an outlet port, and a safety disc that bursts if internal pressure rises beyond the design limit, which must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Large cylinders open with a handwheel and smaller ones with a key or spanner, and the rule is to open slowly, because a sudden blast of high-pressure gas can ignite any trace of oil it meets. A cylinder stored in the cold may read low until it warms, and one left on its side can discharge debris when opened carelessly, so the valve should be closed and the cap replaced before the cylinder is moved. The connection to the regulator is protected by the pin index system, in which two holes on the valve face match two pins on the yoke, and oxygen has its own positions so that a regulator for another gas cannot be seated. The seal is a compressible washer that must be present, intact, and free of grease, because worn pins, removed pins, or a wrongly filled cylinder all defeat the interlock. If a regulator does not seat easily, the operator must stop and find out why rather than forcing it.

The regulator and the flowmeter turn storage into therapy. The regulator uses a diaphragm and a spring to reduce high cylinder pressure to a low working pressure, and a well-made unit does this in two stages so that flow does not drift as the cylinder empties. The high-pressure gauge reads what is left, while a second gauge or the flowmeter reads what is going to the patient. The common flowmeter is a tapered tube with a small ball inside, and the gas lifts the ball to a height proportional to the flow. The ball is read at its top edge, the tube must be held upright and calibrated for oxygen alone, and the setting must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The duration of a cylinder is calculated. The full contents in liters equals the water capacity in liters multiplied by the filling pressure in bar, or in customary units the pressure in pounds per square inch divided by 14.7 gives the number of atmospheres. A simpler field method uses a cylinder factor, a fixed number belonging to each size, so that minutes remaining equals the gauge pressure in pounds per square inch multiplied by that factor and divided by the flow in liters per minute. A medium cylinder with a factor of about 0.3 and a gauge reading of 2000 pounds per square inch delivering 5 liters per minute would last roughly 120 minutes, a figure that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The operator should write the start time and the flow on the cylinder tag, because the calculation assumes a full cylinder and an accurate flowmeter, and both assumptions deserve suspicion.

Because oxygen supports combustion, the handling rules around cylinders are not decoration. Oxygen does not burn, but it lowers the temperature at which other materials ignite and makes any fire burn hotter and faster. Oil and grease are the classic enemies, and a single fingerprint of lubricant on a valve, a regulator, or a tool can start a fire when gas is released quickly. Smoking, open flames, and electrical sparks must be kept away from every cylinder and every oxygen-enriched surface. Cylinders must be stored upright and secured with a chain or strap, separated from flammable gases such as acetylene or hydrogen, and during transport the valve must be closed and the cap in place. An oxygen-enriched room is one in which clothing, bedding, and hair burn with terrifying speed, so ventilation and the removal of ignition sources matter as much as the equipment.

The piped oxygen system replaces the bedside cylinder with a wall outlet and is the standard of care in any large hospital. The gas comes from a central source, which may be a bulk liquid oxygen tank with its own evaporator, a manifold of large cylinders, or an on-site generating plant, and the source is duplicated so that one failure does not stop the supply. A typical pipeline delivers a working pressure of about 50 pounds per square inch to the terminal units, a value that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The gas travels through copper or stainless steel pipe installed and tested under standards that forbid contamination with oil or debris, and a second source is kept ready to take over automatically or by a simple valve change. The whole system is monitored, because the failure of a pipeline is a silent event until a patient needs oxygen.

The terminal unit at the wall is where the clinician meets the pipeline, and it is built so that the wrong device cannot be plugged in. The outlet is a gas-specific socket, and the connector is keyed by a diameter index safety system that admits only the matching gas. A quick-connect fitting locks with a quarter turn, and the flowmeter hanging below it is usually the same tapered tube with a ball. Some outlets supply air or vacuum as well as oxygen, and the three look similar enough that the index system is the only reliable distinction. The operator must check that the flowmeter is seated, that gas flows when the valve is opened, and that the ball moves freely, with the setting confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The piped system carries its own alarms, and the clinician must know what they mean before they sound. An area alarm panel near the ward entrance and a master alarm at the engineering desk monitor pipeline pressure and, in some systems, oxygen concentration. A low-pressure alarm sounds when the supply falls below the set threshold, and a high-pressure alarm sounds when it rises above the design limit, and both thresholds must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The characteristic sound is a repeating electronic tone with a flashing indicator, and the correct response is not to silence it but to establish whether patients are receiving gas and to switch to cylinders. The alarm does not always mean the supply has failed, because a closed valve, a leaking terminal unit, or a maintenance isolation can produce the same reading.

Between the pipeline and the cylinder stands the oxygen concentrator, which makes its own oxygen from the air in the room. The active material is a synthetic zeolite, a mineral with a vast internal surface of tiny pores that trap nitrogen molecules in preference to oxygen. A compressor pushes filtered room air into one of two sieve beds until the zeolite is saturated with nitrogen, and the oxygen-rich gas that passes through is collected in a reservoir. While the first bed loads, the second is vented to the atmosphere and releases its nitrogen, and the two beds alternate every few seconds so that the flow never stops. This cycle is called pressure swing adsorption, and its output is not pure oxygen but a stream of about 90 to 96 per cent, a value that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A concentrator is a small factory with filters, moving parts, and its own failure modes, and it earns its place only when it is maintained. The intake filter sits behind the cabinet and collects dust and lint, and a clogged filter reduces flow and raises the working temperature. The sieve beds have a finite life and slowly lose capacity, so the delivered concentration falls before the machine stops entirely. The compressor wears, the cooling fan clogs, and internal tubing can develop a leak that lets the product gas escape. A concentrator also has alarms for low concentration, low flow, power failure, and high temperature, and the exact thresholds must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The concentrator is unusually sensitive to its environment, and its great enemies are dust and altitude. Dust clogs the intake filter and contaminates the sieve beds, so a machine used in a dusty setting needs its filter cleaned far more often than the manual suggests. Altitude matters because the concentrator works on the percentage of oxygen it can extract from the air, and although it still produces about 90 per cent oxygen, the total amount the patient receives at a low flow is lower when the air is thin. Heat and humidity shorten the life of the compressor and encourage condensation in the tubing. The tubing itself adds resistance, and a very long line reduces the flow that reaches the patient, so its length and internal diameter must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Liquid oxygen is used by large hospitals and by patients who need a portable supply with a high capacity for its weight. Oxygen becomes a pale blue liquid at about minus 183 degrees Celsius, a temperature that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and in that state it occupies a small fraction of the volume of the gas. It is stored in a double-walled vacuum-insulated vessel, and a little is allowed to boil off through a coil so that pressure builds and pushes the gas to the point of use. The bulk tank stands outside the building and connects to the pipeline, and a portable unit can be filled from it for a patient going home. The hazards are cold and concentration, because spilled liquid causes frostbite and the boiling gas enriches the surrounding air, and their maintenance belongs to trained engineering staff rather than the ward.

Once the gas reaches the patient, the delivery device decides how much of it enters the lungs, and the physics divides these devices into two families. Low-flow devices, such as the nasal cannula and the simple face mask, provide less gas than the patient inhales, so the rest is drawn from room air and the inspired concentration depends on how deeply and how fast the patient breathes. High-flow devices, such as the venturi mask and heated high-flow nasal therapy, provide more gas than the patient can inhale, so room air is excluded and the concentration is fixed by the device. This distinction explains why the same flow produces different concentrations in different patients and why a breathless patient may receive less oxygen than the flowmeter suggests. The operator should choose the device by the concentration required rather than by habit, and should confirm every flow and concentration against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The nasal cannula is the most common oxygen delivery device in the world, and it is two soft prongs on a light tube under the nose. Flows of 1 to 6 liters per minute are typical, a range that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the inspired concentration rises from roughly 24 per cent at 1 liter per minute to about 44 per cent at 6 liters per minute. The rule of thumb is that each liter per minute adds about 4 per cent, but the figure varies with the breathing pattern and is only an approximation. The nasopharynx acts as a small reservoir that stores oxygen between breaths, which is why the cannula achieves more than the flow alone suggests. The prongs must point into the nostrils rather than against the septum, and a cannula left kinked under a pillow or a shoulder is a common and invisible cause of desaturation.

The simple face mask covers the nose and mouth and holds a small volume of gas in front of the face, but it has no reservoir and no valves. Flows of 5 to 10 liters per minute are used, a range that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the inspired concentration usually falls between 35 and 60 per cent. The mask must never be run below about 5 liters per minute, because at a low flow the patient re-breathes carbon dioxide collected in the mask and the dead space becomes harmful. The reservoir mask, often called a non-rebreather, adds a soft bag that holds oxygen between breaths, and one-way valves stop exhaled gas from entering it. Flows of 10 to 15 liters per minute are used, a range that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the concentration can exceed 80 per cent when the mask seals well. The bag must be seen to fill before the mask is placed on the face and must not collapse completely, because an empty bag means the patient is pulling room air. The valves are thin plastic flaps that can stick or invert, and a mask whose valves are wet or distorted will not do its job.

The venturi mask solves the problem of variable performance with a physical effect described in the eighteenth century. When gas is forced through a narrow orifice its speed rises and its pressure falls, and the low-pressure jet drags room air in with it, a process called entrainment. The mask has a jet of fixed size and an entrainment port of fixed size, so the ratio of oxygen to air is fixed and the concentration is fixed as long as the total flow exceeds the patient's peak inspiratory flow. The usual jets give concentrations of about 24, 28, 31, 35, 40, and 60 per cent, and each value must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The operator error is to mix parts of different masks, to block the entrainment ports, or to run the device below the minimum flow marked on the jet.

Heated high-flow nasal therapy takes the cannula idea and adds flow, heat, and humidity, and it has become the bridge between simple oxygen and mechanical ventilation. A blender mixes oxygen and air to a chosen concentration, a heater warms the gas to near body temperature, and a humidifier saturates it with water vapor, so flows of up to 60 liters per minute can be tolerated through a soft nasal interface, values that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Because the flow exceeds the patient's inspiratory demand, the concentration is stable, and the high flow also washes carbon dioxide out of the upper airway and provides a small positive pressure. The device demands care, because the circuit must be drained of condensate, the temperature must be set within the maker's range, and the interface must not injure the nose. Water in the circuit and an overfilled humidifier are the commonest faults.

Humidification and nebulization are the two ancillaries that keep the upper airway from drying and deliver medication to it. A bubble humidifier is a bottle of sterile water that the oxygen passes through on its way to a mask or cannula, and it adds water vapor but very little heat. Heated humidifiers are used with high-flow therapy and with ventilators, and they use a heated plate and a temperature probe to control the water content of the gas. Nebulizer drivers are small compressors, or the oxygen flow itself, and they push gas through a jet that breaks a liquid drug into a mist the patient inhales. All of these share the same consumables, which are the water chamber, the tubing, the mask or mouthpiece, and the filters, and their flow and temperature must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Oxygen is a drug with a dose and a toxicity, and the goal is always the lowest concentration that achieves the target. Prolonged breathing of a high concentration can injure the lung directly, causing chest discomfort, cough, and diffuse damage resembling acute lung injury, and it can wash nitrogen out of the alveoli so that they collapse, a condition called absorption atelectasis. At very high partial pressures, as in a hyperbaric chamber or a diving accident, oxygen can cause central nervous system toxicity with visual changes, twitching, and seizures. In the premature newborn, excessive oxygen can damage the developing retina and cause lifelong visual loss, which is why the target in that group is deliberately narrow. For most adults the target is an arterial oxygen saturation between 94 and 98 per cent, a range that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, with lower targets for some patients who retain carbon dioxide.

Fire is the hazard that makes oxygen different from every other drug, and the rules that prevent it are absolute. Oxygen is an oxidizer rather than a fuel, but a fire in an oxygen-enriched atmosphere burns far hotter and faster, and materials that would normally smolder can flare. Grease, oil, and petroleum jelly must never touch a cylinder valve, a regulator, a flowmeter, or the face of a patient receiving oxygen, and alcohol-based hand rub must dry completely before the hands go near a mask. Smoking and open flames are forbidden wherever oxygen is in use, and electrical equipment must be in good repair, because a spark from a worn plug is enough. The diathermy machine, also called the electrosurgical unit, is a particular danger because it produces sparks and heat, and oxygen should be discontinued for as long as is clinically safe before a procedure near the airway, with the decision confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Static electricity can also ignite a fuel in an enriched atmosphere, so bedding and clothing should not be rubbed near a cylinder.

The common operator errors in oxygen therapy are few, and they are repeated in every hospital. The first is to run out of gas, which happens when no one calculates cylinder duration or checks the gauge at the change of shift. The second is to deliver the wrong concentration, which happens when a low-flow mask is used for a patient who needs a controlled fraction or when the flowmeter is read at the wrong part of the ball. The third is to lose the gas between source and patient, through a kinked tube, a disconnected humidifier, a leaking washer, or a mask sitting on the chin. The fourth is to forget the backup, so that a power failure stops a concentrator, and the fifth is to silence the alarm, which hides the information it was designed to give.

The check that prevents these errors follows the gas from source to patient, and it is repeated before every use and at every shift. The cylinder or wall outlet must be identified as oxygen, the gauge or flowmeter must be read and recorded, and the flow must be set and confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The regulator or flowmeter must be seated without a leak, the tubing must be free of kinks and cracks, and the delivery device must match the concentration required. The humidifier, if used, must hold sterile water and sit below the level of the patient, and the saturation, respiratory rate, and work of breathing must be observed together. The spare cylinder, tubing, and mask must be within reach, and the alarm must be audible and tested if the system has one.

Consumables in oxygen therapy are simple, cheap, and constantly used, and their management is what keeps the therapy safe. Nasal cannulas, simple masks, reservoir masks, venturi masks, and their tubing are usually single-patient items, replaced at the interval the maker and the local protocol specify, and a soiled or cracked device is replaced at once rather than cleaned for reuse. Humidifier chambers and sterile water are single-use, and refilling a chamber rather than replacing it is a common source of infection. The reusable parts, including the regulator, the flowmeter, the cylinder valve, and the concentrator cabinet, are wiped with a cloth dampened with a compatible disinfectant, and the intake filter is cleaned or replaced according to its type. Reusable components meant to be reprocessed must be cleaned first and then disinfected or sterilized by a validated method, with the cycle confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

In a mobile or frontier clinic the oxygen system is built from what can be carried, powered, and repaired, and it gives up a great deal. The usual configuration is a portable oxygen concentrator charged by a solar panel during the day and by a battery at night, with a small cylinder as the reserve for resuscitation and transport. The concentrator is lighter and inexhaustible compared with cylinders, but it delivers a limited flow, its concentration falls with altitude and dust, and it stops when the battery is empty. A pulse-dose conserver releases a bolus only when the patient inhales, which stretches the oxygen in a cylinder several times further, but it requires a nose-breathing patient and it fails silently in a mouth breather. Cylinders are refilled by a cascade from a larger supply, and the motorcycle that carries them straps them upright in padded panniers with the valve closed and the cap fitted. The field system demands a maintenance routine that a hospital can delegate and a clinic cannot, with filters cleaned weekly, batteries cycled, tubing inspected, a written log kept, and every setting confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

At the destination the equipment must be assembled in a clean, shaded place, with the concentrator on a hard surface and the tubing kept off the ground. Heat, rough roads, and altitude all raise the pressure inside a cylinder and stress the valve, so a cylinder that has been dropped must be inspected before it is refilled. A carrier that takes two cylinders allows one to be in use and one in reserve, which is the difference between a functioning service and an interrupted one. The field clinician should also carry a self-inflating resuscitation bag as the fallback that works with no gas, no power, and no battery. Every pressure and flow used in this setting must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the reserve must never be spent on anything but a patient.

To summarize, oxygen therapy is a chain that begins with a source under pressure and ends at the patient's nose, and each link has its own physics, its own consumables, and its own failure. A cylinder stores gas by pressure and is emptied by flow, so its duration must be calculated and its identity, seal, and valve checked. A pipeline delivers gas from a central source through keyed outlets and monitored alarms, and its silence must never be mistaken for its health. A concentrator makes oxygen from room air through a zeolite sieve, it depends on electricity, clean filters, and a realistic flow setting, and a liquid system stores oxygen cold and demands trained handling. The delivery device decides whether the concentration is variable or fixed, and the venturi mask and heated high-flow nasal therapy are the tools of precision. Fire safety and the rules against grease, oil, and sparks apply to every one of these systems, and in the field the same chain is rebuilt from a concentrator, a solar panel, a battery, and a motorcycle with cylinders. Whatever the setting, the discipline is the same: identify the gas, calculate the supply, set and confirm the flow, watch the patient, and keep a reserve ready.
