# Chapter 64 — Dressings and Wound Care Supplies

A dressing is not a cover laid over a wound but a controlled environment built on top of it. Broken skin has lost its barrier, and the wound beneath is a wet, warm, active surface that both needs protection and produces fluid. The task of a dressing is to hold that surface at the right moisture, the right warmth, and the right protection while the body repairs itself. Every material in this chapter answers three physical questions, which are how fast water vapor passes through it, how much fluid it absorbs, and how firmly it sticks to what lies beneath. Once those behaviors are clear, the choice follows from the wound rather than from the shelf.

Repair proceeds through overlapping phases, and each imposes a different demand on the material. Bleeding stops first as platelets and fibrin seal the broken vessels, and inflammation follows as white cells clear debris and bacteria, producing the warm, wet, red wound that a dressing must tolerate rather than suppress. New tissue is then built as fibroblasts lay down collagen and capillaries grow into the wound bed, and the scar matures over months. A wound that stays too dry forms a hard crust and heals slowly, while one that stays too wet softens and its edges break down. The target is a moist but not soggy wound bed, and the dressing is the instrument that maintains it.

The quantity that describes a film is its moisture vapor transmission rate, the mass of water vapor crossing a given area in a given time. An impermeable cover traps sweat and wound fluid until the skin beneath softens and breaks down, while a fully permeable cloth lets the wound dry and cool. A semipermeable film sits between the two, passing water vapor while blocking liquid water and bacteria, and that selective passage is the reason such films exist. Warmth matters as well, because cells divide and enzymes act faster in a warm environment, so the balance of vapor loss, absorption, and insulation separates one dressing class from another.

Transparent films are thin adhesive sheets of semipermeable polyurethane that behave as a second skin, letting the operator see the wound without disturbing it while they shed water, and they suit a shallow, clean, minimally draining wound or the securement of a vascular access site. Their weakness is that they absorb nothing, so fluid collects beneath them and lifts the adhesive until the film floats free, and they should not be stretched over a joint or placed on fragile skin, because the pull can shear the epidermis when they are removed. A hydrocolloid is different in kind, an adhesive wafer of a gel-forming powder with an elastomer and a polymer pressed onto a foam or film backing. When it meets wound fluid it absorbs water, swells, and forms a soft, yellowish gel that keeps the wound moist and slightly acidic and bathes the surface without sticking to new tissue, so removal is painless. Hydrocolloids suit a shallow, low to moderate draining wound and a pressure injury that is not infected. The wear time for either material must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A foam dressing is a sheet of open-celled polyurethane with a waterproof backing, and its physics is the physics of a sponge. The open cells draw fluid from the wound surface by capillary action and hold it in the body of the sheet, so the wound stays moist while the surrounding skin stays dry, which lets a moderately draining wound go for several days. It comes in adhesive and non-adhesive forms, and the non-adhesive form goes under a separate cover so that it does not injure fragile skin. A hydrogel is the opposite answer, a sheet or tube of water-based gel that gives water back to a dry wound, cooling the surface, rehydrating dead tissue, and softening the dry crust that must be removed before healing can proceed. Because it adds moisture rather than absorbs it, a hydrogel must not be used on a heavily draining wound, where it would float away. The wear time and change criteria for both must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Alginates are non-woven fibers spun from the polysaccharides of brown seaweed, and they absorb many times their own weight in fluid. As they absorb, the calcium ions in the fiber are exchanged for sodium ions in the wound fluid, which turns the fiber into a soft gel that conforms to the wound bed, holds the fluid, and can be lifted out in one piece with forceps, so alginates suit a deep cavity with heavy drainage. Hydrofibers are spun from cellulose rather than seaweed and swell on contact with fluid into a coherent gel that holds fluid and bacteria within it and does not break apart, so the dressing can be removed complete. A hydrofiber tends to hold drainage against the wound rather than lock it away, so it is often covered by a foam that absorbs the fluid passing through, and it suits a moderately to heavily draining cavity. The characteristic error is to pack either material so tightly that it dries against the wound edge and becomes painful to remove, so the size, the packing density, and the wear time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Silver-containing dressings carry a metal that is toxic to bacteria, and their principle is the slow release of silver ions into wound fluid. Silver ions attach to the bacterial cell wall and to its genetic material, disrupting the organism in several ways at once, and the metal may be held as a salt, a complex, or nanocrystalline silver, so two dressings that look identical may behave very differently. Silver suits a wound that is infected or heavily colonized, and prolonged use on a clean wound can delay healing and stain tissue, so the duration of therapy and the change interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Medical honey is a concentrated sugar solution with low water activity that draws water out of bacteria and generates a small amount of hydrogen peroxide as it is diluted. It also pulls fluid from the tissues and lifts dead tissue away in a process of autolytic debridement, so it suits a sloughy or malodorous wound and a superficial burn. The dressings are prepared as sterile, irradiated honey, because ordinary table honey can carry spores and must never be placed in a wound, and the quantity to apply must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Charcoal dressings contain activated carbon, which is carbon treated to open millions of tiny pores and so to present an enormous surface area. Odor molecules from a necrotic or infected wound are adsorbed onto that surface as they pass through the sheet, and the dressing simply holds them. Because charcoal alone does not absorb fluid well, it is usually combined with a foam or an absorbent layer, and the assembly is covered by a waterproof backing that keeps the odor inside. Charcoal acts on the smell and not on the cause, so it must never be used to hide an odor that signals spreading infection, and the change interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Gauze is woven cotton or a cotton and polyester blend, and it is the oldest and most flexible wound material in the cupboard. Its open weave is permeable to air and fluid, it can be cut to any shape, and it works by absorption and by the mechanical removal of debris when it is lifted away, which is why plain woven gauze dries a wound and adheres painfully as it dries. The family includes flat pads, ribbon and rope forms for packing a cavity, and loose swabs for cleaning, while cotton wool is a mass of carded fibers with no weave that sheds fibers into a wound and belongs on intact skin and never inside a cavity. The most serious hazard is the swab left inside a wound, which is why swabs used in an operating field are counted and why a counted swab must never leave the field unrecorded.

Adhesive tapes are the invisible half of wound care, and their physics is a balance between stickiness and the strength of the skin they hold to. Tape is a backing with an adhesive layer, and the backing may be paper, fabric, foam, or transparent film, while the adhesive may be acrylic, rubber-based, or a soft silicone. The peel force rises with the time the tape has been in place, because the adhesive flows into the microscopic roughness of the skin and the bond matures, so repetitive removal strips the outermost layer of skin, an injury called medical adhesive-related skin injury. Tension during application produces shear and blistering, and prolonged contact can cause irritation or allergy. The common operator error is to tear the tape away in one fast pull, when the correct method is to loosen the edge, support the skin, and pull the tape back slowly along itself.

Bandages are the devices that apply force to a limb, and that force follows a simple relationship in which the pressure under a bandage depends on the tension in the fabric and on the radius of the limb. A narrow limb or a tightly wrapped bandage produces a much higher pressure than the same bandage on a broad limb, so a bandage on a thin ankle can be dangerous while the identical technique on a thigh is gentle. Crepe is a lightly elastic cotton bandage that holds a dressing and gives light support, while a short-stretch compression bandage resists the calf muscle as it pumps and gives a high working pressure during walking that suits a venous ulcer. A long-stretch elastic bandage gives a steadier resting pressure but loses tension and can be applied too tightly by an inexperienced hand, and a tubular bandage is a knitted tube applied with an applicator that holds a dressing without tape on fragile or allergic skin. The type, the number of layers, and the target pressure must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The safety hazard of any compression bandage is that it can reduce blood flow below the level the tissue needs. Pain, numbness, tingling, pallor, or a cool foot after application are signs that the pressure is too high, and the bandage must be removed and reapplied immediately. The pressure is also uneven where the bandage crosses a bony prominence or where layers overlap, and these are the places where skin breaks down. The common operator error is to add a second bandage to a limb that is already swelling, so the pressure climbs with every layer. The characteristic check is the observation of the toes and the distal pulses before and after application, and the frequency of that check must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Cleansing is the removal of debris, bacteria, and dead tissue from a wound surface, and its physical principle is dilution and flow, because a stream of fluid loosens material and carries it away. The volume of fluid matters more than the strength of any additive, and the World Health Organization and every modern wound guideline recommend clean water or normal saline for most wounds. Normal saline at 0.9 per cent sodium chloride matches the salt concentration of body fluid, so it neither draws water out of cells nor pushes water into them, and that concentration must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Antiseptics kill bacteria but also kill the fibroblasts and keratinocytes rebuilding the wound, so a strong antiseptic on a clean healing surface can slow repair, and hydrogen peroxide, iodine, and chlorhexidine are therefore reserved for a contaminated or infected wound or one being prepared for a procedure. The concentration and contact time of every antiseptic must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Debridement is the removal of dead tissue, and its principle is that dead tissue is a barrier between the wound and the healing cells and a culture medium for bacteria. Removing it lowers the bacterial load, exposes the living tissue underneath, and lets the wound edges advance. Sharp debridement with a scalpel or scissors is the fastest and most complete method, and it demands the most skill and the clearest understanding of where living tissue begins, with bleeding from a hidden vessel as its characteristic hazard. The instruments are few, including a fresh blade, curved scissors or a tissue nipper, a spoon-shaped curette drawn across the bed to scoop softened tissue, and forceps and a probe to find the depth of a cavity. Each is used once and either discarded or reprocessed, and a sharp curette or blade is handled as a sharps hazard throughout, with the size of the instrument and the depth of the pass confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Enzymatic debridement applies the chemistry of digestion to dead tissue, and the agent is a preparation of a protein-digesting enzyme, most often collagenase, which acts on the collagen of dead tissue and spares the living tissue around it. The wound must be moist for the enzyme to work, so it is applied under a dressing that keeps it in contact with the surface. It is useful where sharp debridement is unavailable, not tolerated, or unsafe near a tendon or a major vessel, and the frequency and quantity must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Autolytic debridement is the body's own digestion of dead tissue, and it is simply the maintenance of a moist environment that lets the wound's enzymes act, while larval therapy uses sterile fly larvae that liquefy and ingest dead tissue and secrete substances that inhibit bacteria. Larval therapy is offered where other methods have failed and with a clear explanation, with the product, duration, and containment dressing confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Negative pressure wound therapy applies a controlled suction to a wound through a porous interface, and its principle is the removal of fluid together with the mechanical deformation of tissue. A foam or a gauze interface is cut to the shape of the wound, covered by an adhesive film, and connected by a tube to a pump that holds the wound below atmospheric pressure, which draws fluid out, reduces swelling, and pulls the wound edges together while the film seals the surface from contamination. Foam is easier to cut and to seal and distributes pressure across the wound, while gauze is more conformable in a deep or irregular cavity and can be placed in a tunnel. The pressure is typically held between about 60 and 125 millimeters of mercury, a range that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A wound that is actively bleeding, that contains dead tissue not yet removed, or that holds exposed bowel or a major vessel is not a candidate for negative pressure wound therapy until those conditions are resolved. The machine has a small display and a set of alarms the operator must know by ear. A leak alarm sounds when the suction cannot hold the set pressure because the film seal has failed, and the characteristic sign is a hissing edge and a dressing that no longer dimples, while a blockage alarm sounds when the tube is kinked or filled with clot. A canister alarm sounds when the container is full or its seal is not seated, and the canister is the part most often mismanaged, because it must be seated and sealed correctly or the leak alarm sounds and therapy never begins. The alarms, their thresholds, the pressure setting, and the canister replacement rule must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

An ostomy appliance is a dressing for a stoma, and its physics is the management of a seal against a moving, wet, irregular surface. A stoma is a loop of bowel brought through the abdominal wall, and it has no nerve endings at its surface but bleeds easily. The appliance must hold an adhesive barrier against the skin, collect the output without leaking, and protect the surrounding skin from the enzymes in the effluent, and the barrier must match the stoma in size and shape and be cut to fit closely, because skin exposed to effluent becomes raw and painful within hours. A one-piece appliance combines the barrier and the pouch and is replaced as a unit, while a two-piece appliance joins the pouch to a separate barrier with a flange or a ring so the pouch can be changed without disturbing the barrier. Fittings include paste and rings to fill a crease, powders to dry a weeping area, belts to support a heavy appliance, and deodorants, and the correct barrier size and wear time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The common operator error with an ostomy appliance is to cut the barrier opening larger than the stoma to avoid rubbing it, because that leaves a ring of unprotected skin that the effluent will digest. The safety hazard is a leak left in place, because enzyme-rich output strips the skin within a day, and once the skin is broken the seal becomes harder still. The characteristic sign of a failing seal is itching or burning under the barrier, and the patient is the best detector of it. Teaching the patient to measure the stoma, to cut the barrier, and to inspect the skin is the part of ostomy care that lasts beyond the hospital, and the teaching and review interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A wound is measured so that its progress can be judged, and the measurement is useful only if it is repeatable. The three dimensions are length, width, and depth, with length at the longest axis from head to toe and width at right angles to it, while depth is measured with a sterile probe at the deepest point and the amount of undermining is recorded by clock position. Photography is powerful and needs discipline, because a consistent camera distance, a scale marker, a color reference, and the same lighting make two images comparable while a casual snapshot misleads. The patient must consent, and the images must be stored under the same privacy rules as the rest of the record, within the electronic health record or another secured system. The written record carries the date, the site, the measurements, the appearance of the wound bed, the type and quantity of drainage, the odor, the state of the surrounding skin, and the dressing applied.

Dressings are consumables with dates, and their storage is part of their safety. Each class has an expiry date on the package, and a sterile package that has been wet, crushed, or opened is no longer sterile regardless of the date. Stock is rotated so that the oldest package is used first, and new deliveries are placed behind the existing stock. Storage should be clean, dry, out of direct sunlight, and within the temperature range the maker states, because heat and moisture degrade adhesives and some silver and honey products. A damp or hot stockroom can ruin a whole shelf of dressings while every date remains valid.

The hospital-grade half of wound care is not a single machine but a supply chain and a trained team. A wound care service, often led by a specialist nurse, chooses the formulary, sets the assessment schedule, and teaches the ward staff which dressing belongs to which wound, while the pharmacy or the central store holds the stock and the central sterile services department handles the reusable scissors, forceps, probes, and curettes. The ward keeps a dressing trolley restocked on a schedule, and documentation flows into the electronic health record so that the photograph, the measurements, and the product used follow the patient. The trolley is wiped with the approved detergent between patients, and the scissors and the probe are cleaned and steam sterilized at 134 degrees Celsius for about 3 minutes, a cycle that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The reusable instruments are inspected for rust, for a loose joint, and for a blunt edge, and the storage area is inspected for damp, for pests, and for expired stock, with the inspection frequency confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Failure in wound care rarely announces itself as a broken machine, and the characteristic signals are on the patient rather than on a display. A dressing that has lifted at the edge, a rim of fluid, a rising odor, a wound that has enlarged since the last assessment, or skin that is red and softened around the wound are the alarms of this field. The safety hazard is the wound concealed under a dressing for too long, so that an infection or a deterioration is discovered late, and the common operator error is to leave a dressing in place because it still looks clean when the interval has passed. Before a dressing is changed, the hands are cleaned, personal protective equipment is worn, the old dressing is removed slowly, and the wound is assessed before it is cleaned, because cleaning changes the appearance that was just recorded. The new package is checked for an intact seal, for a date within its life, and for the correct product, and only then is the dressing placed, secured without tension, with every step and product confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

In the mobile or frontier clinic the wound formulary is short, and the skill is in using a small set of materials well. The shelf usually holds plain gauze, cotton wool, adhesive tape, saline, a mild antiseptic, a few petroleum-jelly dressings, and perhaps a foam or a hydrocolloid if the supply chain has delivered one. The choice is made by the wound rather than by the catalogue, so a clean surgical wound needs only a dry or lightly moist gauze cover while a sloughy and wet wound needs frequent gauze changes, and the dressing that costs nothing and heals is not one product but a method, because a wound kept clean, moist, and undisturbed will close even under a simple cover. Where the manufactured product is absent, the field team improvises, so a piece of clean plastic film can serve as a semipermeable cover, a petroleum-jelly gauze as a non-adherent interface, a thin clean foam as an absorbent layer, and a clean plastic bag as a holder. Any improvised material must be clean, must not shed fibers into the wound, and must never stick and tear when it is removed, and the characteristic error in this setting is to spend a scarce advanced dressing on a wound that needed only cleaning and time.

Cleansing in the field is done with the best water available, and the volume and the method decide the outcome. Boiled and cooled water or clean saline is poured or gently irrigated over the wound, and a syringe with a soft tip provides a controllable stream. Sharp debridement is performed only by a clinician who can see where the living tissue begins, with a fresh blade and good light, because a deep cut in poor light can open a vessel or a tendon. Where a wound is too painful or too deep for sharp work, a moist dressing and time will soften the dead tissue for a later session, and the instruments are boiled or pressure-cooked between uses, with the timing of that cycle confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Compression for a venous ulcer in the field is achieved with a short-stretch bandage or a carefully applied elastic wrap whose pressure is judged by the patient's comfort and the color of the toes, because the same cloth on two different limbs produces two different pressures. The field variant also demands more of the operator in stock discipline and in judgment about when to refer. Adhesives fail in heat, silver and honey products lose their properties, and a dressing stored in a hot vehicle may be useless before its printed date, so the stock is kept in the coolest, driest place the clinic has and inspected at each use. The characteristic alarm of the field is a wound that smells worse and grows larger despite regular dressing, which means that the diagnosis, the debridement, or the referral has failed rather than the dressing. The common operator error is to keep changing the dressing and never reassess the wound, and what the field gives up is the advanced foam, the silver sheet, the negative pressure pump, and the engineered ostomy range, which all need a supply chain, storage, and training that a mobile clinic cannot sustain. In their place the team relies on gauze, water, and a moist, clean, undisturbed wound, and on a paper chart with a drawn wound map and a dated photograph.

To summarize, a dressing is an environment rather than a cover, and every class in this chapter is defined by how it handles water, how much it absorbs, and how it meets the skin. The wound decides the material, the material decides the interval, and the interval decides whether the wound is left moist and undisturbed long enough to heal. The hospital version buys choice with a formulary, a sterile supply chain, a trained wound service, and an electronic record, while the field version buys access with gauze, water, a clean cover, and disciplined judgment. In both settings the hazards are the same, which are a dressing left too long, a seal that fails, skin torn by adhesive, and pressure that starves the tissue. The operator's assessment, the measurement, the honest documentation, and the confirmation of every product, pressure, and interval against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol are what turn a shelf of materials into safe wound care.
