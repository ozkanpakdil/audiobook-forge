# Appendix B — Quick-Reference Tables: Tube Colors, Needle Gauges, Catheter Sizes, and Oxygen Delivery Devices

This appendix gathers the numbers a clinician reaches for most often. Here are blood tube colors, needle gauges, catheter sizes in French, oxygen device flows and concentrations, suture sizes, glove sizes, and tube sizes by age. Each is a convention rather than a law of nature. Every figure must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before a device is used on a patient.

Color coding deserves a warning before any table. A color is a symbol, and the same closure color may carry different additives in different hospitals. When the color and the label disagree, the label wins, and an ambiguous label means the tube is discarded. The operator who trusts color alone may draw into the wrong additive, and the only alarm is a result that does not fit the patient.

Blood clots because platelets and clotting factors respond to a foreign surface. An additive either interrupts that response or accelerates it, so the additive decides which measurement the sample can support. A tube with a clot activator yields serum, while one with an anticoagulant yields plasma or whole blood. Gentle mixing matters as much as chemistry.

A light blue closure holds sodium citrate, which binds calcium, and it is used for coagulation testing. A red or gold closure holds a clot activator or nothing, and it yields serum for chemistry. A green closure holds lithium or sodium heparin for chemistry and blood gas. A lavender or purple closure holds ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid for blood counts, and a pink closure holds the same additive for blood bank work. A gray closure holds sodium fluoride and potassium oxalate for glucose, and a yellow closure may be sterile for culture. Every closure color and additive must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The order of draw protects the most fragile test, and its logic is contamination. The needle that fills every tube can carry additive into the next one, so culture bottles come first, then the light blue coagulation tube, the serum tube, the heparin tube, the lavender count tube, and finally the gray glucose tube. The common error is drawing the coagulation tube after an anticoagulant tube, and underfilling the light blue tube is the second. Underfilling destroys the fixed ratio of 1 part citrate to 9 parts blood, and these figures must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

In the hospital the vacuum tube sits in a chain of infrastructure. Tubes arrive in sealed trays with lot numbers, needles are single use in shielding holders, and pneumatic transport carries specimens to a centrifuge. Consumables include the tourniquet, swab, sharps container, bedside label, and transport bag. The characteristic failure is a broken chain, such as a label on the wrong tube or a specimen left too long on a counter.

The field variant gives up the vacuum and everything that depends on it. A syringe and needle fill a plain tube or one whose additive was dried in at the clinic, and the operator judges the fill against a mark. Without a centrifuge the serum may separate by gravity before a journey to a laboratory hours away. It demands clean technique, immediate labeling, and knowledge of which tests delay will ruin.

Tubes expire, so the operator checks the date, the vacuum, the color, the printed additive, and the request label. Storage is upright at the stated temperature, out of sunlight and away from radiators, because heat ages the additive. Mixing is gentle inversion a few times and never shaking, since shaking releases potassium and hemoglobin. The failure modes are a lost vacuum, a cracked tube, a clotted specimen, and a hemolyzed specimen.

The gauge of a needle runs backwards, because it describes the outer diameter of the shaft. A larger number means a smaller needle, so 14 gauge is large-bore and 27 gauge is fine. Flow rises with the fourth power of the internal radius, so a slightly wider needle carries far more fluid. That is why a patient in shock receives blood through a short, wide cannula, and why the right length reaches the intended tissue without passing through it.

The larger bores serve volume and speed. A 14 gauge and 16 gauge needle serve rapid transfusion, and 18 gauge serves donation, surgery, and a patient who may need resuscitation. A 19 gauge to 21 gauge needle covers most adult injections and intravenous starts, and 22 gauge is the compromise for difficult veins. A 23 gauge to 25 gauge suits children and subcutaneous injections, while 26 gauge to 27 gauge serves intradermal and fine dental work.

Hub colors are only a memory aid, and they vary by maker. The common teaching pairs 14 gauge with orange, 16 gauge with gray, 18 gauge with pink, 20 gauge with yellow, and 21 gauge with green. It continues with 22 gauge with black and 23 gauge with blue, so a color may serve more than one size. The printed size must always be read, and every gauge must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Hospital practice surrounds the needle with safety hardware. Standardized hubs, needles that retract or shield after use, blunt fill and filter needles, and needle-free connectors all reduce risk. The used needle goes into a rigid sharps container never more than three quarters full. The safety hazard is the sharps injury, the common error is recapping by hand, and the characteristic alarm is the occlusion alarm of a pump when a needle or cannula blocks.

In a frontier clinic the needle is scarce, so syringes are boiled and needles are sharpened on a stone. One needle may serve several doses from a multi-dose vial, which multiplies the risk of infection. The field gives up the engineered safety device and demands a flame, a clean surface, a puncture-proof container closed when full, and a rule that a needle is never bent, broken, or recapped. The operator checks package integrity, shaft straightness, bevel sharpness, and fit, and discards any needle that drags.

Catheter size uses the French scale, where one French unit equals one third of a millimeter of external diameter. A 16 French catheter therefore measures about 5.3 millimeters across. The number describes the outside, which must pass through a narrow opening, while flow depends on the inside. Two catheters of equal French size may drain differently if their walls differ in thickness, and the rule for suctioning is a catheter no wider than one half of the internal diameter of the airway.

Urinary catheters follow patient size. A newborn takes 5 French to 6 French, an infant 8 French, a small child 10 French, and a school-age girl 12 French. An adult woman takes 12 French to 14 French, an adult man 14 French to 16 French, long-term drainage 16 French to 18 French, and irrigation after surgery 20 French to 24 French. Nasogastric tubes run from 5 French in a premature newborn to 16 French in a large adult, suction catheters from 6 French to 14 French, central venous catheters from 4 French to 8 French, and chest drains from 12 French for air to 40 French for blood and pus. Every size must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A catheter too large presses on the urethra or airway and causes pressure necrosis that can leave a stricture, while one too small leaks and blocks. The common error is to confuse the external French size with the drainage channel, and the second is to overinflate a retention balloon. The characteristic alarm is the occlusion alarm of the pump or the absence of drainage where drainage is expected. The response is to check the tube for kinks and the bag for position before forcing any fluid.

The hospital-grade catheter is sterile and single use, with a kit of gloves, drape, antiseptic, lubricant, bag, and securement. The drainage bag is a closed system whose port is wiped before sampling, and it is kept below the bladder. Urine meters, leg bags, and overnight bags are separate consumables. Traceability records the date of insertion, the size, the balloon volume, and the inserter.

In the field the catheter is often reusable, and silicone tolerates boiling better than latex. Clean intermittent catheterization may be the only option where sterile kits cannot be supplied. The field gives up the closed bag and demands cleaning of the meatus, clean water, drying, and storage in a sealed container. Any improvised container must stay off the floor and be washed on a schedule.

The checks that keep a catheter working are position and hygiene. The operator confirms securement without traction, tubing that runs downhill with no loop below the bag, and a bag below the bladder. The failure modes are blockage by sediment, leakage, cloudy or foul urine, and pain suggesting the balloon has migrated. The bag is emptied on schedule, the sampling port is wiped, and the catheter and tubing junction is never opened unnecessarily.

Every oxygen device raises the fraction of inspired oxygen above the 21 per cent found in room air. A low-flow device delivers oxygen into the upper airway and lets the patient's own breath pull in the rest. The achieved fraction then depends on how fast and how deeply the patient breathes, so the printed numbers are approximations. A reservoir device holds oxygen ready for the next breath, an entrainment device mixes oxygen with room air in a fixed ratio, and a leaking mask delivers less than its label promises.

A nasal cannula runs at 1 to 6 liters per minute, giving roughly 24 to 44 per cent. A simple face mask runs at 5 to 10 liters per minute for roughly 35 to 55 per cent, and it must never run below 5 liters per minute or the patient rebreathes exhaled carbon dioxide. A venturi mask entrains room air to hold a set fraction, commonly 24 to 60 per cent. A nonrebreather mask adds a reservoir bag, runs at 10 to 15 liters per minute, and can approach 90 per cent. High-flow nasal therapy warms and humidifies 30 to 60 liters per minute, while continuous positive airway pressure, shortened to CPAP, and bilevel positive airway pressure, shortened to BIPAP, add pressure rather than only oxygen.

Oxygen accelerates fire, so no oil, grease, or petroleum product may touch a regulator, valve, or mask, and no one may smoke nearby. The common error is opening a flow meter to maximum on a nasal cannula in the belief that more flow means more oxygen, when the gas mostly leaks and dries the mucosa. The second error is letting a humidifier bottle run dry. The characteristic alarms are a low oxygen concentration alarm on a concentrator, a low saturation alarm on the oximeter, and a mask leak alarm on a CPAP device. Every flow and fraction must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The hospital system begins at a bulk tank or piped medical gas and reaches the bed through a wall outlet that fits no other gas. The flow meter, regulator, humidifier, tubing, and mask are consumables, and the outlet pressure is reduced to the required liters per minute. Blenders mix oxygen with compressed air, analyzers verify the mixture, and alarm panels warn of low pipeline pressure. A backup cylinder stands at every bed, and the outlets are checked on a documented schedule.

The field variant is built around the concentrator, the cylinder, and conservation. A concentrator draws room air through a filter, compresses it, and passes it through a sieve bed that holds nitrogen, usually delivering 1 to 10 liters per minute at a concentration that falls as the flow rises. A cylinder holds a fixed volume, so its life is calculated from the pressure gauge and the flow. The field gives up the pipeline, blender, and analyzer, demanding charged batteries, spare filters, and a plan for a patient who needs more than the concentrator can make.

Maintenance means washing or replacing the concentrator filter on schedule, because a clogged filter starves the sieve bed and lowers concentration. Sieve beds are replaced after their stated hours, and flow meters are checked against a known standard. Tubing and masks are single patient items replaced when soiled, and humidifier bottles are emptied and dried between patients. The operator checks cylinder pressure, every joint, the mask seal, and the patient's saturation, and listens for the low concentration alarm.

Suture size is written as a number with zeros, and the zeros run opposite to ordinary counting. Size 0 is the starting point, 2-0 is finer than 0, and 5-0 is finer still. Diameter decides tensile strength and tissue drag, so a thread strong enough for fascia cuts delicate skin. A knot holds by friction, so a stiff monofilament needs more throws than a soft braided thread, and braided threads carry fluid by capillarity.

A size 2 or size 1 suture carries the largest loads, such as fascial closure and ligation of major vessels. A size 0 suture serves the abdominal wall, a 2-0 serves fascia and bowel, and a 3-0 serves bowel and small vessels. A 4-0 suture is the usual choice for skin, and a 5-0 is used on the face and hand. A 6-0 suits fine facial work and pediatric skin, while 7-0 to 10-0 belong to microsurgery. Every size must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the choice of absorbable or nonabsorbable material is made at the same time.

The hospital-grade suture arrives sterile in a foil packet with a needle swaged to the thread, a lot number, and an expiration date. The needle is counted with every other sharp before the wound is closed and again before the patient leaves the room. The characteristic alarm is a count discrepancy, which stops the closing until the item is found or the wound is explored. Absorbable sutures lose strength as the body breaks them down, while nonabsorbable sutures hold until removed.

In a frontier clinic the suture cabinet is small and every packet is counted. The field gives up the wide selection of needle shapes and the certainty of factory sterility, so a single sterile monofilament may serve many tasks. The operator must know the strength of what is in hand. The checks before use are packet integrity, needle sharpness, absence of rust, and the match between needle and tissue, and a suture stored in heat or damp should be suspected.

A glove is a thin membrane between two environments, and material, thickness, and fit decide its performance. Latex is degraded by petroleum-based creams, light, and heat, nitrile resists many chemicals, and vinyl is the weakest barrier. Thickness, often given in thousandths of an inch, decides how long the membrane survives abrasion. Fit decides whether the wearer can work, since a tight glove tears and a loose glove reduces feeling.

Glove size is measured as the circumference of the hand around the palm at its widest point, just below the knuckles. That circumference in inches is the size number, so a hand 7 inches around takes a size 7 glove. A hand 6 inches around takes size 6, about 15 centimeters, and size 8 is about 20 centimeters, with half sizes between. The wrist to fingertip length decides forearm coverage, and the size on the box must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The hospital routine is to select the correct size, perform hand hygiene before donning, and change gloves between patients. Double gloving is used where sharp injury is likely, and indicator gloves reveal a puncture as a dark spot beneath the outer glove. The safety hazard is latex allergy, which ranges from a rash to a life-threatening reaction, so a latex-free alternative must be available. The common error is wearing one pair from patient to patient, and in the field gloves are washed and reused when supply fails. Gloves are stored away from sunlight and heat, and the operator checks the cuff for tears and the membrane for pinholes.

Nasogastric and endotracheal tubes are both chosen by age, and in both cases the number is a compromise. Airflow falls steeply as internal diameter falls, so a small tube demands more pressure to move the same gas. The tube must be small enough to pass without injury and large enough to do its work, and that margin is narrow in a newborn. Length is measured from nose to earlobe to breastbone for a nasogastric tube and checked after placement for an endotracheal tube.

A nasogastric tube is usually 5 French for a premature newborn, 6 French to 8 French for a term newborn, and 8 French for an infant. A small child takes 10 French, an older child 12 French, and an adolescent or small adult 12 French to 14 French. A large adult takes 14 French to 16 French, and lavage after poisoning may call for 16 French to 18 French. A wide tube drains faster but is harder to pass and more likely to erode, while a fine tube is comfortable but blocks. Every size must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

An endotracheal tube is usually 2.5 millimeters to 3.0 millimeters of internal diameter for a premature newborn. A term newborn takes 3.0 to 3.5 millimeters, an infant 3.5 to 4.0 millimeters, and a toddler 4.5 to 5.0 millimeters. A school-age child takes 5.5 to 6.0 millimeters and an adolescent 6.5 to 7.5 millimeters. An adult woman commonly takes 7.0 to 7.5 millimeters, and an adult man takes 7.5 to 8.5 millimeters. For a child older than two years, the uncuffed size in millimeters equals the age in years divided by 4 and then plus 4, and every figure must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The shared hazard of both tubes is misplacement. A nasogastric tube in the airway can deliver feed into a lung, and an endotracheal tube in the esophagus delivers no breath. The common error with an endotracheal tube is pushing it too far, sending it into one main bronchus and collapsing the other lung. A cuff inflated above the pressure the mucosa tolerates causes ischemia, so cuff pressure is measured with a gauge. The characteristic alarms are the ventilator high-pressure alarm for a blocked tube, the disconnect alarm when it loosens, and the falling saturation on the oximeter.

Daily tube care is a set of small checks that prevent large failures. The operator compares the depth marking with the depth recorded at insertion, listens to both sides of the chest, and checks cuff pressure. Oral care, suctioning with a catheter no wider than one half of the tube, and rotation when the protocol allows reduce secretion and infection. The consumables at hand are a spare tube, a smaller tube, a suction catheter, a cuff syringe, a bite block, and an oxygen source. The failure modes to fear are blockage, disconnection, displacement, and erosion from a tube pressing on one spot too long.

To summarize, the colors, gauges, French sizes, flows, suture sizes, glove sizes, and tube sizes in this appendix are agreements rather than facts of nature. The physical principles are stable: a smaller bore carries less flow, an additive changes what blood will do, and a French number measures the outside while drainage depends on the inside. An oxygen fraction depends on flow and seal, a suture holds by friction, and a glove is only as good as its fit. The hospital versions wrap these principles in packaging, traceability, alarms, and consumables, while the field versions demand skill, cleanliness, and improvisation. In every setting the safe habit is to read the label, name the hazard, count the sharps, and confirm every figure against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before it is applied to a patient.
