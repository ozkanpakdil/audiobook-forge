# Chapter 78 — Emerging Technologies

This chapter is different from every other chapter in this book, because its subject has not yet settled. The physical principles underneath them are already fixed, and a listener who grasps those principles can judge a new machine without waiting for the literature to agree. Every emerging technology in medicine is built from a small set of physical events, in which a photon is captured, a current is measured, a material is deposited, or a battery is discharged. The clinical promise sits on top of those events, so when the promise outruns the physics the device fails in the field rather than in the advertisement. This chapter keeps the discipline and the two halves of the rest of the book, and it ends by returning to the patient.

A demonstration proves that a thing can work once, in a room chosen for it, with the person who built it standing nearby. A device proves that it works every time, in a room it did not choose, in the hands of someone who did not build it and cannot repair it. The distance between those two statements is crossed by repeatability, calibration, documented failure modes, spare parts, consumables, training, and a regulatory pathway. A device must survive the hundredth patient on the hottest afternoon of the year, when the operator is tired and the cupboard is nearly empty. The question is therefore not whether a technology can work, but what it demands to keep working, counted in power, consumables, connectivity, training, and maintenance skill before it is bought.

Artificial intelligence begins with the fact that an image or a signal is a grid of numbers, and a pattern in those numbers can be learned from examples. A learning program is shown many such arrays with the answer a clinician gave, and it adjusts millions of internal weights until its own answers match those examples closely enough. The result is not a written rule but a statistical surface that responds to shapes, edges, and textures. Screening and triage follow directly, because both sort many normal studies from a few abnormal ones. The same mathematics makes the device excellent on images resembling its training examples and unreliable on images that do not, and it cannot tell the difference by itself.

In a well-resourced hospital, artificial intelligence usually arrives inside the imaging chain rather than as a separate box. The hospital-grade version therefore depends on infrastructure that has nothing to do with intelligence, because it needs a fast network, large storage, calibrated displays, and an electronic health record that can receive the result. Its validation problem is the hard part, because a program tested on one population and one machine can lose accuracy when either changes. Demonstrations report accuracy on a curated set, while a device must report its performance on the patients who actually arrive, and any stated accuracy, confidence threshold, or alarm limit must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The field variant is a program on a telephone or a small laptop, often without a network, because connectivity in a frontier clinic is intermittent and expensive. It gives up the large training sets, the calibrated display, and the continuous updates of the hospital version, and it demands disciplined use instead, meaning a defined population, a defined task, and a written record of which version was used for which patient. It also demands a path to referral, because a tool that raises suspicion without a route to confirmation creates anxiety rather than care, and the software version and model date must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The characteristic hazard of a diagnostic learning program is a confident wrong answer rather than a crash. It is delivered in the same clean font as a correct one. Automation bias is the operator error that follows, because a reader who sees a flag looks for confirmation and stops looking for what the flag did not mention. The device may fail silently when its input changes, so an image with a folded edge, a foreign body, or a poor exposure can produce a result that is fluent and false. There is rarely a traditional alarm, and that absence is itself the safety problem, because listeners expect a machine to announce its own uncertainty. The honest practice puts a quality check before the analysis, refuses to report when the input is out of specification, and treats every result as a second opinion rather than a verdict. The operator must check the image, the stated confidence, and the clinical picture together, and any rejection threshold or confidence value must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Wearable monitoring rests on the fact that the body emits signals a small sensor can capture at the skin. A light emitting diode shines light into tissue and a photodetector measures how much returns, and because oxygenated and deoxygenated blood absorb light differently the pulsation of arterial blood becomes a signal from which heart rate and oxygen saturation are derived. An accelerometer measures acceleration in 3 axes and infers steps, posture, tremor, sleep, and falls, while a pair of electrodes measures the small voltage of the heart or the brain. Each measurement is a physical quantity converted into a number, and each is vulnerable to motion, poor contact, heat and cold, and the placement of the sensor. A consumer wearable and a medical monitor may use the same physics and still differ enormously in calibration and in what their numbers mean.

The hospital-grade wearable is a telemetry device, and it is inseparable from the infrastructure that receives it. A small transmitter on the chest sends an electrocardiogram to a central station through a dedicated wireless network that covers the ward, and the station applies alarm limits, stores the trend, and routes the signal to a monitor at the desk. Batteries are managed as a consumable with a defined life and a charging routine, and electrodes are replaced on a schedule because their gel dries. The safety hazard is an alarm that does not reach a human being, whether because the network has a dead zone, the battery died, or the alarm limit was set outside the protocol. The common operator error is to silence an alarm without resolving it, and the characteristic response is a technical alarm that the staff learn to ignore, so alarm limits, battery replacement intervals, and electrode change schedules must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The field variant is a telephone paired with a low-power sensor, and a patch that records a single-lead electrocardiogram continuously and is read later gives up real-time alarms but can diagnose an intermittent rhythm in a patient who lives far away. It demands a system for retrieving the data and a person responsible for reading it.

Three-dimensional printing is the construction of an object layer upon layer from a digital model, and the physical principle differs with the method. In material extrusion, a heated nozzle lays down a bead of melted polymer that fuses to the layer beneath as it cools, so the strength of the part depends on the print parameters and the bond between passes, and those parameters must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. In vat photopolymerization, a light source cures a liquid resin one thin slice at a time, giving fine detail but requiring washing and a final cure under light. In powder bed fusion, a laser or an electron beam melts particles of metal or polymer into a solid, which produces strong parts for instruments and implants but demands an inert atmosphere, trained operators, and expensive powder. The clinical use follows from customization, because a shape can be made for one patient rather than chosen from a catalog.

In a hospital, three-dimensional printing appears in 3 places that carry very different risks. The next is the orthosis or external aid, a splint, a brace, or a prosthetic socket shaped to the patient, which must fit, tolerate cleaning, and bear load without cracking. The most demanding is the sterile guide or implant, which must be made from a validated material, printed in a controlled environment, cleaned, and sterilized by a cycle proven not to distort it, because a printed part is not sterile merely because it looks clean. Its layered surface harbors organisms more readily than a machined surface, so material lot, print parameters, and the sterilization method must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before any printed object touches a patient. The field variant is a small extrusion printer in a district workshop, and its most valuable product is a spare part rather than a device. A knob, a bracket, a battery cover, a tube clamp, or a tray insert can be copied from a broken original and printed in a tough polymer, which can return a machine to service that would otherwise wait months for an imported piece. It gives up dimensional precision and material certification, and it demands dry filament, a stable power supply, a spare nozzle, and a person who can draw or scan the broken part accurately, while the operator must remember that a printed substitute inside a breathing circuit or a pressure vessel is a hazard rather than a repair.

Surgical robotics begins with teleoperation, in which the surgeon's hands move a master control while a slave mechanism inside the patient reproduces the motion. The link is electronic, so the motion can be scaled down, filtered of tremor, and given more degrees of freedom than a human wrist, while the camera gives a magnified three-dimensional view. The physical principle is the same as any remote manipulator, because position and force are sensed at one end, encoded, transmitted, and recreated at the other, and every joint adds a place where calibration can drift. The clinical gain is precision in a confined space and steadier hands during long procedures, and the cost is measured in purchase price, in a dedicated theatre, in a service contract, and in consumable instruments with a defined number of uses. The characteristic hazard is not the robot moving by itself but the loss of force feedback, which lets a surgeon push harder than intended because the hand feels nothing, and the characteristic alarm is a system fault that halts the instrument in mid-move, so any instrument use limit, calibration interval, and force threshold must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The field variant of robotics is almost nothing, and honesty requires saying so, because a machine that needs a stable floor, conditioned air, a trained engineer, and a service visit from another country does not belong in a frontier clinic. What the field clinic should take from robotics is not the robot but the principles it embodies, which are a stable adjustable table, bright and shadowless light, reliable suction, good instruments, and a second trained pair of hands. The field variant needs some way to confirm what it suspects.

Telemedicine is the transport of clinical information rather than of the patient, and its physical basis is the conversion of a measurement into a signal. Sound becomes a stream of numbers and an image becomes a grid of numbers, and both are compressed by discarding what the eye and ear are unlikely to notice, then transmitted and rebuilt at the other end. Three physical quantities decide whether the encounter works, which are bandwidth, latency, and stability. Bandwidth limits how much can be sent, latency is the delay between sending and receiving, and stability is whether the connection holds long enough to finish. A narrow question over a slow link works, while a moving image of a heart needs a better one. Bandwidth, latency, and image resolution requirements must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The hospital-grade telemedicine system is a room or a cart with a camera, a microphone array, a display, a codec that compresses and decompresses the signal, and a network connection with reserved capacity. It is tied into the picture archiving and communication system so that images can be shared, and into the electronic health record so that the encounter is documented where the rest of the chart lives. The characteristic alarm is a connection or quality warning, and the common operator error is to proceed through poor audio rather than stop and fix the link, because both parties tend to be polite about it. The safety hazard is a decision made on incomplete information, which is why the receiving clinician must state what could not be seen or heard, and connection quality thresholds, camera resolution, and audio levels must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The field variant is usually a telephone, sometimes a very small satellite terminal, and occasionally a radio link on the very high frequency or ultra high frequency bands where no telephone network reaches. It gives up the dedicated room, the codec, the clear image, and the reliable link, and store-and-forward messaging, in which a photograph and a short history are sent and an answer returns later, is often more robust than a live call. What it demands is a disciplined package, namely a measured set of observations, a clear photograph with a scale, a short written question, and a named person at the far end who will answer, because the common operator error is to send an image with no scale, no orientation, and no history.

A modern telephone contains a computer, a camera, a microphone, an accelerometer, a light sensor, a radio, and a touch screen, and any of these can be turned into a medical instrument by software, which is why the telephone is the most consequential emerging device in this chapter. The regulation problem is that most applications escape scrutiny entirely, because they are distributed as general-purpose software and make claims that fall outside the definition of a medical device. A step counter, a menstruation tracker, and a mood diary are consumer products, while a program that calculates a drug dose, interprets a rhythm strip, or photographs a lesion for diagnosis is making a medical claim and should be regulated as a device, whether or not it is. The listener should ask 3 questions of any clinical application, which are what claim does it make, on what data was it tested, and who is accountable when it is wrong. In the hospital, the application that survives is the one wired into the record and the workflow, and the hospital-grade version is managed by the institution with version control and a policy on photographs of patients. The field variant is the opposite, a large unmanaged collection of applications installed by individuals, and what it gives up is institutional oversight. The safety hazard is a dose or a threshold read from an unvalidated application, and the common operator error is to trust a familiar interface over a printed protocol, so the application version, data source, and any calculated value must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Solar power begins with the photovoltaic cell, a slice of semiconductor in which arriving light frees electrons and creates a voltage across a junction, so the current that flows is proportional to the light falling on the panel. A charge controller sits between panel and battery to prevent overcharging and to manage the discharge, and an inverter converts the stored direct current into the alternating current that most hospital equipment expects. Battery chemistry decides how the system behaves, because a lead acid battery is cheap and heavy and tolerates abuse poorly, while a lithium iron phosphate battery stores more energy for its weight and lasts more cycles, but it costs more and must be protected from heat and deep discharge. Panel rating, controller settings, battery type, and inverter capacity must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The hospital-grade solar installation is an engineering project, an array on a roof or a frame sized from a measured load profile, coupled to a battery bank in a ventilated room, integrated with the mains supply, and monitored so that a failing string or a degrading battery is noticed before the ward goes dark. The field variant is a single panel, a small controller, and one battery, sized to run lights, a telephone charger, a refrigerator for vaccines, and perhaps an oxygen concentrator during daylight, and it demands that the clinic know its consumption in watt hours per day before it buys anything, with those load figures confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The characteristic failure is not the panel but the battery, which ages quietly, and the common operator error is to add a load without adding capacity. The low-voltage alarm on the controller is the characteristic warning that the battery is being drawn down. Battery capacity, depth of discharge, and charge controller voltage setpoints must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Sterilization innovations aim at the same goal by new physical routes, because heat and steam damage many modern instruments. Hydrogen peroxide vapor sterilizes at a low temperature by oxidizing the proteins and nucleic acids of organisms, but it requires a sealed chamber, a specific concentration, and a material compatibility check, since some polymers and some long narrow channels resist the vapor. Ultraviolet light damages the genetic material of organisms and is useful for surface and water disinfection, but it travels in straight lines and shadows protect whatever it does not reach. The verification principle matters most, because a sterilization cycle is only trusted when a biological indicator has been killed and a chemical indicator has changed, since the machine's own display cannot prove sterility by itself. The characteristic alarm is a cycle failure that aborts the run and leaves the load unsterile. Cycle temperature, hydrogen peroxide concentration, exposure time, and the biological indicator incubation period must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The field variant is mostly the old technology used well, which is a pressure cooker or a small autoclave heated over whatever fuel is available, with the air removed, the load arranged so that steam can penetrate, and the cycle timed and recorded. It gives up the low-temperature chemistry, the sealed chamber, and the automatic control, and it demands that the operator understand the difference between steam and air, because a chamber full of air is a chamber that is not sterilizing, while solar disinfection of water and simple heat-based disinfection of small items remain limited and verifiable only by careful process control.

Delivery by unmanned aircraft is a problem of energy and distance, because lift costs power continuously and the power is carried in a battery whose mass reduces the payload the aircraft can lift. A rotor turns electrical energy into thrust, the aircraft climbs against gravity, and the range is set by the battery capacity divided by the rate at which it is consumed, so range, speed, payload, and wind are traded against one another. The clinical use is the movement of small, light, urgent, and perishable things, such as a blood sample from a remote clinic to a laboratory, a unit of blood or a vaccine in the other direction, or a rapid diagnostic test that must be read within a short window. Payload mass, flight range, wind limit, and battery state of charge must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The hospital-grade program looks like an airline, with a route survey, a designated landing area, a maintained fleet, trained pilots, and a maintenance log. The field variant is a single aircraft flown by a trained health worker between two fixed points, and it gives up the fleet, the spare parts, and sometimes the cold chain, while gaining a route that no road can match during a flood. It demands a charging routine, a protected landing pad, a container that keeps the specimen cool, and a written protocol for what happens when the aircraft fails or the wind rises, while the safety hazard is a machine with stored energy falling from height near people and the common operator error is to launch with a partially charged battery.

An honest assessment of any emerging technology begins by separating 3 categories. The first is hype, a device that works in a demonstration and cannot be sustained, and whose total cost of ownership is never stated. The second is nearly ready, a device that works in a limited, well-defined task under conditions a district hospital can meet. The third is buy now, which is unglamorous and often invented long ago, and which will still be working when the exciting device is in the back room. The test that sorts them is a set of plain questions, which are what powers it, what does it consume, what does it cost to maintain, who repairs it, how is it cleaned, what does it do when the network fails, and what is the evidence in a setting like mine. A device that cannot answer all of those questions has not yet become equipment, and its performance claims, operating conditions, and maintenance intervals must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before purchase.

For a remote clinic this year, the honest shopping list is short and mostly boring. Reliable solar power with a properly sized battery and a charge controller is the first purchase, because it decides whether anything else can run. A robust oxygen concentrator with a maintenance plan, a set of pulse oximeters, a working refrigerator or cold box for vaccines with a temperature log, and an autoclave that can be repaired locally follow closely behind. So does a telephone with a data plan and a link to a referral hospital, and so does an inventory system that tracks consumption. The exciting items, the diagnostic learning software, the printer, and the delivery aircraft, come after, and only when the questions about power, consumables, maintenance, and training have answers. This is not timidity but arithmetic, because the purpose of equipment is to be working on the day the patient arrives, and the purchase specifications and consumable requirements for each item must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before purchase.

Emerging technology does not escape the maintenance rules of the rest of this book, and in some ways it makes them harder. Firmware and software must be updated, and an update can change an alarm limit, a user interface, or an algorithm without changing the case, so the version in use must be recorded and the changed behavior taught. Batteries are the most common hidden consumable, because their capacity falls with every cycle and with every hour spent hot, and a device that ran all day when new may run half a day a year later. Sensors, electrodes, probe covers, filters, and printer filament all expire or degrade, and each has a replacement interval that must be written into the maintenance plan. Cleaning is its own hazard, because a sealed electronic device tolerates a wiped surface and not a soaked one, and a disinfectant correct for a metal instrument can cloud a plastic screen or corrode a connector. The device's cleaning method, battery replacement interval, and software update procedure must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The invisible component of every emerging device is data, and data has physical requirements that a remote clinic may not meet, because it must be stored with enough capacity, transmitted with enough bandwidth, protected from loss and unauthorized access, and retained long enough to be useful without becoming a liability. A remote monitoring program that sends information to a distant server creates a duty of confidentiality in 2 places at once, so the hospital-grade answer is a governed system with access controls and audit trails, while the field answer holds as little data as the task requires and deletes it when the reason for holding it has passed.

Every device in this book, old or new, ends in the same place, which is a person lying on a bed or sitting in a chair with a question about their own body. A new technology that cannot be explained to the patient, that cannot be cleaned between patients, or that cannot be repaired within reach is not an advance for that patient, however elegant its mathematics. The listener who has followed this book now holds a method that outlasts any particular machine, which is to find the physical principle, name what the device consumes, identify what can fail and how that failure announces itself, and confirm every number against the manufacturer and the local protocol. That method will still be valid when today's emerging technology is ordinary and when something not yet imagined has taken its place. The patient is the reason for the whole inventory, and the equipment exists to serve the patient rather than the reverse.

To summarize, emerging technology is not a separate kind of medicine but the same physical events arranged in new ways, and it must be judged by the same questions that judge an autoclave or a syringe. Artificial intelligence learns patterns from examples and fails when the examples no longer match, so it must be validated where it is used. Wearables turn the light, motion, and voltage of the body into numbers that require a reader and a rule. Three-dimensional printing makes a custom shape from a digital model and is trustworthy only as far as its material and its sterilization. Robotics buys precision at a cost few clinics can carry, telemedicine trades distance for bandwidth, telephone applications mostly escape regulation, and solar power, new sterilization chemistry, and delivery aircraft each add reach while adding a new dependency. What a clinic should buy this year is power, oxygen, cold storage, and repair capacity, and what it should treat with suspicion is any device whose consumable, spare part, or repair cannot be named, because every one of these machines exists to serve a patient and the measure of the technology is whether the patient is better served.
