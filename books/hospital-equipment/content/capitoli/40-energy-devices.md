# Chapter 40 — Energy Devices

Surgery has always used a blade, and the energy device is the blade replaced by heat, by vibration, or by light. Radiofrequency electrosurgery passes an alternating current through the body and lets the resistance of the tissue turn that current into heat. Ultrasonic devices vibrate a blade tens of thousands of times each second so that friction generates heat at the point of contact. Lasers deliver light whose energy is absorbed by water or by pigment inside cells, and argon plasma coagulation carries current through a channel of ionized gas. A listener who understands where the heat is made, and how deeply it reaches, will recognize the moment when a device begins to injure rather than to treat.

Electrosurgery begins with an alternating current whose frequency is far above the range that nerves and muscles can follow. Tissue conducts because it holds dissolved salts and water, so a current passed through it stimulates a nerve until the frequency rises above roughly 100 kilohertz, a figure that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. At that point the ions can no longer follow the reversal, the stimulation disappears, and the only useful effect left is heating where the tissue resists the current. A modern generator therefore produces about 300 to 500 kilohertz, a range that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Heating depends on current density, so a small electrode boils the water inside cells while a large electrode barely warms the skin, and this one idea explains the active tip, the return pad, and most electrosurgical burns.

In the monopolar configuration the current enters at a small active electrode, crosses the patient, and leaves through a large return electrode stuck to the skin. The active tip concentrates the current until the cells beneath it explode into steam, while the dispersive pad spreads the same current over a wide area so the skin stays cool. The current follows the path of least resistance rather than a straight line, so the pad must lie on a large muscle mass, clean and dry, with no hair, moisture, or bony point beneath it, and the exact placement and the adhesive supplied by the maker must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The characteristic alarm is the return electrode monitoring alarm, which measures the impedance of the pad circuit and stops the output when the pad is loose or missing, and the common operator error is to press a loose pad down instead of replacing it.

The generator shapes the current into two waveforms, and the difference between them is the difference between cutting and coagulation. A continuous cutting waveform with moderate voltage boils the water in cells and divides tissue along a clean line with little charring. An interrupted coagulation waveform leaves pauses for the tissue to cool, so proteins denature and shrink into a coagulum that seals small vessels, but the higher peak voltage needed spreads heat more deeply and less predictably. Typical settings lie between about 30 and 80 watts for cutting and between about 30 and 60 watts for coagulation, a range that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the common error is to raise the power whenever bleeding obscures the field, because more power rarely improves a seal and often deepens the injury.

The bipolar configuration puts both poles into one instrument, so no return pad and no long path through the body are needed. The surgeon holds a forceps whose two insulated jaws carry the active current and the return, and current passes only through the small volume of tissue grasped between them. Because the path is short and the tissue is thin, the voltage is lower than in monopolar surgery and thermal spread beyond the jaws is limited to a millimeter or two, a distance that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, which makes bipolar forceps suitable near nerves, in the pediatric patient, in the patient with a pacemaker, and inside the laparoscope. The characteristic alarm is the impedance alarm that sounds when the jaws are shorted by a metal clip or by saline, and the common error is to grasp too much tissue so that only the outer surface seals and bleeding resumes when the jaws are released.

The generator is a box of power electronics that turns mains current into a controlled radiofrequency output, and everything the surgeon holds hangs from it. A handpiece cable carries current to the pencil, which is activated by a button or by a foot switch while a tone tells the whole team that energy is being delivered. The active electrode is a small metal tip, and the choice of blade, ball, needle, or loop decides whether the current is concentrated on a line, a point, or a surface. Pads, tips, pencils, cables, foot switches, and cords are consumables with a stated life, and their replacement intervals and reuse rules must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, while the common error is to run a handpiece cable under a trolley wheel and damage the conductor invisibly until a patient is burned.

The complications of electrosurgery are variations on one theme, which is current concentrated where it should not be. A return pad burn occurs when poor adhesion, fluid, hair, or a fold in the drape reduces the pad area, so current leaves through a small patch of skin and heats it. An accidental contact burn is classically caused by the pencil left resting on the patient or on a drape, because the weight of the handpiece can press the activation button, and a burn may also appear at an electrocardiography electrode that offers an unintended return path. The characteristic warning is often the smell of burning rather than any alarm, and the common error is to leave an active pencil on the sterile field without an insulated holster to hold it.

Fire is the most catastrophic complication, and it occurs because an oxygen-rich atmosphere turns an ordinary spark into an ignition source. The ignition triangle needs fuel, oxygen, and heat, and all three are routinely present: a drape or an alcohol-based preparation that has not dried, oxygen from a face mask or an open airway, and the active tip, a laser, or a hot wire, while nitrous oxide supports combustion as readily as oxygen. An alcohol preparation is the commonest fuel because its vapor is heavier than air and can pool under the drapes, so the preparation must be allowed to dry completely before the drapes are applied. When an airway fire occurs, the response is to stop the gas, remove the burning material, flood the area with saline, and secure the airway, because the injury is a burn of the trachea and not only of the surface, and the oxygen concentration used must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Electrosurgery can interfere with implanted electronics because the device may read the radiofrequency current as the heart's own electrical signal. A pacemaker may inhibit its output while energy is applied, or it may sense nothing and pace at its own rate, which is usually harmless. An implanted defibrillator may read the interference as a lethal rhythm and deliver a shock, or it may be reprogrammed, so the device must be interrogated before and after the operation according to a plan made with the device clinic. Bipolar current is safer because it is confined between the jaws, pad placement must keep the current path away from the device and its leads, and the exact magnet behavior must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, while the common error is to assume that a modern device is safe.

The laparoscopic route adds a hazard with no open-surgery equivalent, because the current may travel where the surgeon cannot see it. A monopolar instrument passed through a metal cannula is insulated, and a pinhole, a crack from repeated sterilization, or a break at the tip lets current leak to the cannula and from there to the bowel or the abdominal wall. Current can also be induced in a nearby cannula by capacitive coupling, or pass by direct coupling into another metal instrument that burns the tissue at its far end. These injuries happen outside the field of view and appear days later as a perforated bowel, so every laparoscopic electrode must be inspected with a magnifier under good light before it is passed, and the inspection standard and replacement interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The generator performs a self-test each time it is switched on and refuses to deliver energy if a circuit fault, a missing pad, or an internal error is found. The display shows the selected mode, the power, and the pad status, and a return electrode monitoring alarm, a pad contact quality alarm, or an error code stops the case and must be understood rather than bypassed. A biomedical technician should measure the output into a test load, check the calibration of the displayed power against that measurement, verify the alarm thresholds, and perform an electrical safety test on the chassis and the patient circuit. The acceptable output range, the alarm thresholds, and the test interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the common error is to keep using a generator whose display flickers or whose tone has become unreliable.

Much of electrosurgical maintenance is the inspection and testing of insulation, because the reusable active electrode, the laparoscopic instrument, and the handpiece cable all carry high voltage. A visual inspection with magnification under bright light finds the cracks, the charred spots, the pinched segments, and the exposed conductor that no electrical test can see. An insulation tester applies a high voltage between the conductor and a conductive probe moved along the shaft, a leakage current above the limit set by the maker condemns the instrument, and a continuity test confirms that the electrode and the cord conduct. The instruments that pass are stored straight or loosely coiled, never bent sharply, and never sterilized in a way the maker forbids, because heat and chemicals degrade insulation over time, and the acceptable leakage current, the test voltage, and the test interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The consumables of electrosurgery follow the label and the instructions of the maker rather than habit. Return pads are single-use and must never be re-stuck, re-trimmed, or reused even when they look intact, because their adhesive and their conductive gel are designed for one application. Many tip electrodes and cables are reusable and must be cleaned of charred tissue with the approved method, dried, and inspected, and some tips can be steam sterilized while others cannot, because a tip whose insulation has been softened by the wrong process is a hazard. A non-stick coating that has worn away should be replaced rather than scraped with a blade, and the cleaning agent, the disinfection method, the sterilization cycle, and the permitted number of reuses must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, while the common error is to stack hot electrodes so that the tips bend or the insulation is scored.

Ultrasonic surgery replaces electrical current with mechanical vibration, and its physics is the physics of friction. A stack of piezoelectric crystals expands and contracts when an alternating voltage at about 55,500 hertz is applied, a frequency that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The vibration travels along a metal blade whose tip moves a few tens of micrometers, and pressing that blade against tissue raises the temperature to between about 50 and 100 degrees Celsius, a range that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, which is enough to denature protein and to seal small vessels without boiling water or charring. The same blade cuts when it is advanced and coagulates when it is held still, and because no electrical current passes through the patient the device avoids return pad burns and most interference with implanted devices.

The ultrasonic handpiece demands care in its assembly and its blade. The blade is tightened with the torque wrench the maker supplies, because a blade that is loose will not transmit the vibration and may overheat at the junction, while a blade that is overtightened can strip the threads. The blade is a consumable with a rated life measured in hours of activation or in numbers of procedures, and a worn blade loses its edge and its sealing ability, with the replacement interval and the activation limit confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The tip stays hot after the generator stops, so it must be placed in its holder and never laid on a drape or on the patient, and irrigation through or beside the blade cools the tip and clears the field. The characteristic alarm is the handpiece error or blade fault alarm, which appears when the blade is loose, when the connection is wet, or when the load is outside the expected range, and the common error is to clean a hot blade with a wet cloth, which warps it.

Vessel-sealing devices fuse the walls of a vessel into a permanent seal using pressure and heat. One jaw vibrates or delivers a bipolar current while the other applies pressure, and the combination melts the collagen and elastin in the vessel wall so that the two walls fuse together. The strength of a seal is described by the burst pressure the maker claims, often several times the systolic blood pressure, and the width of the seal and the largest vessel it can close, commonly up to about 7 millimeters in diameter, must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A feedback system ends the cycle when the seal is complete and the generator sounds a distinct tone to mark the moment, but a seal that looks complete may fail later, so a large artery or a vessel under high pressure still needs a suture or a clip, and the common error is to release the jaws before the cycle tone sounds.

A laser is light that is monochromatic, coherent, and collimated, which means a single wavelength with all its waves in step, traveling in a narrow beam that spreads very little. Stimulated emission inside a medium causes each photon to trigger more photons of the same wavelength and direction, so the medium rather than the machine sets the wavelength, and the wavelength decides how deeply the light penetrates and what absorbs it. A carbon dioxide laser at about 10,600 nanometers in the far infrared is absorbed by water within a few cell layers, so it vaporizes superficially and is used for skin lesions, for the cervix, and for the airway. A neodymium yttrium aluminum garnet laser at about 1,064 nanometers penetrates several millimeters and coagulates deeply through a fiber, a holmium yttrium aluminum garnet laser at about 2,100 nanometers fragments stones, a potassium titanyl phosphate laser at about 532 nanometers is absorbed by hemoglobin and pigment, and a diode laser emits between about 800 and 980 nanometers. Every wavelength, power, and pulse setting must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The delivery system must match the wavelength, because glass and water absorb some wavelengths and pass others. A carbon dioxide laser travels through an articulated arm of mirrors or a hollow waveguide, while neodymium and holmium lasers travel through a flexible silica fiber that can be passed through an endoscope, and the fiber tip is fragile and burns back as it is used. The surgeon controls the spot size by focusing and the depth of injury by power density, which is the power divided by the area of the spot, so a smaller spot at the same power is far more intense. Eye protection is wavelength-specific and is not interchangeable, because goggles that block green light will not block invisible infrared, so everyone in the room, including the patient, must wear the correct protection, and the nominal hazard zone, the eyewear specification, the power, and the pulse settings must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A laser is managed as a controlled area rather than as a tool, with a warning light at the door, an interlock that stops the beam when the door opens, a key or code that prevents untrained use, and a nominated person responsible for its records. The beam must never be aimed at a shiny instrument, because the reflection is as dangerous as the direct beam, and the field must stay dry because a wet drape or a pool of fluid can reflect light and can also conduct heat. The greatest fire risk is the airway laser, where a tube or a gauze lies inside an oxygen-rich atmosphere, so the special tubes and the saline-filled cuff are used and the oxygen is kept as low as the saturation of the patient allows. Surgical smoke is produced by every laser and every electrosurgical device, and it carries particles small enough to reach the depths of the lung, together with benzene, formaldehyde, and the genetic material of the patient, so a high-flow evacuator with a filter must capture it close to the source, with its flow setting and filter replacement interval confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Argon plasma coagulation is a monopolar method that carries current to tissue without touching it, and its physics is the ionization of a gas. Argon flows through a probe at a few liters per minute, a high voltage between the central electrode and the tissue ionizes the argon into a conducting plasma, and the current follows that channel to the nearest earthed tissue and produces a superficial burn. The probe never touches the tissue, so nothing sticks and the tip does not char, and the injury is usually shallower than that of a laser or an ultrasonic blade, a depth that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The current still needs a return pad, and because argon is heavier than air and is not absorbed from the bowel it can distend a loop of intestine or cause gas embolism, so a probe used inside the gut must come with a plan for the gas it delivers, and the common error is to use it there without one.

A standard suction tip does not clear surgical smoke, because it captures only what passes close to its opening and the capture velocity falls with distance. The hospital-grade energy device is a small system rather than one box, and the operating theatre is built to support it. A grounded mains supply, often routed through an isolated circuit that limits the current of a first fault, feeds the generator from a dedicated socket within reach of the surgeon. The trolley also carries the smoke evacuator, the argon console, or the laser console, so the consumables that must be in the room before the case include correctly sized return pads, the tip electrodes for the planned mode, a spare handpiece cable, ultrasonic blades and their torque wrench, sealing cartridges, laser fibers, and smoke filters, and the socket, circuit, and accessory arrangement must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The field variant begins with the battery-powered thermal cautery, which is a different device from the electrosurgical generator even though it performs a similar task. Its physics is simple resistive heating, because a small battery drives a current through a fine wire tip whose resistance makes it glow dull red and char the tissue it touches. The instrument is a disposable pen with a button, a battery, and one or two wire tips, and it needs no mains supply, no return pad, and no generator, which is what lets it survive in a clinic without reliable electricity, but it cannot cut because its few watts only char the surface. The tip reaches several hundred degrees Celsius, a temperature that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and it remains hot after the button is released, while its characteristic alarm is silence, because the device has no monitoring and no alarm, and the common error is to leave it switched on in a pocket or on a drape.

Where mains power exists, a small bipolar generator is the field instrument of choice, because it needs no return pad and confines the current between the jaws. Fine bipolar forceps seal a bleeding point in a thyroid, a scalp, or a limb with little deep tissue damage, and the same forceps can be reused for years if they are cleaned and their insulation is inspected. A compact generator can run from mains or from a small inverter connected to a vehicle battery, and the output depends entirely on the quality of that supply, with the voltage and the duty cycle confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, so a display showing a high setting may still not deliver the power a large monopolar case needs. The bipolar generator has the same impedance alarm as its hospital cousin, but an alarm helps only if someone recognizes it, and the common error is to trust a low-power machine with a vessel that needs a suture to hold.

Before any energy device is used, the operator must complete a short check that is the same in spirit everywhere. Confirm that the generator passes its self-test, that the pad is the correct size and firmly applied to clean dry skin away from the current path, and that the tip, cord, and foot switch are intact. Confirm that the planned mode and power match the tissue and the protocol, that the smoke filter is seated, that the correct blade or fiber is fitted and torqued, and that the active tip stays in its holder until the surgeon asks for it. Name the alarm that would stop the case, know what each message means, and confirm every setting against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, because a check performed from memory is only as good as the memory behind it.

The field energy device gives up a great deal, and what it gives up is what the operator must replace with technique. It offers no monopolar cutting with a return pad, no argon plasma coagulation, no laser, no ultrasonic or sealing instrument, and usually no smoke evacuation, and it also lacks impedance monitoring, a patient circuit test, and often any electrical safety measurement. What it demands instead is a surgeon who can tie a ligature, who can control bleeding by pressure and packing, who inspects every reusable forceps for a broken jaw or a cracked insulator, and who keeps the cautery in its holder, together with a fire-safe field, a tested battery, spare tips, and the honesty to admit that a vessel too large for the instrument needs a suture rather than a longer press of the button. The hospital machine gives monitored current, a choice of waveforms and powers, a pad that reports its own contact, smoke evacuation, and scheduled maintenance, while the field kit substitutes the judgment of the surgeon for the feedback of the generator, and neither version is simply better, because an uninspected generator is more dangerous than a simple pen used with care.

The safety of any energy device rests on three habits that apply everywhere. Inspect the instrument and its insulation before use, keep the active tip in its holder and away from the patient when it is not in use, and understand the path the current will take or the heat will spread. A device that is understood can be used to its limit and not beyond it, while a device that is trusted without understanding will eventually injure someone. Every power, temperature, frequency, depth, and alarm threshold in this chapter must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, because the numbers that suit one tissue and one patient may not suit the next.

To summarize, the energy devices of surgery replace the blade with heat, vibration, or light, and each works by concentrating energy at a small point and letting tissue convert it into a change. Electrosurgery passes a radiofrequency current above the range that nerves can follow, concentrates it at a small active electrode, disperses it through a large return pad, and shapes it into a continuous waveform for cutting or an interrupted waveform for coagulation, while the bipolar configuration confines the current between two jaws and needs no pad. The complications are pad burns, contact burns, fire in an oxygen-rich field, interference with a pacemaker or an implanted defibrillator, and the unseen injury from current that channels through a laparoscopic cannula or a defective insulation. Ultrasonic dissectors vibrate a blade so that friction seals and cuts, vessel-sealing devices fuse vessel walls under pressure and heat, lasers deliver a wavelength chosen for the depth of penetration, argon plasma coagulation conducts current through ionized gas without contact, and all of them produce smoke that must be evacuated rather than breathed. In the field the battery cautery and the small bipolar generator replace the hospital systems and give up cutting power, monitoring, and smoke control in exchange for independence from mains electricity, so what they demand is a surgeon who ties and packs and inspects, an understanding of the heat path, and the honesty to ask for a suture when the device has reached its limit.
