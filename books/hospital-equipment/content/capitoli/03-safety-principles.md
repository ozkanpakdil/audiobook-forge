# Chapter 3 — Safety Principles

Safety is not a poster on a wall, and it is not a subject read once and filed away. It is a property of a system, and like every property of a system it can be designed, measured, tested, and lost. The devices in this book are safe only when 3 conditions hold at once: the machine is built and maintained to a standard, the building supplies what the machine expects, and the person using it understands what it is doing to the patient. Remove any of the 3 and the same device that saves a life in one hospital can injure a patient in another. This chapter explains the physical principles behind the safety systems that surround medical equipment, because a listener who understands why a protective earth conductor exists will notice the moment it is missing. It then walks the same territory again in a mobile or frontier clinic, where the infrastructure is absent and discipline has to take its place. The subject is large, so it is divided into the electrical, the gaseous, the informational, the radiological, and the mechanical, with the human being who operates all of it at the center.

Begin with the physical fact that makes electricity dangerous: current needs a complete circuit, and the human body can become part of one. A device is connected to the mains supply by a pair of conductors that carry the current out and back, and inside the device the current is meant to stay in copper. Insulation wraps the copper, and insulation is never perfect, so a small current always finds a path around it, and this unwanted current is called leakage current. In a healthy device the leakage is tiny, on the order of a few tens of microamperes, and a value of that kind must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the device is used on a patient. The danger appears when leakage reaches a conductive surface that a patient or a nurse can touch, because the body then offers a path to the floor and the current completes its circuit through flesh. The current that can pass depends on the voltage driving it and on the resistance of the path, and dry intact skin has a high resistance while wet, broken, or catheterized tissue has a much lower one.

The first defense is the protective earth conductor, the third wire in the mains cable that connects the metal chassis of the device to the earth of the building. Its purpose is not to carry operating current but to give leakage current a harmless road back to the source, so that a fault blows a fuse or trips a breaker instead of waiting for a hand to touch the chassis. When the earth path is intact, a fault inside the device produces a large current that the electrical protection of the building recognizes and interrupts. When it is broken, the same fault leaves the chassis at a dangerous voltage with nothing to announce it, and the device appears to work normally. This is why a missing earth pin, a damaged plug, an adapter that defeats the third pin, or an extension lead with a broken earth is a latent hazard rather than a housekeeping matter. The operator check takes seconds: look at the plug and the cord, feel for the loose connector, and refuse to use a device whose earth path is doubtful.

Equipotential bonding is the second defense, and it attacks the problem from another direction. If 2 conductive surfaces are connected by a thick conductor, they cannot sit at different voltages, and no current can flow between them through a patient who touches both. In an operating theatre or an intensive care unit, or ICU, the metal of the table, the infusion stands, the anesthesia machine, and the monitor chassis are bonded to a common point so that the whole environment rises and falls together. The principle is that current flows only when there is a difference in potential, so a difference of zero cannot hurt. Bonding does not remove a fault and does not protect against contact with a live conductor; it removes the gradient across the patient's body that a fault would otherwise create. The hazard it addresses is invisible, because a patient lying on a conductive table and connected to a monitoring cable is a circuit waiting for a small voltage to appear between 2 points.

A third layer, found in operating theatres and some critical care areas, is the isolated power supply, which separates the mains from the room by a transformer so that neither output conductor is connected to earth. The reasoning is that a first fault to earth in an isolated system cannot complete a circuit through a person, because there is no return path, and the room can keep working until the fault is found. A line isolation monitor watches the system continuously and sounds a characteristic alarm when the leakage to earth crosses a threshold, and any such threshold must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the room is used for a patient. Water changes the arithmetic completely, because a wet floor and wet skin collapse the resistance that normally limits the current through a body. A person standing in a puddle beside a faulty device can receive a current many times larger than the same person would receive on a dry floor, and the current that passes through the heart is the one that stops it. The most sensitive patient is the one with a catheter or a pacing wire inside the heart, and currents a healthy person would never feel can provoke a dangerous rhythm in that patient.

Applied-part classifications, defined by the International Electrotechnical Commission, or IEC, in its standard for medical electrical equipment, describe how closely a device touches the patient and therefore how strictly it must limit leakage. A type B applied part may touch the patient but not the heart, a type BF applied part is isolated from earth for direct patient contact, and a type CF applied part is isolated more strictly still and is the only type suitable for a connection that reaches the heart. A further marking states whether the applied part is defibrillation-proof, which means it survives a defibrillation discharge without passing a dangerous current back into the patient or the operator. The common operator error is to connect a general-purpose probe or an unmarked cable to a cardiac monitor, or to substitute an accessory from another device when the correct one is unavailable. No alarm can help here, because the error is silent, and the only defense is to read the marking and to keep a stock of the correct consumables.

Much of what fails in electrical safety fails in the consumables rather than in the machine. Patient cables break at the strain relief where they bend every day, electrode gel dries and raises the impedance until the trace is unreadable, reusable sensor probes crack, plugs lose their grip, and extension leads multiply until a single socket carries more than it was designed to carry. Cleaning is a hazard in its own right, because liquid pushed into a ventilator grille, a socket, or a switch can bridge insulation, and aggressive solvents can dissolve the plastic that keeps the patient separated from the current. The operator check before every use is visual: the plug, the cord, the strain relief, the housing, the connectors, and the absence of spills or condensation. A frayed cord, a cracked case, a scorch mark, or a burning smell removes the device from service immediately, and the fault is recorded rather than concealed.

Behind the daily check sits a program of scheduled testing that only a biomedical technician or an electrician can perform. A safety analyzer measures the resistance of the protective earth path and the leakage current that flows when the device is powered in each of its operating modes, including the mode with the supply conductors reversed and the mode with the earth lifted to reveal how the device behaves without it. The measured limits that decide a pass or a failure differ by device type and by country, and every limit must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the test result is accepted. A device that passes on the bench can still fail on the ward, which is why the schedule, the test label, and the register that shows what is overdue matter as much as the instrument reading. The failure mode to fear most is the intermittent one, the fault that appears only when the cable is bent a certain way, and it is found by repeated testing and by listening to the staff who report a tingle.

Medical gases carry a different physics, and the first fact is compression. A gas stored under pressure in a cylinder is a great deal of energy in a metal bottle, and a cylinder whose valve is struck can leave the room like a missile. The second fact is that the gas is invisible, so the identity of the cylinder rests on its label, its color code in the system in use, and the pins that index its valve to the correct regulator. The pin index system exists because a cylinder of one gas must not fit the regulator of another, and the common operator error is to force a connection with an adapter or a homemade coupling exactly when the correct cylinder has run out. A wrong connection delivers the hazard to the patient directly, and a cylinder of the wrong gas can be fatal before anyone notices the mistake. A cylinder must be secured upright, its valve closed when it is not in use, its contents checked on a gauge rather than by shaking it, and its regulator and tubing inspected for cracks and for the wrong grease.

Oxygen deserves its own paragraph because it is not flammable by itself and yet it makes everything else burn. A material that smolders in air can burst into flame in an oxygen-rich atmosphere, and clothing, grease, oil, and alcohol-based skin preparation become dangerous when oxygen is near. The physical rule is that oxygen lowers the ignition temperature and greatly increases the speed of combustion, which is why no oil or grease may touch a regulator, a valve, or a fitting, and why hand cream and adhesive residues matter. The fire risk is highest where a diathermy device, an electrosurgical unit, or a laser works near an oxygen mask, and where a procedure is performed on the face or airway of a patient receiving oxygen. Piped oxygen and vacuum systems add the hazards of the building itself, including terminal units that accept the wrong probe, connections that come apart under a trolley wheel, and the cross-connection of gases during construction or repair. Every terminal, every flowmeter, and every alarm threshold on a piped system must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol, and the gas identity must be verified at the point of use rather than assumed from the color of the wall outlet.

Alarms are a safety system in their own right, and like every safety system they can be designed well or badly. An alarm is a chain that begins with a sensor, passes through a decision rule, and ends in a sound and a light, and a failure anywhere in the chain removes the protection while leaving the device apparently working. The design of alarm limits determines whether the alarm means something: a limit set too wide never fires, a limit set too narrow fires constantly, and a limit left at the factory default may be wrong for the patient in the bed. The limit that suits a patient must be chosen deliberately at the start of a shift and confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before monitoring begins. Alarm fatigue is the predictable result of too many alarms, and it is dangerous because the staff stop hearing the one that matters; the response is to reduce nuisance alarms, to set limits per patient, and never to silence an alarm and walk away. The characteristic alarms of the devices in this book are named where each device is described, and each means something specific: a low airway pressure alarm, an air-in-line alarm, an occlusion alarm, a low oxygen supply alarm, a ventricular fibrillation alarm.

Human factors is the study of how the design of a device invites or prevents a mistake, and it deserves to be treated as engineering rather than as blame. The classic traps are the 2 connectors that fit each other but carry different gases, the 2 buttons that look and feel identical, the syringe whose label is hidden by the hand that holds it, the knob that turns the wrong way, and the screen whose most important number is the smallest one. The device that invites the wrong action is a design failure, and the operator who falls into the trap is its victim, not its cause. The defenses are unglamorous and effective: standardized connectors, distinct shapes and colors, clear labeling on every container and line, a hard stop before a dangerous action, and the checklist that catches what memory drops. The read-back habit is the strongest defense in communication, and it means that the person receiving an order or a setting repeats it aloud and the giver confirms it, so that a number heard wrongly is caught before it reaches the patient. Labeling is part of the same discipline, and any syringe, bag, line, or container that leaves the hand of the person who prepared it must carry a label that says exactly what is inside.

Incident reporting is how a system learns, and the culture around it decides whether it works. A report is useful when it records what the device was, what it was doing, what the patient was doing, what the operator saw and heard, and what happened next, without turning the page into an accusation. The reports that teach the most are often the near misses, the events that injured nobody because someone noticed in time, and a system that collects only injuries learns only from its worst days. A culture that makes reporting work answers every report, separates the person from the system, and closes the loop by changing the equipment, the label, the protocol, or the training and then telling everyone what changed. A hospital without a reporting habit is not a hospital without incidents; it is a hospital that does not know about them.

Radiation protection rests on a principle that can be stated in one line: dose falls with time, rises steeply as distance falls, and is reduced by whatever dense material stands between the source and the body. The dose a worker receives is proportional to the time spent near the source, so the first control is to be there briefly and to work efficiently. Distance is the most powerful control, because the intensity of a beam from a point source falls with the square of the distance, which means that stepping back twice as far leaves one quarter of the dose. Shielding is the third control, and lead aprons, thyroid shields, leaded glass, and the collimator that narrows the beam reduce the dose to the patient and to the staff at once. A dosimeter worn at the collar or the waist measures what the worker actually received, and the dose limits that define a safe working life must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before any individual is permitted to work with radiation. The common operator error is to hold a patient during an exposure, to stand in the primary beam, or to repeat an image because the first was badly positioned, since every repeat doubles the dose to everyone.

Not all harm comes from machines that push current, gas, or radiation into a patient, and the quiet hazards of a ward deserve the same attention. Falls happen at the bed when a side rail is left down, when a call bell is out of reach, when a patient is left on a wet floor, and when a chair slides because its brakes were never set. Entrapment happens when a patient's head, limb, or body becomes caught between a mattress and a rail, between bars, or in the gap of a bed assembled from mismatched parts, and the risk grows with confusion, sedation, and a patient who is small or very old. Burns arrive from heating pads applied to skin that cannot feel them, from hot packs, from spilled disinfectant, from a diathermy return pad placed over a bony point or a scar, and from oxygen tubing that lies against the skin. The injuries that hurt staff are equally real: the back injured by a manual lift, the shoulder torn by a patient who grabs an arm during a fall, the needle that punctures a hand in a hurry. Safe patient handling is therefore an equipment subject, because the hoist, the sliding sheet, the transfer board, the adjustable bed, and enough staff to do the job are the devices that prevent the injury.

Cleaning and reprocessing are safety procedures in their own right, and they fail in ways that are easy to see once they are described. A device wiped with a cloth that is too wet pushes fluid past its seals, a device cleaned with the wrong chemical loses its housing and its labels, and a reusable instrument that is rinsed but not disinfected carries the last patient into the next one. Single-use consumables are marked as such for reasons that include the material, the geometry, the sterility, and the inability to clean the inside of a narrow lumen, and reusing them saves money in a way that is paid for later. The failure modes to name plainly are the cracked housing that admits fluid, the perished seal that admits dust and moisture, the battery that swells and leaks, the tubing that kinks and occludes, the filter that clogs and stops the flow, and the sensor that drifts until its reading is a lie. The check is short: the device is clean and dry, its consumables are within their expiry date, its alarms are enabled, its battery is charged or its plug is secure, and its accessories match the device and the patient. Anything that fails the check is removed, labeled as faulty, and reported rather than used with an apology.

Now walk the same ground in a mobile or frontier clinic, where the building does not supply what the hospital supplies. The electrical supply may come from a generator, from a solar array and a battery, or from an inverter tied to a vehicle, and the protective earth may be a local rod driven into dry soil or may not exist at all. The hospital-grade defenses are largely absent: there is no line isolation monitor, no equipotential bonding grid in the walls, no scheduled leakage test, and often no technician within a day's travel. What the field keeps is the physical principle and the discipline that follows from it: double-insulated devices are preferred because they do not depend on an earth connection, battery-powered devices are preferred for anything that touches the heart, and every device is unplugged before it is cleaned or opened. A residual current device, a small switch that compares the current leaving and returning and trips when they differ, is cheap and valuable where the supply is unpredictable, and its trip threshold must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before it is relied upon. The generator introduces its own hazards, including exhaust gas that must never enter the clinic, fuel stored away from every ignition source, and a frame that must be bonded and grounded rather than left floating.

The field gas supply is a cylinder on a trolley or a concentrator beside the bed, and the safety rules travel with the physics. A cylinder carried on a motorcycle must be strapped upright, protected at the valve, and separated from fuel and from the sun, because a heated cylinder raises its internal pressure and becomes more dangerous. There is no pipeline, no wall outlet color code, no central alarm, and no scavenging system, so the identity of the gas rests on the label and on the person who reads it, and the waste anesthetic gas goes into the room unless a window and a fan are used deliberately. The most dangerous field habit is the improvised connection, the adapter made to fit because the correct regulator is missing, and the second most dangerous is the oxygen cylinder left open at a low flow through the night beside a patient who is also being warmed. Where no alarm exists, a person must take its place, and a routine of checking the gauge, the tubing, and the patient at fixed intervals is the field substitute for a monitoring system.

Radiation protection in the field is the distance rule applied with discipline, because a portable unit used in a room with no lead lining is safe only when everyone who does not have to be there is somewhere else. The operator stands behind the cassette rather than beside the beam, uses the longest cable or the remote exposure switch, collimates to the smallest field, and records who was exposed and how often. Where no dosimeter exists, the arithmetic of distance and time becomes the record, and the local rules that set the annual limit must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol. Human factors get harder in the field rather than easier, because equipment arrives without its manual, labels are handwritten, and the same bottle is used for 2 different fluids. The device that invites the wrong action is just as dangerous when it is battery-powered and carried in a bag, and the response is the same: separate the look-alike items, mark the connectors, and keep the 2 gases in 2 different places. The field worker who cannot change the equipment can still change the arrangement of it, and the arrangement is most of the safety.

In the field, the mechanical and handling hazards are the ones most often overlooked, because the machines are simple and the room is small. A canvas stretcher on the ground, a wooden bench used as a bed, and a rope bed give no side rail, no brake, and no height adjustment, so the fall from a low bed is still a fall and the confused patient will not be stopped by a rail that does not exist. Lifting is done by people rather than hoists, which makes the injured back a common field injury, and the practical answers are the ones that need no purchase: 2 people rather than 1, a sheet used as a slide, the bed placed at the height of the workers' hips, and the patient taught to help where the patient can. Burns come from hot water bottles, from a fire used for warmth, from a lamp used for light, and from a generator exhaust, and each of these is a design decision rather than an accident. Cleaning is done with whatever water and cloth are available, and the discipline is to keep fluid out of the openings of a device, to dry it fully before use, and to separate the cloth that cleans the floor from the cloth that touches a wound.

Maintenance in the field is a different discipline rather than an absent one. There is no service contract, no calibration laboratory, and often no spare part, so the work moves to the operator and the visiting technician, and the equipment that survives is the equipment that can be repaired with what is on the shelf. Cannibalization, the practice of taking a part from one broken device to keep another working, is honest and useful when it is recorded and dangerous when it is silent, because a device repaired from 2 others may no longer match its own specification. Consumables are the field's weakest point, and the discipline is to track expiry dates, to protect the cold chain, to keep the correct accessories with each device, and to refuse a substitute that changes what the device does. Incident reporting survives even where the system behind it does not, because a notebook that records every failure, the date, and the action taken turns one clinic's experience into everyone's warning. Safety in the field is therefore not a poster and not an inspection, but the daily habit of checking, labeling, cleaning, reporting, and asking, performed by people who understand why each step exists.

To summarize, safety is a physical property of a system and not a sentiment, and it rests on principles that can be learned and checked. Electrical safety keeps leakage current small, gives it a harmless path through the protective earth conductor, holds conductive surfaces at one potential through bonding, isolates the supply where the patient is most vulnerable, and classifies every applied part so that only the right accessory touches the heart. Gas safety rests on compression, on identity, on the pin index that prevents a wrong connection, and on the fact that oxygen accelerates fire rather than causing it. Alarms are a chain that must be designed, tested, and set for the individual patient, and alarm fatigue is a hazard in its own right. Human factors, labeling, and the read-back habit prevent the errors that no alarm can detect, and incident reporting with a culture that answers every report is how a hospital and a clinic both learn. Radiation protection is time, distance, and shielding, measured by a dosimeter and confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol, while falls, entrapment, burns, and the injuries that staff suffer while moving patients are equipment problems with equipment answers. The field variant gives up the infrastructure, the service contract, and the central alarm, and demands in their place discipline, redundancy, and understanding. In the chapters that follow, every device is described with the same questions in mind: what can it do to the patient, what can the operator do to it, what alarm announces the failure, what consumable keeps it honest, and what check proves it is ready.
