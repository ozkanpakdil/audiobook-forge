# Chapter 50 — Oncology and Radiotherapy

Cancer treatment rests on two families of hardware that could hardly be more different. One delivers ionizing radiation, from outside the body or from a source placed within it, and the other delivers poisonous molecules through a vein, a muscle, or the mouth. Both exploit the same weakness, because a cell dividing rapidly and repairing its genetic damage poorly is easier to kill than a resting cell with intact repair. Radiation strips electrons from atoms and breaks chemical bonds in deoxyribonucleic acid, while chemotherapy interferes with the machinery that copies that acid, and the equipment chain keeps the two apart.

Ionizing radiation carries enough energy to remove an electron from an atom, and treatment beams carry millions of electronvolts. A photon beam deposits its energy unevenly, reaching a maximum at a depth that depends on its energy and then falling away as photons are absorbed and scattered. Tumor cells are more sensitive when oxygen is present and repair damage between exposures, so the dose is divided into small treatments called fractions, usually once each day for several weeks, and that schedule must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Dividing the dose lets normal tissue recover between sessions while tumor cells accumulate damage they cannot repair.

The linear accelerator, the central machine of external beam radiotherapy, accelerates electrons in a straight line of cavities rather than relying on a radioactive source. An electron gun injects electrons into a copper waveguide, where a powerful radiofrequency field from a magnetron or a klystron pushes them forward in bunches at nearly the speed of light. A bending magnet turns the beam toward the patient and selects the energy that reaches the treatment head. The assembly sits in a gantry that rotates around the patient, so beams from many directions concentrate dose in the tumor while sparing the skin.

Inside the treatment head the electron beam meets either a target or a scattering foil. Striking a small tungsten target, the sudden stopping produces a broad spectrum of high-energy photons by a process called bremsstrahlung, the radiation emitted whenever a charged particle is deflected. A conical flattening filter then absorbs part of the central beam so the field is nearly uniform, giving the photon beam used for deep tumors. When the target is withdrawn and a thin foil inserted, the electrons themselves travel to the patient, which suits shallow lesions, and ion chambers in the head monitor the beam continuously.

The field is shaped twice, first by two pairs of jaws and then precisely by the multileaf collimator, a bank of thin tungsten leaves that slide into the beam. A modern collimator may hold 40 to 80 pairs of leaves, each projecting a width of 5 to 10 millimeters at the isocenter, and that figure must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Moving the leaves produces a field that follows the tumor outline, and shaping the beam from several directions, a technique called intensity-modulated radiotherapy, lets the dose wrap around sensitive structures. An error in one leaf can create a hot or cold spot invisible on the skin, so the machine verifies its leaves before every field.

Treatment machines are built around a small palette of energies, most commonly photon beams of 6, 10, and 18 megavolts and electron beams of 6 to 20 megaelectronvolts, and the chosen energy must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Higher photon energies deposit their maximum dose deeper and spare the skin better, but scatter more widely. Electron beams deposit their dose within a few centimeters and then stop abruptly, suiting skin cancers, chest wall treatments, and scar boosts. Flattening filter free beams run at a much higher dose rate and shorten each treatment.

The gantry, its stand, and the treatment couch all rotate about a point called the isocenter, and the distance from source to that point, conventionally 100 centimeters and always to be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, is a fixed reference. Three wall lasers project thin red lines that cross at the isocenter, and the patient lies on a carbon fiber couch top that absorbs little radiation. Mechanical accuracy is not a luxury, because a couch that sags under a heavy patient shifts the tumor relative to the beam. Therapists therefore check the lasers, the couch readout, and the gantry angle at the start of every session.

Because the tumor moves with breathing, bladder filling, and the position of the bowel, the machine carries its own imaging so the patient can be corrected just before the beam is switched on. The simplest system uses the treatment beam itself to make a portal image on an electronic detector, while better systems add a kilovoltage tube and detector at right angles to the beam. A third option reconstructs a cone-beam computed tomography volume in the treatment position, and the computer calculates the shift so the couch can be moved. The common operator error is to accept the automatic match without looking at the anatomy, so a second person should confirm the images belong to the patient.

Everything begins in a planning computed tomography scan taken with the patient in the exact position treatment will use. The images are exported in the Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine format and loaded into the treatment planning system, where the physician draws the tumor volume and every organ at risk. The planner then chooses beam directions and weights, either forward or by inverse planning, and the result appears as dose volume histograms whose every constraint must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A second physicist then checks the plan independently, after which a record and verify system compares the intended delivery with the approved plan.

Immobilization makes a plan reproducible and deserves as much attention as the accelerator. A thermoplastic mask is warmed in a water bath, laid over the face or chest, and molded while it cools into a rigid shell with holes for breathing and seeing. Vacuum cushions filled with beads are sucked free of air and become rigid supports, while bite blocks, ankle stocks, and stereotactic frames serve the same purpose elsewhere. The usual tolerance for repositioning is 3 to 5 millimeters, which must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the common operator error is to store several masks on one shelf so the wrong one is used.

Radiation safety is built into the architecture before any machine arrives. The treatment room walls are poured with dense concrete, sometimes with a steel or lead lining, thick enough that the dose outside falls to a level fixed by national regulation. The entrance is usually a maze, and the door carries an interlock that removes power from the beam the instant it opens, so propping it open defeats the interlock. A survey meter, an area monitor, and personal dosimeters complete the chain, with their readings confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the characteristic alarm is the interlock trip, never to be silenced while a patient remains in the room.

The accelerator reports its own faults through alarms. A door interlock alarm means the beam stopped because the entrance opened, a dose rate servo fault means the output no longer matches the request, and a leaf mismatch alarm means the collimator position disagrees with the plan. A collision interlock stops the gantry or couch when a pressure sensor or an imaging arm touches something, while a water temperature alarm warns that the cooling circuit is drifting. Every threshold must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the therapist should stop, note the code, remove the patient if there is any doubt, and call the physicist rather than reset the fault repeatedly.

Every treatment day begins with checks the therapists perform at the console, and the results are recorded in a log the physicist reviews. The output constancy check confirms the beam still delivers the expected dose, and a drift beyond 2 per cent, which must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, usually stops treatment until the physicist has investigated. The lasers, the imaging system, the door interlock, and the collimator are all checked against known references. Weekly and monthly checks add imaging quality and dosimetry, and only a qualified medical physicist may change a calibration.

Between these checks the machine is kept alive by maintenance that is mostly invisible to clinical staff. The accelerating waveguide is cooled by a closed circuit of deionized water, and its conductivity, temperature, and flow must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, because a small leak or a failing pump will cook the machine. Air and water filters are replaced on schedule, the magnetron or klystron has a limited life measured in hours of beam on time, and annual maintenance includes a full mechanical alignment. A linear accelerator cannot be repaired locally, because it needs the manufacturer's service engineer and sometimes a shipped part, so the department needs a written plan for the days when the beam is unavailable.

The consumables of external beam radiotherapy are few, cheap, and necessary. Bolus is a sheet of tissue-equivalent material laid on the skin to pull the maximum dose toward the surface, and it must be labeled with the patient's name. Thermoplastic sheets are the raw material of masks, electron cutouts are shaped from wax or plastic and lined with lead, and wedges and compensators modify the beam so a flat field becomes a sloped one. Diodes and ionization chambers verify dose in a phantom, radiochromic film and gel dosimeters record a distribution in two or three dimensions, and each item's expiry must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Brachytherapy places a radioactive source directly in or beside the tumor, and its physics is the mirror image of external beam treatment, with the hazard that a source outside its shield threatens everyone in the room. Because the dose falls with the square of the distance, a source a few millimeters away gives a very high dose there and a much smaller dose a few centimeters away, so the tumor is treated while the rectum or bladder is spared. The sources are sealed capsules of iridium 192, iodine 125, cesium 137, or cobalt 60, chosen for the energy and half-life that suit the implant, and their activity is measured in gigabecquerels. High dose rate treatments deliver the whole dose in a few minutes through a remote afterloader while low dose rate treatments leave the source in place for hours or days, and every activity and dwell time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The afterloader keeps the source safe until the moment it must travel, and the source sits in a shielded safe inside a heavy cabinet. From the safe it runs along a flexible cable through transfer tubes to the applicator, which may be a tandem and ring for the uterus, needles for the prostate, or a balloon catheter for the breast. A stepping motor pushes the source to a series of dwell positions and pauses at each, but if the source does not return to the safe, the machine sounds a characteristic alarm and staff must use the manual crank to retract it. They must then confirm with a survey meter that the room is clear, remove the applicator only at background, and test the crank and emergency kit before every treatment, with those checks confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Nursing care of a brachytherapy patient is an exercise in distance, time, and shielding. A patient with a low dose rate implant stays in a shielded room, visitors keep as far away as the task allows, because doubling the distance quarters the dose, and a lead apron and bedside screen reduce exposure further. A dosimeter worn under the apron tells the truth about what the body received, and the room is surveyed after every source removal while linen that might have touched the source is bagged until monitored. The common operator error is to assume the source has returned because the machine says so, and the second is to let a patient walk to the bathroom with a catheter still loaded, while waste follows its own stream with dedicated containers, labels, and decay storage.

Superficial and orthovoltage units treat the skin and the structures just beneath it with X-rays generated at much lower energies. A superficial unit runs at about 50 to 150 kilovolts and deposits its dose in the first few millimeters, while an orthovoltage unit runs at about 150 to 500 kilovolts and reaches several centimeters, and every kilovoltage and treatment time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. These beams come from an X-ray tube and have no skin-sparing effect, so the skin receives the highest dose, which suits a basal cell cancer or a keloid scar. Cones and lead shields define the field, and the characteristic failure is a drifting output that changes the dose silently.

Beyond the ordinary accelerator lie several specialized machines. Stereotactic radiosurgery uses many small beams converging on a tiny target, often with a rigid head frame fixed to the skull, while stereotactic body radiotherapy treats small tumors in the lung, the liver, or the spine. Proton and heavier ion therapy accelerates particles that deposit most of their energy at a precise depth called the Bragg peak, sparing tissue beyond the target, but the equipment costs many millions and needs a staff of physicists. Total body irradiation prepares a patient for a bone marrow transplant with very large fields and very low dose rates, and every tolerance in these techniques must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

In the field, none of this travels. A linear accelerator weighs several tons and needs a shielded vault, a stable power supply, chilled water, and a medical physicist on site, and the afterloader needs a licensed, surveyed, and physically secure room for its source. What travels instead is the patient, so the equipment of the field position is the equipment of referral, palliation, and honest communication, and the most useful items may be a vehicle, a bed, and a working telephone.

The referral chain is itself a piece of equipment, and it fails in predictable places. A computed tomography scan taken at the district hospital must reach the radiotherapy center in a form the planning system can read, usually a Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine file sent through the Picture Archiving and Communication System or carried on a disk. If the scan was taken without contrast, without the right immobilization, or with the patient in the wrong position, the center will ask for it again and the patient will lose weeks, so a telemedicine link that sends images and a short history helps the oncologist decide. The hub and spoke arrangement works only when the spokes keep records of who was referred and who returned, and every appointment interval should be confirmed against the local protocol first.

Where radiotherapy cannot reach, palliation is the treatment, and its equipment stands in the place of the beam. Morphine and other strong analgesics, given by mouth or by a subcutaneous infusion pump, control pain, while dexamethasone reduces the edema around brain metastases and spinal cord compression. Bisphosphonates slow the destruction of bone, a nerve block interrupts a painful plexus, a stent opens a passage that a tumor has closed, and a catheter and dressing kit may do more for comfort than any machine. The emergencies that radiotherapy would have relieved, such as bleeding, obstruction, and cord compression, must be recognized early, and every drug dose and route here must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Chemotherapy brings its own discipline, and it begins with the biosafety cabinet, the central machine of every compounding room. The cabinet draws room air through a high-efficiency particulate air filter, pushes it downward over the work surface, and exhausts it after filtering, so particles and vapor are carried away from the operator. A class II cabinet protects both the drug and the person, with an air curtain at the front opening and a filter rated to remove 99.97 per cent of particles at 0.3 micrometer, and those ratings and the airflow alarm threshold must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A cytotoxic drug is a poison that can be absorbed through skin, inhaled as aerosol, or swallowed from a contaminated hand, and the cabinet must be certified at installation and at fixed intervals.

Closed system transfer devices and needleless connectors are the second line of defense, because the drug escapes most easily when a vial is vented or a syringe is disconnected. A closed system transfer device seals the vial and the syringe together so pressure equalizes without releasing aerosol, while a needleless connector lets the nurse attach and detach tubing with a simple twist. The common operator error is to prime the tubing outside the cabinet, which sprays a fine mist that nobody sees and everybody inhales, or to fill a syringe past its capacity and let the plunger separate. Another error is to reuse a connector meant for one drug and one patient, and every assembly and priming step must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Personal protective equipment for hazardous drugs is chosen for permeation rather than comfort, and it is worn in a fixed order that leaves no gap. Chemotherapy gloves are tested against a recognized permeation standard, and two pairs are worn, with the outer pair changed every 30 minutes and after every spill, an interval that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A long-sleeved gown with closed cuffs, a face shield, and a respirator for spill work complete the set, and gloves must never touch a door handle, a telephone, or a keyboard. The common operator error is to answer the phone in the same gloves, and the second is to remove the gown before the gloves.

Every compounding room needs a spill kit, and every treatment area needs an extravasation kit, and both must be complete, sealed, and within reach. The spill kit holds absorbent pads, a spill pillow, a detergent, a chemical inactivator, gloves, a gown, a respirator, eye protection, and waste bags, while the extravasation kit holds the antidotes and compresses appropriate to the drugs in use. When a drug leaks from a vein into surrounding tissue, the infusion is stopped at once, the tubing is not flushed, the limb is elevated, and the prescribed compress is applied, with every drug-specific measure confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The error that causes the most harm is to ignore a patient's complaint of burning at the cannula site, because extravasation is a clinical diagnosis made on symptoms.

Cytotoxic waste is separated at the point of generation, and its containers announce what they hold. A rigid sharps container receives needles, ampoules, and broken vials, while a leakproof bin with a lid receives gloves, gowns, tubing, and absorbent material. All of it is labeled as cytotoxic, stored away from food and clean supplies, and sent for high-temperature incineration, never to a landfill, and trace waste such as outer packaging belongs in the same stream. The common operator error is to throw a chemotherapy glove into the ordinary bin because it looks clean, and transport and storage time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Infusion pumps used for hazardous drugs are chosen and programmed with the same care as the drug. A peristaltic pump moves fluid by squeezing tubing, so the drug never touches the mechanism, a cassette pump uses a disposable chamber, and an elastomeric pump empties by its own recoil and needs no electricity. A syringe driver is the usual choice for a continuous subcutaneous infusion in palliative care, and every pump carries an occlusion alarm, an air-in-line alarm, and often a dose error reduction library. Each threshold must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the common operator error is to override the library when a drug is not listed, which removes the only check on a decimal point in the wrong place.

The cabinet and the room need cleaning that is both physical and chemical. The work surface is wiped before and after every session with a detergent, then with 70 per cent alcohol or a chlorine solution, and the spill tray and floor are cleaned on a fixed schedule, with those concentrations and contact times confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The high-efficiency particulate air filter is checked for pressure drop, the prefilter is changed when it loads, and the cabinet is recertified yearly and after any repair. The common operator error is to switch the cabinet off at the end of the day, and alarms that warn of low airflow, high filter pressure, or a blocked exhaust must stop the work until the cause is found.

Drug storage is part of the equipment chain, because a cytotoxic drug kept too warm or too long on the shelf may be less potent or more toxic. Many agents are kept between 2 and 8 degrees Celsius, and that range, with the acceptable excursion time, must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Some drugs are light sensitive and need their original carton, some must never be frozen, and a refrigerator with a temperature record and a calibrated thermometer is therefore a piece of safety equipment. The common operator error is to store food in the drug refrigerator, and an inventory that records batch and expiry allows a recall to be answered in minutes.

The field version of chemotherapy preparation accepts smaller volumes, fewer patients, and much less protection. Where there is no biosafety cabinet, some units set aside a dedicated room with a window, an exhaust fan, and a washable surface, and work over an absorbent pad while wearing double gloves and a gown. Oral chemotherapy moves the hazard to the patient's home, where the caregiver is taught to wear gloves, avoid crushing tablets, and treat vomit, urine, and stool as contaminated material for several days. Every dilution and storage time must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the honest limit is that a field clinic can deliver safe oral treatment and simple infusions but cannot compound complex regimens without exposing its staff.

Before any treatment, the operator runs through a short list that has prevented more accidents than any alarm, beginning with two identifiers checked against the wristband and stated by the patient. The plan number, treatment site, field shape, energy, and dose are compared with the approved plan, and the mask is examined for fit while the imaging is matched. The common error in radiotherapy is a wrong site or wrong shift treatment, and the common error in chemotherapy is a wrong dose. A formal pause before the start, in which the team stops, is the cheapest safety device in the department.

The characteristic failures follow the pattern of everything else in the hospital. A multileaf collimator can lose a leaf to a motor or sensor fault, and the machine then refuses to treat that field, while a water leak or a chiller failure shuts the beam down in the middle of a treatment. A network failure can prevent the plan from reaching the machine, and a power failure stops everything unless an uninterruptible power supply carries the console and record system through the moment needed to close the file. In the compounding room, a cabinet airflow alarm, a failing refrigerator, or an occluded pump are everyday troubles, and each alarm threshold must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A linear accelerator is never a do-it-yourself repair, so a written downtime plan is as important as any spare part.

Staff who work here carry a dose record and a set of habits. A personal dosimeter is worn at chest level, and a ring badge is worn on the hand that handles sources or drugs, read on a fixed schedule. International guidance sets an annual limit for radiation workers of 20 millisieverts averaged over 5 years, with no more than 50 millisieverts in any single year, and those limits, which must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, are ceilings rather than targets. A pregnant worker must declare the pregnancy so her workload and dose can be managed, and the World Health Organization's guidance on occupational radiation protection is a useful reference for a department writing its own rules. A dosimeter left in a drawer reads a comforting zero and protects nobody.

To summarize, oncology and radiotherapy equipment divides into two chains that meet at the patient. The radiotherapy chain begins with a computed tomography simulation and a plan, passes through immobilization and imaging, and ends in a linear accelerator or an afterloader whose beam or source must be aimed with mechanical precision, and its safety is architecture, interlocks, dosimetry, and daily verification by a medical physicist. The chemotherapy chain begins in a biosafety cabinet with closed transfer devices and personal protective equipment, ends in a pump that delivers a measured dose, and its safety is airflow, gloves, spill kits, and a waste stream that never mixes with ordinary rubbish. Both chains depend on consumables that are cheap and checks that are tedious, and both fail when a busy person decides that a step can be skipped. In the field, radiotherapy does not travel at all, so what remains is the referral pathway, the analgesic, the steroid, the stent, and the pump. A clinic that knows exactly what it cannot do, and refers early and honestly, treats its patients better than one that pretends to have a machine it does not.
