# Chapter 21 — Resuscitation Equipment

Resuscitation equipment is not one machine but a system of parts that must work together within minutes, and its physical principle is that two failures kill at the same time. The first is the failure of ventilation, because without a moving column of gas the alveoli receive no fresh oxygen and the blood leaving the lungs carries nothing usable. The second is the failure of circulation, because a stopped heart delivers no oxygen to the brain even when the lungs are full. Chest compressions squeeze the heart between the sternum and the spine and raise the pressure inside the chest so that blood is pushed out and flows back as the chest recoils. Every device here restores one of those two functions or keeps the brain alive while both are restored, and the arrangement of the equipment matters as much as the equipment itself. A resuscitation is a race against the metabolism of the brain, which tolerates only a short interruption of flow.

The crash cart turns that collection of parts into something a team can use in the dark. It is a cabinet on four casters, usually waist high to chest high, with a flat lipped top, three to five drawers that grow deeper toward the bottom, and a handle for pushing. Two casters swivel and two are fixed, with brakes, because the cart must turn a corner and cross a threshold without being lifted. The top carries the defibrillator on a bracket, a monitor, a small oxygen cylinder in its own holder, and a suction unit with a canister. The shallow upper drawers hold airway items and drugs, while the deep lower drawers hold fluids, giving sets, suction catheters, and spare tubing. The exact layout of every drawer must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before the cart is placed in service.

The order of the drawers is a deliberate use of spatial memory, because under stress the mind loses the ability to search systematically. A team that has opened the same cart in the same order many times reaches for a laryngoscope, a syringe, or a fluid bag along a path the hand already knows, without the eyes leaving the patient. Every cart in an institution should therefore be laid out identically, and a cart borrowed from another department can slow a team even when nothing is missing. The numbered single-use seal through the hasp completes that discipline, because it cannot be reopened without leaving evidence of the fact. The commonest error in the whole system is the hurried clinician who takes one item during an arrest and pushes the drawer shut with the same seal, leaving a gap nobody discovers until the next patient needs it. The seal number, the lock, and the inspection interval must be confirmed against the local protocol before the cart enters service.

The defibrillator sits on the top surface because it is the only device on the cart that can restart a heart, and its physical principle is the capacitor. Two conducting plates separated by an insulator store charge and release it across the chest in a few thousandths of a second, and that stored energy is measured in joules. A typical adult biphasic shock delivers 120 to 200 joules, an energy level that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The pads are adhesive electrodes that both sense the rhythm and carry the shock, so their gel must be moist and their expiry date current, because a dried pad burns the skin and conducts poorly. The characteristic sound is a rising charge tone followed by a discharge, while a steady low-battery tone warns that the device may not deliver its full stored energy. A shock advisory that keeps repeating means the algorithm still detects a treatable rhythm.

The oxygen supply on the cart is a small cylinder with a regulator and a flowmeter, and its physics is the release of a compressed gas. The cylinder holds gas at many times atmospheric pressure, the regulator reduces that to a safe working level, and the flowmeter, a tapered tube with a small ball, shows the rate in liters per minute. A common resuscitation flow is 10 to 15 liters per minute through a reservoir mask, a setting that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The hazard is a cylinder that only reads full because the gauge was never checked, and the commonest error is to open the valve part way, which starves the flowmeter and lets the reservoir bag collapse. A low-pressure whistle or a gauge that falls during use is the characteristic alarm, and the correct response is to switch to the spare cylinder rather than hunt for a leak. Every cylinder should be exchanged at the end of a call.

Suction keeps the airway clear of blood, vomit, and secretions, and it works by a pressure difference between the patient's airway and a collection jar. A pump or wall vacuum line removes air from the jar, so fluid is drawn through the catheter because the pressure inside the tubing is lower than the pressure in the mouth. The vacuum is set on a regulator, usually between 80 and 120 millimeters of mercury for an adult, a value that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A manual suction unit replaces the pump with a hand-operated piston or bellows and is the fallback when there is no vacuum line and no electricity. The characteristic alarm is the float valve of a full canister, which rises and blocks the suction path with a sudden loss of vacuum, and the commonest error is to keep suctioning through a full jar. The catheter must be measured against the patient before insertion, because one driven too deep can injure the airway or provoke a slow heart.

The bag-valve-mask is the most important single object on the cart, and it works by elastic recoil rather than by any motor. Squeezing the self-inflating bag pushes its volume through a one-way valve into the mask, and when the hand releases the bag springs back and draws the next breath through an inlet valve while the patient valve closes so exhaled gas leaves by a separate port. A reservoir bag or an oxygen inlet raises the delivered concentration toward 100 per cent when the flow is high enough, a target that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The mask must seal over the bridge of the nose and the cleft of the chin, and the usual technique is a two-hand grip with a jaw thrust while an assistant squeezes the bag. The hazard is gastric insufflation, in which gas forced too fast enters the esophagus, distends the stomach, and pushes the diaphragm up so the lungs cannot fill. The characteristic failure is silent, because a leaking mask raises no alarm, only poor chest rise and a falling oxygen saturation.

The oral and nasal airways are simple plastic tubes that hold the tongue away from the back of the throat, and their principle is geometry. In an unconscious patient the tongue falls backward because the muscles that hold it forward have lost their tone, and a curved tube placed over the tongue follows the arc of the mouth and pharynx. An oral airway is measured from the corner of the mouth to the angle of the jaw, and sizes are marked in millimeters or by color, values that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. A nasal airway is a soft beveled tube passed along the floor of the nose, and it is tolerated by patients who still have a gag reflex. The commonest error is an airway so long that it pushes the epiglottis down over the larynx instead of lifting it, and the second is placing an oral airway in a patient awake enough to vomit. The supraglottic airway forms a low-pressure seal above the larynx with a soft cuff that inflates behind the tongue, and its sizes are chosen by body weight, values that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A tracheal tube is the definitive airway because it seals the trachea with a cuff and protects the lungs from stomach contents. The physics is a balloon against a tube wall, and the cuff is inflated until the space around the tube closes, typically to a pressure of 20 to 30 centimeters of water, a value that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Tube sizes are given as the internal diameter in millimeters, so a typical adult tube is 7 to 8 millimeters, figures that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The laryngoscope is a handle of batteries and a blade with a light, and a video laryngoscope puts a camera at the blade tip and shows the cords on a screen when the neck cannot be moved. Placement must be confirmed by watching the chest rise, listening over both lungs and over the stomach, and above all by detecting exhaled carbon dioxide with a colorimetric detector or a waveform capnograph. A dead battery in the laryngoscope handle is the classic maintenance failure discovered during a resuscitation.

The drug compartment carries a small pharmacy chosen for speed, and the first principle of that pharmacy is concentration. Drugs arrive as a glass ampoule that must be snapped open, a powder that must be dissolved, a rubber-capped vial from which a dose is drawn, or a prefilled syringe that needs no preparation. The commonest resuscitation drug is adrenaline, also called epinephrine, and it exists in at least two strengths, one of which is meant to be diluted before intravenous use, so the label must be read at arm's length and then again at the syringe. A common dilution is 1 milligram of adrenaline in 10 milliliters for a bolus and 1 milligram in 1000 milliliters for an infusion, and every concentration must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Other drugs usually present include amiodarone for a shockable rhythm, atropine for a slow heart, sodium bicarbonate, calcium, magnesium, dextrose, and naloxone for opioid overdose. The hazard is the wrong dilution, which is the same as the wrong drug.

The prefilled syringe removes preparation from the most hurried moment in medicine, and its physics is a plunger in a sealed barrel. Because the maker filled and sealed it, the volume and concentration are fixed and the operator needs only to connect the syringe to a port and push. The trade is cost and shelf life against seconds, so each hospital must decide which drugs are worth carrying prefilled, and the expiry dates on prefilled syringes are shorter than many people expect. The drug drawer is arranged by color coding, dividers, and labels that print the differing parts of similar names in larger letters, and the compartments follow the order in which the drugs are likely to be needed. The commonest error is not a wrong drug but a missing one, because a vial returned to the wrong compartment makes the next search slower. An opened ampoule, a punctured vial, or an unlabeled syringe is waste and must be removed and recorded rather than left for the next team.

Circulation equipment begins with access, and access is a problem of flow. A peripheral cannula lets fluid and drug enter the circulation, and its size is given in gauge, where a smaller number means a larger bore, so a 14 gauge cannula carries far more than a 22 gauge one. Flow through any tube depends far more on its radius than on its length, which is why a short wide cannula in a large vein outperforms a long narrow catheter at the same pressure. When no vein can be found, an intraosseous needle is driven into the marrow cavity of a long bone, usually the upper tibia or the humerus, and the cavity acts as a non-collapsible vein that a falling blood pressure cannot shut. Adult needles are commonly 15 or 25 millimeters long and 15 gauge, measurements that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The hazard is a needle that passes through the bone or an entry site that becomes infected.

Fluids are carried as bags of crystalloid solution, and getting them in quickly is again a problem of pressure. A bag hung high on a pole flows because gravity provides the pressure, but the rate is usually limited by the narrowest part of the giving set, so a pressure cuff around the bag or a hand pump on the tubing raises the driving pressure. A rapid infusion device warms and pumps fluid at high flow and is reserved for massive hemorrhage, while a simple blood warmer with a coil is the common substitute. The commonest error is to expect a narrow cannula to deliver a rapid bolus, which it cannot, and the second is to forget that an unspiked bag may contain a drug someone added. Every pressure, rate, and temperature used must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the same rule applies to the volume of any bolus given to a child.

The compression feedback device measures what the hands are actually doing, and it works with a small accelerometer and a force sensor inside a pad or a puck. The accelerometer detects the depth and rate of each compression, and the force sensor detects whether the chest is fully released, because a hand resting on the chest during recoil stops the heart from refilling. The usual targets are a depth of 5 to 6 centimeters and a rate of 100 to 120 compressions per minute with full recoil, values that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The characteristic alarm is a spoken prompt or a changing tone telling the operator to push harder, to push faster, or to release fully, and a device that never complains is often one placed under the patient out of position. The commonest operator error is to silence the tone as an annoyance, when it is the only objective witness of compression quality in the room. The device is an aid and not a substitute for rotation, because the compressor tires and quality falls.

The mechanical compression machine replaces the tired human hand with a motor and comes in two broad forms. The piston device presses a pad down on the sternum at a set depth and rate, while the load-distributing band wraps around the chest and tightens cyclically, and both depend on a battery and on strapping that holds the patient in one position. Its principle is repetition without fatigue, because the machine never tires and never leans on the chest, and it frees one member of the team for other tasks. The hazards are a piston set too low and pressing on the abdomen, a band that slips up onto the neck or down onto the belly, and a battery that empties during a long resuscitation. The characteristic alarms are a low-battery tone and a device fault tone, and the thresholds for both must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The commonest operator error is to apply the device without pausing compressions for longer than a few seconds.

The checklists, the seal, and the expiry dates make a cart trustworthy, and they work on the principle that a closed drawer cannot be seen. The daily and shift check follows a fixed route: the seal number is read against the log, the lock is tested, the defibrillator runs its self-test, the pads and their expiry dates are inspected, the oxygen pressure is recorded, the suction is run, the laryngoscope light is switched on, the bag is squeezed to test its valves, and the drugs are counted. Expiry dates are managed by rotation on a first in and first out rule, because a deep drawer can hide expired ampoules beneath fresh ones and the only defense is to empty each compartment and rebuild it in order. The commonest error is to check the front row and the outward-facing labels, since the items that fail are the ones that cannot be seen. A cart without a completed log is treated as unchecked, and every pressure and expiry interval used in the check must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The pediatric cart differs from the adult cart in scale and in logic, because a child is not a small adult. Every device is duplicated in smaller sizes, so there are masks from newborn to adolescent, airways in a range of lengths, tracheal tubes from 2.5 millimeters upward, smaller self-inflating bags of about 250 milliliters, and smaller suction catheters, all of which must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The defibrillator uses pediatric pads with a smaller contact area, and the energy selected is usually 2 joules per kilogram for the first shock, a figure that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. Drugs are almost always diluted because the dose is calculated per kilogram, and the cart usually carries a length-based tape that estimates weight from height and prints each dose in a colored band. The characteristic hazard is the decimal point, because a misplaced point can multiply a dose tenfold. A child also consumes the small sizes faster than an adult consumes the large ones.

The failures that matter most are discovered during a resuscitation, and they are almost always the same handful. A laryngoscope whose battery was left switched on in the drawer is dead when it is needed, an oxygen cylinder used for transport was never exchanged, pads whose gel has dried adhere poorly and burn the skin, a suction canister left full has no vacuum at all, and a compression device has a battery that no longer holds charge. The characteristic alarms each mean something specific: the defibrillator gives a rising charge tone, a discharge, and a steady low-battery tone, the compression device signals a low battery or a fault, the suction unit blocks at the canister float, the oxygen system falls silent at the gauge or whistles, and the monitor repeats a lead-off beep. Every one of those thresholds must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The commonest error is to silence the alarm without naming its cause, which turns a warning into silence, and the correct habit is to say the alarm aloud and assign someone to fix it. A cart can also be too heavy to move or blocked by a bed.

Maintenance is a scheduled program with a biomedical technician, and it exists because a device that is never used still ages. The defibrillator is tested at intervals for delivered energy, and the interval and method must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, because a capacitor that has drifted may deliver far less than its display promises. Batteries are the shortest-lived part of almost everything on the cart and are replaced on a calendar rather than on failure, with the replacement date written on the casing. Mechanical compression devices need their straps and contacts inspected, feedback devices need their calibration verified against a known reference, suction regulators need their gauges checked, and the cart itself needs its wheels and latch maintained. Cleaning follows the same division as every department, so laryngoscope blades are cleaned of all visible soil and then disinfected or sterilized by a validated method, often by steam at 134 degrees Celsius for 4 minutes or at 121 degrees Celsius for 30 minutes. Those cycles, and every disinfectant contact time used on the cart, must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The consumables of the crash cart are ordinary and inexpensive, and their management is what keeps the expensive machines useful. The list includes gloves and other personal protective equipment, syringes and needles, alcohol swabs, gauze, tape, a tourniquet, intravenous cannulae, intraosseous needles, fluid bags and giving sets, airways, tracheal tubes, suction catheters, defibrillator pads, batteries, a sharps container, biohazard bags, and the forms for recording the event. Infection is a real hazard of resuscitation because the work is fast, close, and often bloody, so gloves, gown, mask, and eye protection go on before contact even though the delay is genuine. The commonest injury is a needle left on a tray or recapped by hand, which is why a sharps container must sit within arm's reach of the drug drawer and must never be overfilled. Documentation turns the event into information that can be reviewed, and a designated recorder writes the times of the arrest, of each drug, of each shock and its energy in joules, and of the return of circulation as they happen. The record is entered into the electronic health record afterwards, the cart is restocked and resealed, and the new seal number is written in the log on the same shift.

In a mobile or frontier clinic the resuscitation kit is a soft bag that one person can carry, and its principle is that everything must work without a wall, a pipe, or a mains outlet. The bag is a rucksack or shoulder satchel with padded compartments and a reflective strip, and it holds a self-inflating bag with masks in several sizes, a set of oral airways, a pocket mask, a manual suction pump, a small oxygen cylinder with a regulator, a portable automated external defibrillator, intraosseous needles, a tourniquet, gauze and dressings, a few essential drugs, and a laminated checklist. The weight is the central design problem, because a bag that is too heavy is left in the vehicle, so the field kit is built around what the first responder can carry for a kilometer. Every pressure, flow, and energy setting in the bag must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use. The bag is repacked in daylight after every call, against the written list, because a soft bag has no drawers to keep an item in its place.

The field variant gives up a great deal, and an honest list of the losses is more useful than optimism. There is no mechanical compression machine, no multiparameter monitor, no rapid infusion device, no blood bank, no cold chain for temperature-sensitive drugs, no piped suction, no wall oxygen, and usually no waveform capnography to confirm a tracheal tube. What remains is the hand, the bag, the defibrillator, and a small set of drugs, and the discipline that replaces the missing hardware is rotation of the compressor. Because no device measures the quality of compressions, the team must change the person compressing every 2 minutes, an interval that must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and the leader must count aloud. A mechanical metronome or a timer with an audible tone substitutes for the feedback device, and a written tally of shocks and drugs substitutes for the monitor's memory. The field kit therefore demands more skill from the operator, not less, because there is no alarm to announce a failure.

Some equipment cannot be carried at all, and the field clinician must know that boundary before the patient arrives. Pacing, invasive monitoring, mechanical chest compression, blood products, surgical airways beyond a basic cricothyrotomy kit, and any device needing a stable mains supply are hospital resources, and a resuscitation that needs them must move toward the hospital rather than wait for the equipment to arrive. The decision to transport is itself a clinical decision made early, and the field kit must include the means to keep the patient alive during the movement, which means a bag, an oxygen cylinder, a defibrillator, and secured access. The commonest error in this setting is to spend time on a procedure the kit cannot support while the transport window closes. Every drug, energy level, and interval used during transport must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The field environment attacks the kit in ways a hospital cart never experiences, so the maintenance routine is built around weather rather than around a calendar. Heat softens the adhesive of defibrillator pads and shortens the life of batteries, dust clogs suction filters and valve seats, humidity corrodes contacts and grows mold inside tubing stored damp, and altitude changes the pressure inside a partly used cylinder. Batteries are charged from a solar panel where mains power is absent, and the state of charge is written on a tag at the end of each day, because a discharged defibrillator is an empty bag with a handle. Restocking happens after every call, in daylight, from the written list, and the seal or zip tie is replaced and its number logged. A field kit checked once a week is not checked, and the person who carries it must confirm that every setting and expiry date in it has been confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A resuscitation succeeds or fails as a team, and the equipment exists to give each role its own instrument. One person compresses, one manages the airway, one operates the defibrillator and the monitor, one gives drugs and manages access, one keeps time and records, and one leads by stepping back and calling the plan aloud. The cart is arranged so that each of these people reaches a different part of it without colliding, which is why the compressor stands at the chest and the drug person stands at the drawer behind the shoulder. Closed-loop communication is a piece of equipment as real as the laryngoscope: the leader names the task and the person, the person repeats it back, and the person reports when it is done. The commonest organizational error is the silent room in which everyone works hard and nobody knows what has been given. The final guarantee of readiness is practice rather than purchase, because a cart that has never been opened in a drill is a cabinet of unverified assumptions, and regular simulation with the actual cart exposes the jammed wheel and the drug nobody can find.

To summarize, resuscitation equipment is a system built around two physical failures, the failure to move gas and the failure to move blood, and every item on the cart answers one of them. The crash cart is a cabinet with a fixed geography, a numbered seal, and a checklist, and its drawers hold the airway, the drugs, the circulation supplies, and the bulk items in an order the hand can learn. The defibrillator stores energy in a capacitor, the bag inflates by elastic recoil, suction works by a pressure difference, and the intraosseous needle turns the marrow into a vein. Compression feedback devices and mechanical compression machines protect the quality of a task that tires the human hand, while the pediatric cart duplicates everything in smaller sizes with doses calculated by weight. The cart is kept trustworthy by the daily check, by date rotation, by scheduled maintenance, by disciplined cleaning and reprocessing, and by honest recording after every event. In the field the same system shrinks into a bag that one person can carry, it gives up the monitor, the pipeline, the blood, and the machine, and it demands instead a timer, a counted rotation, a charged battery, and the judgment to move early. Whatever the setting, the discipline is the same: know the order of the cart, check the seal, confirm every setting against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol, watch the chest and the clock, and restock before the next patient.
