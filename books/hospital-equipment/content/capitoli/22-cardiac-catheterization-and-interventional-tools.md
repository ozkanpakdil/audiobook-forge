# Chapter 22 — Cardiac Catheterization and Interventional Tools

The cardiac catheterization laboratory is a room built around one large machine. Its purpose is to see the inside of blood vessels and the chambers of the heart while a physician works within them. The physical principle is differential attenuation, because a beam of X-rays is absorbed in different fractions by structures of different density. Bone and metal absorb a great deal, soft tissue and blood absorb little, and an iodinated liquid absorbs far more than the blood it displaces, so an injected vessel appears dark against a pale field.

The images are made while they are needed rather than afterwards, which is what makes the procedure interventional. Fluoroscopy is X-ray imaging performed at a low dose for each frame and displayed continuously. The operator therefore sees a moving picture rather than a single exposure. The older detector was an image intensifier, a vacuum tube that turned the pattern into a small bright image for a camera to record. The modern detector is a flat panel of semiconductor material that converts X-rays directly into an electrical charge at every pixel.

A fixed angiography system is usually mounted as a C-arm, a curved gantry holding the tube on one side and the detector on the other. The gantry rotates to give many angles of view without moving anyone, and a biplane system has 2 arms roughly perpendicular to each other. The patient lies on a table that can be raised, tilted, and floated, so its top slides and a catheter can be advanced without moving the patient. The table has a stated weight limit and must be locked while the patient is positioned. That limit, the allowable tilt, and the floating function must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Several features of the system reduce the radiation received by the patient and the staff. Collimation narrows the beam to the region of interest, and added filtration removes the low-energy X-rays that would be absorbed in the skin. Pulsed fluoroscopy produces brief exposures instead of a continuous beam, and last image hold keeps the final frame on the screen with the beam off. The system displays fluoroscopy time, the dose area product, and often an estimated skin dose. The frame rate, the dose for each frame, and the warning level at which the system alerts the operator must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Radiation protection here is a set of habits and devices checked before every case. The operator and the assisting staff wear lead aprons covering the torso and the gonads, a thyroid shield covering the neck, and leaded glasses that protect the lens of the eye. A lead skirt hangs from the table edge to shield the legs, and distance remains the most powerful protection because dose falls steeply with the square of the distance from the beam. Each person wears a dosimeter badge, and the dose limits and the badge exchange interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Pregnancy changes the calculation for both the patient and the staff. A woman who may be pregnant should be identified before the procedure begins, and the decision to proceed is made with the fetal dose weighed explicitly. The beam should be collimated tightly and the pelvis shielded where the examination allows. A pregnant staff member should be reassigned away from the beam or wear a second dosimeter under the apron. The occupational dose limit for a declared pregnant worker must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use, and skipping this conversation is one of the more serious operator errors in the room.

The contrast injector makes angiography possible without a human hand on a syringe. To render a coronary artery opaque for a fraction of a second, contrast must be delivered at a high flow rate and at a pressure well above what a hand can generate. The injector holds a large syringe in a cradle, and a motorized piston drives the plunger at a programmed flow rate. The flow rate in milliliters per second and the pressure limit in pounds per square inch at which the motor stops must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The injector carries 2 characteristic hazards, and both have alarms. The first is air, because a bubble driven into a coronary artery can stop the heart, and most modern injectors detect it so the air alarm halts the injection. The second is extravasation, because contrast delivered outside the vessel dissects into the tissue and can cause severe injury that the machine cannot see. A pressure limit alarm during a coronary injection usually means a blocked or engaged catheter, and it must never be overridden. The air alarm, the pressure limit, and the over-temperature alarm of a contrast warmer must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The contrast medium is a consumable with its own storage rules and its own risks. It is an iodinated solution, and the iodine makes it opaque to X-rays, so its concentration determines how dark the vessel appears. The bottle is kept warm so it is less viscous and flows more easily through a narrow catheter, and a dedicated warmer holds it at a set temperature. Contrast is drawn up through a spike or a closed transfer device and purged of air before it reaches the injector. That set temperature, the over-temperature alarm, and the expiry check before every case must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Every case begins with access, and the vascular sheath holds that access open. A sheath is a short plastic tube with a hemostasis valve at its hub and a side arm for flushing. A dilator passes through it so the tapered tip can be advanced over a wire without tearing the vessel. Sheaths are sized in French, where one French equals one third of a millimeter, and the common coronary sizes are 5 French, 6 French, and 7 French. The size must match the largest device that will pass, and the flush pressure and the sheath size must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A sheath is removed at the end of the case, when bleeding is most likely. Manual compression is held over the puncture point until hemostasis is achieved, and then a pressure dressing is applied. A closure device may be used instead, either a suture-based or a collagen-based plug, and each has its own failure modes. A sheath left in place for a long procedure can kink and will then accept neither a wire nor a balloon. The compression time, the closure device instructions, and the dressing schedule must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The guidewire is the device that leads everything else. It has a central core of stainless steel or nickel titanium alloy that carries the push, a coiled or braided wrap that gives flexibility, and a shaped tip the operator bends before insertion. The diameter is usually 0.014 inch for coronary work and 0.035 inch for larger vessels, and the length is chosen so a device can be advanced over it while a length still protrudes. Guidewires are single-use devices, and a resterilized wire loses its coating, its tip shape, and its fatigue life. A fractured wire can leave a fragment in the coronary circulation that only a snare or an operation can retrieve. The diameter, the length, the tip shape, and the reuse policy must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Diagnostic and guiding catheters are distinguished almost entirely by their curve. A diagnostic catheter is shaped so its tip can be seated briefly in a coronary opening. A guiding catheter is stiffer and larger in bore so it can sit coaxially and support balloons and stents. Common shapes include a curve for the left coronary artery, a curve for the right, and a pigtail whose curl keeps the tip off the vessel wall. The catheter is advanced over a wire rather than pushed blindly, and when it is seated the pressure trace is watched for damping and for the waveform change that signals the tip is against the wall. Damping means the catheter is blocked against the vessel, so the catheter curve, the size, and the exchange technique must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The balloon is the tool that opens a narrowing, and its behavior is governed by compliance. A compliant balloon stretches as pressure rises, so its diameter grows with every increment and it can dilate a range of sizes. A non-compliant balloon holds its diameter nearly constant, so the final size can be known and not exceeded. Each balloon has a nominal pressure at which it reaches its stated diameter and a rated burst pressure above which it may rupture. A rupture in a coronary artery releases contrast and debris and can dissect the vessel, so the nominal pressure, the rated burst pressure, and the inflated diameter must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The inflation device is a syringe with a screw thread and a pressure gauge. The screw thread gives a large mechanical advantage, so a small turn of the handle produces a large change in pressure. The device is filled with a mixture of contrast and saline, usually 1 part contrast to 2 parts saline, so the balloon is visible while it is inflated. A common operator error is to overfill the device, leaving no room to draw negative pressure, so the balloon cannot be deflated quickly. The mixture ratio, the gauge range, and the maximum pressure must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A stent is a metal scaffold mounted on a balloon or a delivery catheter and left in the vessel to hold it open. The balloon-expandable stent is crimped onto a balloon and expanded by inflating it, and the self-expanding stent is compressed inside a sheath and springs open when withdrawn. A drug-eluting stent carries a medication in a polymer coating that reduces tissue regrowth, and that coating can be damaged by rough passage or a dry wipe. A stent can dislodge during passage through a tight curve, so the operator confirms under fluoroscopy that it lies across the lesion with adequate margins before inflation. After deployment the balloon is withdrawn and inspected to confirm the stent is no longer on it, and the stent diameter, the deployment pressure, and the inventory check must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Hemodynamic monitoring rests on one small component, the pressure transducer. The transducer is a strain gauge whose electrical resistance changes when fluid pressure stretches it, and it is connected to the patient through a column of fluid. Because it measures pressure relative to the air on its other side, the system must be referenced to a fixed external point. That point is the phlebostatic axis on the chest wall, a level that approximates the right atrium, and the transducer is placed there and zeroed to the atmosphere. The square wave test flushes the system briefly and watches the trace, because a properly damped system shows a sharp rise and 1 or 2 small oscillations. A trace that rises and falls slowly is overdamped, usually because of a bubble, a clot, or a kink, and blood left to stagnate clots and can be flushed into the patient. The reference level, the zeroing technique, the square wave criteria, and the tubing change interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

With a reliable pressure signal, the laboratory measures the pressures and flows that guide the decision. A catheter passed into the right side of the heart gives right atrial, right ventricular, pulmonary artery, and pulmonary capillary wedge pressures. The shapes of those waveforms reveal narrowing of valves, shunts between chambers, and the filling of the heart. Cardiac output is often measured by thermodilution, where cool saline is injected and a downstream thermistor records the temperature change. The injection volume, the measurement technique, and the reference values must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Fractional flow reserve measures the physiological significance of a narrowing, because a picture of a narrowing does not prove that it limits flow. A very thin pressure wire is advanced across the lesion, and the pressure beyond it is compared with the pressure in the aorta. The measurement is taken during maximal hyperemia, a state produced by a drug such as adenosine given intravenously or into the artery. The 2 errors that ruin the measurement are failing to achieve full hyperemia and failing to notice that the wire has drifted from its calibration, so the wire is rechecked against the guiding catheter after every reading. The drug dose, the hyperemia protocol, and the decision threshold must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Intravascular imaging answers questions that angiography cannot, because angiography shows only the silhouette of the lumen. Intravascular ultrasound uses a tiny transducer that emits high-frequency sound and builds a cross-sectional image from the echoes. It reveals the true vessel size, the plaque, and the position of a stent strut against the wall. Optical coherence tomography uses near-infrared light and measures the reflection from the tissue, giving finer resolution over a shorter depth. Both need the vessel cleared of blood during the pullback, and the pullback speed, the flush volume, and the interpretation criteria must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Radiation dose management is a discipline of its own, and it belongs to the operator rather than the machine. The system reports the dose area product, a figure combining beam intensity with the area covered, and it may estimate the peak skin dose where the beam entered. A long and difficult case can produce a skin dose high enough to cause a delayed burn, so the patient should be told to watch the site. Dose rises with magnification, steep angles, a large patient, and long fluoroscopy time, so the operator uses the lowest frame rate that answers the question and keeps the detector close. The dose alert level, the skin dose threshold, and the follow-up arrangements must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The consumables for a case must be gathered and checked before the patient is on the table. The list includes the access needle and sheath, guidewires in at least 2 shapes, catheters in the expected curves, balloons in a range of diameters, and stents in the sizes the anatomy suggests. It includes the manifold and stopcocks, the pressure tubing and transducer, the inflation device, the contrast, the saline, the heparin, the drapes, the gloves, and the lead protection. It also includes the emergency equipment, which is the part most often taken for granted. The full list, the quantities, and the expiry dates must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The emergency equipment is what converts a controlled procedure into a survivable one. A defibrillator with pacing capability stands in the room because a coronary injection can provoke a rhythm that stops the circulation. A crash cart with airway equipment, suction, oxygen, and resuscitation drugs stands where the operator can reach it without leaving the table. A temporary pacing wire and generator are available, and vasopressor and antiarrhythmic drugs are drawn up and checked by 2 people while blood products and a rapid infuser are on call for a perforation. The defibrillator charge, the drug doses, and the cart seal must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Anticoagulation during the case is managed by drug and by measurement. Heparin is given to prevent thrombus from forming on catheters, wires, and stents, and its effect is followed with the activated clotting time, a bedside test on a small blood sample. The test device has its own cartridges, its own quality control, and its own expiry, and a device that has not been warmed will give a falsely short time and lead to underdosing. The measured time is compared with a target range that depends on the procedure, and because a very long time also increases bleeding the operator balances the 2 risks. The timing of the sample, the target range, and the reversal plan must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Certain operator errors recur in catheterization laboratories, and naming them is the cheapest prevention. Failing to zero and level the transducer is the most common cause of a wrong pressure. Leaving air in an injector syringe or in a manifold filled in a hurry carries the most immediate danger. Advancing a balloon while the guidewire is being withdrawn can strip a device or lose a wire. Overriding a pressure limit alarm instead of investigating it converts a warning into a perforation, and the alarm limits, the zeroing routine, and the handling rules must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The alarms in the room speak in different voices, and each one means something specific. The injector gives an over-pressure alarm when it cannot deliver the programmed flow, which usually means a blocked catheter, a closed stopcock, or a kink, and it also gives an air alarm and a low-volume alarm. The contrast warmer gives an over-temperature alarm, the angiography system gives a fluoroscopy-time alert and a dose alert, and the table gives a collision alarm when its movement is obstructed. The monitor alarms on the electrocardiogram, the blood pressure, and the oxygen saturation. The alarm limits, the volume settings, and the response to each alarm must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Preventive maintenance is scheduled, documented, and shared between the biomedical engineering department and the manufacturer's service organization. The X-ray tube is warmed through a specified sequence, the detector is calibrated so its dark field and gain references are current, and the table brakes, float mechanism, and weight limit are inspected. The injector is verified against a reference gauge, the contrast warmer is checked against a reference thermometer, and the pressure transducer modules are calibrated. The lead aprons are inspected for cracks and screened under fluoroscopy, because an apron with a hidden crack looks intact. A failing flat panel detector produces columns of dead pixels that no setting change will correct, and a stalling injector motor produces a truncated injection. The maintenance intervals, the calibration tolerances, and the tube warm-up routine must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Cleaning between cases is a fixed sequence, not an optional courtesy to the next patient. The table, the arm boards, the control panel, and the cables are wiped with a compatible disinfectant, and blood spills are cleaned immediately with an agent effective against bloodborne viruses. Reusable transducer modules, cables, and cuffs are cleaned and dried, while sheaths, wires, catheters, balloons, stents, and tubing are single-use and go into the regulated waste stream. Reusable devices go to the central sterile services department to be cleaned, inspected, packaged, sterilized, and returned with a record. The disinfectant contact time, the sterilization cycle, and the waste segregation rules must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Documentation is part of the equipment discipline, because an intervention that is not recorded cannot be audited. The case log records the access site, the catheters and wires used, the balloons and stents with their sizes and lot numbers, the contrast volume, the fluoroscopy time, and the dose area product. The implant card for each stent is given to the patient, and the images are stored in the picture archiving and communication system. The dose record is filed so a late skin injury can be traced, and the record fields, the retention period, and the reporting format must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The patient is monitored throughout the case, and the monitor is never a substitute for looking at the patient. The electrocardiogram is displayed continuously, and the operator watches for the waveform change that signals ischemia. The blood pressure is displayed from an arterial line or a cuff, and the oxygen saturation and respiratory rate are watched during sedation. Sedation is titrated to a level at which the patient is comfortable and responsive, because an unresponsive patient cannot report chest pain. The monitor alarm limits, the sedation targets, and the observation interval must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Now the same subject must be described from the other side, and the honest statement is that a catheterization laboratory does not travel. The fixed angiography system weighs as much as a small truck and needs a shielded room with a floor that carries its load. It demands a high-voltage supply, cooling, and a service contract that keeps a specialist within reach. The equipment cannot be loaded into a vehicle, and the patient cannot be brought to it without a road and a level of stability that the journey itself may destroy. The room's shielding, its power quality, and its environmental control must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

A district hospital that attempts coronary intervention without that package is taking a large and poorly measured risk. The honest alternative is to build the best possible diagnostic and referral service instead. If a portable C-arm is available, it may be used for peripheral angiography, line placement, and procedures that do not demand coronary resolution. A single-plane portable unit paired with hand injection, a limited stock of catheters, and manual compression can answer a few questions about the vessels of the leg. It cannot safely place a coronary stent, because that demands a surgical backup, many device sizes, and an operator who performs the procedure often enough to stay competent. The portable unit's dose output, image quality, and table limits must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The clinical consequence is that the referral decision must be made early. The equipment that matters in a frontier clinic is the equipment of recognition and safe transport. The clinic needs a reliable electrocardiogram machine, a monitor, a defibrillator with functioning pads and a charged battery, oxygen, and intravenous access supplies. It needs a blood pressure cuff, a pulse oximeter, and a thermometer that work, and a way to send a tracing to the referral center before the patient leaves. The equipment needed for this pathway, the drugs carried, and the transport arrangements must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The mobile or frontier clinic gives up the interventional capability and demands something else in its place. What it gives up includes fractional flow measurement, intravascular imaging, the hemodynamic measurements that require a right heart catheter, and the ability to reopen a blocked coronary artery on site. What it demands is staff trained to read an electrocardiogram, a defibrillator checked every day, and a documented referral agreement with a center that can accept the patient. It also demands a realistic inventory, because a clinic that cannot maintain a defibrillator should not pretend to own one. The monitoring, the drug storage, and the referral criteria must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

The laboratory also illustrates a general rule about heavy medical equipment, namely that its value depends on infrastructure invisible in the procedure. The angiography system needs electrical power stable enough for its generator and an uninterruptible power supply to carry it through a brief interruption. It needs heating, ventilation, and air conditioning that holds the room within a narrow temperature and humidity range, because the detector drifts when the room does. It needs a water supply for the scrub area, a shielded room whose walls contain the scatter, a floor that carries the gantry, a store that keeps consumables in date, and a central sterile services department. The power quality, the room conditions, and the shielding survey must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

Quality assurance is a set of measurements taken even when nothing appears wrong. The image quality is checked with a phantom at a defined interval, and the dose output is measured at the same time. Complications are recorded and reviewed, including access site bleeding, contrast reactions, contrast-induced kidney injury, and device failure. The maintenance records are reviewed so a device that fails repeatedly is replaced rather than repeatedly repaired. The listener who has followed this chapter should be able to read the room, seeing the C-arm as the eye, the injector as the hand, and the aprons and badges as the protection that makes the work survivable. The audit interval, the acceptance criteria, and the review process must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local protocol before use.

To summarize, the cardiac catheterization laboratory is a room organized around a fixed angiography system, and every other device in it exists to move a catheter and watch what happens while it does. The physical basis is differential absorption of X-rays, made visible by an iodinated contrast medium and captured by a detector that turns the pattern into a moving image. The tools are sheaths that hold access, wires that lead, catheters that select a vessel by their curve, balloons that dilate, stents that scaffold, and inflation devices that control the pressure. Pressure transducers, leveled at the phlebostatic axis and zeroed to the atmosphere, convert the pulse into a number and a waveform, while fractional flow measurement and intravascular imaging answer questions that a picture cannot. Radiation protection, consumable discipline, scheduled maintenance, and understood alarms make the room safe rather than merely powerful. In a frontier clinic none of this travels, so the equipment that matters is the equipment of recognition, stabilization, and an early and well documented referral.
