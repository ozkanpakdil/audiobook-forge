# Hospital Equipment — Section Briefs

One brief per section. The chapter must cover every point named here, in depth, and
must follow the contract in PLAN.md. Length and format rules live in PLAN.md, not
here.

---

## Chapter 1 — How a Hospital Is Organized

The departments as an equipment map: the front door and emergency department, the
wards, the operating theatre, the intensive care unit, the laboratory, the imaging
suite, the pharmacy and stores, the sterilization department, the laundry, the
kitchen, the morgue. The flow of patients through them, and the parallel flow of
supplies in and waste out, with the equipment that lives at each point. Who owns a
machine, who may move it, who repairs it. The equipment reasons a patient waits: the
machine in use, the one that is broken, the consumable that ran out. The mobile or
frontier clinic as a compressed hospital holding a fraction of these departments in
two rooms, and what that compression costs.

## Chapter 2 — Classification of Medical Devices

The risk classes of medical devices and what each class means in practice, from the
lowest risk to the implantable and life-supporting. The regulatory frameworks that
name them: the European conformity marking system, the American clearance and
approval pathways, and the guidance of the World Health Organization. In vitro
diagnostic devices, sterile devices, reusable against single-use devices, capital
equipment against consumables, and active against non-active devices. How the class
determines who may sell it, who may install it, who may service it, how it must be
tracked, and what the buyer can demand. The practical consequence for a clinic that
receives a device without its paperwork.

## Chapter 3 — Safety Principles

Electrical safety explained physically: leakage current, protective earth,
equipotential bonding, isolated power, and why a wet floor changes everything.
Applied-part classifications and defibrillation-proof types. Gas safety, including
cylinders, connections, and the fire risk of oxygen. Alarms as a safety system, alarm
fatigue, and the design of alarm limits. Human factors: the device that invites the
wrong action, labeling, and the read-back habit. Incident reporting and the culture
that makes it work. Radiation protection in principle. Falls, entrapment, burns, and
the patient-handling injuries that hurt staff. Safety as a discipline, not a poster.

## Chapter 4 — The Lifecycle of Equipment

The whole life of a device from the recognition of a need to the day it leaves the
building. Needs assessment and the question of what the clinic can actually sustain.
Procurement, specification, tender, and the total cost of ownership including
consumables and service. Acceptance testing on arrival and the paperwork that proves
it. Inventory, asset tagging, and the equipment register. Installation and
commissioning, training, and the handover to users. Calibration and preventive
maintenance. Repair against replacement, and the moment a device becomes unsafe to
keep. Decommissioning, cannibalization, and disposal. Donation chains and their
habitual failures. The role of the biomedical engineering department, and what a
clinic does without one.

## Chapter 5 — Hospital Beds and Patient Handling

Manual, electric, and intensive care beds, their frames, cranks, motors, side rails,
brakes, and the positions they achieve including backrest, leg section, and the
Trendelenburg and reverse Trendelenburg positions. Mattresses, pressure-relieving
surfaces, alternating pressure, and low-air-loss systems, and how each is powered and
maintained. Stretchers and trolleys, wheelchairs, and examination couches. Hoists and
ceiling lifts, transfer boards, sliding sheets, and the mechanics of moving a patient
without injuring the mover. Weight limits and the labeling that states them. The field
variants: the canvas stretcher, the locally welded frame, the rope bed, and the
ground, with the pressure injury and dignity consequences of each.

## Chapter 6 — Vital Sign Monitoring

The instruments of the four vital signs, one at a time. Thermometers of every kind,
including glass mercury, digital probe, tympanic infrared, temporal artery, and
single-use chemical dot, with the physical principle and the accuracy problem of
each. Sphygmomanometers, including mercury columns, aneroid gauges, and automated
oscillometric devices, their cuffs by size, their calibration, and the errors that
come from a cuff that does not fit. Pulse oximeters and the absorption of red and
infrared light, the probes for finger, ear, and foot, the false readings from motion,
cold, nail polish, and poor perfusion. Capnography as a vital sign. Respiratory rate
and the devices that count it. The field version of every one of them, and what
survives dust and a battery.

## Chapter 7 — Patient Monitors

The multiparameter monitor as the central bedside machine: its screen, its modules,
its cables, its alarm system, and its configuration. The measurement channels and how
each is acquired, including electrocardiography, blood pressure by cuff and by
arterial line, oximetry, temperature, and exhaled carbon dioxide. Electrodes, lead
wires, cable management, and the artifact that comes from a loose connection.
Central stations and telemetry, and the difference between monitoring a patient and
watching a screen. Alarm limits, alarm fatigue, and the discipline of setting limits
deliberately. Battery life and the transport problem. Cleaning between patients.
Maintenance and calibration. The portable monitor of a field clinic, and the manual
observations that replace it when the battery dies.

## Chapter 8 — Basic Examination Tools

The small tools that carry the physical examination. The stethoscope, acoustic and
electronic, its parts, its care, and the difference between the bell and the
diaphragm. The otoscope, its specula and bulbs, and the pneumatic attachment. The
ophthalmoscope, its apertures, and the technique its design assumes. The reflex
hammer and the tuning fork of each frequency. The penlight, the tongue depressor, the
tape measure, the measuring tape for limbs and the one for infants. The neurological
pin and the cotton wisp. Gloves and the examination surface. How each is cleaned.
The field substitutes: the phone torch, the phone camera as a magnifier, and the
improvised tuning fork.

## Chapter 9 — Vascular Access

Every route into a vein and an artery. Peripheral cannulas by gauge and color, their
lengths, their materials, and the veins they suit. Central venous catheters by lumen
number and length, the sites used, and the insertion bundles that reduce infection.
Peripherally inserted central catheters, midline catheters, and implanted ports with
their needles. Arterial lines, their catheters, their transducers, and their
leveling. Intraosseous access by manual needle and by powered drill, and the sites
used in adults and children. Securement devices, transparent dressings, and the
flush and lock solutions. Ultrasound guidance and the probe cover. The risks of each
route, including infection, thrombosis, extravasation, and air embolism. The field
reality where intraosseous access is the emergency default.

## Chapter 10 — Infusion Devices

From the simplest to the most complex. Gravity sets, their spikes, chambers, roller
clamps, and filters, and the drop factor that lets a nurse count drops for a rate.
The burette and the volume-control set. Volumetric infusion pumps, their cassettes
and tubing, their rate range, their air-in-line and occlusion alarms, and the
free-flow hazard when a set is removed from a pump. Syringe pumps and their syringes.
Patient-controlled analgesia pumps and their locked settings. Enteral feeding pumps
and their sets. Elastomeric pumps that need no power. Blood and fluid warmers.
Infusion stands, poles, and the pumps that fall. Alarm handling, troubleshooting, and
the daily checks. The field version: counting drops, the improvised rate, and the
danger of a wide-open clamp.

## Chapter 11 — Syringes, Needles and Injection Devices

Syringes by volume and by tip, luer lock against luer slip, and the syringes made for
pumps. Needles by gauge, length, and bevel, the colors of their hubs, and the use

aspiration. Filter needles, blunt fill needles, and drawing-up devices. Safety-
engineered needles and needleless systems, and how their protection is defeated by
habit. Insulin pens, their cartridges and needles, and their storage. Autoinjectors
and their use in an emergency. Reconstitution devices, vial adapters, and the
sharps container at the point of use. Reuse, resterilization, and why both fail.
The field constraints, and the discipline of one needle, one injection, one disposal.

## Chapter 12 — Fluids, Blood and Blood Products

## Chapter 13 — Medication Management

The systems that keep drugs safe from the shelf to the patient. Dispensing cabinets,

## Chapter 14 — Oxygen Systems

Medical oxygen from its source to the patient's nose. Cylinders by size, their
colors and markings, their valves, pin index systems, and pressure regulators, with
the calculation of how long a cylinder will last at a given flow. Piped systems,
their sources, their terminal units, their flowmeters, and their alarms. Oxygen

therapy. Oxygen toxicity and fire safety, including the prohibition on grease and
the behavior of oxygen near a diathermy machine. The field configuration: a
concentrator, a solar panel, and a motorcycle that carries cylinders.

## Chapter 15 — Airway Management

The devices that keep a passage open. Oropharyngeal airways by size and the

their cuffs, their inner cannulae, and their ties. Cricothyrotomy kits and the
surgical airway. Confirmation of placement by auscultation and by exhaled carbon
dioxide. The field decision: which of these belongs in a bag carried up a mountain.

## Chapter 16 — Ventilation

Every way of breathing for a patient. Self-inflating bags by size, their valves,
their reservoirs, and the oxygen tubing that feeds them. Flow-inflating bags and
their different feel. Transport ventilators, their turbines or compressed gas
supply, their battery life, and their limited modes. Intensive care ventilators,
their controls for volume and pressure, their modes including assist control, volume

Ventilator alarms and their meaning. Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation as the
extreme, with the infrastructure it demands. The field ventilator, and the manual bag
that remains when it fails.

## Chapter 17 — Suction, Nebulizers and Chest Drainage

Three related systems. Suction from wall pipelines, from electric and battery units,

Nebulizers by type, including jet nebulizers with their compressors, ultrasonic

## Appendix A — Visual Atlas of Equipment by Department

A spoken atlas, and the substitute for the photographs this audio guide cannot carry.
Walk through each department in the order a person would enter it, naming what stands
where and what it looks like: the emergency department and its trauma bay, the general
ward and its bed space, the operating theatre and its lights and tables, the intensive
care unit, the delivery room, the laboratory and its benches, the imaging suite room
by room, the sterilization department, the pharmacy and stores, the laundry, and the
morgue. For each room, describe the fixed equipment first and the movable equipment
second, and the sounds and alarms that identify the room from the corridor. Explain
explicitly at the start that this appendix is written because a photograph cannot be
heard.

## Appendix B — Quick-Reference Tables

The tables that are hardest to remember, spoken as prose and organized so a listener
can hold them in mind. Blood collection tubes by closure color, additive, and their
order of draw. Needle gauges with their hub colors and the use each serves. Catheter
sizes in French and the patient each is meant for. Oxygen delivery devices with their
flow ranges and their approximate inspired concentrations. Suture sizes and their
typical uses. Glove sizes and their measurement. Nasogastric and endotracheal tube
sizes by age. For each table, the numbers are given plainly and then qualified: the
listener must confirm against the local protocol, because these tables differ between
institutions and are memorized precisely so that they can be checked precisely.

## Appendix C — Crash Cart and Department Checklists

The lists that make a department ready. The crash cart drawer by drawer, in the order
it is opened, with the adult and pediatric differences named. The daily check and the
shift check, and the seal system that makes a cart trustworthy. The code checklist,
from the first compression to the debrief. The theatre checklist, including the
surgical safety pause and the count. The intensive care bed space check. The ambulance
check. The delivery room check. The emergency tray. The refrigerator and freezer
checks. Each checklist is spoken as a sequence to be performed, and the appendix
should explain why a checklist works when memory does not.

## Appendix D — Glossary of Devices and Acronyms

A spoken glossary, alphabetically ordered and readable aloud. Device entries give the
name and one or two sentences of description, written to be understood by someone who
has not yet met the object. Short-form entries expand the short form and explain the
concept in plain words, so that a listener who hears an unfamiliar short form on a
ward can find it here. The glossary must cover the short forms allowed elsewhere in
the book, and must state the expansion of each fully rather than referring to another
entry. It closes the guide the way a good index opens a reference.

## Appendix E — Troubleshooting Flowcharts for Common Alarms

The alarms a listener will actually meet, each told as a spoken sequence rather than
as a diagram. For each: what the alarm sounds like, what it means, and the checks in
order, first, second, third, and finally the point at which the device must be
stopped and help called. Cover the patient monitor and its leads and limits, the
ventilator and its pressure and volume alarms, the infusion pump and its occlusion
and air alarms, the dialysis machine and its conductivity and temperature alarms, the
autoclave and its cycle failure, the refrigerator and its temperature excursion, the
pulse oximeter and its poor signal, and the oxygen concentrator and its low
concentration. Each sequence ends with the rule that patient safety precedes
equipment diagnosis.
