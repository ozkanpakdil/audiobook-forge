# Hospital Equipment — Style Contract

This file is the binding contract for every section of *Hospital Equipment: A
Complete Guide to Devices, Machines, Tools and Consumables*. It overrides habits,
preferences, and anything else you have read. Each section is written by a different
author and read aloud by the same voice, so the rules below are what make seventy-eight
chapters sound like one book.

## What this book is

A narrated reference for the person who has to understand, choose, use, maintain, and
improvise the hardware of medicine. It describes what each device is, what it is for,
how it works in plain physical terms, what it looks like in the hand, how it is set up
and checked, what can go wrong, and how it is reprocessed or discarded. It is written
for nurses, clinical officers, biomedical technicians, and the people who order,
receive, or repair equipment far from the manufacturer's service contract.

Every chapter holds two versions of the same subject. First the **hospital-grade**
device as it exists in a well-resourced institution, with its infrastructure, its
consumables, and its service requirements. Then the **field variant**: the portable,
robust, battery-powered, hand-operated, or improvised alternative used in a mobile or
frontier clinic, what it gives up, and what it demands instead. The comparison is the
heart of this book, because the same clinical need is met by very different machines
depending on where the patient lies.

It is written to be **listened to**. The listener may be a technician walking between
wards with headphones on. Nothing can be re-read, looked up, or highlighted. If a
sentence only works when seen on a page, it does not belong in this book. Where a
photograph would help on a page, this book must describe the object in words precisely
enough that the listener can picture it.

## Contract of style

- One heading per section, exactly the heading given in the syllabus below. Nothing
  else may be a heading.
- Prose only. No bullet points, no numbered lists, no tables, no markdown emphasis, no
  footnotes, no citations, no web addresses, no references to figures or tables.
- Paragraphs of four to eight sentences, separated by one blank line. The final
  paragraph begins with exactly `To summarize, ` and carries no heading.
- Explain the physical principle before the clinical use. A listener who understands
  why a device works will use it correctly and will recognize when it is failing.
- Never use a brand name, a trademark, or a model number. Describe the device by its
  generic name: "laryngeal mask airway" rather than a maker's product name. This keeps
  the book durable and avoids advertising.
- Every mention of a setting, a pressure, an energy level, a temperature, a
  sterilization cycle, or an alarm threshold must be followed by the reminder that the
  number must be confirmed against the manufacturer's instructions and the local
  protocol before the device is used on a patient.
- Safety is not an appendix. A serious hazard, an alarm, or a common operator error is
  named where the device is described, not deferred to a later chapter.
- Length: chapters run between three thousand eight hundred and four thousand four
  hundred words. Appendices run between two thousand two hundred and three thousand
  two hundred words. A little over is fine; badly under is not.
- Write the file once, carefully. Do not rewrite, restructure, or trim it afterwards.

## Numbers, units and symbols

- Numbers are digits. Units are always written out in full: "5 centimeters",
  "14 gauge", "16 French", "120 kilovolts", "0.5 tesla", "250 joules",
  "134 degrees Celsius", "15 minutes", "50 hertz".
- Never a unit abbreviation: not cm, mm, kg, mL, mg, Hz, kV, mA, J, Fr, G, mmHg.
- Pressures are "millimeters of mercury"; gas volumes are "liters per minute";
  radiation is "milligray" or "millisievert"; magnetic field strength is "tesla".
- Fractions as words: "one half", "one third". Ranges use "to": "10 to 15 minutes".
- Routes and frequencies are written out: "intravenously", "by mouth", "every six
  hours". Never IV, IM, PO, q6h, b.i.d.
- No symbols at all: no per cent sign, equals, plus, minus sign, arrows, degree sign,
  ampersand, greater than, less than, or angle brackets. Write "per cent", "equals",
  "leads to", "degrees", "and", "greater than", "less than".
- Centuries and figure numbers in words: "the nineteenth century", never "19th".
- Latin letters, ordinary punctuation, and the em dash in the heading only. Never
  Cyrillic, Greek, or mathematical characters.

## Acronyms

A synthesized voice reads an uppercase token as letters, which is usually correct for
equipment: ECG, CT, and MRI are spoken as letters by every clinician. The rule is
therefore: **spell the term out at its first mention in each chapter, in full, then use
the short form freely.**

These short forms are explicitly allowed, because they are the normal spoken name of
the device or the concept: CT, MRI, ECG, EEG, EMG, PET, DEXA, PACS, ICU, PPE, CPR,
AED, CPAP, BIPAP, PICC, PCA, CRRT, CSSD, UPS, HVAC, EHR, ISO, IEC, FDA, MSF, VHF, UHF,
USB, LED, LCD, PVC, PTFE, RFID, GPS, SIM, AI, DICOM, HL7, BF, CF, ENT.

Everything else stays written out in full, every time. These in particular must never
appear as short forms, because the voice turns them into ordinary words or into
nonsense: never WHO (write "the World Health Organization"), never WASH (write "water,
sanitation and hygiene"), never DALY (write "disability-adjusted life year"), never
IRIS (write "immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome"), never TB (write
"tuberculosis"), never ORS (write "oral rehydration solution"), never RDT (write "rapid
diagnostic test"), never MDA (write "mass drug administration"), never ART
(write "antiretroviral therapy"), never NTD (write "neglected tropical disease").
