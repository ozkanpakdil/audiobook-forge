# Emergency Wound and Trauma Care — Style Contract

This file is the binding contract for every section of *Emergency Wound and Trauma
Care: A Practical Manual for First Responders and Health Professionals*. It overrides
habits, preferences, and anything else you have read. Each section is written by a
different author and read aloud by the same voice, so the rules below are what make
forty-nine chapters sound like one manual.

## What this book is

A narrated manual for the person who arrives first and has to act: the lay first aider
at a road crash, the trained first responder, the emergency medical technician or
paramedic on an ambulance, the nurse in a district hospital with one operating theatre,
and the physician or trauma surgeon who receives what they send. It covers wounds,
bleeding, penetrating and firearm trauma, chest injuries, fractures of every limb,
burns, amputations, head, neck and spinal injury, and the complications that kill
patients after the injury itself: shock, compartment syndrome, crush syndrome and
infection.

Every subject is treated at three levels, and the level is always named:

- what the **lay first aider** may do with bare hands and whatever is in the car,
- what the **trained responder, technician or paramedic** may do with a kit, a protocol
  and authorization,
- what belongs to **definitive hospital care**, where the surgeon, the imaging and the
  operating theatre take over.

The distinction is the spine of this book. The same wound demands a different act from
each pair of hands, and a manual that blurs the three teaches dangerous habits. A
procedure that belongs to definitive care must be described as definitive care even
when the listener is a first responder, because knowing what one must not attempt is as
much a part of competence as knowing what to do.

It is written to be **listened to**. The listener may be on scene with gloves on, in an
ambulance, or walking between the emergency department and the ward. Nothing can be
re-read, looked up, or highlighted, and no diagram can be consulted. If a sentence only
works when seen on a page, it does not belong in this book. Where a flowchart, a table
or a photograph would help on a page, this book must say it in words, in the order the
actions must be taken.

The book's rule for every emergency is the one the outline states: the first responder's
job is not to make the injury look normal. The priorities are to keep the patient alive,
to stop life-threatening bleeding, to keep the airway open and the breathing working, to
prevent further harm, to protect wounds and exposed tissue, to immobilize what needs
immobilizing, to recognize shock and the other lethal complications, to keep the patient
warm, to watch continuously, and to reach definitive care as quickly and safely as
possible.

## Contract of style

- One heading per section, exactly the heading given in the syllabus below. Nothing
  else may be a heading.
- Prose only. No bullet points, no numbered lists, no tables, no flowcharts, no arrows,
  no markdown emphasis, no footnotes, no citations, no web addresses, no references to
  figures, tables or boxes.
- Paragraphs of four to eight sentences, separated by one blank line. The final
  paragraph begins with exactly `To summarize, ` and carries no heading.
- Where the outline gives an algorithm, a list, or a reference table, it must be
  rendered as prose that follows the order of the actions: "the first action is, and
  only when that is done does the next become possible". A sequence spoken in order is
  a service to the listener; a list on the page is useless in the ear.
- Actions come in the order they must be performed, and each step says what it is for
  and what finding tells the responder to move on.
- Length: chapters run between three thousand eight hundred and four thousand four
  hundred words. Appendices run between two thousand two hundred and three thousand two
  hundred words. A little over is fine; badly under is not.
- Write the file once, carefully. Do not rewrite, restructure, or trim it afterwards.
- Vary openings and structures between sections: this is a manual of fifty-three
  sections, and fifty-three openings of the same shape would be heard as one.

## Scope of practice rules

These rules are not decoration. A manual that ignores them can kill someone who follows
it.

- **Name the provider level for every intervention.** Say who may do it: the lay first
  aider, the trained first responder, the emergency medical technician or paramedic, the
  nurse, the physician, the trauma surgeon. Where an act requires equipment,
  authorization or a protocol, say so in the same breath.
- **Never present a definitive procedure as a first-aid measure.** Extraction of a
  bullet, surgical exploration of a wound, reduction of a fracture, internal or external
  fixation, repair of a vessel, tendon or nerve, fasciotomy, and deep debridement belong
  to definitive surgical care. Name them in the sections on hospital treatment, and
  where a listener might be tempted, say plainly that this is not a field procedure.
- **Never remove an embedded object in the field.** It is stabilized in place and the
  patient is moved with it, because removal may cause hemorrhage that cannot be
  controlled or further damage to tissue. State the reason, not only the rule.
- **First responders do not extract bullets.** Removal is a surgical decision made when
  it is clinically indicated, and the section on retained bullets must say so.
- **Never straighten a severely deformed fracture to make it look normal.** It is
  splinted in the position found, and the reason is the risk to vessels and nerves.
- **Never bind a chest tightly**, and never let an apparently uncomplicated rib fracture
  be treated as a minor injury.
- **The tourniquet is not loosened in the field** once it has controlled the bleeding,
  and the time it was applied is recorded, on the patient, in a way the receiving team
  will see. State why the time matters.
- **No drug dose, no regimen, no route, no frequency.** This manual teaches decisions,
  not prescriptions. Where analgesia, tetanus protection or antibiotics must be
  discussed, describe who decides, what finding makes the decision urgent, what the
  scope of practice forbids, and that the substance and the amount belong to the local
  protocol and the prescriber. A listener must not be able to take a prescription from
  this book.
- **Scene safety comes before the patient.** No section may begin care without saying
  what must be made safe first: traffic, fire, electricity, water, animals, violence,
  structural collapse, and the hazards of the patient's own body fluids.
- **Every number is an illustration.** A time, a pressure, a temperature, a depth, a
  burn extent, a rate or a threshold named in this book must be followed by the reminder
  that it must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, and
  the reason is that such numbers differ between services, countries and editions. Vary
  the wording; never paste the same sentence fifty-three times.
- **Protect against hypothermia** in every chapter where a patient is bleeding, burned,
  exposed, immobilized or waiting, because cold is a complication of trauma and not a
  detail of comfort.
- No real patient, no identifiable case, and no institutional detail. Where an example
  is needed, it must be a constructed illustration and must be described as one.
- This is educational material. It does not replace training, certification,
  supervision, local protocol or the law of the country where you work, and no section
  may suggest otherwise.

## Numbers, units and symbols

- Numbers are digits. Units are always written out in full: "38 degrees Celsius", "110
  beats per minute", "90 millimeters of mercury", "5 centimeters", "2 liters", "15
  minutes", "6 hours".
- Never a unit abbreviation: not cm, mm, kg, mL, mg, bpm, mmHg, m/s. Write
  "millimeters of mercury", "beats per minute", "liters", "milliliters".
- Fractions as words: "one half", "one third", "two thirds". Ranges use "to": "10 to 15
  minutes", "70 to 90 millimeters of mercury". Ratios are spoken: "one in four".
- Symbols are never used: no per cent sign, equals, plus, minus sign, arrows, degree
  sign, ampersand, greater than, less than, slash used to mean "or", or angle brackets.
  Write "per cent", "equals", "leads to", "degrees", "and", "greater than", "less than".
- Routes and frequencies are written out: "into a vein", "into the muscle", "under the
  skin", "every six hours". Never the abbreviated forms, and never a route abbreviation
  such as IV, IM or PO.
- The degree of a burn is a word, not a symbol: "a partial-thickness burn", never a
  numeral with a degree sign.
- Centuries and ordinals in words: "the nineteenth century", never "19th".
- Latin letters, ordinary punctuation, and the em dash in the heading only. Never
  Cyrillic, Greek, or mathematical characters.

## Acronyms, short forms and mnemonics

A synthesized voice reads an uppercase token as a sequence of letters, which is
sometimes what a clinician would say and sometimes nonsense.

- The only short forms this book tolerates are: CT, MRI, CPR, PPE, ICU and ABCDE. ABCDE
  is kept because it is spoken aloud as five letters, and because it is the name of the
  primary survey.
- Spell everything else out at its first mention in every section, and prefer the full
  form: "emergency medical services", "emergency medical technician", "paramedic",
  "personal protective equipment" (after which PPE is allowed), "cardiopulmonary
  resuscitation" (after which CPR is allowed), "chest radiograph", "ultrasound",
  "computed tomography" (after which CT is allowed), "magnetic resonance imaging" (after
  which MRI is allowed), "intensive care unit" at first mention and ICU thereafter,
  "traumatic brain injury", "tetanus", "compartment syndrome", "crush syndrome".
- Never write EMS, EMT, GCS, JVP, CXR, OR, ED, BP, HR, RR, SpO2, TQ or any other
  invented short form. A chapter is listened to on its own, so the full term is spelled
  out again in each chapter, however often it appeared in the one before.
- Use AMERICAN spelling throughout, never British: "hemorrhage", "edema", "anemia",
  "tumor", "color", "behavior", "center", "defense", "pediatric", "fetal", "dyspnea",
  "esophagus", "anesthesia".
