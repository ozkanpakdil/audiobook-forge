# Chapter 47 — Wound Closure

Closing a wound is a decision taken in the middle of treatment rather than a finishing touch, and it can save a hand, a face, or a life while it can undo all three. The lay first aider never closes a wound; the lay first aider covers it with a clean dressing, presses on it if it bleeds, keeps the patient warm, and reaches emergency medical services, because a protected wound is a treated wound. The trained responder or paramedic dresses the wound and, where the local protocol authorizes it, may bring the edges of a small, clean, superficial wound together with an adhesive strip, but deliberate closure belongs to definitive hospital care. The lethal possibility named first here is infection rather than hemorrhage, and the finding that reveals it is a wound sealed over contamination, dead tissue, or a retained object, because such a wound can become an abscess, a spreading infection, or sepsis. Scene safety comes before the patient, so traffic, fire, electricity, water, animals, violence, collapsing structures, and the patient's own body fluids are dealt with before any hand is laid on a wound. This chapter does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

Closure is the final act of a sequence, and the sequence decides whether closure is safe. The first step is control of bleeding, because a wound that is still bleeding cannot be examined or cleaned. The next is examination of the wound and of the structures beneath it, including the tendon, the nerve, and the vessel, because a closure performed too early hides the injury that matters most. Cleaning follows, and it removes the bacteria, the grit, and the old clot that a closed wound would keep inside. Then dead tissue and reachable foreign material are removed, and only then does the question of closure arise. Closure restores the barrier of the skin and holds the edges of a clean wound in contact so that it heals by primary intention, while a wound left open heals by secondary intention, filling from its base with granulation tissue. Each path is a choice rather than a failure, made by the clinician who has examined the wound, the patient, and the injury.

Certain conditions must be met before any wound is closed, and checking them in order protects the patient from a decision that cannot be taken back. Bleeding must be controlled, the wound must be clean enough, and there must be no dead tissue, no foreign body, and no established infection, because closure over any of those seals the problem inside the body. The edges must meet without tension, since skin pulled together under tension dies, separates, and leaves a wider scar than an open wound would have. The tissue must be alive and the patient must be able to heal it, which brings the circulation and the patient's other illnesses into the decision. Before the skin is closed, the function beneath it is examined and written down, because a divided tendon, a divided nerve, and an injured vessel each change the operation the patient needs. The examination asks the patient to move the fingers or the toes, tests feeling at the wound and beyond it, and checks the pulse and the color of the skin downstream, and it is repeated after closure so that a change is noticed. A constructed illustration, offered as a construction and never as a real patient, is the hand wound over a knuckle that is closed as a simple laceration while the tendon beneath has been cut, so that a finger stops working.

Time is one of the conditions that decide whether a wound may be closed, because bacteria multiply as the hours pass and because the tissue at the edge of a wound loses resistance as it dries and swells. A clean wound seen soon after the injury is a candidate for closure, while the same wound seen much later is treated with more caution. The interval that separates those two situations is taught as a number that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because services, countries, and editions place the line differently and because the site changes the answer. The clinician uses the interval as one factor among the contamination, the site, the depth, and the patient rather than as an arithmetic rule. What the field responder contributes is the time of the injury, recorded and handed over, because a receiving team that does not know when the wound was made cannot decide well.

Sutures are the method most listeners picture when they hear the word closure, and they suit the wound that needs precise approximation and support. A suture holds the edges of a full-thickness skin wound together, and it can be placed in layers, so that a deep layer closes a dead space and a superficial layer brings the skin into a fine line. Sutures suit clean lacerations of the face, where the cosmetic and functional stakes are high, and they suit the scalp, the trunk, and the limbs, and they suit wounds whose edges will not stay together without a hold that adhesive strips cannot give. They harm the contaminated wound, because a suture line crosses a surface that already carries bacteria and gives those bacteria a closed space in which to multiply. They harm the wound with a cavity beneath it, because blood and serum collect there and become an abscess while the surface still looks tidy. They harm skin pulled together under tension, because tight sutures strangulate the tissue along the line so that the skin dies and the knots cut through. The finding that should make a clinician choose another method is skin that blanches and stays pale when the edges are brought together.

Staples are a closure method for wounds that are long, straight, and under little tension, and they are used most often on the scalp and the trunk, and sometimes on a limb where the cosmetic result matters less. Their advantages are speed and a strong hold, and speed is an advantage in a patient who must go to the operating room for another injury. Staples harm the face, where a fine scar matters, and they harm the hand and the fingers, where delicate structures lie beneath and the scar must not restrict movement. They harm a wound across a joint, because the pull of the joint opens the line between the staples. They are not a method for a contaminated wound, a bite, or a wound with a dead space beneath it, and they are placed by a physician or a nurse under a protocol rather than by a first aider. The finding that tells the clinician to choose fine sutures instead is a wound on the face or over a moving part.

Adhesive strips are a gentle method for small, superficial, low-tension wounds whose edges fall together on their own, and they suit a child or an anxious patient who will not tolerate a needle. They harm a wound under tension, because the strips lift and the wound gapes again, and they harm a wound across a joint, and they harm skin that is wet, hairy, or sweating, because they do not stick and therefore do not hold. Tissue adhesive is a liquid that bonds the dried edges of a wound, and it suits small, clean, superficial wounds that are not under tension, particularly in children and on the face when an experienced clinician applies it. Adhesive harms a wound under tension, a wound across a joint, and a wound that is still bleeding, and its worst harm is a wound in which the liquid runs into the depth instead of staying on the surface, because then the contaminant is sealed inside and an abscess forms beneath an intact surface. Neither strips nor adhesive is a method for a contaminated wound, a bite, a puncture, or a wound with a retained foreign body, and where a protocol authorizes a trained responder to use strips, deeper wounds still belong to definitive care.

The four methods are choices rather than rungs of a ladder, because no method is better than another in itself and each is better or worse for a particular wound. The clinician chooses by asking what the wound needs, whether that is a fine line on the face, a strong hold on the scalp, speed in a patient who is bleeding elsewhere, or the gentlest method for a frightened child. The site, the depth, the tension, the contamination, the time since the injury, the state of the tissue, and the patient's ability to heal all enter the decision. The experience of the operator enters it as well, because a method a clinician cannot perform well is not a method for that clinician to choose. The cosmetic and functional stakes decide the site, since a scar on the back and a scar across a knuckle are different problems. The wrong method placed in a dirty wound harms the patient far more than the right method placed in a clean one, so the question of whether to close at all comes before the question of how.

Some wounds must not be closed immediately, and the list is short enough to remember even though the reasoning behind it is long. The bite, the heavily contaminated wound, the puncture, and the wound with a retained foreign body are the four that this chapter names, and each is a wound in which closure would seal contamination inside the body. To those are added the wound with dead tissue, the wound that is already infected, and the patient whose circulation is too poor to heal a closed wound. A bite carries the bacteria of the mouth that made it deep into the tissue rather than across the surface, and an animal bite is often a laceration with a crush that leaves a rim of dead tissue which cannot resist those bacteria. A human bite is at least as dangerous, and the wound over a knuckle that follows a blow to a tooth is the classic example, because the joint and the tendon beneath may already be inoculated and because the patient often delays seeking care. Closing a bite primarily is a decision for the surgeon, usually made only after the wound has been cleaned, explored, and sometimes left open under a dressing for a planned interval. The finding that makes a bite urgent is a wound over a joint, a wound on the hand, a wound with loss of movement, or a patient who is unwell.

A heavily contaminated wound is one in which the bacterial load is high enough that the patient's defenses cannot clear it, and the common sources are the roadway, the soil, the farmyard, the manure heap, and stagnant water. The wound that follows a fall from a motorcycle onto a road is the example most responders recognize, because it carries grit and asphalt into tissue that is already bruised and deprived of blood. Contamination is not measured by the appearance of the wound, since a wound that looks shallow may hold a deep pocket of dirt, and the only reliable answer is proper inspection and cleaning. A wound of this kind is left open after cleaning, covered with a dressing, and reviewed, and any closure is done later when the wound has shown that it is clean and that its tissue is alive. The field responder shortens the journey by dressing the wound without probing it and by reporting the mechanism of injury, because that report tells the receiving team what contamination to expect.

A puncture wound has a small opening and a deep track, and its danger lies in the part that cannot be seen. A nail through a shoe, a splinter, a thorn, and a fine fragment of metal all produce a wound whose surface may seal over a track that still holds dirt, clothing, and bacteria, and the sealed surface creates the closed space in which infection can grow. A puncture of the foot through a shoe is a particular trap, since rubber and sock are carried into the depth of the wound and since the bones and joints of the foot lie close beneath the skin. A wound with a retained foreign body is never closed over that body, and the object is never removed in the field, because a knife, a fragment of glass, or a piece of metal may be plugging the vessel beneath it and removal may release hemorrhage that no available measure can control. Such an object is stabilized with padding built around it, the wound is covered, and the patient is moved with the object still in position, because removal is a decision for the operating room. The finding that requires imaging and a surgical plan is any wound whose depth is unknown or whose pain and swelling are out of proportion to the size of its opening.

Dead tissue and dead space are two conditions that a closure can create as easily as it can correct, and an established infection is a third reason to leave a wound open. Tissue dies when its blood supply is torn, when it is crushed, when it is burned, or when it is pulled together under tension, and dead tissue inside a closed wound is a culture medium that the patient's defenses cannot reach. Dead space is the cavity beneath a closed skin line, and it fills with blood and serum that become a place for bacteria to grow, so a wound with a cavity must be closed in layers or left open. An infected wound needs to drain rather than to be sealed, because closure would spread the infection along the tissue planes. Infection announces itself with pain disproportionate to the wound, with redness that spreads beyond its edge, with warmth, swelling, discharge, and a patient who feels unwell, and the most dangerous form is the necrotizing infection, which travels quickly along the deep tissue and can progress within hours. The finding of skin that is tense, discolored, or numb, with gas in the tissue, is a surgical emergency, and the clinician who suspects it does not close the wound and does not wait. The field responder's contribution is to recognize that a wound is not behaving as a simple wound should, to avoid pressing into its depths, and to move the patient to care without delay.

Delayed primary closure is the deliberate plan of cleaning a contaminated wound, leaving it open under a dressing, and closing it later when it has shown that it is not infected and that its tissue is alive. The philosophy behind it is that a contaminated wound should be given time to declare itself before it is sealed. The interval is measured in days rather than in hours, and a figure such as 3 to 5 days is often quoted and, like every interval in this chapter, must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the timing depends on the wound, the patient, and the service. A wound managed in this way is examined at the planned review, and it is closed only when the redness has settled, the discharge has stopped, and the base of the wound has become clean and pink. Healing by secondary intention is the related plan in which the wound is left open to fill with granulation tissue from its base, and it suits the wound whose edges cannot be brought together without tension, the drained abscess cavity, and the patient too ill for a long operation. The choice among delayed closure, secondary intention, and closure during the first operation belongs to the surgeon, who weighs the scar and the function against the risk of infection.

The patient decides as much as the wound does, because closure asks the body to heal a wound that has been brought together and some bodies can answer that request and some cannot. A patient in shock has diverted blood away from the skin and will not heal a closure well, and a patient who is cold has the same problem, so resuscitation and warmth come before the cosmetic decision. Diabetes, disease of the arteries of the limbs, immunosuppression, treatment with steroids, poor nutrition, and smoking each reduce the ability of tissue to resist infection and to knit. Tetanus and antibiotics are part of the wound decision, and neither is taken from this book as an amount, because the tetanus answer depends on the vaccination history and the nature of the wound and is given by the provider authorized under the local protocol. The substance, the amount, the route, and the timing of an antibiotic belong to the prescriber and the local guideline, and the field responder records what is known and states plainly what is not, because the receiving team cannot weigh what it has not been told.

Closure is done in conditions that allow it to be done well, and a wound closed in poor light with no assistance and no time is a wound closed badly. The clinician needs to see the whole wound, to feel its depth, to have the instruments and the material that the local protocol provides, and to have help if the patient is a child or is in pain. Cleaning comes before closure and makes closure possible, because a wound closed with grit, clot, and bacteria still inside it has been given a sealed environment in which to infect. The method of cleaning and the volume of fluid used are decisions of the clinician and the local protocol, and this chapter teaches only the order, which is that the wound is cleaned, inspected, and prepared before any method of closure is chosen. Closing a wound is painful, and the control of that pain is a clinical decision made by the authorized provider before the closure begins, because a comfortable patient holds still and allows a better closure than a struggling one. The substance, the amount, the route, and the timing of any medicine belong to the prescriber and the local protocol, and no reader should take any of them from this manual.

Two sites deserve their own words, and the first is the face, where closure is a functional and cosmetic decision that belongs in the hands of the most experienced clinician available. The face has a rich blood supply, which helps it heal, and it also has the eye, the ear, the nose, the lips, and the muscles of expression, whose function can be changed by a scar that crosses them, so a clinician who is not experienced in facial closure refers the patient rather than practicing on the face. The scalp is the opposite problem, because it bleeds heavily, it hides wounds in the hair, and the skull beneath it may be fractured, so a scalp wound is closed only after the head injury has been considered. The hand and the limbs carry a further set of considerations, because their wounds threaten movement, feeling, and the circulation beyond them, and a wound of the hand is examined for tendon, nerve, and vessel injury before it is closed. A wound over a joint is not closed with a method that cannot tolerate movement, and the limb is often immobilized after closure so that the repair is not pulled apart by motion. The finding that sends the patient to the operating room is any wound over a fracture, a joint, a tendon, or a vessel, or a scalp wound with a palpable fracture or an abnormal level of consciousness.

A patient who is dying from another injury does not have a wound closed, and this is the principle of life before limb applied one step further as life before skin. The primary survey comes first, so hemorrhage, airway, and breathing are controlled before anyone considers the appearance of a laceration, because a patient bleeding to death in the abdomen will not benefit from a neat closure of the scalp. The damage-control philosophy accepts that some wounds are dressed and left open while the patient is resuscitated and taken to the operating room, and that closure is performed later, when the patient is stable enough to heal it. The repair of a tendon, a nerve, or a vessel is not wound closure, and it is performed in the same operation while the skin may be left open or only loosely approximated, because a repaired tendon must glide, a repaired nerve must not be compressed, and a repaired vessel must not be kinked. A closure that is too tight can cause the same disaster as a bandage that is too tight, because skin pulled together over a swollen limb raises the pressure inside that limb, and the finding that warns the clinician is a limb that is tense and painful with pain that increases when the fingers or toes are stretched. The surgeon who expects severe swelling leaves the skin open on purpose, and a fasciotomy is a procedure of the operating room that no field responder performs.

A wound closed when it should have been left open produces a predictable set of complications, and the responder who recognizes them early shortens the patient's course. The first is the abscess, a collection under the closed skin that produces increasing pain, swelling, and fever and that must be opened and drained. The next is the spreading infection of the soft tissue, which moves beyond the wound, reddens and hardens the skin, and can become the necrotizing infection that threatens the limb and the life. The last is the infection of bone in the open fracture that was closed over, which takes weeks to declare itself and months to treat, and a common teaching places the first signs of wound infection within 24 to 48 hours of the injury, an interval that is only an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the timing depends on the organism, the wound, and the patient. After a wound is closed, the patient is told what to watch for, where to return, and whom to call, because increasing pain, spreading redness, discharge, a bad smell, a fever, or a wound that opens all require review. The intervals at which any material is removed differ with the site, and a figure such as 5 days for the face and 10 to 14 days for a limb is taught only as an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the site, the patient, and the technique all change the answer.

A patient waiting for closure is also a patient who is exposed, so the team keeps the patient covered, insulates the patient from the cold surface beneath, and keeps the room warm. Cold impairs clotting, deepens shock, and makes the heart more likely to develop a dangerous rhythm, and it is a complication of trauma rather than a detail of comfort. A body temperature below 35 degrees Celsius is often named as the point at which cold itself becomes dangerous, and that figure must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, like every other figure in this chapter, because the accepted line differs between services and editions. The finding that warns the team is a patient who has stopped shivering while still cold, and the warming continues through the closure and after it.

Every act of closure belongs to a named provider, and the level is set by training, equipment, authorization, and protocol rather than by the urgency of the moment. The lay first aider covers a wound with a clean dressing, presses on it if it bleeds, keeps the patient warm, and sends for emergency medical services, and the lay first aider does not remove an embedded object and does not close the wound. The trained responder and the paramedic dress and protect the wound, report its position and the mechanism of injury, and, where the local protocol authorizes it, may approximate a small, clean, superficial wound with adhesive strips. The nurse and the physician close wounds within the scope that the local protocol and the law define, while the trauma surgeon owns the closure of the contaminated wound, the bite, the wound with dead tissue, and the wound that needs exploration, debridement, or repair beneath the skin. The techniques of this chapter are learned with a teacher, practiced under supervision, and certified by an authority that also defines who may close a wound and where.

To summarize, wound closure is a decision that comes at the end of a sequence and not at the beginning, and every step before it decides whether it is safe. Bleeding is controlled, the wound is examined for tendon, nerve, and vessel injury, the wound is cleaned, dead tissue and foreign material are removed, and only then is the method chosen. The methods are choices rather than a ladder, and sutures, staples, adhesive strips, and tissue adhesive each suit one wound and harm another, so the site, the tension, the depth, the contamination, the time, and the patient all enter the decision together with the experience of the operator. A bite, a heavily contaminated wound, a puncture, and a wound with a retained foreign body are not closed immediately, and a wound with dead tissue or established infection is never closed, so such wounds are cleaned and dressed open and are often closed later by delayed primary closure under the surgeon's plan. The wound that is closed when it should have stayed open becomes an abscess, a spreading infection, or a sepsis, which is why the conditions for closure are checked before the skin is brought together. Every interval, temperature, and figure in this chapter is an illustration whose exact value must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, the provider level is named for every act, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.
