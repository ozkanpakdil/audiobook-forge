# Chapter 5 — External Bleeding

External bleeding is the injury a responder sees before any other, and it kills faster than any other, so the scene is made safe before the wound is touched. Traffic, fire, electricity, water, animals, violence, structural collapse, and the hazards of the patient's own body fluids must be made safe before hands are laid on anyone, and personal protective equipment, or PPE, with gloves at the least, protects the responder and the patient at once. The lethal possibility to be named first is hemorrhage that cannot be controlled, and the finding that reveals it is blood that spurts with each beat of the heart, blood that soaks through dressing after dressing, or a swelling that grows while the responder watches, while a patient who is anxious, pale, and restless with a rapid pulse shows the same warning in the body rather than in the wound. This chapter follows bleeding from its source, through the ladder of control, to the point where definitive hospital care takes over, and it is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country where you work. Every intervention is named with the provider level that owns it, because the same wound demands a different act from a lay first aider and from a trauma team.

Bleeding is classified by the vessel it comes from, and the classification guides behavior rather than naming a label. Capillary bleeding comes from the smallest vessels in the skin and the tissue just beneath it, and it oozes rather than flows, seeping from a raw area that may be large but shallow. Its pressure is low, so it usually stops when the surface is covered and pressed, and this is the one source that a lay first aider can control with a clean dressing and the flat of the hand. Capillary bleeding becomes serious when the area is vast, as in a large abrasion from a road crash, because a continuously weeping surface loses fluid and heat and can exhaust a child or an older patient. The physics is simple, in that a hand laid flat over the whole surface exceeds the pressure inside the smallest vessels, and the flow stops without any need for a tourniquet or a hemostatic agent.

Venous bleeding comes from the vessels that carry blood back to the heart, and it appears as a steady, low-pressure flow of dark blood that does not spurt and does not stop on its own as readily as the capillary ooze. The pressure within a vein is only a little above the pressure of the surrounding tissue, so a firm dressing is often enough, but the size of the vessel decides the danger, and a torn vein in the neck, the groin, or the thigh can empty the circulation in minutes without ever spurting. A wound of the neck or the upper chest near a large vein carries a second hazard, because air can be drawn into the open vessel with each breath, and the finding is a wound that bubbles or sucks at the moment of inspiration. The lay first aider covers the wound and presses firmly without probing and without removing a dressing that has become soaked, adding further layers on top instead. The trained responder may pack an appropriate deep wound and must move the patient toward definitive care without delay when a large vein is torn, because the volume lost rather than the appearance of the wound is what kills.

Arterial bleeding comes from the vessels that carry blood away from the heart under the highest pressure in the circulation, and its behavior is the opposite of the venous flow. The blood is bright, it leaves the wound in jets that keep time with the heartbeat, and it can travel some distance from the patient, while a wound in a limb may bleed into the tissues so that the limb swells and feels tight while the surface looks almost dry. The finding that marks an arterial injury is the pulsatile jet, but the absence of a jet proves nothing, because a torn artery can retract and spasm and bleed into a closed space, and a deep wound with an expanding swelling is an arterial injury until proved otherwise. An artery divided cleanly may clot and stop for a time, then bleed again when the pressure of the circulation rises or when a dressing is lifted, and that pause is one of the traps of early assessment. Control of arterial bleeding is the most urgent task in this chapter, and the ladder of pressure begins with a hand and ends with a tourniquet for the limb and with hospital care for the wound that pressure cannot reach.

The mixed bleeding of a crushed wound follows a heavy blow, a vehicle wheel, a machinery injury, or a fall under a heavy object, and it combines all three sources at once. The vessels are torn rather than cut, the tissue around them is bruised and swollen, and the bleeding may stop quickly because the swelling itself presses on the torn vessels from within. That calm is deceptive, because the pressure that stops the surface flow also raises the pressure inside the limb, and the finding that should worry the responder is a limb that is tense, cool, and painful out of proportion to the wound, with numbness or weakness below the injury. A crush wound may also hide a fracture and a divided vessel at the same time, and the responder must not straighten a deformed limb to make it look better, because movement can release a clot and turn a contained injury into a free hemorrhage. The lay first aider keeps the limb in the position found, covers the wound, keeps the patient warm, and does not try to reduce a swelling by binding the limb tightly. Definitive hospital care is where the pressure inside the limb is measured, the fracture is assessed, and the vessels are examined, and no field technique substitutes for that assessment.

Recognition of life-threatening hemorrhage depends on the behavior of the blood and on the behavior of the patient rather than on the color of what is on the ground. The wound that matters is the one that keeps bleeding through every dressing, the one that pools under the patient so that a hand placed beneath the back comes away wet, and the one whose surrounding swelling grows while the responder watches, and these findings speak of a rate of loss that the eye cannot measure. The patient tells the same story in a different language, with a level of consciousness that is becoming dull, a pulse that is fast and thin, a breathing rate that is rising, skin that is pale and cool, and a restlessness or a thirst that appears before the collapse. The teaching that arterial blood is bright and venous blood is dark is useful in the classroom and unreliable at the roadside, because lighting, contamination, and the depth of the wound all change the color, and a wound can bleed from more than one source at once. The responder therefore asks whether bleeding continues despite control and whether shock is appearing, because either answer moves the patient toward definitive hospital care at once. A measured pressure such as 90 millimeters of mercury may be quoted as a threshold, but it is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the line that matters shifts with age, with pregnancy, and with the patient's usual state.

The ladder of control rests on one physical idea, that bleeding stops when the pressure applied at the bleeding point exceeds the pressure inside the torn vessel, and every step brings more pressure to bear on a smaller area or holds that pressure without human hands. A flat hand over a broad dressing spreads the force and is the first move for surface bleeding, while fingertips or a fist concentrated over the exact point of a spurt produce a far higher local pressure and are the correct move when a jet continues despite the first attempt. The mistake that costs the most is the repeated lifting of the dressing to see whether the bleeding has stopped, because each look breaks the forming clot and returns the wound to the beginning, so the dressing is left in place and further layers are added if blood shows through. The second mistake is to press on a foreign body embedded in the wound, because a knife, a fragment of glass, or a piece of metal may be plugging the vessel beneath it, and the correct act is to build padding around the object and stabilize it while the patient is moved with it in place. Pressure works best when it is directed along the track of the wound rather than across the surface, which is why packing a deep wound can succeed where a dressing over the skin fails. Each step is held until a finding tells the responder to move on, since bleeding controlled and the patient stable means the step is right, while continued soaking, a rising pulse, or a shrinking level of consciousness means the next step is required now.

Direct pressure is the first step and the one most patients need, and it belongs to the lay first aider as much as to the paramedic. The bare hand, protected by gloves or by any barrier that keeps blood from broken skin, is placed over a clean cloth or a dressing on the wound, and the pressure is applied firmly and without interruption while help is called and the patient is kept warm. If blood soaks through, the pad is not removed, and a second pad is laid over the first while the hand returns to the same place with the same force. Pain is expected and does not mean the pressure is wrong, but a patient who becomes unresponsive or whose pulse becomes impalpable means the step has failed and that the next rung of the ladder and the fastest journey to definitive care are now required. A period such as 10 minutes of uninterrupted pressure is often described as enough time to judge whether a wound will stop with direct pressure, and that figure is an illustration which must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the time differs with the size of the vessel and with the patient's clotting.

A pressure dressing is the second step, and it is the trained responder's way of handing the work of the hand over to a bandage so that the patient can be moved without losing the pressure achieved. The principle is unchanged, in that the dressing is applied directly over the wound and the bandage is wound over it tightly enough to keep pressure on the bleeding point and loosely enough that the tissue beyond it is not starved. After the dressing is secured, the responder checks what lies beyond it, looking at the color and the warmth of the skin, feeling the pulse at the wrist or the foot, and asking the patient to move the fingers or the toes if the injury allows. A finger that has turned pale or blue and a pulse that can no longer be felt below the dressing are findings that the bandage has become a tourniquet by accident, and the correct response is to release it and reapply with less tension rather than to wait and watch. A limb that continues to swell inside a pressure dressing, or a patient whose pain becomes severe and out of proportion, points toward compartment syndrome, and the responder must escalate and move the patient rather than tighten the bandage further.

Wound packing is the third step, and it belongs to the trained responder or to the paramedic working within a protocol, because it requires the wound to be appropriate, the technique to be learned, and the pressure to be delivered to the exact source. Packing is used for deep wounds in the groin, the armpit, and the neck, where a tourniquet cannot reach and a dressing across the surface cannot press on the vessel; these junctional wounds are among the most lethal of external bleeding sites. The gauze is pushed to the bleeding point and packed firmly back along the track until the cavity is filled, and then pressure is held over the packed wound with the flat of the hand. Packing is not a technique for a chest wound, it is not a technique for a wound of the abdomen with bowel exposed, and it is not a technique for the lay first aider, who covers and presses from the outside and waits for trained help. The finding that packing has worked is blood that stops rising through the gauze while pressure is held, together with a patient whose level of consciousness and pulse remain steady; the finding that it has failed is gauze that keeps filling with blood, and that finding means the tourniquet or the operating room, not another attempt. A foreign body embedded in the wound is stabilized in place and packed around, and it is never removed in the field, because removal may release hemorrhage that cannot be controlled and may injure a structure that is still intact.

A hemostatic dressing is a gauze or a sponge carrying an agent that helps blood to clot where it is pressed, and it is a tool for the trained responder or the paramedic, not for the lay first aider with bare hands. These dressings exist in some services and not in others, they are carried under a protocol, and their use requires training, because the agent works only when it touches the bleeding vessel and only when pressure is still applied over it. The most important thing to say is what they do not replace, since they do not replace direct pressure, they do not replace packing technique, they do not replace the tourniquet when a limb is bleeding beyond control, and they do not replace the operating room where a vessel is torn beyond the reach of any dressing. A hemostatic dressing is therefore not a reason to delay transport or to stop watching the wound, and the responder who has used one records what was used, because the receiving team must know what is in the wound. The choice of agent and the decision to carry it belong to the local protocol and to the service, and no amount or duration of use should be taken from a manual rather than from that protocol.

The tourniquet is the fourth step and the one most often misused, and its place is narrow and owned by the trained responder or the paramedic under a protocol. The indication is life-threatening bleeding from a limb that has not been controlled by direct pressure and by packing, and a further indication is a scene with many casualties in which a responder must free both hands for the next patient. The contraindication is bleeding that does not come from a limb, because a tourniquet cannot be placed around the neck, the chest, the abdomen, or the groin, and attempting to do so causes harm without controlling the source; bleeding from those places belongs to packing and to immediate surgery. A tourniquet is placed between the wound and the heart, as close to the wound as the anatomy allows and never over a joint if a length of limb above the joint is available, and it is tightened until the bleeding stops rather than until some particular number of turns has been made. The lay first aider does not apply a manufactured tourniquet unless instructed to do so by emergency medical services and trained to use it, and a makeshift band used as a tourniquet can tighten a limb without stopping the flow, which adds pain and tissue damage while the patient continues to bleed. Once a tourniquet has controlled the bleeding, it is not loosened in the field, and that rule is stated again below because it is the rule most often broken.

The time a tourniquet was applied is as important as the tourniquet itself, and it must be recorded on the patient in a way the receiving team cannot miss. The reason is that a limb deprived of blood begins to suffer, and the receiving team must know how long the limb has been without flow in order to judge the risks of releasing the tourniquet and the urgency of restoring the circulation. The record is written on the skin near the tourniquet, on a tape fixed to the limb, or in a place agreed by the local protocol, and it is spoken aloud at handover, because a time not written down is a time that will be lost. A tourniquet is not loosened in the field once it has controlled bleeding, because the clot that has formed behind it may not hold and the patient may hemorrhage again where no one can restore control; release is a decision for definitive hospital care, made with preparation for what may follow. A period such as 2 hours is often quoted as the point at which the risk to the limb becomes serious, and that interval is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the tolerance of muscle and nerve differs with temperature, with the patient, and with the injury. Reassessment after a tourniquet means checking that bleeding has truly stopped, watching for the signs of shock, checking the limb beyond the tourniquet, and looking for a second wound missed while attention was fixed on the first.

Reassessment is not a single act performed once but a rhythm that continues from the scene to the handover, and it follows the same order as the primary survey. The responder returns to the airway, the breathing, and the circulation, and then to the level of consciousness, the pupils, and the limbs, because a patient who was talking a minute ago and is silent now has told the responder more than any wound can. The findings that mark a patient who is still bleeding are a dressing soaked through, a swelling that has grown, a pulse that has risen and softened, a breathing rate that has climbed, skin that has become cold and clammy, and a level of consciousness that is dulling, and these are recorded and compared with the previous set rather than judged alone. The Glasgow coma scale is used where the responder is trained in it, and it turns the impression of drowsiness into something two people can compare, while the trend over time carries more meaning than any single score. A blood pressure that falls and a pulse that rises are the language of concealed blood loss, and the responder must suspect a wound that has stopped bleeding into a cavity, because a limb and a chest can hold a large volume before anything is seen outside.

Hypothermia is a companion of hemorrhage rather than a detail of comfort, and it begins the moment a patient bleeds, lies on cold ground, or has clothing cut away. Blood loss reduces the volume that carries heat, exposure removes heat from the surface, and the cold that follows makes the clotting system work less well, so a patient who is cold bleeds more and a patient who bleeds more becomes colder, and that circle can become impossible to break outside a hospital. The responder breaks it by covering the patient before the journey rather than after, by insulating the patient from the ground, by removing wet clothing and replacing it with dry coverings where that can be done without exposing the wound, and by keeping the ambulance or the vehicle warm on the way. A patient who is bleeding, burned, or exposed is protected against hypothermia at the same moment as the bleeding is controlled, and warming is not delayed until the bleeding stops, because the two tasks run together. A temperature such as 35 degrees Celsius is often named as the line at which cold itself becomes dangerous, and that figure must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the threshold shifts with age and with the way the measurement is taken.

Shock is the state in which the circulation can no longer deliver enough oxygen to the tissues, and hemorrhage is its most common cause in trauma, so every patient who is bleeding is watched for it from the first minute. The early patient compensates, with a fast pulse, a narrowing pulse pressure, cool hands, and a blood pressure that is normal or slightly raised, and that stability is the most dangerous moment, because the loss is already large while the numbers still look acceptable. The patient who can no longer compensate becomes confused, then restless, then drowsy, with rapid shallow breathing and a pressure that falls, and by that stage the loss is severe and the remaining time is short. Fluid replacement, the choice of what is given into a vein, and the decision to give blood products belong to the paramedic under a protocol and to definitive hospital care, and they are decisions about a patient and a local system rather than instructions that can be carried away from a manual, so nothing here names a substance, an amount, a route, or a frequency. Analgesia for the patient with a severe wound is decided in the same way, by the provider authorized to prescribe it and by the protocol in force, because pain is real and poorly treated pain worsens the circulation, while the wrong choice in a bleeding patient can worsen the injury. The responder's own contribution to shock is to stop the bleeding, keep the patient warm, keep the patient flat and still unless a head or chest injury forbids it, and shorten the time to the surgeon.

Definitive hospital care owns everything that repairs rather than contains, and knowing where that line lies is part of competence for every responder. Exploration of a wound, repair of a torn vessel, removal of a bullet or a fragment, deep cleaning of dead tissue, release of pressure inside a swollen limb, and closure of a wound that may be contaminated all belong to the surgeon and to the operating room, and none is a field procedure no matter how urgent the patient appears. The tetanus question is raised at the first contact and answered by the provider authorized to make that decision under the local protocol, because the patient's vaccination history and the nature of the wound both change the answer, and no reader should take the choice from a chapter rather than from the protocol and the prescriber. Antibiotics follow the same rule, in that the finding which makes the decision urgent is a wound that is deep, contaminated, or caused by a bite, while the substance and the amount belong to the prescriber and to the local guideline. Every patient who has bled must also be watched for the complications that follow, including infection, compartment syndrome, and the delayed effects of a large transfusion, and the handover must carry the wound history, the bleeding history, and the times at which each intervention was performed.

Some sites deserve a word of their own because their anatomy changes the technique. A scalp wound bleeds heavily and frightens everyone, and its control is the same direct pressure as anywhere else, but the responder must not mistake a dramatic flow for a deep head injury and must watch the level of consciousness at the same time, because a patient who is bleeding from the scalp may also be bleeding inside the skull. A wound of the face or the neck may bleed into the airway, so the first question is whether the patient can breathe and speak, and pressure is applied around the wound rather than over a structure that is keeping the airway open. A nosebleed that does not stop is controlled by pinching the soft part of the nose and leaning the patient forward, which belongs to the lay first aider, while bleeding that continues belongs to the trained responder and to hospital care, and the patient who is swallowing blood must be watched for vomiting. A traumatic amputation has a stump that bleeds, and the stump is controlled with direct pressure, packing, and a tourniquet where the protocol allows, while the severed part is wrapped in clean material, kept cool, and sent with the patient, because reattachment is a decision for a trauma center and the part must not be placed directly on ice.

To summarize, external bleeding is read by its source and by its behavior, and the responder treats the patient rather than the wound. Capillary bleeding oozes and yields to a covered, pressing hand; venous bleeding flows steadily and becomes deadly when the vein is large; arterial bleeding jets with the heartbeat and may hide inside a swollen limb; and a crushed wound mixes all three while the swelling itself conceals the loss. The ladder of control runs from direct pressure, through a pressure dressing, through packing of an appropriate deep wound, through a hemostatic dressing where the service carries one, to the tourniquet for a limb that will not stop, and each rung is held until the finding that tells the responder to move on, while every step is named with the provider level that owns it and no definitive procedure is presented as first aid. The tourniquet is not loosened in the field, its time is written on the patient, and reassessment continues in a rhythm until handover. Hypothermia and shock travel with hemorrhage, so warmth, monitoring, and the shortest safe journey to definitive care are part of bleeding control and not additions to it. Every time, pressure, temperature, depth, and figure in this chapter is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country where you work.
