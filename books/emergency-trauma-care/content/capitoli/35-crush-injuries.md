# Chapter 35 — Crush Injuries

A patient pinned beneath a fallen wall, a vehicle, or a collapsed floor is living through two emergencies at once, and only one of them can be seen. The local injury is the crushed muscle, the broken bone, and the torn vessel held under the weight, and the systemic injury is the syndrome that begins when the weight is removed and the contents of dead muscle enter the blood. The lethal possibility that must be named first is cardiac arrest after extrication, because a patient who is talking and conscious under the load may lose the heartbeat within minutes of being freed. The scene is made safe before any hand reaches the patient, since the structure, the machinery, the traffic, and the live current that caused the crushing can easily produce another casualty. This chapter follows the crushed patient from entrapment to the hospital and names the provider level that owns each act. It is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country where you work.

The hazards of a crush scene repeat, and each of them has killed rescuers who entered without looking. A slab that has already fallen may rest on unstable debris and may shift when the debris is disturbed, so the pile is treated as a structure and not as a heap. A vehicle resting on a patient may be held by a jack, a winch, or a second vehicle that can fail, and the ground beneath it may be soft enough to settle. Machinery may still be powered and a press may still hold pressure in a line that nobody has isolated, so the supply is shut down by a person authorized to do it and the shutdown is confirmed rather than assumed. Electricity, fuel, dust, and the gases of a confined space add their own dangers, and where a trench has collapsed its walls remain a threat to everyone at the edge. Contaminated blood and tissue are the quieter hazard, so personal protective equipment, meaning gloves and eye protection at the least, is worn before the first hand is laid on anyone. The lay first aider who cannot make the scene safe does not enter it, and calling for those who can is the correct first act rather than a delay.

The severity of a crush is written in the weight, the surface, and the time, and none of the three can be judged from the wound that shows. A heavy load over a broad area does more damage than a sharp load over a small one, a hard edge concentrates the force while a soft surface spreads it, and the duration decides how much muscle has been deprived of its blood. A patient pinned for a few minutes may have a bruise and a fracture, while a patient pinned for hours may have a limb in which the muscle has died in place, and the outside of that limb can look almost unremarkable at first. The responder therefore asks how long the patient has been trapped, what is pressing, and whether the pressure was continuous or released and reapplied, because that history changes what the receiving team expects. A teaching illustration, offered as a construction and never as a real patient or a real incident, is the driver trapped by a collapsed seat for a long period who is alert when found and whose only visible injury is a swollen thigh.

Crushed muscle has been squeezed until its blood supply can no longer reach it, and the tissue at the center of the compression dies while the tissue at the edge is merely bruised. Dead muscle does not stay inert, because its cells break open and release what they contain into the fluid around them, and that material sits within the limb until the pressure is removed. When the weight is lifted, the blood returning to the limb carries those contents into the general circulation in a single load, and the patient who was stable under the weight may become unstable within minutes. This is the distinction that governs the chapter, because a crush injury is the local damage to the limb, while crush syndrome is the systemic illness produced when the products of that damage reach the heart, the kidneys, and the rest of the body. The breakdown of muscle fibers with the escape of their contents into the blood has a name, which is rhabdomyolysis, and the most familiar of those contents is myoglobin, the protein that stores oxygen within muscle and gives it its color. Myoglobin is joined by potassium, phosphate, and uric acid, while fluid and calcium are drawn into the injured tissue so that the circulating volume falls. The finding that raises the suspicion is a limb that is swollen, firm, and painful in a patient who was trapped for a long period and whose pulse and awareness change after the release.

Of all the substances released by dead muscle, the one that kills soonest is potassium, and the disturbance it produces is hyperkalemia. Potassium governs the electrical behavior of the heart, and when its concentration in the blood rises, the rhythm becomes unstable, first slowing and weakening and then losing its coordination altogether. The finding is a pulse that becomes slow, irregular, or difficult to feel, often in a patient who is still conscious and still talking, and the change can be abrupt rather than gradual. This is why cardiac arrest after extrication is named as the lethal possibility of a crush, because the load of potassium that enters the circulation at the moment of release arrives at the heart within minutes. The trained responder watches the pulse and the level of consciousness without interruption after a release and is ready to begin cardiopulmonary resuscitation, or CPR, at once if the patient stops breathing and loses a pulse.

The second organ to suffer is the kidney, and the injury it sustains is acute kidney injury, which may appear hours or days after the crush rather than at the moment of release. Myoglobin passes into the tubules of the kidney, and in the presence of a low circulating volume and acid conditions it can block and injure them, so the kidney that was healthy before the injury begins to produce less urine and to clear less waste. The finding in the field is a patient who is not passing urine, or whose urine is scanty and dark, and this is an observation that the trained responder records and passes on rather than treats. Low blood pressure from the loss of fluid into the crushed tissue makes the kidney injury worse, which is why restoring the circulation and protecting the kidney are managed together in hospital rather than separately. The treatment that clears the muscle products and supports the kidney belongs to definitive hospital care, and where the kidney does not recover it may need a machine to take over its work while the patient heals. The field task is to reach that care sooner rather than later.

Compartment syndrome is the companion of the crushed limb, and it is the injury that continues to develop after the patient is free. Muscle lies within compartments bounded by fascia that does not stretch, and when the tissue inside swells after a crush, the pressure within the compartment rises until it exceeds the pressure that drives blood through the capillaries. The findings that mark it are pain that is severe and out of proportion to the visible wound, pain that increases when the fingers or toes are straightened, a limb that feels tense and firm to the touch, numbness or tingling in the territory of the nerve that runs through the compartment, and weakness of the muscles the compartment contains. A pulse that is still present below the injured area does not exclude compartment syndrome, because the pressure that starves the muscle can be high while the main artery remains open. The definitive treatment is surgical release of the fascia, which is a hospital procedure and never a field procedure, and the field task is to remove anything constricting the limb and to move the patient toward the surgeon. A patient whose pain is escalating while a bandage is in place has told the responder that the bandage, and not the injury alone, may be part of the problem, so the dressing is reassessed rather than tightened further.

Extrication is a medical intervention rather than a rescue of pure muscle, because the manner and the timing of the release change the patient's survival. Rapid release without preparation can kill, and the mechanism is by now familiar, in that the moment the weight is lifted the trapped contents of dead muscle enter the circulation, while the blood that had been held in the compressed limb returns to a circulation that is already short of volume. The patient can also deteriorate because the pressure of the weight was the only thing closing a torn vessel, and its removal restores a hemorrhage that no one is prepared to control. A further hazard is the rescue itself, because a load shifted carelessly can fall on the patient or on the rescuers. The decision about when and how to release the weight therefore belongs to advanced providers and to the incident commander, who weigh the danger of the scene against the danger of the release. The lay first aider does not attempt to move the weight alone, does not pull the patient free, and does not dig into an unstable pile, but calls for a rescue team and stays with the patient.

The preparation before a release is where the outcome of a crush is often decided, and it follows a fixed order. The patient is assessed where they lie, with the airway, the breathing, and the circulation examined before any weight is moved, because a patient who cannot breathe will not benefit from being freed. The mechanism, the duration of entrapment, the part of the body that is compressed, and any bleeding the responder can see are reported to the receiving team and to the medical controller while the rescue is still being organized. Equipment and personnel are gathered for what may follow, including the means to control bleeding, to immobilize the limb, to keep the patient warm, and to begin resuscitation at the moment it is needed. The plan for lifting the weight is agreed in advance, including who will lift, in what direction the load will move, and where the patient will be placed once free, so that the release is a controlled act rather than a sudden one. The finding that tells the team to proceed is a scene made stable and a patient examined and prepared, and until both are true the weight is left where it is unless it is the immediate cause of death.

Monitoring during the release and after it is continuous, because the danger of a crush is concentrated in the minutes that follow the lifting of the weight. The trained responder watches the airway and the breathing, feels the pulse for rate and strength, looks at the level of consciousness and the pupils, observes the color and warmth of the skin, and repeats those observations at short intervals rather than once. The crushed limb is examined for swelling, for tension, for bleeding that has restarted, and for the pulse and movement beyond the injury, and anything constricting it is removed if that can be done without disturbing the wound. The patient is covered and insulated from the ground before the journey begins, because a patient trapped in cold debris is already losing heat and the release adds exposure to that loss. A sudden change for the worse, whether a pulse that slows, a consciousness that dulls, or a breathing that becomes shallow, is treated as the cardiac emergency it may be and is reported at once. The finding that should reassure nobody is a single normal set of observations, because the trend and not the single value tells the responder whether the patient is holding.

Cardiac arrest after extrication is the specific catastrophe this chapter exists to prevent, and it deserves to be described plainly so that no responder is surprised by it. The heart can stop within a short time of the release, driven by the potassium that has entered the blood, by the loss of circulating volume, by the cold, and by the sudden return of acid blood from the crushed tissue. The patient may arrest in front of the rescuers, sometimes while being carried, which is why the pulse and the breathing are watched without interruption from the moment the weight begins to move. If the patient stops breathing and has no pulse, CPR is begun at once by whoever is trained, compressions are continued without pause, and the patient is moved toward a hospital that can continue resuscitation while treating the cause. A duration such as 4 hours of continuous compression is often described in teaching as the point at which the risk of a serious crush syndrome becomes high, and that figure is an illustration which must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the timing differs with the size of the crushed mass, with the duration of entrapment, and with the patient. The responder who expects the arrest is the responder who is ready to treat it.

The lay first aider has a defined and valuable part in a crush, and it is built on restraint as much as on action. The lay first aider calls for emergency medical services and for a technical rescue team, reports the location, the number of patients, the nature of the entrapment, and the time the patient has been trapped, and stays with the patient until help arrives. While waiting, the lay first aider keeps the airway open with the hands if it is obstructed, covers the patient and insulates them from the ground, and speaks to the patient in a calm and continuous way, because a trapped patient who is being talked to is a patient who is being observed. Bleeding that can be reached is controlled with direct pressure through a clean cloth or a dressing, and a foreign body or a piece of debris that is embedded is stabilized in place and never pulled out, because removal may release a hemorrhage that cannot be controlled. The lay first aider does not lift the weight, does not give anything by mouth, does not attempt to straighten a deformed limb, and does not attempt to free the patient by pulling. The finding that tells the lay first aider to stop and wait is any uncertainty about the stability of the scene or the weight, because a second casualty made by a premature rescue helps nobody.

The trained responder and the paramedic work within a protocol, and their contribution is monitoring, preparation, and safe transport rather than definitive repair. They assess the patient with the primary survey, control external bleeding with the measures their service authorizes, immobilize the injured limb in the position found, and protect the patient against hypothermia with blankets and insulation from the ground. They may give oxygen where the protocol and the prescriber authorize it, and they may begin the fluids or the medicines that the local protocol provides for a crushed patient, but the substance, the amount, the route, and the timing are decisions of that protocol and that prescriber and are not taken from a manual. They record the time of entrapment and the time of release as carefully as they record the time a tourniquet is placed, because the receiving team treats a patient whose history includes those times differently from one whose history does not. They make contact with a receiving hospital early, describe the mechanism and the expected complications, and follow the advice they are given about destination and about preparation. The finding that changes their plan is any deterioration after the release, and their response to it is to escalate, to resuscitate if they are trained and authorized, and to shorten the journey.

Definitive hospital care owns every act in this chapter that repairs, corrects, or replaces, and naming that boundary protects both the patient and the responder. The hospital measures the chemistry of the blood, including the potassium and the products of muscle breakdown, monitors the rhythm of the heart, and treats the electrolyte disturbance with the medicines and the amounts that the local guideline and the treating physician decide. It supports the circulation with fluids and blood products chosen for that patient, protects the kidney by restoring the circulation and by other measures within its protocol, and uses a machine to replace kidney function when the injury is severe enough to require it. It measures the pressure within the compartments of the crushed limb and performs the surgical release of the fascia, the removal of dead muscle, and the repair of vessels and bones in the operating room, with the patient under anesthesia. It treats the wound, decides about protection against tetanus and about antibiotics, and provides the pain relief that a crush demands. None of those things is a field procedure, and no responder should delay transport in order to attempt any part of them.

Cold is a complication of crush injury rather than a detail of comfort, and the crushed patient is unusually vulnerable to it. A patient who has been trapped under debris has often been lying on cold ground for a long time, may be wet from rain or from water used in the rescue, and will be exposed again when the weight is removed and the clothing is cut away. Cold impairs clotting, deepens the circulatory failure, and makes the heart more likely to develop a dangerous rhythm, which is precisely the outcome a crushed patient is already at risk of. The responder removes wet clothing where that can be done safely, covers the patient with blankets, coats, or foil, insulates the patient from the ground beneath, and keeps the vehicle or the ambulance warm during transport. A body temperature below 35 degrees Celsius is often named as the point at which the effects of cold become serious, and that figure, like every other in this chapter, is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the accepted line differs between services and between editions. The finding that warns the responder that cooling has already begun is a patient who has stopped shivering while still cold.

A patient who is trapped for a long time is frightened, in pain, and often alone in a space that the responder cannot easily enter, and the care of that patient begins long before the release. Someone should be assigned to stay with the patient, to speak in a steady voice, to explain what is being done, and to keep the patient oriented to time and to place. The patient is encouraged not to struggle against the weight, because struggling consumes muscle that is already short of blood and increases the load of muscle products that will later be released. Sips of fluid are not offered by the lay first aider, because the decision about anything taken by mouth in a patient who may need surgery belongs to the clinician, and because a patient who vomits while trapped is in danger of inhaling what is brought up. Pain is acknowledged and reported, and the decision about how it is treated belongs to the provider authorized to prescribe it under the local protocol, since the substance and the amount are not taken from a manual. The finding that is passed to the rescue team is any change in the patient's speech, awareness, or breathing, because those changes help decide how quickly the release must be made.

The crushed wound itself demands the ordinary care of any wound together with a heightened suspicion of infection. The surface may be deeply contaminated with soil, with clothing, and with fragments driven in by the weight, and the tissue beneath it may be dead, which is a combination that favors infection. The responder covers the wound with a clean dressing, keeps it protected during the journey, and does not attempt to clean or explore it in the field, because deep cleaning and the removal of dead tissue belong to the surgeon. The question of protection against tetanus is raised at the first contact and answered by the provider authorized to decide it under the local protocol, because the vaccination history of the patient and the nature of the wound both change the answer. Antibiotics follow the same rule, in that the finding which makes the decision urgent is a wound that is deep, contaminated, or accompanied by dead muscle, while the substance and the amount belong to the prescriber and to the local guideline. The finding that should be reported at handover is any wound that is spreading, foul smelling, or accompanied by fever, because infection in a crushed limb can progress rapidly.

Crush injuries arrive from a small number of recurring settings, and knowing them helps the responder anticipate the syndrome. A structure that collapses in an earthquake, a blast, or a demolition traps people beneath slabs and can produce many crushed patients at once. An industrial accident traps a hand, an arm, or a whole body in machinery, a press, or a roller, and the machine may have to be taken apart rather than reversed, because reversing it can pull the patient further in. An agricultural accident traps a limb under a tractor, a bale, or a falling implement on a farm far from a hospital, and a road crash traps a patient between a vehicle and the ground or beneath a wheel. A patient who lies unconscious on a hard surface for hours after a fall, a seizure, or an overdose suffers the same muscle injury without any external weight, and the responder who knows the pattern will suspect it. In every one of those settings the two problems of this chapter are present together, and the local injury is treated while the systemic one is anticipated.

Some patients tolerate a crush far less well than others, and the difference changes how quickly care must be delivered. A child has less muscle mass and less circulating volume than an adult, so the same weight over the same time releases a proportionally larger load of muscle contents and produces a larger fall in circulating volume. An older patient may have less reserve in the heart and the kidney before the injury begins, and may be taking medicines that affect the heart rhythm and the blood pressure, which the receiving team must know about. A pregnant patient has a circulation that is already altered, and the concerns of the fetus are added to those of the mother, so the care of the mother remains the care of the fetus and the receiving hospital is told that the patient is pregnant. A patient trapped with a head injury, with a chest injury, or with heavy bleeding has a second lethal problem that may kill before the crush syndrome does. The finding that must be reported in every one of these patients is the duration of entrapment, because time is the measure that most changes what the receiving team will do.

The handover is part of the treatment of a crushed patient, and a complete handover changes what the hospital does in the first minutes. The receiving team needs the mechanism, the time of entrapment, the time of release, and the part of the body that was compressed, because those four facts predict the complications and the speed with which they may appear. It needs to know what bleeding was found and how it was controlled, whether a tourniquet was placed and when, whether the limb was splinted, and whether the patient has been moved or turned. It needs the observations that were recorded over time, including the trend of the pulse, the breathing, and the level of consciousness, and it needs to know whether the patient has passed urine and what it looked like. It needs to know what the patient has been given, by whom, and under what protocol, and it needs to know what has not been given. The finding that must never be omitted is any deterioration that occurred during the journey, because a patient who changed on the way to hospital is a patient who is changing still.

To summarize, the crushed patient carries a local injury and a systemic syndrome at the same time, and the systemic one begins when the weight is released. Crushed muscle dies in place and releases potassium, myoglobin, and other contents into the blood, so the heart may stop from hyperkalemia within minutes of extrication, the kidney may fail from acute kidney injury over the following hours, and the crushed limb may develop compartment syndrome even while the patient is being rescued. The scene is made safe first, rapid release without preparation is a cause of death, and the decision about how and when to release the weight belongs to advanced providers and to the incident commander. The lay first aider calls for help, stays with the patient, keeps the airway open, controls what bleeding can be reached, covers the patient, and never pulls an embedded object free. The trained responder monitors the pulse, the breathing, and the level of consciousness continuously, protects the patient against hypothermia, records the times of entrapment and release, and reaches definitive care as quickly as is safe. Hospital care owns the correction of the electrolyte disturbance, the support of the kidney, the surgical release of the fascia, the removal of dead tissue, and the treatment of the wound. Every number, time, and temperature in this chapter is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country where you work.
