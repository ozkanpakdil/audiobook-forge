# Chapter 8 — Special Wounds

Some wounds are special not because they are larger than others but because the way they were made adds risks that a simple cut does not carry. An animal bite drives living organisms deep into tissue, a puncture wound conceals how far it has traveled, and an embedded object may be the only thing holding a torn vessel closed. This chapter covers bites from animals and from people, puncture wounds, foreign bodies, and objects left in place, at the three levels of care this manual keeps separate. It is educational material, it does not replace training, certification, supervision, or local protocol, and every example is constructed rather than drawn from a real patient or incident.

Scene safety is a sequence rather than a slogan, and it begins with the hazard that is still active. An attacking dog must be controlled or contained, and where an animal cannot be secured the responders wait at a distance and let the animal control service work, because a rescuer who is bitten becomes a second casualty and reduces the help available to the first. Where the bite happened in a conflict between people, the police must be present or the scene left until it is calm, and a responder does not step between two people who are still fighting. Where the wound was made by a needle, a blade, or broken glass, every surface is treated as contaminated, and personal protective equipment, after which PPE is the short form, is worn before the first glove touches the patient. A sharps container, never a pocket or a bare hand, receives anything sharp that is recovered. Only when the scene is safe does the assessment begin, and it begins with the lethal possibilities rather than with the wound.

Three lethal possibilities stand behind these wounds, and each announces itself with a finding that can be sought in the first minutes. The first is hemorrhage that will not stop, shown by blood that keeps coming through every dressing, a fast and thready pulse, pale and cool skin, and a level of consciousness that is drifting. A systolic pressure below 90 millimeters of mercury is often quoted as the line at which such bleeding is critical, but that figure is only an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. The second is injury to a deep structure beneath a small wound, shown by a limb that cannot move as it should, a hand or foot that is cold or blue, or a pulse that has disappeared beyond the wound. The third is infection, which may be established already or may follow, and the finding may be absent at first, so the history of a bite or a puncture through a shoe matters more than the skin. Rabies and tetanus belong in the same opening thought, because a bite from an animal carrying the virus is a lethal exposure rather than a wound, and a small puncture with dead tissue and no bleeding is where the tetanus organism thrives.

An animal bite is a crush injury and a contaminated wound at once, and the teeth act as a delivery system rather than as a blade. The jaw crushes muscle and drives oral organisms along the track of the tooth, so the injury extends farther than the visible puncture. Dogs produce ragged lacerations and avulsion, while cats produce fine punctures that seal over and trap organisms beneath the skin. The organisms include Pasteurella species and anaerobic organisms, which flourish where the blood supply has been crushed. Capnocytophaga canimorsus, carried by dogs and cats, can cause severe infection in a person whose spleen has been removed, whose liver is diseased, or whose immune defense is weakened, and that history must be asked out loud. The bite is not cleaned by scrubbing it, because scrubbing drives organisms deeper and tears crushed tissue.

A human bite is treated more seriously than its size suggests, because the human mouth carries aggressive organisms and because the way the injury is made hides its depth. A bite on the hand made when one person strikes another in the mouth is the classic example, and the skin wound may look tiny while the tendon, the joint capsule, or the bone beneath has been opened. The injury often lies over the knuckle, so a wound that looks superficial while the hand is relaxed may slide over a joint and a tendon when the fist is closed. Eikenella corrodens and other organisms of the mouth, with anaerobic organisms, produce infection quickly, and that infection can destroy a tendon sheath or a joint. A human bite therefore belongs to definitive hospital care and surgical assessment, not to a dressing and reassurance at the roadside. The lay first aider controls bleeding and covers the wound without probing it, the trained responder or paramedic documents it and arranges transport, and the surgeon decides whether it must be explored in an operating theatre.

A puncture wound is defined by what it hides, because the surface opening says almost nothing about the depth of the track or the structures along it. A nail driven through a shoe reaches the fat pad, the tendon, the joint, or the bone, and a needle may break and leave its tip inside. The deep structures a puncture can reach include a tendon sheath, a joint space, a bone, a nerve, a blood vessel, the peritoneum, the pleura, and the globe of the eye. A puncture over the chest or the abdomen is a penetrating wound in this manual until proven otherwise, because the track can enter a cavity while the skin closes behind it. The findings that demand definitive hospital care are a joint that will not move fully, a tendon that does not pull when its muscle is used, a bone tender over a point, and a foreign body that can be felt but not seen. A puncture through the sole is the wound most often underestimated, because the patient walks on it, the bleeding is small, and a nail that passed through rubber carries a fragment of the sole into the tissue. The lay first aider does not probe the wound, squeeze it, or open it to search for a fragment, because a blind search in an unclean field turns a closed wound into an open one.

A foreign body is any object inside the wound that did not belong to the patient, and the assessment asks what it is, where it lies, and what it is touching. The material matters because wood and thorns are soft, they break further on removal, and they are poorly seen on a plain radiograph, while metal and glass are usually visible. The location matters because an object in the skin can be removed in a controlled setting, while one near a vessel, a nerve, a tendon, or an organ must be left to the surgeon. The relationship matters most, because an object against a major vessel may be sealing it, and its removal may open a hemorrhage that cannot be controlled in the field. The lay first aider therefore does not remove a foreign body larger than a splinter at the surface, and never one that is embedded, over the eye, in the neck, or in the chest or the abdomen. Where an image is available, the choice among a plain radiograph, an ultrasound, a computed tomography, after which CT is allowed, and a magnetic resonance imaging, after which MRI is allowed, belongs to the clinician who knows the material and the tissue near it. A retained object is often diagnosed late, because the wound closes over it, and the findings are tenderness that persists after healing, a swelling that appears long afterward, a discharge from a small opening, and a limb that will not move normally. Removal is a decision for definitive hospital care, where the tissue can be explored under vision, and some objects are deliberately left in place because removal is more dangerous than keeping them, which is a judgment for the surgeon.

An embedded object is the most dangerous of these wounds because it may be the only barrier between the patient and fatal hemorrhage. A knife, a piece of metal, a wooden stake, a shard of glass, or a broken bone in a limb or the trunk is stabilized in the position found, and it is not withdrawn by anyone in the field. The reason is mechanical, because the object may be pressing on a torn vessel, and pulling it out removes the plug and releases bleeding that no dressing can reach. A second reason is that the object may be tangled in tissue, so dragging it out tears nerves, tendons, and vessels that it had cut cleanly. A third is that the track is contaminated, and moving the object spreads that contamination along structures that were not yet involved. The rule holds at every level below the operating theatre, where the object is finally removed with hemorrhage control and surgical repair immediately available.

The technique of stabilization changes nothing about the object except how safely it can travel. The lay first aider controls bleeding beside the object with direct pressure on the skin around it and never on the object, then builds a ring of padding so that it cannot move. Rolled bandages, cloth, or clean dressings are packed on every side and held with further bandage, so the object is cradled rather than gripped. The object is not shortened, cut, bent, or straightened, because each act moves it inside the tissue and can convert a controlled wound into an uncontrolled one. Where it passes through a limb, both ends are padded and the limb is immobilized as a whole, and the trained responder or paramedic may splint the joints above and below. Where an object projects about 10 centimeters from the skin, that figure is an estimate by eye and is only an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the description in words matters more. Transport is a plan rather than a lift, so the airway and breathing are checked, the level of consciousness is recorded with the Glasgow coma scale where it is available, and the patient is kept still. An object in the eye is protected with a rigid shield resting on the bones around the eye and never on the eye, and an object in the neck is left alone with the airway watched continuously.

The local care of a bite is a sequence that begins with the safety of the responder and ends with a decision rather than with a dressing. Bleeding that is not life-threatening is allowed to flow briefly, because flow helps to carry organisms out, and then pressure is applied with a clean dressing. The wound is washed with clean water or saline and the surrounding skin is cleaned, while the inside of the wound is not scrubbed, not rubbed with a brush, and not packed with an antiseptic that the local protocol does not authorize. High-pressure irrigation that forces fluid into the tissue is avoided in the field, because it can drive organisms and fragments deeper and injure the tissue further. A bite wound is generally not closed by a first responder, and primary closure is a decision for definitive hospital care, made after cleaning, after assessment of the deep structures, and after the risk of infection has been weighed. The lay first aider does not attempt to close the wound with tape, glue, or a suture, and does not apply a tight band that would hide the wound from the team that receives it.

Tetanus is a disease of a wound that has been forgotten, and it is prevented by a decision made at the time of the wound rather than by a dressing. The organism lives in soil, in dust, and in the intestine of animals and people, and it produces its poison in tissue that has no oxygen, which is why a deep puncture, a crush injury, a bite, a wound with dead tissue, and a wound containing soil or a foreign body carry the greatest risk. The lay first aider cannot make the tetanus decision alone and must pass the question to the trained responder, the paramedic, the nurse, or the physician who holds the protocol. The finding that changes the answer is the patient's vaccination history and its documentation, with the nature of the wound and the time since the injury, and where the history is unknown the question is treated as unanswered rather than as reassuring. The substance given, the amount, and the route belong to the local protocol and to the prescriber, and this manual names the decision rather than the dose. Antibiotic prophylaxis after a bite or a puncture is also a clinical decision and never a routine, and the finding that makes it urgent is a wound closed over contaminated tissue, a wound that involves a joint, a tendon, or a bone, a wound with crushed and devitalized tissue, or a patient whose immune defense is weakened. The choice of substance, amount, route, and interval belong to the prescriber and to the local protocol, and no listener should be able to take a prescription from this chapter, so the field team records the animal, the circumstances, the time, the allergies, and the patient's other illnesses for the decision made later.

Rabies is the most feared consequence of a special wound because it is almost always fatal once symptoms begin, and because the wound that transmits it may be trivial. The virus travels in the saliva of an infected animal and enters through a bite, a scratch, or contact of saliva with a break in the skin or with the moist surface of an eye, a nose, or a mouth. The animals that matter most are dogs, bats, monkeys, raccoons, skunks, and foxes, and a bite from a bat may be so small that the patient does not remember being bitten at all. The immediate care is thorough washing of the wound with soap and water and the application of a substance that the local protocol specifies for the virus, done as soon as possible because washing removes virus from the tissue. The decision about vaccination and about the treatment given after exposure belongs to the physician and to the local public health protocol, and the substance, the number of visits, and the route are set there rather than here. The animal must not be killed if it can be observed safely, because observation of a healthy dog or cat over a defined period can change the whole plan, and where that period is given as 10 days the figure is only an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since public health authorities differ.

Infection in a special wound announces itself in a defined way, and the responder who knows the sequence recognizes it before it becomes an abscess or a spreading infection. The early findings are pain that increases rather than settles, redness that spreads beyond the wound edge, warmth of the surrounding skin, swelling, and a discharge that becomes thick or foul. The later findings are a fever, a fast pulse, a rising breathing rate, a level of consciousness that changes, and streaks of redness running along a limb toward the body. A fever may for teaching purposes be described as a temperature of 38 degrees Celsius, but that figure is an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the line differs with the site of measurement and with the patient. A wound that becomes painful and red within 24 hours of a bite suggests a fast organism, and that interval is also only an illustration to be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, while a wound that becomes painful long afterward suggests a retained foreign body or a deep infection of bone. The specific deep infections that must be named are infection of a tendon sheath, infection of a joint, infection of bone, and a spreading infection of the deep tissues that kills muscle and skin. An infection of a tendon sheath makes the finger swell evenly, holds it in a bent position, and makes it painful along the whole length of the sheath, and a spreading deep infection produces pain out of proportion to the wound, tense or discolored skin, and sometimes air beneath the skin that can be felt as crackling, so both are surgical emergencies rather than a course of medicine.

Some wounds appear minor and are not, and the responder who can name them prevents the delay that costs a hand or a life. A small puncture over a joint, a bite over the knuckle, a wound made by a needle that may have broken, a puncture through a shoe, a wound with a retained fragment, and any wound over the eye are all in that group. The wounds that require operative care include a wound that has opened a joint, a wound that has divided a tendon, a nerve, or a major vessel, a wound with an open fracture, a wound with dead tissue that must be removed, and a wound with an object that must be extracted under vision. Surgical exploration, removal of a bullet, deep cleaning of a wound, repair of a tendon, a nerve, or a vessel, and removal of an embedded object all belong to definitive hospital care and are never field procedures. The lay first aider may control bleeding, cover the wound, immobilize the part, keep the patient warm, and arrange transport, and that is the whole of the field task. The trained responder or paramedic may add wound cleaning that the protocol allows, splinting, pain relief under authorization, and a report that names the structures that may be involved. Where a limb has lost its pulse, where movement has been lost, where the skin beyond the wound is pale or blue, or where pain is severe and growing, the transfer to a trauma center is urgent rather than routine.

Pain in these wounds is treated as a finding rather than as a complaint, because pain out of proportion to the wound may be the first sign of a deep infection or of a compartment syndrome. The lay first aider offers comfort, position, quiet, and warmth, and does not give any medicine. The trained responder or paramedic may give analgesia only where the protocol authorizes it and only after the assessment has recorded what was found, because pain relief that hides a worsening limb delays the operation that saves it. The substance, the amount, the route, and the interval belong to the prescriber and to the local protocol, and this chapter names the decision rather than the dose. Bleeding from these wounds follows the same rules as any other bleeding: direct pressure is applied to the skin around an embedded object and never to the object, and a tourniquet is used only where the protocol authorizes it and only with the time of application written on the patient where the receiving team will see it. A tourniquet is not loosened once it has controlled the bleeding, because loosening releases the clot and the pressure holding the vessel closed, and every patient with a bite, a puncture, an embedded object, or blood loss is kept warm, since cold impairs clotting, worsens shock, and makes the injured heart work harder.

The handover for these wounds is short and it decides much of what happens next, so it is prepared while the patient is being moved rather than at the door of the receiving unit. The report names the mechanism of injury, the animal or the object where it is known, the time of the injury, the time the dressing or the tourniquet was applied, and the treatment already given. It names the findings that point to deep injury, which are the loss of movement, the loss of sensation, the loss of a pulse, the pain that is growing, and any bleeding that continues despite pressure. It names the patient's vaccination history where that is known, the allergies, the illnesses that weaken the immune defense, and whether the patient is pregnant, because each of those changes the decisions made in the hospital. It names the object still in the wound, its material, its visible size, and the direction it entered, and it says plainly that the object was stabilized rather than removed, so every measured figure in that report is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. Definitive hospital care then begins with the same priorities as the field, which are the airway, the breathing, and the circulation, and the surgeon examines the wound for damage beneath it, tests movement, sensation, and pulses, and decides whether it must be explored in an operating theatre.

Where a foreign body is suspected, an image is chosen for the material, with a plain radiograph for metal and glass, an ultrasound for the soft tissue, a computed tomography, after which CT is allowed, for the deeper planes, and a magnetic resonance imaging, after which MRI is allowed, where soft tissue detail is needed and the object is safe in a magnetic field. Where the wound has entered a cavity, imaging of the chest or the abdomen and consultation with the appropriate surgeon follow, and the patient is watched for the hemorrhage, the air, or the infection that the track may have created. Operative care includes exploration under vision, control of bleeding that the field could not reach, removal of dead tissue, removal of the embedded object, repair of the structures that were divided, and closure only when the wound is clean enough that closure will not trap infection, and all of it is performed under anesthesia. Antibiotics, tetanus protection, and analgesia are decided here by the clinician who holds the protocol, and a patient who remains unstable after the first operation is cared for in an intensive care unit, after which ICU is the short form. A pediatric patient is smaller, so the same bite covers a larger part of the body and carries a greater risk of blood loss, an older patient may have thin skin and a reduced immune defense, a patient whose spleen has been removed or whose immune defense is weakened faces a higher risk of overwhelming infection, and a pregnant patient is assessed with attention to the circulation and to the fetus, while a patient who cannot give a history is treated on the findings rather than on the silence.

Several errors recur in the care of these wounds and each has a reason that makes it worth remembering. The first is probing a puncture to see how deep it goes, which introduces organisms and can open a track that was closed. The second is pulling out an embedded object because it looks loose, which can release bleeding that cannot be stopped in the field. The third is closing a bite, which traps organisms beneath the skin and turns a contaminated wound into an abscess. The fourth is judging a wound by its size, which fails for the human bite, the puncture through a shoe, and the bite in a patient whose immune defense is weak. The fifth is forgetting tetanus and rabies until the patient has left, which loses the time in which the decisions must be made. The responder must also consider the exposure the responder may have suffered, because a bite that broke the skin, a needle that pierced a glove, or blood that reached the eyes or the mouth must be reported at once and the wound washed with soap and water.

A constructed illustration may make the sequence concrete, and it is a teaching example rather than a report of a real patient or a real incident. Imagine a patient who steps on a nail that passes through a rubber sole, walks on it, bleeds very little, and seems to need only a dressing, so the trained responder cleans and covers the wound, records that the nail came through the shoe, and sends the patient to be seen where the deep structures can be examined. At the hospital the surgeon finds that the track reaches the flexor tendon sheath, and the early recognition of the mechanism, rather than the size of the wound, is what made the patient arrive in time. No detail in that illustration belongs to any real person, and no outcome described here may be expected to repeat itself.

To summarize, the special wounds are special because of what they hide and what they carry, not because of how they look. Scene safety comes first, the lethal possibilities are hemorrhage, deep structure injury, and infection, and the biting animal or the violent person may still be present when the responder arrives. Animal bites and human bites are contaminated and often deeper than they appear, puncture wounds must be judged by the structures they can reach rather than by their opening, and foreign bodies must be assessed by material, location, and what they touch. An embedded object is stabilized in the position found and is never removed in the field, because removal may release hemorrhage that cannot be controlled or damage tissue further, and the object travels with the patient. Tetanus, rabies, and antibiotic decisions belong to the trained responder, the paramedic, the nurse, the physician, or the surgeon who holds the protocol, and this chapter names the decision while the substance and the amount belong to the local protocol and the prescriber. Every figure in this chapter, whether a time, a temperature, a distance, a depth, or a rate, is an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, and this material is educational and does not replace training, certification, supervision, or the law of the country where you work.
