# Chapter 11 — Immediate Management of Gunshot Wounds

A gunshot wound is the injury in which the first minutes decide the most, and in which the strongest instinct, which is to run straight to the patient, is the instinct that must be resisted. The scene is made safe before anything else, and the hazard particular to a firearm is a second shot, because the person who fired may still be present, may still be armed, and may not have finished. The lethal possibility is named before any wound is examined, and it is hemorrhage that will not stop, whose finding is blood that soaks through dressing after dressing, blood that spurts with each beat of the heart, or a swelling in a limb or a buttock that grows while the responder watches. The lay first aider who arrives first can control that bleeding with a gloved hand, a clean cloth, and steady pressure, while the trained responder and the paramedic bring wound packing, a tourniquet, oxygen, and a protocol that authorizes them. Definitive hospital care brings the surgeon, the imaging, and the operating theater, and it is the destination of every action taken at the scene. Nothing in this chapter replaces training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country where you work, and calling emergency medical services early is itself a clinical act.

Safety at a shooting is not a formality, and the responder who kneels beside the patient while the threat is unresolved may become the next patient. Cover is taken before contact, the patient is approached only after the threat has been controlled by those whose task that is, and a patient moved out of the line of fire is moved under direction rather than on personal initiative. When the shooting happened on a road, as happens in a drive-by incident or after a pursuit, traffic becomes the second hazard, and reflective clothing, a warning triangle, and a vehicle placed to shield the working area protect more lives than any dressing. The patient's own blood and body fluids are a hazard as well, so personal protective equipment, or PPE, with gloves, eye protection, and a barrier for the mouth and nose where it is available, goes on before hands are laid on anyone. Bystanders are given a useful task or moved away, because a crowd hides the scene, blocks the ambulance, and may contain the assailant. The scene is declared safe by the person responsible for it, and only then does the sequence of care begin.

Attention at the scene goes to the wounds that bleed, not to the wounds that look worst, and that reversal of natural sympathy is the central skill of this chapter. A small puncture in the groin or the thigh may have divided an artery and may be filling the leg with blood, while a shattered jaw or a torn scalp can look appalling and lose almost nothing. The first action is direct pressure, applied with the flat of the hand over a dressing or any clean cloth, held firmly and without interruption, and the reason is that pressure from outside the limb exceeds the pressure inside the vessel. If blood strikes through the pad, the pad is not lifted to see what is beneath it, because lifting removes the clot that has begun to form, and a second pad is laid over the first while the hand returns to the same place with the same force. Packing a deep wound belongs to the trained responder for sites where a hand cannot compress the vessel, such as the groin, the armpit, the buttock, or the root of the limb, and the packing is held with continuous pressure rather than released to check whether it has worked. A tourniquet belongs to the trained responder within the protocol of the service, it is applied when pressure and packing have failed or cannot be performed, and once it has controlled the bleeding it is not loosened in the field.

The moment a tourniquet is applied is recorded on the patient where the receiving team cannot miss it, and the reason is that the team must know how long the limb has been without flow in order to judge the risk of release and the urgency of restoring circulation. A period such as 2 hours is often quoted as the point at which the risk to nerve and muscle becomes serious, and that interval must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because tolerance changes with temperature, with the patient, and with the injury. Reassessment follows every attempt at control, and it asks whether bleeding has truly stopped, whether the dressings are still doing their work, whether a limb beyond a tourniquet is changing, and whether a wound was missed while attention was fixed on the first one. A patient who is pale, restless, thirsty, and breathing quickly is telling the responder that the bleeding is not controlled, however dry the dressing looks. A tourniquet is painful, and pain is expected, but pain is never a reason to loosen it in the field, because the bleeding it has stopped may return where no field measure can restore control. The tourniquet is released only in definitive hospital care, where preparation for renewed hemorrhage exists, and a field release is never justified by the appearance of a limb.

The number of wounds is a poor guide to the danger, and the search is not finished until the whole body has been examined. A bullet that entered the chest may have left through the back, a wound in the flank may have crossed the abdomen, and a single projectile may have made several openings as bone fragments were driven outward as secondary projectiles. Every surface is therefore looked at, including the back, the buttocks, the armpits, the scalp, the mouth, and the perineum, because a wound hidden under clothing or hair will be found later by the hospital only if the responder looked first. Turning a patient to see the back belongs to the trained responder and is done with movement precautions for the spine when the mechanism of injury suggests it, with the head and the trunk kept in line as a unit. The wound that is visible is only the visible part of an injury whose depth is unknown, and the structures in its path matter far more than the size of the opening. A calm patient with a small hole and no bleeding to show is not a reassuring patient, and the finding that should worry the responder is any change in the level of consciousness, in the breathing, or in the pulse over the minutes of the assessment.

The airway is the next question once hemorrhage is controlled, and in gunshot wounds it is threatened by blood, vomit, broken teeth, and a swollen tongue rather than by the injury alone. A wound of the face, the mouth, or the neck can fill the throat with blood, and a wound of the neck can produce a swelling that closes the airway from outside as it expands, with a hoarse voice, a difficulty in swallowing, or air that bubbles from the wound as the findings that announce it. The lay first aider keeps the airway open by positioning, by turning the patient onto the side when breathing is adequate and no spinal injury is suspected, and by clearing loose debris from the mouth with a finger only when it can be seen and reached safely. The trained responder, the emergency medical technician or paramedic, may use suction, an airway adjunct, and a jaw thrust, and each of those acts requires equipment, training, and authorization that the lay first aider does not have. A cervical spine precaution must never be allowed to prevent the opening of an airway, because a patient who cannot breathe has no use for an intact neck, and the airway is always the first priority. A patient who is talking has an airway for the moment, and the responder who hears the voice change must treat that change as an emergency and move faster.

Breathing is assessed by looking at the chest, listening at the mouth, and feeling the movement of air, and the most sensitive sign available without equipment is the breathing rate and the effort it costs. A rate that climbs, a patient who can speak only a few words at a time, a chest that does not move on one side, or a wound that sucks and bubbles with each breath are findings that must change the plan at once. The injury that kills fastest in the chest is a tension pneumothorax, and the findings that reveal it are severe breathlessness, absent breath sounds on the affected side, a neck whose veins are distended, and a blood pressure that is falling, with a shift of the windpipe as a late sign that must not be waited for. An open chest wound is covered by the trained responder with an occlusive dressing that is monitored closely, because such a dressing can itself trap air and create the tension it was meant to prevent. A breathing rate above 30 breaths per minute is the sort of figure that is taught as a warning, and that number must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the line that matters shifts with age, with pain, and with the patient's usual state. The chest is never bound tightly by anyone, because a bound chest cannot expand and the patient suffocates inside the bandage.

Circulation is judged by the pulse, the color and the temperature of the skin, the return of color after pressure on a fingernail, and the level of consciousness, and none of those signs excludes hidden bleeding. A patient who has no visible wound of consequence may still be dying into the chest, the abdomen, the pelvis, the thighs, or the space behind the peritoneum, so the search for bleeding never depends on what can be seen. The findings that suggest internal hemorrhage are a pulse that is fast and thin, skin that is pale and cool, restlessness or unusual quietness, thirst, and a level of consciousness that is slipping, and the trend over several minutes carries more information than any single observation. A measured pressure such as 90 millimeters of mercury may be quoted as a threshold, but it is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the line that matters depends on the age of the patient, on pregnancy, and on the patient's usual pressure. The pelvis is treated as a bleeding space rather than as a bone, and a pelvic binder belongs to the trained responder within the protocol of the service, applied at the level of the greater trochanters with the position and the monitoring that the protocol requires. The responder who suspects bleeding into a cavity does not wait to prove it, because proof belongs to imaging in the hospital and the patient belongs in an operating theater.

The neurological assessment is short, and it is repeated more often than any other part of the sequence, because a change in the level of consciousness is the earliest sign of a brain injury that is expanding or of a circulation that is failing. The level of consciousness is recorded in a consistent way, the Glasgow coma scale is the standard spoken scale for that record, and the pupil size and reaction, the movement of each limb, and the symmetry of the face are written down at the same time. The finding that must be taught and remembered is the lucid interval, in which a patient who was speaking and oriented becomes drowsy and then unresponsive, and that interval is the signature of bleeding inside the skull rather than a reason for reassurance. A gunshot wound of the head, the neck, or the back raises the question of spinal injury, and the movement precautions for the spine are maintained without ever preventing the airway from being opened. The record is made on the patient where the receiving team will find it, because a neurological observation spoken once and never written is an observation that will be lost. Every movement, every dressing, and every splint is followed by the same neurological check, and a patient whose examination has changed is a patient whose journey must accelerate.

The sequence of care is one habit performed in the same order every time, and that order is scene safety, catastrophic hemorrhage control, airway, breathing, circulation, neurological assessment, exposure with protection from cold, and transport. Its letters are taught as the ABCDE sequence, and its value lies in the fact that a finding which stops the sequence restarts it, so that a patient who vomits during the examination returns to the airway before anything else continues. Exposure means that the whole patient is looked at, with clothing cut away rather than pulled over a wounded limb, and it ends with the patient covered and warm because the examination itself creates the cold that harms. The sequence is performed by the lay first aider in the parts that belong to bare hands, by the trained responder with equipment and authorization, and again on arrival at the hospital as the definitive team takes over. A patient who looks well is the patient who must be watched hardest, because the body compensates for blood loss for a time and then fails quickly, and the observation that matters is the direction of travel rather than the single reading.

Shock is a state of failing tissue perfusion rather than a number, and it is recognized by the same signs that reveal hidden bleeding, which are a fast and thin pulse, cool and pale skin, a rising breathing rate, and a level of consciousness that is dulling. The lay first aider treats it by keeping the patient flat, keeping the patient warm, controlling any bleeding that can be reached, and withholding food and drink, because either may be vomited into an airway and may complicate the operation that follows. The trained responder brings oxygen according to the protocol of the service, with the amount and the method set by that protocol and never by this manual, and continues the warmth and the monitoring while the vehicle moves. Fluids and blood are decisions of definitive hospital care, made by a physician who can measure what the patient has lost and can watch what the patient does with what is given, and no amount, no route, and no rate belongs in a field manual.

Cold is a complication of gunshot trauma rather than a detail of comfort, and it begins on the ground in the first minutes while the patient is bleeding and undressed for examination. A patient who is bleeding, exposed, or receiving fluid at room temperature loses heat quickly, and cold makes the clotting system work poorly, deepens shock, and makes the heart more likely to develop a dangerous rhythm. A body temperature below 35 degrees Celsius is often used to describe the point at which those effects become serious, and that figure must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the accepted line differs between services and between editions. The lay first aider cuts away wet clothing rather than pulling it over a wound, covers the patient with blankets, coats, or foil, and insulates the patient from the ground, because much of the heat is lost downward. The trained responder continues that protection inside the vehicle, warms the cabin, limits the time the patient is uncovered for each examination, and reports the protection given at handover. Warming runs alongside hemorrhage control rather than after it, and it is continued in the hospital, because a cold patient remains a bleeding patient.

No embedded object is removed in the field, and the rule includes a knife, a fragment of glass, a piece of metal, and a bullet that can be seen sitting in the wound. Removal may release hemorrhage that no field measure can control, and it may cause further damage to vessels, nerves, and tissues that the object is currently tamponading, so the object is stabilized in place with bulky padding built up around it and the patient is moved with it. The wound is not probed with a finger or an instrument, because the depth of a track cannot be judged from its opening and because probing introduces contamination and may start the bleeding that everyone is trying to prevent. A bullet that has come to rest inside the body is not pulled out by a first responder, and the decision to remove it is a surgical decision made in the hospital when the bullet is causing harm where it lies. The information that a projectile is retained is passed to the receiving team, and every wound is described in relation to fixed landmarks, because a description travels and a gesture does not. The lay first aider's task ends with a clean cover and pressure kept off the object itself, and any attempt at extraction is a definitive procedure and never a first-aid measure.

Every wound that is not bleeding dangerously is covered to protect it, to keep it clean, and to allow the patient to be moved, and the covering is a temporary act rather than a treatment. Exposed tissue is protected, including exposed bone, exposed tendon, and exposed bowel, and the last of these is covered with a clean dressing that is kept moist by the trained responder according to the protocol and never pushed back inside the abdomen. A dressing that is soaked is reinforced rather than removed, and a circumferential bandage that tightens with swelling is avoided, because a bandage that becomes a tourniquet without a tourniquet's purpose can cost the limb. The dressing is checked for bleeding through it and for its effect on the circulation beyond it, and the check is repeated often enough that a change is noticed while there is still time to act. Materials that must never be placed in a wound include soil, ashes, powders, and any remedy brought by a bystander, because each adds contamination and none controls bleeding. The lay first aider covers, the trained responder packs and seals within protocol, and the definitive team explores, cleans, and closes, and the three levels are never blended.

Limbs are immobilized to prevent further injury and to control pain, and a gunshot wound of a limb is treated as a fractured limb until the hospital proves otherwise. A deformed limb is splinted in the position found, and it is never straightened to make it look normal, because the ends of a broken bone lie near vessels and nerves and moving them may convert a limb that could be saved into one that cannot. The neurovascular examination, which is the pulse, the color, the warmth, the sensation, and the movement beyond the injury, is recorded before and after every splint, every dressing, and every move, and the second examination is compared with the first rather than with the memory of it. The trained responder splints with the joints above and below the injury supported and every bony prominence padded, and the lay first aider supports the limb in the position found without attempting any realignment. The spine is treated with movement precautions when the mechanism of injury suggests it, which means that the head and the trunk are kept in line and that immobilization follows the protocol of the service. Immobilization is never allowed to delay the transport of a patient who is bleeding internally, because a limb that is protected has no value to a patient who dies of a wound in the abdomen.

Three decisions belong to someone other than the responder at the roadside, and each is described here only as a decision, because a listener must never be able to take a prescription from this manual. The first is analgesia, whose urgency is decided by pain that threatens breathing, by pain that prevents splinting or transport, and by pain that is out of proportion to the wound, while the substance, the amount, the route, and the interval belong to the prescriber and the local protocol. The second is tetanus protection, whose urgency is decided by the wound itself, since a deep, contaminated, devitalized wound from a projectile carries a risk that a simple graze does not, and the decision is made by a clinician who knows the patient's immunization history. The third is antibiotic prophylaxis, whose urgency is decided by contamination, by the cavity of the wound, and by the delay before surgical care, and which is again the decision of the prescriber and the local protocol. The trained responder may recognize all three needs and must report them at handover, and the hospital decides what is given and when. The rule is absolute, in that no dose, no regimen, no route, and no frequency appears anywhere in this manual, and the provider who needs them consults the current guideline and the local protocol.

The hardest decision at the scene is the balance between staying to stabilize and leaving to operate, and the balance is decided by the mechanism of injury, by the distance to the trauma center, by the number of patients, and by the scope of practice of the provider who is present. Where the distance is short and the patient is bleeding internally, the correct action is a fast dressing, a warm blanket, and immediate movement, because the only treatment for that bleeding is an operation that cannot be performed at the roadside. Where the distance is long, the trained responder may gain by doing what the protocol authorizes, and by calling for a faster form of transport where one exists. The time spent on scene is time removed from the operating theater, and a responder who performs a procedure that changes nothing while an artery continues to bleed has spent the patient's reserve rather than saved it. The destination is a trauma center rather than the nearest building, because a hospital without a surgeon, without an operating theater, and without blood is a place where the patient will be stabilized and then transferred again. A transport interval such as 30 minutes is sometimes quoted as the point at which the choice of destination changes, and any such figure must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because systems differ in what they can reach and how quickly.

The receiving hospital is notified before the patient arrives, and the notification is short, specific, and given in the order the receiving team needs it. That same information is what must travel with the patient, so it is written on the patient and spoken aloud at handover rather than trusted to memory. The message names the mechanism of injury, the number and the location of every wound, the observations of breathing, pulse, and level of consciousness together with their trend, the treatment already given and the time each act was performed, and the estimated time of arrival. The time a tourniquet was applied, the warmth and the covering already provided, and any retained projectile are part of that message, because each changes what the receiving team does in the first minutes. A pediatric patient is taken to a center with pediatric capability where the distance allows it, because a child compensates differently and the equipment and the expertise are not the same.

An illustration helps here, and it is constructed for teaching rather than drawn from any real patient or incident. In a training hall, a mannequin is laid beside a wall with a single small wound in the left thigh, painted blood on the trousers, and no other visible injury, and the learners are told only that a shot was heard and that the scene has been declared safe. The first group goes straight to the wound and applies a dressing, and the mannequin's simulated pulse rises and its simulated pressure falls, while the second group turns the mannequin, finds a second wound in the back, controls it, keeps the patient warm, and calls for transport while the first group is still dressing the thigh. The debrief names the traps, which are the search that stopped at the first hole, the reassurance taken from a small opening, the pressure that was released to look, and the time spent on a wound that was not the one killing the patient. The case is invented, and no real patient, family, or service is represented by it.

To summarize, a gunshot wound is managed in a fixed order that begins with scene safety against a second shot and against traffic, moves to the control of catastrophic hemorrhage, and then follows the ABCDE sequence through airway, breathing, circulation, and neurological assessment to exposure, warmth, and rapid transport to a trauma center. The wounds that receive attention first are the ones that bleed rather than the ones that look worst, because the size of an opening says nothing about the structures inside. Every act is named with the provider level that owns it, and no bullet is extracted, no wound is probed, and no fracture is straightened outside definitive hospital care. The balance between staying to stabilize and leaving to operate is decided by the mechanism of injury, the distance to the trauma center, and the scope of practice of the provider, and the time spent on scene is time taken from the operating theater. Every figure in this chapter, whether a pressure, a rate, a temperature, or an interval, is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such numbers differ between services, countries, and editions. The information that travels with the patient is part of the treatment, since a tourniquet time and a trend in the level of consciousness can change what the receiving team does in the first minutes. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country where you work.
