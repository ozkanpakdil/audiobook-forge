# Chapter 33 — Compartment Syndrome

A compartment syndrome is the strangling of muscle and nerve inside a limb whose outer wrapping cannot stretch, and it decides within hours whether a patient keeps an arm or a leg. The limb is divided by sheets of tough tissue called fascia into closed spaces, and each space holds muscle, nerve, and the vessels that feed them. When blood or fluid collects in a space, or when injured tissue swells within it, the fascia holds the volume fixed while the pressure climbs and the tissue that depends on that pressure begins to starve. The danger is not always a wound that can be seen, because a patient may have no break in the skin and still lose the limb. The first responder's task is to suspect the condition early, to remove what constricts, to keep the limb in the position found, to keep the patient warm, and to reach surgical care without delay. This chapter explains the mechanism, the findings, the causes, the referral, and the operation, and it is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

No hand is laid on the patient until the scene has been made safe, because a responder struck while reaching a struck patient turns one casualty into two. Traffic, fire and smoke, live electrical cables, machinery that may restart, deep water, collapsing structures, loose animals, violence, and the patient's own blood and tissue are the hazards that kill rescuers. A limb pinned under a vehicle, a wall, or a machine is not freed by a bystander, because lifting the weight may crush the rescuer, may release a clot, and may let the trapped limb bleed or swell when the pressure comes off. The weight is lifted by a team with the training, the equipment, and the authority to do it, and the machine is isolated and locked before anyone reaches beneath it. Only when the scene is held does clinical work begin, the lay first aider who cannot make it safe stays at its edge and calls for those who can, and gloves or whatever barrier is available are worn before the first contact.

Fascia is the strong sheet of fibrous tissue that wraps the muscles of a limb and divides them into compartments, keeping the muscles in place and making their pull efficient. The lower leg contains several compartments, the forearm contains several, and the thigh, the upper arm, the hand, and the foot each have their own, separated by walls of the same unyielding tissue. Each compartment is a closed box whose only openings are the small passages for the vessels and nerves that enter and leave it. A normal compartment stretches a little for the swelling of exercise, but not enough for the blood and edema that follow a fracture, a crush, or a torn vessel. When the contents increase and the walls cannot give, the pressure inside must rise, and the finding that explains the rest of this chapter is the compartment that has become tense, swollen, and tender.

Blood reaches the muscle of a compartment because the pressure inside the small arteries is higher than the pressure of the tissue around them, so flow depends on that difference rather than on arterial pressure alone. As tissue pressure rises, the difference narrows, the small vessels are compressed, and the muscle receives less blood even though the heart is beating normally. The veins that drain the compartment are soft and are compressed first, so blood enters more easily than it leaves, which adds to the swelling and raises the pressure further. The artery that enters is tougher than the vein and resists compression longer, which is why a pulse can still be felt at the wrist or the ankle while the muscle inside is already dying. The teaching that follows is the most important sentence in this chapter, which is that a pulse that is present does not exclude a compartment syndrome, so the responder who waits for the pulse to disappear is waiting for the limb to be lost.

Nerve tissue is the most sensitive to the loss of blood supply and fails before the muscle around it, which is why a change in sensation often appears while the limb still looks almost normal. Muscle follows, and the time it can survive without flow is measured in hours rather than days, with an interval such as 4 to 6 hours sometimes quoted for the beginning of irreversible damage, offered here only as an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because tolerance differs with temperature, with age, and with the injury. When the pressure is finally relieved, blood returns to tissue that may already be dead and carries into the circulation the contents of the dying muscle cells. That washout can poison the patient even as it appears to rescue the limb, because the substances released may injure the kidneys, disturb the rhythm of the heart, and deepen shock, which is the territory of crush syndrome. Definitive hospital care watches for those effects with blood tests, with the color and volume of the urine, and with attention to the heart, while recognition in the field remains the first link in that chain.

The causes are the events that add volume to a closed space or squeeze it from outside, and the first of them is a fracture. A break of the tibia, of the forearm bones, or of any long bone can bleed into the compartment and swell enough to raise the pressure, and the fracture does not have to be open or dramatic. A crush injury, a wheel that has passed over a limb, a heavy object that has rested on it, or a machine that has caught it produces the same effect through torn muscle and torn vessels. A cast, a circumferential bandage, a splint strap, or a tight dressing can become the constricting layer itself, especially when it was applied before the swelling reached its full size. Prolonged positioning is the cause most easily forgotten, because an unconscious patient lying on an arm or a patient pinned in a vehicle compresses the limb for hours. A burn that hardens into a tight eschar, a torn vessel that bleeds into a compartment, and fluid that has leaked from a vein into the tissue during hospital care complete the list, and each is a reason to look at the limb rather than at the wound.

The patients in whom the diagnosis is most often missed are the ones who cannot describe pain, so the responder carries the suspicion into every examination. A patient with a head injury, a patient who has been drinking, a patient who has received sedation or anesthesia, a frightened child, and a patient whose attention is fixed on a more obvious injury may all fail to report the pain that would give the condition away. A regional nerve block given in hospital for the relief of a fracture can remove the pain while leaving the swelling untouched, so the examination must be made through the other findings rather than through the complaint. A constructed illustration, offered as a construction and never as a real patient or incident, is the person found on a cold floor hours after a fall, confused, with one calf swollen and firm and no wound anywhere on the skin, whose only chance lies in a responder who examines the limb. The finding that should raise the suspicion is any limb that is tense, swollen, or painful when its muscles are stretched, even when the patient says nothing.

The cardinal early symptom is pain that is out of proportion to the injury, and it is heard precisely in the moment when action still changes the outcome. The pain is deep and boring rather than sharp and superficial, it is felt within the limb rather than at the wound, and it often burns or throbs in a way that is hard for the patient to place. It does not settle when the limb is rested, it is not relieved by the splint that has just been applied, and it may be worse with any movement, so the patient cannot find a comfortable position. The pain may be the first complaint and the only one, which is why a patient who reports severe pain after an injury that looks minor must be examined rather than reassured. The absence of severe pain proves nothing in a patient whose sensation has already begun to fail, or in a patient who is unconscious or sedated. The finding that should move the responder from observation to action is pain that is increasing on repeated questioning, because a trend is more informative than any single report.

The most useful bedside test is pain on passive stretch, and the other early finding is the compartment that is tense, swollen, and tender along its whole length. The trained responder moves the muscles of the compartment gently in the direction that lengthens them, moving the foot and toes of a leg, or the wrist and fingers of a forearm, while asking where the pain is felt. The finding that matters is pain reported within the compartment and not in the joint that was just moved, because a painful joint points to the injury while a painful muscle belly points to the pressure. The test is performed gently, an unstable fracture is supported during the movement, and the injured limb is compared with the other side. The responder feels for tension with a flat hand rather than probing fingertips, because force on a compartment under pressure adds injury and teaches nothing. The finding that settles the matter is the combination of a tense compartment with pain on stretch, since either sign alone is common and the pair together is the syndrome.

The earliest objective sign of a failing compartment is often a change in sensation, and it appears before weakness and long before the loss of a pulse. The patient may describe tingling, pins and needles, or a numbness that spreads over the area supplied by the nerve passing through the affected compartment, and the responder maps that area with a light touch and compares it with the other side. Weakness of the muscles inside the compartment follows, tested by asking the patient to perform a movement those muscles produce, so that a foot that cannot be lifted or fingers that cannot be straightened are findings rather than impressions. Weakness may at first be blamed on pain, since a patient who hurts will move less, so the two limbs are compared and the patient is asked to try the movement gently. Sensation and movement are recorded with the area, the movement, and the time, and both are tested before and after every dressing, every splint, every cast, and every move. The finding that demands escalation is any deterioration between two examinations, however small it appears.

The late findings are the signs of a limb that has already been lost, and they are taught not so that the responder may wait for them but so that the responder may recognize how far the condition has gone. A limb that has become pale or mottled, that is cold and pulseless below the injury, that is paralyzed, and that no longer hurts is describing dead tissue rather than an injury that is merely severe. The painless limb is the most deceptive of these findings, because the patient who stops complaining appears to be improving while the nerve and muscle within are dying. Paralysis and numbness together mean the nerve has failed, and a pulse that has disappeared means even the tough artery has at last been compressed, and neither finding can be relied upon to appear in time. The responder who has been taught to look for pallor and pulselessness has been taught to arrive too late, and the purpose of the early findings is to make the late ones unnecessary.

The diagnosis of an acute compartment syndrome is clinical, made from the history and the examination, and it must never wait for a number to confirm it. A device may be used by a physician to measure the pressure inside a compartment, and a value in the range of 30 millimeters of mercury, or a difference between the tissue pressure and the diastolic pressure, is sometimes quoted as a threshold, but such figures must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because accepted values differ between services and editions. The measurement supports a decision that has usually already been made by the hands and the ears, and it cannot make the diagnosis on its own, because the pressure in a compartment changes from minute to minute. A normal measurement does not exclude the syndrome when the patient has the pain, the tension, and the sensory change, and a high measurement without those findings does not by itself send the patient to the operating theatre. The rule that must survive every detail of local practice is that a patient with the clinical findings is treated as having the condition, and that no test, no image, and no number is allowed to delay the referral.

Each provider level has its own part in the recognition of this condition, and the boundary between them is decided by training, equipment, authorization, and protocol rather than by the urgency of the moment. The lay first aider cannot measure pressure and cannot make the diagnosis, but may notice a limb that is swollen and tight, may hear the patient describe severe pain, may see that a foot or hand has changed color, and must pass those observations to the professionals with the time they were made. The lay first aider may also remove a ring, a watch, or a tight bracelet before the swelling makes removal impossible, and may loosen a garment that circles the limb, because those acts require no diagnosis and prevent harm. The trained responder and the paramedic examine the limb in a fixed order, compare it with the other side, record the findings with a time, repeat the examination after every intervention, and are authorized by protocol to release a bandage or splint strap that has become a tourniquet by accident. The decisions about a cast, about the release of a tourniquet, and about the operation belong to definitive hospital care and to the surgeon, and crossing that boundary without authority can convert a limb that was savable into one that is not.

The immediate measures in the field follow an order, and the first is to remove anything that circles the injured limb. Rings, watches, bangles, and tight clothing are taken off before the swelling arrives, and a ring that will not move is never forced over a swollen finger, because the finger may need to be cut free in hospital. The next measure is to examine every bandage, splint strap, and cast for tightness and to release whatever the protocol permits, since the finding that a dressing has become a tourniquet is a limb that is pale or cool beyond it, a pulse that can no longer be felt, and pain that is increasing. The limb is then positioned at the level of the heart rather than raised above it, because elevation lowers the arterial pressure that feeds the compartment, and this is one of the few places in trauma where the instinct to elevate is wrong. The limb is not massaged, pressed hard, or packed with ice, and it is covered lightly rather than bound, because none of those acts lowers the pressure inside the fascia. The patient is kept warm, kept still, and prepared for a possible operation, with the decision about food and drink left to the receiving surgical team.

Cold is a complication of trauma in its own right, and it travels with compartment syndrome because the patient is often trapped, exposed, and undressed for examination. A cold patient clots poorly, tolerates blood loss badly, and is more likely to develop a dangerous rhythm of the heart, and a limb that is already short of blood suffers sooner when the whole body is cold. The responder covers the patient, insulates the patient from the ground, removes wet clothing and replaces it with dry covering without disturbing the limb, and keeps the vehicle warm during transport. A body temperature below 35 degrees Celsius is often named as the point at which these effects become serious, and that figure is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the line differs with age and with the way the temperature is measured. The patient is watched at the same time for shock, since the swelling of a crushed limb can hold a large volume of blood and dying muscle releases substances that disturb the circulation. The findings that send the responder back to the beginning are a falling level of consciousness, a pulse that has become fast and weak, skin that has become cold and clammy, and breathing that has become rapid and shallow.

Pain relief can make this condition easier to endure, and it can also hide it, so the decision belongs to the provider authorized to prescribe and to the local protocol. The finding that makes relief urgent is severe pain not controlled by splinting and positioning, and the finding that makes the decision delicate is the same severe pain that is the earliest sign of the syndrome. The responder records the examination before any relief is given, including the pain, the tension, the sensation, and the movement, so that the effect of the treatment on the findings can be judged rather than guessed. The substance, the amount, the route, and the timing are decisions of the prescriber under the protocol in force, and nothing in this manual names them, because a listener must not be able to take a prescription from these pages. Analgesia must never be used to make a patient quiet enough to be transported without an examination, because a quiet patient is not the same as a safe one.

A cast or a circumferential dressing applied before the limb finished swelling is a common cause of a compartment syndrome, and the patient who reports increasing pain, numbness, or a limb that feels too tight must be seen again at once. Splitting or bivalving a cast, and cutting a bandage down to the skin, belong to a provider authorized by the local protocol or to definitive hospital care, because releasing a constriction in the wrong way can destabilize a fracture and open a wound. Four rules of trauma care matter unusually much in this chapter, and each exists because breaking it can create the very pressure the responder is trying to relieve. A severely deformed fracture is never straightened in the field to make the limb look normal, because the movement can tear a vessel and force blood into a compartment that was intact. A tourniquet that has controlled bleeding from a limb is not loosened in the field, and the time it was applied is written on the patient where the receiving team will see it. An object embedded in a limb is never removed, because it may be plugging the vessel it has torn, so it is stabilized in place and packed around while the patient is moved with it.

The referral is an emergency referral, and it begins as soon as the clinical findings are recognized rather than when the diagnosis is confirmed. The receiving facility is called ahead so that the surgeon and the operating theatre are ready on arrival, and the call names the limb, the time of the injury, the time any cast or tourniquet was applied, and the findings on the examination. The destination is the nearest facility that can open a compartment, which usually means a hospital with an orthopedic or general surgeon and an operating theatre, and if it cannot provide that care, the local system arranges stabilization and transfer. Transport is urgent and gentle at the same time, because speed that throws the patient around adds injury, and because the limb must be examined again before the journey, during it where possible, and at the handover. No image, no laboratory result, and no measurement of pressure is allowed to delay the journey, because the diagnosis is clinical and the treatment is time-dependent. The handover passes on the pain, the tension, the sensation, the movement, the pulses, the warmth, and the times of every intervention, and it is spoken as well as written.

Definitive hospital care takes over on arrival, and its first act is to examine the limb again, because the findings may have changed during the journey. The physician may measure the pressure within the compartments with a device and may order blood tests that reveal the breakdown of muscle and the condition of the kidneys, but those investigations support a decision rather than replace the examination. The treatment of shock, the choice of fluids, the correction of the blood chemistry, and the monitoring of the heart belong to the treating clinician under the local protocol and to the intensive care unit, and the amounts and substances are decisions of that setting rather than instructions carried away from a manual. Fasciotomy is the definitive treatment, an operation performed in an operating theatre by a surgeon, never a procedure for the field and never a task for a first responder. The surgeon cuts through the skin and through the fascia that does not stretch, opening every compartment involved so that the muscle can swell outward, and the wound is left open and covered, to be closed later when the swelling has settled, sometimes with a skin graft. The operation does not repair the fracture and does not restore muscle that has already died, and it cannot bring back a limb in which the tissue has been lost.

Fasciotomy performed outside an operating theatre, by someone without the training and the instruments, risks infection, bleeding, injury to nerves and vessels, and an incomplete release that leaves the pressure in place. A circumferential burn can produce the same emergency through a different layer, because an eschar that has hardened can constrict a limb even when the fascia beneath it is not the cause, and the operation that releases that constriction is an escharotomy rather than a fasciotomy, performed by a burn surgeon. Compartment syndrome can also follow an electrical injury or a prolonged crush in which muscle has been damaged without a break in the skin, and those patients must be watched for the same findings. The consequence of a compartment syndrome recognized too late is measured in limbs and in lives, because muscle that has died is replaced by scar that contracts over the weeks that follow, so that a forearm may stiffen into a fixed deformity and a hand may lose the ability to open. Nerve tissue that has been starved may recover slowly and partially or not at all, and in the most severe cases the limb is amputated, or the patient develops kidney failure, a dangerous rhythm of the heart, or shock, and may die even after the limb has been lost.

Prevention is part of treatment for every responder who applies a dressing, a splint, or a cast, and it rests on a few habits repeated at every stage of care. A bandage is applied firmly enough to control bleeding and loosely enough that the tissue beyond it is not starved, a splint is padded rather than pressed against the skin, and the limb is left at the level of the heart rather than raised on a pillow above it. The patient is asked about pain and sensation after every intervention rather than only at the beginning, and the examiner writes down what was found and when and hands the record to the receiving team in speech as well as in writing. A patient who leaves with a cast is warned in words the patient can repeat to return without delay if the pain increases, if the fingers or toes change color, or if sensation alters.

To summarize, a compartment syndrome is the rise of pressure inside a closed fascial space until the muscle and the nerve within it lose their blood supply, and the fascia that cannot stretch is the reason the pressure has nowhere to go. The early findings are pain out of proportion to the injury, pain when the muscles of the compartment are gently stretched, a compartment that is tense and tender, and a change in sensation, while pallor, pulselessness, paralysis, and a limb that has stopped hurting are the findings of a limb that may already be lost, and a pulse that is still present proves nothing. The diagnosis is clinical and must not wait for a number, and a measurement of pressure supports the decision rather than making it. The causes include fractures, crush injuries, tight casts and dressings, prolonged positioning on a limb, burns, and bleeding within a compartment, and the patients most easily missed are those who cannot report pain. The first response is to remove what circles the limb, to release what the protocol allows to be released, to keep the limb at the level of the heart, to avoid massage, ice, and tight binding, to keep the patient warm, and to move the patient to a surgeon without delay, while a severely deformed fracture is splinted in the position found, a tourniquet is not loosened in the field, and an embedded object is left in place. Fasciotomy is the definitive treatment, performed in an operating theatre by a surgeon, opening every involved compartment and leaving the wound open for later closure, and it is never a field procedure. Every time, pressure, temperature, and interval named in this chapter is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.
