# Chapter 7 — Wound Cleaning and Infection Prevention

Before any wound is examined the scene is made safe, because a responder struck by traffic, shocked by current, burned by fire, or bitten while kneeling becomes a second patient and removes the only help the first patient had. Moving vehicles, unstable structures, spilled fuel, water that carries current, animals, and the violence that caused the injury are settled first, and violence may still be present when help arrives, so the emergency medical services are called early and the responder does not enter until the scene is safe. The patient's own blood and body fluids are a hazard too, so gloves, eye protection, and a barrier over the mouth and nose go on before contact. The lethal possibility to name early is an infection already spreading through tissue, revealed by pain far greater than the wound appears to explain with skin that is tense, dusky, or discolored. Skin that fails to blanch, a crackling sensation in the tissues, and a falling level of consciousness mean a surgical emergency rather than a dirty wound. Hypothermia is prevented from this first moment, because a patient who is bleeding, burned, wet, or lying on a cold surface loses heat quickly, and cold worsens bleeding and infection alike.

A wound is cleaned to reduce the number of organisms and the load of dead tissue and foreign material that would otherwise feed them, and the aim is not a wound that looks clean but a wound that heals. Cleaning does not sterilize a wound and no field method can, so the honest goal is to remove what can safely be removed and to leave the remaining tissue able to defend itself. Contamination means organisms are present, colonization means they multiply without injuring tissue, and infection means they invade and the patient shows the signs of that invasion. The movement from one state to the next is not always visible from outside, so a laboratory result is read together with the wound, the patient, and the clock. Infection begins where organisms find dead tissue, a pool of blood, or a closed cavity in which to multiply, which is why every careful step of cleaning is also a step of prevention.

The first action in cleaning a wound is irrigation, which washes loose contamination and organisms out of the wound instead of driving them deeper. The fluid is delivered as a gentle stream directed across and into the wound, not as a jet aimed straight into the depths, because force can push contamination into tissue planes where it cannot escape. The volume of fluid matters more than the name of the fluid, and clean water that is safe to drink is better than an ideal solution that is not there. The lay first aider may pour clean water over a wound and let it run, the trained responder or paramedic may irrigate with the device the service provides, and irrigation under controlled pressure belongs to definitive hospital care. The pressure at which irrigation should be delivered is described differently by different services, so the figure for the equipment in use must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

Cleaning means lifting contamination away from a wound, and scrubbing means rubbing the surface to detach material that has stuck to it, and confusing the two turns a helpful act into a harmful one. Scrubbing belongs to the intact skin around a wound, where it removes dirt and grease without injuring tissue that must heal, and it does not belong to the wound bed, where it tears the surface and opens raw areas. Some tissue must not be disturbed at all, among it the fine layer over a healing surface, the cut ends of tendons, the sheath of a nerve, the wall of a vessel, the surface of bone, the lining of a joint, and any exposed bowel or organ. Those structures are protected with a clean, moist cover and left for definitive hospital care, because a responder who cleans them can turn a survivable wound into a damaged limb or a contaminated abdomen. The finding that tells the responder to cover rather than continue is the sight of a structure that looks like tendon, nerve, vessel, bone, joint surface, or bowel. Dead tissue and firmly embedded material also stay in place in the field, because their removal is a surgical decision made where bleeding can be controlled.

Sterile technique is a method rather than a supply, and it can be practiced well with modest materials when the method is understood. It begins with hand hygiene, continues with single-use gloves changed when soiled or torn, and depends on never touching the part of a dressing or instrument that will enter the wound. The field is kept as clean as the setting allows, packaging is opened without contaminating its contents, and an item that has touched the ground or the patient's skin is treated as dirty and discarded. Where personal protective equipment, or PPE, is available it is worn for every wound and every body fluid, and where it is scarce the responder protects the eyes, the mouth, and the hands in that order. Needles, blades, and broken glass are handled with a device rather than with fingers, a used sharp goes into a rigid container and is never recapped by hand, and any exposure through a needlestick, a splash, or a bite is reported at once.

The removal of superficial contamination begins with the skin around the wound, which is cleaned outward from the wound edge so that dirt is not carried into it. Visible grit, grass, clothing fragments, and dried blood at the margin are lifted away with clean forceps or clean gloved fingers, and the trained responder or paramedic may do this with the instruments the service provides. The lay first aider who has only hands and water washes the surrounding skin and pours water over the wound, and does not probe inside it. A foreign body that is embedded, that resists gentle traction, or that lies in the depths is stabilized in place and never pulled, because removal in the field may start a hemorrhage that cannot be controlled. Hair around a wound is not shaved, because shaving creates small cuts that become new sites of infection, and hair that obstructs the view is parted or clipped only where a trained provider judges it necessary.

Certain substances must never be poured into a wound, because they injure the tissue that has to heal while leaving the organisms that matter untouched. Strong antiseptic solutions, alcohol, hydrogen peroxide, and concentrated cleaning agents damage the cells of the wound surface, and the choice of any solution belongs to the trained provider and the local protocol. Powders, ointments, leaves, soil, ash, coffee grounds, engine oil, and traditional preparations placed in a wound become foreign material that provokes inflammation and traps organisms, and petroleum jelly or an oily dressing packed into a cavity seals the surface while the depths collect fluid. A wound heals best in a clean, moist environment, so a dressing that keeps the surface moist without soaking the skin supports healing, while a wound left to dry forms a crust under which organisms collect. A cavity closed over a pool of blood gives organisms a protected place to multiply, which is why a wound that becomes tense, swollen, and dark beneath the skin needs surgical assessment rather than a tighter dressing.

Field cleaning reaches only what water can reach, and everything deeper belongs to definitive hospital care. Surgical exploration, extension of a wound, excision of dead tissue, removal of deeply embedded material, and irrigation of the depths under controlled conditions are surgical procedures, and they are not field procedures however contaminated the wound appears. The trained responder or paramedic may clean, irrigate, cover, and immobilize, and may not explore a wound with a finger or an instrument beyond what the protocol permits. The finding that tells the responder the operating room is needed is tissue that is dead, gray, or insensate, a wound that extends into a cavity, a wound over a joint, or a wound whose depth cannot be judged. A wound that reached care many hours after the injury, with the safe interval confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol because services define it differently, needs surgical judgment rather than field cleaning alone, and the patient is taken to the facility able to provide it, which may be a trauma center.

Tetanus is a disease of muscle rigidity and lockjaw caused by a toxin from organisms that live in soil, dust, and manure, and it is prevented rather than treated, because once the spasms begin it is severe and often fatal. Tetanus begins with stiffness of the jaw and neck, difficulty in swallowing, and rigidity of the muscles of the back and abdomen, and it can follow a wound that appeared to heal. The time between injury and the first symptom varies with the wound and the patient, so no interval is offered here, and any figure used for it must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. The decision about protection rests on the wound and on the patient's history, and the history is what the first responder can gather at no cost. The questions are whether the patient has completed a course of vaccination, when the last protection was received, and whether the wound is of the kind that favors the organism, such as a deep puncture, a wound contaminated with soil or manure, a burn, or a bite. The finding that changes the answer is an incomplete or unknown history together with such a wound, and the substance and the timing are chosen by the authorized prescriber or by the protocol that governs the service. A patient whose vaccination status cannot be established is treated as unprotected, and that judgment is stated clearly at handover rather than left in the margin of a note.

Antibiotics given before infection is established are prophylaxis, antibiotics given to treat an established infection are treatment, and the two decisions differ even when the same substance is used. The finding that makes prophylaxis the right answer is usually a wound at high risk, such as a bite from an animal or a human, a wound contaminated with soil, feces, or saliva, a deep puncture, a wound with dead tissue, a wound crossing a joint or a bone, or a wound in a patient whose immune defense is weakened. The finding that makes prophylaxis the wrong answer is a clean, superficial wound in a healthy, vaccinated patient that has been properly cleaned, because antibiotics there add risk without benefit and encourage resistance. Who is authorized to decide is set by law and by the service, so the trained responder or paramedic may follow a standing protocol where one exists, and the prescriber at definitive hospital care decides where no protocol applies. The substance, the amount, and how long it continues are decisions for the prescriber and the local protocol, deliberately not stated here so that no listener can take a prescription from this book. The responder's contribution is to describe the wound, the contamination, the delay, the patient's reported allergies, and the patient's other illnesses, because those facts change the answer, and a patient who already has the signs of infection is a candidate for treatment and surgical assessment rather than for prophylaxis.

Infection announces itself first at the wound, and the earliest signs are easy to dismiss as the ordinary soreness that follows any injury. The wound becomes more painful rather than less, the pain spreads beyond the original margin, the surrounding skin becomes red, warm, and swollen, and the discharge changes from clear fluid to cloudy or foul fluid. The margins may separate, the tissue inside may turn gray or black, and the wound may smell offensive, a finding the patient often reports before anyone inspects it. A wound that was dry and now weeps, a dressing that is soaked sooner than expected, and a dressing that must be changed more often are all signs that the wound is not following the expected course. The lay first aider can see redness, swelling, discharge, and a change in color and can report them, the trained responder or paramedic can clean and inspect the wound and record the findings, and the physician at definitive hospital care decides whether it must be opened, sampled, or taken to the operating room.

The timeline on which infection appears depends on the organism, the wound, and the patient, and it is a guide to watchfulness rather than a schedule to trust. The earliest infections become visible on the first day or the second, when a wound that was quiet becomes painful and red, and that interval is an illustration which must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol because it varies with the organism and the setting. Other infections appear over several days, and some appear after the first week, so a wound that looked well at the first inspection is not finished with being watched. A wound is checked at a fixed interval after closure, examining the same signs each time, and the interval chosen belongs to the protocol of the service and must be confirmed there rather than taken from a book. The patient who leaves care is given clear instructions about which changes must bring a return, among them spreading redness, increasing pain, fever, discharge, and a wound that opens, because a patient told only to watch for infection has not been told anything actionable.

Infection that has moved beyond the wound produces general signs, and those signs are the ones that kill. Fever, shaking chills, a rapid heart rate, rapid breathing, nausea, and rising confusion are the findings of a body fighting a spreading infection, and a temperature such as 38 degrees Celsius is a common illustration of that fever, though the threshold that should raise alarm must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol because it differs with age and with the way the temperature is taken. A patient who is unusually confused, drowsy, difficult to rouse, or whose level of consciousness falls is assessed with the Glasgow coma scale by the trained provider and treated as seriously ill. Cold, clammy skin, a weak and rapid pulse, and a falling pressure are the signs of shock from infection, and that patient needs definitive hospital care at once and possibly the intensive care unit, or ICU. The lay first aider who sees shivering with fever, confusion, or a change in behavior reports it immediately, because such findings are easily attributed to fear or to the injury itself. Warmth, cover, and continuous observation accompany every transfer, since a patient who is losing blood, cold from exposure, or immobilized deteriorates faster when infection is added.

Some wound infections destroy tissue at a speed that field care cannot follow, and they are recognized by their behavior rather than by their appearance at the first look. Pain out of proportion to the wound, skin that is tense and discolored, a bronze or dusky color that advances along a limb, blisters filled with dark fluid, and a crackling sensation under the skin are the findings of an infection spreading through deeper tissue. When muscle is involved the limb may become swollen, firm, and painful, the patient may be agitated or withdrawn, and the general signs of severe illness appear alongside the local ones. This condition and the gas-producing infection of muscle that follows deep contamination are surgical emergencies, and their treatment is surgical removal of dead tissue at definitive hospital care together with the medicines chosen by the treating team. No field cleaning, dressing, or medicine outside the hospital changes this course, and the exact interval within which such a limb must reach surgery must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because it differs between services.

The decision to send a wound for surgical evaluation is made on features the responder can see and describe, and it does not require a diagnosis. A wound that extends into a cavity, over a joint, across the line of a tendon or a nerve, or one that contains a retained foreign body, dead tissue, or continued bleeding is an indication for definitive hospital care. A wound of the hand, the foot, the face, the genital area, or the eye deserves surgical review even when it looks small, because structures there are packed closely and the consequences of loss are great. A wound whose depth cannot be judged, whose contamination cannot be seen, or that comes with a fracture, a dislocation, or lost sensation also belongs to surgical care. Imaging such as a plain radiograph, an ultrasound, a computed tomography, or CT, or a magnetic resonance imaging, or MRI, may be used at the hospital to find a foreign body or to define the path of a wound, and each of those decisions belongs to the treating team. The finding that suggests a wound may wait is a clean, superficial wound in a healthy patient with intact sensation, intact movement, and no foreign material, and even then the wound is documented and reviewed.

Certain wounds are dangerous precisely because they look minor, and a responder who judges by size will be misled. A small puncture from a nail, a thorn, a splinter, or a tooth may carry organisms into a joint, a tendon sheath, or the deep tissue of the foot while the surface closes over the contamination. A bite from a human being carries the organisms of the mouth into a crushed wound, and it is treated more seriously than its size suggests. A wound in a patient with diabetes, poor circulation, a weakened immune defense, or an implanted joint or heart valve may become severe while the patient feels little, because the warning pain and redness are muted. The finding that turns a minor wound into a serious one is anything that delays care, such as a patient who lives far away, who is alone, who is confused, or who cannot return, and each of those findings argues for treatment now rather than advice to watch.

When a patient arrives with a wound that is already infected, the task changes from prevention to recognition and referral. The wound may be swollen, red, hot, and discharging pus, the patient may have a fever and feel generally unwell, and the limb may be painful to move. The finding that makes the situation urgent is skin that is advancing, tissue that is gray or black, or discharge that is foul and thin, because those features suggest infection in the deeper tissue rather than on the surface. The first responder covers the wound with a clean dressing, does not squeeze it, does not open it, applies no heat and no poultice, and moves the patient to definitive hospital care. Definitive care decides whether the wound is opened, sampled, or taken to the operating room, and the medicines used there belong to the treating team and the local protocol.

The patient's own condition decides much of the outcome, and it is part of the assessment rather than a background detail. Poor blood supply, diabetes, a weakened immune defense, malnutrition, smoking, and medicines that suppress inflammation all slow healing and raise the risk of infection, and an older patient, an infant, or a pregnant patient deserves early review because each has less reserve. A patient who is anemic, who has lost blood, or who is in shock carries less oxygen to the wound, so warmth, rest, and the relief of pain improve the flow of blood while cold and agitation work against it. Pain control is a decision for the trained responder or paramedic under protocol and for the prescriber at definitive care, and no substance, amount, or timing is given here, so the lay first aider keeps the patient warm, still, and reassured and gives no medicine at all. The finding that makes the wound urgent in any of these patients is a wound that would be minor in a healthy adult, and the safe interval for review in each group must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. A patient who is shivering or whose skin is cold is warmed before and during transport, because a cold patient bleeds more, resists infection less, and suffers more pain.

What is written and what is said at handover decides how the wound is followed, and the record is part of the treatment. The record names the time of injury, the time of first care, the mechanism of injury, the environment of the injury, what the wound looked like when first seen, what was removed, what was left in place, what was used to clean and cover it, and whether tetanus protection was discussed. It also names the patient's reported allergies, other illnesses, medicines, and vaccination history, because each of those changes the decisions made later. The finding that must never be omitted is a wound left with an embedded object, dead tissue, or a covered structure such as tendon, nerve, vessel, or bone, because the next team must know before the dressing is removed. A dressing that is soaked, shifted, loose, foul, or painful under its edges has failed and is replaced by the trained responder or paramedic using the same clean method, while bleeding that restarts, swelling that increases, a limb that becomes tight or numb, or a patient who becomes more confused calls for immediate reassessment and faster transport.

At definitive hospital care the wound is assessed where the light is good, the instruments are sterile, and the patient can be kept warm and monitored. The surgeon or clinician in charge may extend the wound to see its depths, remove dead tissue and foreign material, irrigate the whole wound, sample tissue or fluid for the laboratory, and decide whether the wound is left open or closed. The care of an established infection may include surgical removal of dead tissue, drainage of a collection, and medicines chosen by the treating team according to the organism and the patient. Nothing in this chapter authorizes a field provider to perform surgical exploration, to remove an embedded object, to open a joint, or to give a medicine, and those boundaries are stated so that a listener knows what competence forbids as well as what it permits. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, the local protocol, or the law of the country in which the responder works, and any wound described here is a constructed illustration rather than a real patient or a real incident.

To summarize, a wound is cleaned to reduce organisms and dead material so that the tissue can defend itself, and cleaning is never the same as sterilizing. Scene safety, protection from body fluids, and the early naming of a spreading infection come before any wound is touched, and the finding that reveals that lethal infection is pain out of proportion to the wound with tense, discolored skin and a falling level of consciousness. Irrigation with clean fluid is the first cleaning action, the wound bed is never scrubbed, and tendon, nerve, vessel, bone, joint, and bowel are covered and left undisturbed. Sterile technique is a method of clean hands, clean gloves, clean fields, and untouched wound surfaces, and its value lies in how it is performed rather than in what it is called. Tetanus protection and antibiotic prophylaxis are decisions made by the authorized prescriber under the local protocol, and the responder's part is to gather the history, describe the wound, and name the findings that change the answer. Infection is watched for at the wound and in the patient on a timeline that varies, and the wounds that look minor, such as punctures, bites, and wounds in patients with diabetes or poor circulation, most often surprise the unwary. The patient is kept warm, moved to definitive hospital care when the wound needs surgery, and given a plan for review in words that can be repeated, while every number in this chapter remains an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.
