# Chapter 2 — The Primary Survey

The primary survey is a single habit performed the same way every time by everyone who touches an injured patient, so that what kills first is found first. It is called ABCDE, and each letter is a station that must be cleared before the next is entered. The survey is not a complete examination of the injury; it is a search for the conditions that will end the patient's life in the next few moments, and it is blind to everything that can wait. A lay first aider performs it with bare hands, a trained first responder or paramedic performs the same sequence with a kit and an authorization, and the physician or trauma surgeon repeats it in definitive hospital care with tools the field does not carry. This chapter is educational material, and it does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol or the law of the country in which you work.

Before any letter is spoken, the scene must be made safe, because a rescuer struck by traffic, burned by fire, electrocuted, attacked by an assailant or exposed to contaminated body fluids becomes a second patient and takes away the help the first one needed. Personal protective equipment, or PPE, is used as far as it is available, and the emergency medical services are called before the patient is touched whenever there are hands enough to do both. A patient who is silent, snoring or gurgling may have an airway that is already closing. A patient who is pale, restless and lying in a pool of blood may be losing blood faster than any hand can replace it. Cold is a complication of trauma and not a detail of comfort, so the patient is covered, insulated from the ground and shielded from wind and rain from the first moment, whatever the injury.

The order of the letters is not negotiable, because an airway kills before breathing and breathing kills before circulation, and each station prevents the death that the next would reach too late. The rule that holds the survey together must be said aloud: any finding that stops the sequence restarts it at the beginning, from the airway, because an act at one station can undo the work of another. A patient who vomits while the chest is examined has an airway problem again, and a patient whose bleeding is controlled but who then becomes unresponsive has a breathing problem and a disability problem again. The sequence loops as many times as the patient demands, and each station answers one question that makes the next possible.

The airway is the first station because an unconscious patient cannot protect it, and the most common obstruction in trauma is the patient's own tongue, which falls back against the throat when the muscles of the jaw lose their tone. The finding is a patient who is not moving air quietly, who snores, who gurgles, or whose chest and abdomen move in opposite directions as the patient fights for a breath. The first action belongs to anyone present, including the lay first aider, and it is position: the head is tilted back gently and the chin is lifted, which raises the tongue away from the back of the throat. When an injury of the neck is possible, a trained first responder or paramedic holds the head steady in the position found while the jaw is lifted forward without tilting the neck, so the spine is protected and the airway is still opened. A cervical collar never takes priority over an airway and is never applied so tightly that it presses on the throat. Quiet air moving in and out with the chest rising on both sides is the finding that lets the responder move on.

Foreign material is the next question, asked in the same order every time: blood, vomit, loose teeth, broken dentures, and whatever the patient was carrying in the mouth when the injury happened. The lay first aider may remove material that is visible and within reach, using a hooked finger along the inside of the cheek, but never a blind sweep of the throat, because a finger that pushes material deeper turns a partial obstruction into a complete one. Suction, forceps and a laryngoscope belong to the trained responder or paramedic acting under a protocol, and the decision to use them rests on material that can be seen but not reached. The recovery position is the next tool and the one that most often saves an unconscious patient who is breathing but cannot protect the airway, and the lay first aider rolls the patient onto one side with the upper knee brought forward and the head resting slightly downward, so blood and vomit run out of the mouth instead of back into the lungs. A pregnant patient is turned onto the left side where possible, because the weight of the womb on the great vessels can reduce the blood returning to the heart, and a patient with a suspected injury of the spine is kept in the position found by the lay first aider while the trained responder or paramedic turns the body as one movement. Any sound of obstruction, any pause in breathing or any vomit appearing in the mouth forces a change of position and a return to the start of the survey.

Advanced airway management is not a first-aid measure at any level, and it belongs to the paramedic working under a protocol and to definitive hospital care. Placing a tube in the windpipe, cutting the front of the neck to create a passage, and using a mechanical device to push air into the lungs require training, equipment and authorization, and a manual that describes them as first aid teaches a dangerous habit. For the lay first aider the correct acts remain the head tilt, the chin lift, the jaw thrust and the recovery position, and the correct act when these fail is to call for help without delay. Children are not small adults at this station, because a child's airway is narrower, the tongue is proportionally larger and the head is proportionally heavier, so the neck is not tilted as far back.

Breathing is the second station because injuries of the chest kill here, and the two signs that carry the most information without equipment are the respiratory rate and the work of breathing. Effort is read by the responder from the shape of the patient: the muscles of the neck standing out, the spaces between the ribs drawing in, the nostrils flaring, the sentence that cannot be finished, the tripod position with the arms braced, and the grunt on each breath. A respiratory rate above 30 breaths per minute should be treated as an alarm, and the exact line that turns a rate into an emergency is fixed by the current guideline and the local protocol rather than by this page. A rate below 8 breaths per minute or above 30 in an adult warns that breathing may be failing, and both figures are illustrations whose values shift between services, countries and editions, so they must be confirmed against the guideline in force and against local protocol where you work. A quiet, effortless, symmetric breath at a rate the patient tolerates lets the responder move on, and that same breath is rechecked after every intervention and every move, because breathing changes fastest.

Oxygenation is judged at the bedside by the color of the lips, the tongue and the nail beds and by the level of consciousness, because a brain short of oxygen becomes restless before it becomes quiet. A probe placed on the finger to measure the oxygen saturation belongs to the trained responder or paramedic, and its reading is interpreted with the patient present, since cold hands, nail polish and poor circulation can mislead it. The decision to give oxygen belongs to the trained responder or paramedic acting under authorization and local protocol, and the substance, the amount and the way it is delivered are set by that protocol and that prescriber. The lay first aider does not administer oxygen but keeps the airway open, keeps the patient warm and keeps the patient still. A patient who is blue, confused, breathless at rest or unable to finish a sentence makes oxygen urgent, and a patient whose color and awareness have improved lets the responder move on.

Several injuries of the chest kill quickly, and each announces itself with a finding that can be sought before any equipment arrives. A patient whose chest does not move on one side, whose neck veins are swollen and whose pulse is fading may have air trapped under pressure around a lung, a tension pneumothorax, and that patient needs decompression by a paramedic under protocol or in definitive hospital care, never by a lay first aider. A patient with a wound of the chest that makes a sucking or bubbling sound may have an open chest wound, and the first action belongs to the trained responder, who covers the wound so that air cannot enter while the patient breathes in, without sealing it so tightly that trapped air cannot escape. A patient with a large collection of blood around a lung becomes pale and breathless, and that bleeding is internal and can only be treated in definitive hospital care. A patient whose chest moves in one part while the rest moves the opposite way has a flail chest, and a simple rib fracture is not a minor injury, because a broken rib may have injured what lies beneath it. Tight binding of the chest is never done at any provider level, because it reduces the depth of every breath and invites infection of the lung, and a chest moving on both sides with quiet air lets the responder continue.

Recognizing respiratory distress before failure is the skill that separates a responder who waits for the arrest from one who prevents it. The early signs are restlessness, anxiety, a rising rate, a rising effort, and a patient who wants to sit up and forward. The late signs are the opposite of noise: a rate that begins to fall, a chest that moves less, a patient who becomes drowsy and quiet, and a chest in which air is barely moving at all, and that quiet is the most dangerous moment in the survey because it looks like improvement and is in fact exhaustion. A needle or tube placed into the chest to release trapped air is a paramedic or hospital procedure performed under protocol, and a patient whose effort is falling while the rate and the color remain normal must travel toward definitive care without delay.

Circulation begins with the search for major external hemorrhage, because blood leaving the body is the fastest killer in trauma and the one most often missed. The findings are blood pooling under the patient, clothing dark and heavy with blood, a stump from an amputation, a limb spurting in time with the heart, or a wound that keeps filling no matter how many dressings are placed on it. The first action, owned by the lay first aider, is firm direct pressure with whatever clean material is at hand, held without lifting to look. A tourniquet belongs to the trained responder or paramedic, and it is used when pressure cannot control bleeding from a limb, when a limb is mangled, or when the patient must be moved before control can be achieved. Once a tourniquet has controlled the bleeding it is not loosened in the field, and the time it was applied is written on the patient where the receiving team will see it, because the length of time the limb has been without blood changes what the surgeon can do with it. Packing a deep wound with gauze and holding pressure inside it is a trained responder or paramedic act performed under protocol, and the lay first aider is not asked to reach into a wound. Bleeding that has stopped under the dressing lets the responder move on, and a dressing that keeps soaking through holds the responder at this station.

Shock is the state in which the circulation can no longer deliver enough blood to the organs, and it is recognized by a pattern of findings rather than by a single number. The patient becomes restless, anxious or confused before becoming drowsy, the skin turns pale, cool and clammy, and the patient feels thirsty and cold. The pulse is felt at the wrist for its rate and its strength, and a fast, thin pulse is the earliest sign the hand can detect; a pulse above 100 beats per minute in a calm patient is a teaching illustration that changes with age, fitness and pain, so it must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. The pressure is measured by the trained responder or paramedic, and a systolic pressure below 90 millimeters of mercury is a figure quoted only to show how a measurement becomes a decision, one that has to be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol before it is relied on. The combination of a fast pulse, cool skin and a change in behavior makes shock urgent even when the measured pressure still looks acceptable, because the pressure falls late and a normal pressure is not reassurance. Children compensate for a long time before they collapse, and older patients who take medicines for the heart may not develop a fast pulse at all, so the pattern is read against the patient's own baseline. A warm patient with a strong pulse, clear skin color and calm behavior lets the responder move on, and any move away from that picture restarts the search from the airway.

Tissue perfusion can be assessed with the hand alone, and the lay first aider can be taught to do it in the first moments. The fingertips are pressed against the skin of the chest or the knee until the color leaves, and the time for the color to return is counted; refill that takes longer than 3 seconds is a warning, and that number is only an illustration of the reasoning, because the technique and the cut-off are described differently by different services, so it must be verified against the current guideline and the local protocol. The temperature of the hands and feet is compared with the temperature of the trunk, and a line of cold that climbs from the toes toward the knee shows that the body is closing down the outer circulation to protect the center. Warm, dry skin with a brisk return of color and a strong pulse lets the responder move on, and cold makes every one of these signs worse, because warmth is part of the treatment of shock, not a separate concern.

Bleeding that cannot be seen is suspected from the mechanism of injury, and the mechanism is the first piece of evidence the responder collects. A fall from a height, a strike by a vehicle, a crush, a blast or a penetrating wound of the trunk predicts damage inside the abdomen, the pelvis or the thigh, where blood can be lost in large quantity without a drop appearing on the ground. The abdomen is examined by the trained responder or paramedic for tenderness, swelling and guarding, the pelvis is tested once and only once, and a swollen, tense thigh after a fracture can hold a great volume of blood. Fluid given to a bleeding patient belongs to the paramedic or the physician acting under the local protocol, and the substance, the amount and the way it is given are decisions made by that protocol and that prescriber, while nothing is given by mouth to a patient who may need an operation. The lay first aider keeps the patient lying flat unless breathing demands otherwise, keeps the patient warm, does not raise the legs of a patient with a suspected injury of the spine or the pelvis, and treats worsening signs of shock despite control of the visible bleeding as a reason for immediate transport.

Disability is the fourth station, and it asks one question above all others: is the brain receiving enough blood and oxygen, and is the brain itself injured? The level of consciousness is the most useful single measure, and it is described in plain words at the bedside as alert, drowsy but rousable, responding only to voice, responding only to pain, or unresponsive. The Glasgow coma scale puts the same observation into a total, and a scale whose total runs from 3 to 15 is recorded by the trained responder or the physician to follow a patient over time, with the way that total decides urgency set by the current guideline and the local protocol. Any fall in the level of consciousness from the level first found makes this station urgent, because a patient who is becoming less awake is a patient whose airway is about to become the problem again, and every change in consciousness restarts the sequence from the airway. A patient who is fully alert and oriented to place, time and person lets the responder move on, and even that patient is rechecked, because consciousness can fall quickly after an injury of the head.

The pupils are examined with a light, one at a time, and what matters is the size of each pupil compared with the other, the shape, and whether the pupil narrows when light is shone into it. A pupil that is larger than its fellow and does not react to light suggests pressure inside the head, and that finding belongs in the report to the receiving team whether or not the patient looks unwell otherwise. The neurological examination that can be done in the first moments is a short pass for the trained responder: ask the patient to show the teeth and squeeze both hands, to lift each arm and each leg against resistance, and to say a sentence, then look for a side that moves less. A face that droops on one side, a grip that is weak on one side or a limb that does not move at all makes an injury of the head or the spine likely. A patient who is unresponsive is placed in the recovery position by whoever is present, with the neck kept in line, and a patient who is alert, moving every limb equally, speaking clearly and showing equal, reactive pupils lets the responder continue.

Exposure is the fifth station, and it means what it says: the patient is undressed enough to be examined completely, because injuries hide under clothing and the wound that was missed is the wound that kills. Clothing is cut away rather than pulled over a broken limb, and the whole surface of the body is looked at, including the back, the buttocks, the scalp, the armpits, the groin and the soles of the feet. A log roll, in which the patient is turned as a single unit with the head and the trunk kept in line, belongs to the trained responder or paramedic when an injury of the spine is possible, and the lay first aider does not roll a patient alone but keeps the patient still and warm until help arrives. Blood on the clothing that matches no wound already found, a limb that is deformed or shortened, or a wound in a place the patient did not mention makes this station urgent. A body fully examined with every wound found and every source of bleeding accounted for lets the responder move on, and any injury found during exposure restarts the sequence at the airway, because a wound that was hidden may be the one that changes the whole picture.

The moment the patient is exposed is the moment the patient begins to lose heat, and the prevention of hypothermia must follow exposure as closely as a dressing follows a wound. Wet clothing is removed, the patient is covered with blankets or whatever insulating material is at hand, the head and the back are insulated, and the patient is separated from the cold ground and from the wind. A patient who is bleeding, burned, immobilized or simply waiting is a patient who is losing heat, and cold makes blood clot poorly, makes the heart irritable and makes every sign of shock harder to read. Warm fluid belongs to the paramedic or the physician under the local protocol, and the decision to warm a patient actively is made by the trained responder or in definitive hospital care, with the method and the amount set by the current guideline and the local protocol. The aim is to keep the patient warm enough that cold is not adding to the injury, and 35 degrees Celsius is named only as an illustration of the line below which cold itself becomes dangerous, a figure that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

The survey is not finished when the fifth letter has been spoken; it is finished when the patient has been handed over and the findings have been passed on in a form the next team can use. The sequence is repeated from the beginning after every intervention, after every move, and at intervals set by the current guideline and the local protocol, because a patient who was stable at the scene can be dying by the time the ambulance doors close. The handover names the mechanism of injury, the time the injury happened if it is known, the level of consciousness, the airway findings, the breathing rate and effort, the signs of circulation, the wounds found, the acts performed by each provider, and the time any tourniquet was applied. The receiving team includes the nurse, the physician and the trauma surgeon in definitive hospital care, and they repeat the same survey with imaging, including computed tomography, or CT, and ultrasound, and with the operating room when it is needed. A trauma center is a hospital organized to receive these patients at any hour, and the survey has done its work when the patient reaches it alive, with the airway open, the bleeding controlled, the breathing supported, the brain protected and the body warm.

Across all five stations, who does what is answered by the reach of the hands, not by the urgency of the moment. The lay first aider keeps the airway open, counts the breathing, applies direct pressure to bleeding, keeps the patient warm and calls for help, and that list saves more lives than any procedure in this book. The trained first responder, the emergency medical technician or the paramedic adds oxygen under protocol, suction, a tourniquet, wound packing, a pressure dressing, the measurement of the pressure, restriction of spinal movement and transport, within the authorization and equipment of the service. The nurse, the physician and the trauma surgeon in definitive hospital care add decompression of the chest, the tube in the windpipe, the surgical airway, blood and fluid given under protocol, the imaging, the operation and the intensive care unit, or ICU. None of those hospital acts becomes a first-aid measure because the patient is far from a hospital, and a procedure performed without training, equipment and authorization is a second injury waiting to happen. Restraint of a patient, extraction of a bullet, exploration of a wound, reduction of a fracture and repair of a vessel are never field procedures, and a problem that will not yield to the tools at hand tells any provider to stop and call for a higher level of care.

A constructed illustration may fix the sequence in the mind, and it is offered as a scene built for teaching rather than as a real patient or a real incident. A responder arrives at a crash in which a driver is still in the seat, the engine is running and fuel is on the ground; the scene is made safe by switching off the ignition and standing clear of traffic, and only then is the patient approached. The patient is unresponsive, breathing with a snore, pale and cool, with a swollen abdomen and blood on the clothing, and the airway is opened with a chin lift and a jaw thrust that keeps the neck in line until the snore stops. The breathing is fast and shallow with the muscles of the neck working, the chest moves less on one side, the pulse at the wrist is fast and thin and the skin is clammy, so the paramedic dresses a wound hidden under the clothing, measures the pressure and records a low reading. The patient is covered, insulated from the ground and kept warm while the ambulance moves toward a trauma center, the survey is repeated each time the patient is moved, and any figure in this illustration must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

To summarize, the primary survey is one habit performed in the same order every time, and the order is the treatment. The airway is opened by position and protected from foreign material before anything else is done, and the neck is kept in line without closing the passage. Breathing is judged by the rate and the work of breathing, and the injuries of the chest that kill quickly are named at the bedside with the finding that reveals them. Circulation is judged by the bleeding that can be seen and by the pulse, the skin and the perfusion that the hand can feel, and shock is treated as a pattern rather than as a number. Disability is judged by the level of consciousness and by a short neurological pass, and exposure completes the examination and is followed at once by warmth. At every level the provider stays inside the reach of the hands and the authorization of the service, and any finding that stops the sequence restarts it from the airway. Every figure in this chapter, whether a rate, a pressure, a time or a temperature, is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such numbers differ between services, countries and editions, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, supervision or local protocol.
