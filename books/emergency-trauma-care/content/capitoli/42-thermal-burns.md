# Chapter 42 — Thermal Burns

Fire is the only injury in this manual that may still be happening when the responder arrives, so the scene is made safe before anyone is touched. The patient may still be on fire, against a hot surface, or in smoke, and traffic, a fuel tank, a falling wall, and molten material are the other hazards to name. The lethal possibilities are the airway that narrows over the hours that follow and the burn that encircles the chest or a limb. They are revealed by a hoarse voice, singed nasal hair, and a burn running all the way around a part, and this chapter cannot replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of your country.

The burning is stopped before it is cooled, because water poured on skin that is still being heated accomplishes very little. A patient whose clothes are on fire is stopped, laid down, and rolled, and the flames are smothered with a blanket, a coat, or any heavy cloth. The lay first aider may remove loose clothing that is no longer burning, but must leave clothing fused to the skin in place, because pulling it away tears living tissue. The trained responder enters only after the fire service declares the structure stable, and the burning has stopped when there is no flame, glow, or heat near the skin.

A thermal burn is a transfer of heat into tissue, and its depth depends on how hot the source was, how long it touched the skin, and how thick the skin was there. Scalds, flame, contact with a hot object, a flash of heat, and hot gases are the ordinary causes, and each leaves a pattern that says something about the patient's position. A scald that runs in a line speaks of liquid flowing, a burn of the palm speaks of a hand that closed around the source, and a burn of the face and chest speaks of a flash. A constructed illustration, never a real patient, is the cook whose sleeve catches fire at the stove, where the visible forearm burn draws every eye while the smoke she breathed may close her airway.

The superficial burn, once called a first-degree burn, has injured only the epidermis, the thin outer layer of the skin, and it shows redness, dryness, and pain without any blister. The skin blanches when pressed and refills when the pressure is released, the surface is intact, and the deeper structures are unharmed, so it usually heals without a scar. Sunburn is the common example, and it shows how a burn that looks trivial can cover a great area and cost fluid and heat. A large superficial burn is not minor in a small child or an older person, and the finding that should end home care is a burn of the face, hands, feet, or genitals.

The partial-thickness burn has destroyed the epidermis and part of the dermis beneath it, and it is the burn that blisters. The surface is moist and weeping, the color is pink or mottled red, it blanches when pressed, and the pain is often severe because nerve endings in the surviving dermis are exposed. A blister is a sterile roof lifted by fluid, and while it stays intact it is the best dressing the body can make, so the lay first aider does not break it. Healing depends on how much dermis survives and may take one to three weeks, a period to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, while the danger is infection and fluid loss shown by spreading redness or fever.

A deep partial-thickness burn destroys most or all of the dermis, and it looks different because the surface may be pale, waxy, or mottled and may blanch sluggishly. Sensation is often reduced because many nerve endings have been destroyed, so the wound the patient feels least may be the one that will need an operation. A full-thickness burn destroys every layer of the skin and may reach fat, muscle, or bone, and it looks leathery, firm, and white, gray, brown, or charred. It is painless in its center, because no nerves are left, and it cannot heal from its own edges, so it belongs to definitive hospital care, where dead tissue is removed and the wound is grafted.

Depth is difficult to judge at the roadside and often changes in the first days, so the responder should not argue about a label that even the receiving team will revise. The purpose of the field assessment is not to name the burn correctly but to recognize the features that make it major and to move the patient toward the care they demand. A burn is major when it is deep or large, when it involves the face, hands, feet, genitals, perineum, or a joint, when it encircles a part, when it came from an enclosed fire, or when the patient is very young, very old, pregnant, or ill. The finding that should stop the debate is any one of those features, because a responder who cannot decide the depth can still decide the destination.

Extent is estimated with the eye and spoken in words, because no chart can be consulted on scene and a number remembered badly is worse than a proportion understood well. The most portable measure is the patient's own palm, held with the fingers together, taken as about one per cent of the body surface, and that convention must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. The older spoken rule makes the head and neck about one tenth, each arm about one tenth, each leg about one fifth, the trunk about two fifths, and the perineum a small remainder. The proportions change with age, because a small child's head is a larger share of the body, and the estimate is used above all to decide whether the patient must go to a burn center.

A burn of large extent leaks plasma from the circulation into the wound and the tissue around it, and the patient can enter shock without losing blood to the ground. The patient becomes restless, thirsty, pale, and cool, with a pulse that quickens and breathing that rises, and the loss continues for hours after the burning stops. The lay first aider cannot replace that fluid and must not try, keeping the patient warm and still instead, covering the burn, and getting help moving. The trained responder may begin fluid replacement within the protocol of the service, and the choice of fluid, amount, and route belongs to that protocol and the prescribing authority rather than to this manual.

A circumferential burn runs all the way around a part of the body, and it is dangerous not because of its depth but because burned skin can no longer stretch. The eschar that forms is like a band of stiff leather, and beneath it the tissue swells with the fluid of inflammation, so pressure inside the part rises while the band refuses to give. The two emergencies that follow are the burn that encircles a limb and the burn that encircles the chest, and both declare themselves as the swelling grows rather than at the first look. A burn that closes a full circle around an arm or a leg points to the limb, and a burn of the torso that meets itself from front to back points to the chest.

When a limb is encircled, the rising pressure compresses the veins first, then the arteries, then the nerves and muscle beneath the skin, in the same process that produces compartment syndrome. The findings are pain out of proportion, a limb that feels tight and hard, numbness in the fingers or toes, weakness, pallor or a bluish color, coolness beyond the burn, and a pulse at the wrist or foot that becomes hard to feel. The lay first aider does not cut the burn, does not wrap it tightly, and does not try to relieve the pressure by any field method, because the only relief is an incision through the eschar made by a physician or surgeon. What the lay first aider does is remove rings and tight clothing before swelling makes that impossible, keep the limb in the position found, and report the findings as they change.

When the chest is encircled, the band of eschar limits the movement of the chest wall, so the patient cannot take a deep breath and must work harder for every breath. The findings are rapid shallow breathing, visible use of the neck and rib muscles, a chest that does not rise well, restlessness, and a patient who becomes tired and then drowsy. The lay first aider keeps the patient sitting up if that eases breathing, gives nothing by mouth, keeps the patient warm, and does not bind or press on the chest. The relief is an escharotomy, an incision through the burned skin of the chest wall, which is a hospital procedure for a physician or surgeon and never a field act.

Both constrictions develop over hours rather than minutes, because the swelling that drives them grows after the burn, so a burn that looks stable cannot be left unobserved. The period in which swelling is most likely to become dangerous is often given as the first 24 to 48 hours, and that interval must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. The responder returns to the same observations in the same order, looking at breathing, color, warmth, pulse, and pain, rather than treating a first impression as a diagnosis. Any worsening in those observations should send the responder back to the beginning and move the patient on the fastest safe journey to a place where the constriction can be released.

Inhalation injury is suspected from the story of the fire before it is seen in the patient, and the story is as important as the wound. A fire in an enclosed space, an explosion, a volatile fuel, a steam release, or any fire in which the patient was trapped or unconscious should raise the question at once. Heat usually injures the upper airway above the vocal cords, while smoke injures the airway lower down and the lung itself, so a serious inhalation injury can exist with few signs in the mouth. The finding that changes the whole plan is a history of enclosed fire combined with any abnormality of the voice, the breathing, or the level of consciousness.

The findings that predict an airway burn are looked for on the face and listened for in the voice. Singed hairs in the nostrils, soot around the mouth or nose, soot on the lips or tongue, and burns of the face, mouth, or throat are the visible signs. A hoarse or changing voice, a barking cough, noisy breathing, drooling, and trouble swallowing are the signs that are heard, and black sputum or chest tightness points to injury lower down. These findings are progressive, so a clear voice now is no reassurance, and a changing voice or a new noise on breathing should command the fastest journey.

Because the airway narrows as the tissue swells, the definitive treatment is to secure the airway before it closes, and that act belongs to a clinician able to pass a tube into the windpipe. It may be done in hospital or by an advanced prehospital team under protocol, but it is not an act for the lay first aider or an untrained responder. Until then, the lay first aider keeps the patient sitting upright if that eases breathing, gives nothing by mouth, and places no object in the mouth. Oxygen and any drug that might make the patient comfortable belong to the protocol and the prescriber, and the state of the voice and breathing must be passed on at the call and again at the handover.

Smoke poisons as well as burns, and a patient can be dying of the gases of combustion while wearing a burn that looks minor. Carbon monoxide takes the place of oxygen on the red cells of the blood, so the tissues are starved of oxygen even while the patient breathes, and the skin may keep a misleading pink color. Headache, nausea, confusion, drowsiness, and a falling level of consciousness should make the responder suspect this poisoning, especially after an enclosed fire or the burning of fuel. The trained responder may give oxygen within the protocol, and definitive care measures the gases in the blood, while the lay first aider removes the patient from the smoke and keeps the patient warm and still.

Cooling is the field act that actually changes a burn, because it carries heat out of the tissue before that heat can deepen the wound, and it also eases pain and limits swelling. The lay first aider cools a small burn with clean, cool running water poured gently over the area, and does not use ice, ice water, frozen packs, or iced cloths, because cold of that intensity injures the skin further. The trained responder cools a larger burn within the protocol, and the water temperature, the duration, and the method belong to the current guideline and the local protocol. A figure such as 20 minutes of cooling is often named as a teaching illustration and must be confirmed where the responder works, and cooling is stopped when the burn is extensive or the patient is already cold, because hypothermia then becomes the immediate danger.

What is put on a burn matters less than what is kept off it, and the list of things that must not be applied is long and familiar. Butter, oil, toothpaste, herbs, mud, flour, powders, and ice have all been placed on burns, and each holds heat in the wound, introduces infection, or causes further injury. The lay first aider covers the burn with a clean, dry cloth and leaves it alone, and the trained responder applies a dressing intended for burns under the protocol. A blister is left intact where it can be, because its roof is a covering the body has made, and the decision about an antibacterial dressing, tetanus protection, and antibiotics belongs to the prescriber and the local protocol.

Covering keeps contamination out of the wound and keeps the patient's heat in, and both matter when the skin no longer performs those jobs. The covering is laid on loosely and is not wound tightly around a limb or the chest, because a tight band over a burn can become the constriction the burn was threatening. The material is clean and, where available, non-adherent, so it can be lifted later without tearing the wound, and the responder does not pack a burn as a bleeding wound is packed. The finding that the covering is too tight is a change in color, warmth, or pulse beyond it, or pain that grows after the dressing is applied.

Hypothermia is a complication of burns rather than a detail of comfort, and it begins when the skin loses its ability to hold heat and the wound weeps fluid. A patient who is burned, wet, undressed for examination, lying on cold ground, or carried through cold air loses heat quickly, and cold worsens the circulation and deepens shock. The responder removes wet clothing that is not stuck to the skin, replaces it with dry covering, insulates the patient from the ground, covers the head, and warms the vehicle or room. A body temperature below 35 degrees Celsius is often named as the point at which cold becomes dangerous, and that figure, like every figure here, is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

Every act in this chapter belongs to a named level of provider, decided by training, equipment, authorization, and protocol rather than by the urgency of the moment. The lay first aider may stop the burning, cool a small burn, remove loose clothing and jewelry, cover the burn, keep the patient warm and still, send for emergency medical services, and give nothing by mouth. The trained responder may assess the airway, give oxygen within the protocol, treat pain within the authority to prescribe, dress the wound, monitor the patient, and choose the destination. The physician, the surgeon, and the burn team own the acts that repair rather than contain, which are securing a closing airway, cutting the eschar that strangles a limb or chest, removing dead tissue, grafting the wound, and treating infection, and none of those is a field procedure.

Pain after a burn is severe and continuous, and it deserves to be treated as a problem in its own right. The cooling of a fresh burn eases pain, and so does a clean covering that keeps air away from exposed nerves, and both are within reach of the lay first aider. Analgesia belongs to the provider authorized to prescribe it under the local protocol, and the substance, the amount, the route, and the interval between doses are decisions for that provider and that protocol. Untreated pain drives up the pulse and the work of breathing, so the responder reports pain in plain words and asks for what the protocol allows.

A burned patient is also a trauma patient, because the fire, the explosion, the fall, or the crash that caused the burn may have caused injuries the burn disguises. A patient pulled from a vehicle, thrown by a blast, or forced to jump from a window may have a broken spine, a head injury, a fractured pelvis, or bleeding inside the chest or abdomen. The primary survey is therefore performed in its fixed order in every burned patient, and the burn itself is examined only after the airway, breathing, and circulation are dealt with. Any difficulty with the airway, any abnormality of breathing, or any sign that the circulation is failing should pull attention away from the burn, because a burn can wait for minutes that a hidden injury cannot.

Children and older adults deserve their own words, because their burns behave differently and their bodies compensate differently. A child's skin is thinner, so the same hot liquid that causes a partial-thickness burn in an adult may cause a deep burn in a child, and a child's head is a larger proportion of the body. A child also loses heat and fluid faster, has less reserve, and can move from looking well to looking critically ill within a short time, so the responder watches a burned child more closely. An older patient has thin skin that heals slowly, a heart and lungs with less reserve, and illnesses that change what the burn will do, and a pregnant patient requires care for two patients at once. The finding that should make a burn more serious than its size suggests is the condition of the patient rather than the appearance of the wound.

The burn wound is warm, moist, and open, and it is one of the easiest places for infection to begin, because the barrier that keeps organisms out has been destroyed. The findings that suggest infection are pain that increases after the first days, redness spreading beyond the wound, swelling, pus, a foul smell, fever, and a patient who becomes unwell in a way the burn does not explain. Prevention begins with a clean covering in the field and continues in definitive care with the cleaning of the wound and the removal of dead tissue. The decision about tetanus protection and about antibiotics belongs to the provider authorized to make it, so nothing here names a substance, an amount, or an interval.

Some burns must go to a burn center even when they look small, and the reasons are grouped around the site, the depth, the extent, the cause, and the patient. A burn of the face, hands, feet, genitals, perineum, or across a joint goes there because those areas demand fine surgery and long rehabilitation to keep their function, and a circumferential burn goes there because it may need an escharotomy without warning. An inhalation injury, a full-thickness burn of any size, an electrical or chemical burn, a burn with another injury, and a burn in a patient who is very young or very old also belong in a center with the whole team. The figures often used for a large burn, such as more than 20 per cent of the body surface in an adult or more than 10 per cent in a child, are teaching illustrations to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

A constructed illustration, never a real patient, is the man carried from a house fire who is awake and speaking, with soot around his mouth, singed nasal hairs, a hoarse voice, and a burn of the chest that meets itself from front to back. He looks like a patient who can wait, and he is in fact a patient with two emergencies, because his airway is narrowing and his chest wall cannot expand against the encircling eschar. A second constructed illustration is the toddler who pulled a cup of hot tea onto the arm and chest, whose small burn covers a larger share of the body than the same wound would in an adult. A third is the older woman found beside a space heater with a burn she could not feel, because the nerves in the skin had been destroyed.

The handover must carry the history that the receiving team cannot reconstruct from the wound, beginning with the time of the injury and the agent that caused it. The responder reports whether the fire was in an enclosed space, whether the patient was unconscious, how long the burning lasted, what was applied, how the patient was kept warm, and what the voice and breathing have done since first contact. The extent is reported in words, with the sites named, and the airway findings are reported as findings rather than as a diagnosis. The time matters because the burn continues to evolve after the heat is removed and the swelling that produces the constrictions grows over the hours that follow.

The burned patient is watched continuously from the first contact to the handover, because such a patient can change within a single journey. Watching means repeating the same observations in the same order and comparing them with what was found before, rather than trusting the impression of the moment. The observations that matter are the level of consciousness, the work and sound of the breathing, the pulse, the color and warmth of the skin, the pain, and the state of the wound. A voice that has become hoarse, breathing that has become noisy, a dulling level of consciousness, or a limb that has become hard and cold should send the responder back to the beginning.

The journey to definitive care begins as soon as the patient is stable enough to move, and the destination is chosen before the patient is placed in the vehicle. The nearest hospital that can stabilize the patient is not always the right destination, because the burn center may be distant and the patient may need to pass through a local facility first, and that choice belongs to the trained responder or clinician under the local protocol. Transport is both urgent and gentle, because speed that throws the patient around adds pain and injury, and a patient who arrives colder and more shocked has gained nothing from the hurry. The responder keeps the patient warm throughout, protects the wound from contamination, keeps the burned part still, and continues the observations begun at the scene.

Certain sites deserve a word of their own because their anatomy changes what the burn means. A burn of the face is feared because it may involve the airway and because the scar that follows changes how a person is seen and how that person eats, speaks, and sees. A burn of the hand is feared because the hand must remain supple, and a scar across the palm or fingers can steal the grip forever, which is why a burned hand belongs to a specialist even when it is small. A burn of the foot matters because the patient must walk, a burn of the genitals and perineum is hard to keep clean, and a burn across a joint may tighten into a scar that prevents movement.

The lay first aider is most often met with a scald in a kitchen or a bathroom, and the same few actions serve in almost every case. The burning is stopped by removing the patient from the source or the source from the patient, and by removing hot liquid soaked clothing that is not stuck to the skin. The burn is cooled with clean, cool running water for as long as the local teaching advises, covered with a clean, dry cloth, and the patient is kept warm while help is called, and nothing is given by mouth. The finding that should make the lay first aider call emergency medical services is a burn of the face, hands, feet, or genitals, a white or charred burn, or a patient who is very young, very old, or unwell.

A burn is not a finished injury when the heat is removed, because the tissue continues to react and some skin that looked alive may die in the days that follow. This is why the depth is reassessed in hospital and why a wound that looked partial may prove full-thickness, so the responder's early estimate is a starting point rather than a verdict. The wound is also most vulnerable in those first days, when the surface is open and the patient's defenses are weakened, so the covering and the cleanliness with which it is applied both matter. Keeping the patient warm is part of the same work, because the burn has removed the skin that held the heat in, and cool skin, shivering, or new drowsiness means the patient is not warm enough.

The record that travels with the burned patient is as important as any dressing, and it should carry the time, the agent, the extent estimate, the sites involved, the airway findings, what was applied, and every observation that changed. The time is written and spoken because the burn and its swelling run on a clock that began at the injury, and the receiving team uses that clock to decide when the airway must be watched closely and when an escharotomy may become necessary. The responder writes the record on the patient in a way the receiving team will see, or on the form the local protocol provides, and repeats it aloud at handover. No field responder performs an escharotomy, removes dead tissue, grafts a burn, or gives a fluid or a drug by a schedule of their own invention, because those acts belong to definitive care and its protocol.

To summarize, the burned patient is treated first as a trauma patient and always by provider level, and the burning itself is stopped before anything else is done. Scene hazards of fire, smoke, traffic, electricity, falling structures, and hot material are made safe first, and the two lethal emergencies to name early are the airway that is narrowing and the circumferential burn that strangles a limb or the chest. Superficial, partial-thickness, and full-thickness burns are told apart by what remains of the skin, the deepest burn is often the least painful, and the extent is estimated in words with the patient's palm and the spoken proportions of the body rather than with a chart. Cooling with clean, cool running water is the field act that changes a burn, but it is not applied over a large area when the patient is already cold, and covering, warmth, and the prevention of hypothermia run beside it throughout. The lay first aider stops the burning, cools a small burn, covers it, keeps the patient warm, and calls for help, while the trained responder adds airway assessment, oxygen and pain relief within the protocol, dressings, monitoring, and the choice of destination, and definitive care owns the airway, the escharotomy, the excision, the graft, and the treatment of infection. Burns of the face, hands, feet, genitals, perineum, or a joint, circumferential and full-thickness burns, inhalation injuries, electrical and chemical burns, and burns in the very young, the very old, or the already ill must go to a specialist unit, and every figure in this chapter is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. The record of the time, the agent, the extent, the airway findings, and every change travels with the patient, and this chapter does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country where you work.
