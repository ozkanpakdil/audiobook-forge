# Chapter 18 — Open Chest Wounds

An open chest wound is a wound of the chest wall that opens a path into the pleural space, the thin sealed gap between the lung and the inside of the rib cage. When that path opens, the slight negative pressure that holds the lung against the ribs is lost, and the lung on that side begins to collapse while the patient breathes with the other lung alone. The scene is made safe before the patient is touched, since traffic, fire, electricity, water, animals, violence, structural collapse, and the patient's own blood are hazards that turn one casualty into two. The lethal possibility to name at once is air trapped under pressure inside the chest, and the finding that announces the wound is the movement of air at the opening, heard as a hiss and seen as froth with each breath. The lay first aider, the trained responder, and the paramedic each have a different part here, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

The pleural space is a potential gap, a film of fluid between two membranes that slide on each other, and its slight negative pressure keeps the elastic lung stretched against the chest wall. A wound that stays open lets air move in and out with each breath, so the lung cannot expand, while a wound that closes, whether by its own edges, a clot, a flap of tissue, or a dressing laid over it, lets air in during inspiration and traps it during expiration. That trapping is the mechanism of the emergency that follows, and it is why this chapter treats the dressing both as a treatment and as a hazard. The finding that tells the responder which situation is present is whether air can still escape, and that question must be asked again each time the wound is checked, because the answer can change within minutes.

Recognition begins with the mechanism of injury, because the story predicts where the wound is likely to be and what lies beneath it. A knife, a bullet, a blast fragment, a rib broken inward, a fall onto a sharp object, and a collision with a fence post can each open the chest, and the opening may hide in the armpit, the back, the flank, or a fold of skin the patient is holding. Because the track cannot be judged from its opening, no responder may probe it with a finger or an instrument, and no responder may assume that a wound low on the chest has spared the cavity, since the diaphragm moves with every breath. A constructed illustration, offered as a construction and never as a real patient or incident, is a young person who fell against a metal stake, whose small wound below the shoulder blade whistled quietly each time he breathed in. The finding that settles the question is the behavior of the wound at the moment of breathing rather than its size.

The sound and the movement of air are the signature of this injury, and a responder who listens at the wound before covering it learns more than one who only looks. Air may leave with a soft hiss that keeps time with the breath, with a bubbling like liquid through a straw, or with a suck loud enough to be heard across a quiet room. Froth may gather at the opening, blood may bubble pink where it mixes with air, and a loose dressing may flutter against the skin with inspiration. The sound is not always present, and its absence proves nothing, because a wound may be sealed by clot or by the patient's position and may open again when the patient is moved. The patient's condition carries the same message, with rapid shallow breathing, a forward lean, and a level of consciousness that stays clear at first and then dulls as the circulation fails. Air that has escaped into the tissues may be felt as a crackling under the skin of the neck, the chest wall, or the armpit, which must be reported even when the wound looks small. A pressure of 90 millimeters of mercury measured in the arm is sometimes taught as a warning level, and any such figure must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the line differs with age, with pregnancy, and with the way the measurement is taken.

Bleeding travels with this injury and must be named beside the air, because the track may also have torn a vessel between the ribs, the lung, or the heart. Blood collecting in the space compresses the lung and the heart much as air does, and the wound whose own edges act as a flap may let air out and then fall shut, so a dressing applied too completely can turn a wound that was venting into a wound that traps. The findings that point to blood rather than air are a patient who is pale and restless with a rising pulse and a falling pressure, a side of the chest that is dull where it was resonant, and a wound that keeps draining dark blood beneath the dressing. The finding that ends this assessment is any sign of shock in a patient with a chest wound, and that finding moves the patient immediately toward definitive hospital care, because the treatment for bleeding into the chest is an operation and not a dressing. Fluids and blood belong to a decision that only the paramedic under a protocol and the prescriber may make, and the choice of what is given, the amount, the route, and the timing cannot be carried away from a manual.

The immediate action for the lay first aider is small and specific, and it is the only chest procedure this manual permits without equipment, authorization, and training. The lay first aider covers the wound, presses a gloved or otherwise protected hand over it, calls emergency medical services, keeps the patient warm, and stays until help arrives. If a dressing is at hand it is laid over the opening, and if nothing is at hand the flat of the hand is enough, and the hand is not lifted to look beneath it, since covering the wound and leaving for help is the whole of the task. The patient is left in the position that makes breathing easiest, usually sitting up and leaning slightly forward, unless the patient is unresponsive or another injury forbids it. The lay first aider does not cut, probe, pack, remove an embedded object, or try to release air from the chest with any instrument, and if the patient stops breathing, cardiopulmonary resuscitation, after which CPR is allowed, must begin while help is summoned again.

An occlusive dressing is a covering that does not allow air to pass, and it is placed by the trained responder or the paramedic under a protocol rather than by the lay first aider, who has only a hand. It works by sealing the opening against the outside atmosphere, so the patient's own breathing no longer pumps room air into the chest. The classic instruction is to tape the dressing on most of its edges and leave one edge free, so that the free edge lifts when pressure inside rises and lets air out while the taped edges keep air from entering. A dressing that seals the wound completely on every side becomes a one-way valve that lets air in during inspiration and keeps it in during expiration, and it can create the very emergency it was meant to prevent. The finding that says the dressing is doing its work is a quiet wound and a patient who is not struggling more, while any deterioration after it is applied is a signal to reassess the dressing before anything else.

Occlusive dressings fail in a small number of predictable ways, and each failure has a finding that announces it. A dressing may fail to seal at all, lifting at one edge with every breath so that air keeps moving in and out and the lung keeps collapsing. It may fail in the opposite direction, sealing too well and turning the wound into a valve, which is the most dangerous failure and the reason monitoring is inseparable from the dressing. It may fail because the wound is not the only opening, since a second wound in the back or the armpit that no one has seen keeps admitting air, or because the dressing becomes soaked with blood and lifts. The finding that requires the responder to replace or readjust it is any new difficulty in breathing, any change in the sound at the wound, and any change in the patient's color.

The emergency that must be watched for after every chest dressing is air trapped under pressure in one side of the chest, and it develops because air enters the space and cannot leave. As the pressure rises, the lung on that side is compressed, the mediastinum, the central partition that holds the heart and the great vessels, is pushed toward the other side, and the return of blood to the heart is obstructed, so the patient dies of a circulation problem caused by a breathing injury. The warning findings are a patient who becomes more breathless and more distressed, a breathing rate that climbs, a pulse that becomes rapid and weak, neck veins that become visibly distended, and a level of consciousness that dulls. The finding that demands the fastest possible response is a patient who deteriorates after a chest wound has been covered, because the dressing is now part of the problem. Any figure quoted for the time in which this emergency can develop, whether 15 minutes or another interval, is an illustration to be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the pace differs with the size of the leak and with the patient.

Monitoring is a rhythm rather than a single check, and it begins the moment the dressing is placed and continues until the receiving team takes over. The trained responder repeats the observations of the primary survey in the same order, returning to the airway, then breathing, then the circulation, then the level of consciousness, and compares each set with the one before. The Glasgow coma scale, used where the responder is trained in it, turns the impression of drowsiness into something two people can compare, and the trend over time carries more meaning than any single score. The lay first aider monitors with the eyes and the voice, noticing whether breathing has become harder, whether the patient speaks less, and whether the skin has become pale, cool, or damp, and reports every change to emergency medical services. The finding that returns the responder to the beginning of the sequence is any worsening at any point, and each such return is recorded with the time it was observed.

Positioning is a treatment here because it changes the work of breathing and the movement of air. A patient who is awake and breathing should be allowed to sit up and lean forward, since that lets the diaphragm descend and the muscles of the chest and neck work more efficiently. A patient who is unresponsive must be managed with the airway protected, and the responder rolls the patient as a unit where a roll is needed and looks at the back as well as the front, because a wound in the back can be the only opening. The lay first aider does not force a position on a patient who fights it, and does not leave an unresponsive patient on the back without help, because the tongue and the stomach contents can close the airway of a patient who cannot protect it.

Oxygen is a clinical decision rather than a first-aid routine, and the decision to give it belongs to the trained responder or the paramedic under a protocol and to the prescriber, while the substance, the amount, the means of delivery, and the frequency are set by the local protocol and never by this book. The finding that makes the decision urgent is a patient who is working to breathe, who is confused or restless, or whose color and level of consciousness are worsening. The lay first aider cannot give oxygen and is not asked to, and the lay first aider's contribution is to keep the airway open, to keep the patient in the position that eases breathing, and to shorten the time until trained help arrives. The trained responder who is authorized to assist the patient's breathing must do so with attention to the chest, because air pushed into a chest that cannot vent can raise the pressure and worsen the emergency, and any target or threshold mentioned here is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

Pain in an open chest wound is severe, it worsens with every breath, and it restricts the breathing the patient most needs to do, so the relief of pain is part of the treatment rather than a courtesy added at the end. The decision to treat pain belongs to the provider authorized to prescribe, and the substance, the amount, the route, and the frequency belong to the local protocol and to that prescriber, so no reader may take a prescription from this manual. The finding that makes the decision urgent is a patient who guards the chest, breathes in short shallow breaths, and becomes exhausted, because that pattern leads to a collapse of the lung and a worsening of the circulation. The tetanus question and the question of antibiotics are raised at the first contact and answered by the provider authorized to decide, and the substance and the amount belong to the prescriber and the local guideline, with the finding that makes the answer urgent being a wound that is deep, contaminated, or caused by a bite.

Hypothermia is a companion of chest trauma and not a detail of comfort, because a patient who is bleeding, exposed for examination, lying on cold ground, or receiving cool fluids loses heat quickly. Cold makes clotting work poorly, deepens shock, and makes the heart more likely to develop a dangerous rhythm, and the patient with an open chest wound is especially vulnerable. The lay first aider covers the patient before the journey rather than after it, removes wet clothing and replaces it with dry covering where that can be done without exposing the wound, and insulates the patient from the ground. A body temperature below 35 degrees Celsius is often named as the line at which cold itself becomes dangerous, and that figure is an illustration which must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the threshold shifts with age and with the way the measurement is taken. The finding that warns the responder that cooling has already begun is a patient who has stopped shivering while still cold, because shivering is the body's attempt to make heat.

Emergency evacuation is the definitive field treatment for this injury, and the reasons not to delay are specific and must be spoken plainly. The first reason is that only an operating room can repair the track, stop the bleeding inside the chest, and place a drain that lets air and blood leave the space under control. The second is that the pressure emergency can develop within minutes and can kill a patient who was talking, and no dressing can be guaranteed to prevent it. The third is that the patient's own breathing drives the injury while the wound remains in contact with the outside air, so no minute on the scene improves the wound. The lay first aider covers the wound, keeps the patient warm, and hands the patient to a moving team as quickly as the scene allows, and the finding that ends the argument about whether to move is any deterioration at all.

The destination is chosen before the patient is moved, and it is the nearest facility that can provide the care the patient needs, which for a penetrating chest wound usually means a trauma center with imaging, an operating room, and an intensive care unit. Where such a center is far away, the local protocol decides whether the patient goes first to a nearer hospital for stabilization and a drain or travels directly to the trauma center, and that decision belongs to the paramedic and to the medical control that supports them. The receiving team is given a report in a fixed order, with the location, the number of patients, the mechanism of injury, the wound and its behavior, what has been done, and every change observed. A delay of more than 6 hours before definitive care is often described as a point at which infection and other complications become more likely, and that interval is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the risk depends on the wound, the patient, and the system. The finding that must be written without fail is any deterioration after the dressing, with the time of that deterioration, because the receiving team reads the trend rather than a single value.

Some interventions here belong to the trained responder, to the paramedic, or to definitive hospital care, and each is named with the level of training and authorization it requires. Needle decompression, in which air under pressure is released from the chest through a needle passed through the chest wall, may be performed by a trained responder or a paramedic only where the service protocol authorizes it, only after training, and only when the findings of a pressure emergency are present. It is not a first-aid measure, it is not a lay skill, and it is not a substitute for the operation that follows, because the release is temporary. The finding that tells the responder it has worked is an improvement in breathing and in the circulation together, while a patient who keeps deteriorating points toward a further attempt by an authorized provider or toward immediate surgery. The lay first aider must never attempt this, because a needle placed blindly into a chest can injure the lung, the heart, and the vessels, and because the act without the operation behind it buys very little time.

Placement of a chest drain, a tube passed into the pleural space and connected to a system that lets air and blood out while keeping air from returning, is a definitive procedure that belongs to a physician or a surgeon in a hospital or to a trained provider under a specific protocol in a service that authorizes it. It is not a field procedure for a lay first aider, it is not the same act as covering a wound, and it must be described as what it is so that no listener mistakes the dressing for the drain. The drain lets trapped air escape and lets blood out so that the lung can re-expand and the source can be assessed, and surgical exploration, repair of a torn vessel or lung, and control of bleeding from the heart or the great vessels belong to the operating room. Definitive hospital care also owns the imaging that confirms the diagnosis, including the chest radiograph, ultrasound, and computed tomography, which is called CT. Each of those studies and operations is chosen by the hospital team rather than by the field responder, and no field measure takes their place.

A short set of acts must never be performed on an open chest wound, and each is forbidden for a reason the listener can carry away. No object embedded in the chest wall is removed in the field, because it may be plugging a torn vessel or sealing the opening, and its removal may release a hemorrhage that cannot be controlled. No wound of the chest is packed with gauze in the field, because packing does not seal the pleural space and may push contamination deeper, and packing the chest is a different matter from packing a bleeding groin or armpit. No wound of the chest is tightly bound, because binding restricts the movement of the chest wall and hides the very breathing the responder must watch. No chest wound is explored with a finger or an instrument, because probing can convert a sealed wound into a venting one, and no chest wound is left uncovered while the responder searches for equipment, because a hand is a better cover than nothing.

Certain patients deserve a word of their own, because the same wound behaves differently in each. A child has a smaller chest and a more mobile central partition, so the pressure emergency can develop faster and the signs can be harder to read, and children compensate for blood loss until they suddenly do not. A pregnant patient has a diaphragm pushed upward by the uterus, so a wound that appears to lie below the chest may still open the pleural space, and two patients are being moved at once, since the fetus depends entirely on the mother's breathing and circulation. An older patient may have less reserve in the heart and the lungs and may be taking medicines that change the response to bleeding, and a patient with long-standing lung disease may already be short of breath, so the responder compares the patient with the patient rather than with a textbook.

A constructed illustration, offered as a construction and never as a real patient or a real incident, may show how the decisions fit together. A factory worker is struck in the side of the chest by a piece of metal thrown from a machine, and the wound is small and partly hidden under his shirt, but the responders hear a soft hiss each time he breathes and see a few bubbles of blood at the opening. The lay first aider who arrives first presses a clean dressing over the wound with a protected hand, keeps the worker sitting up and warm, and calls emergency medical services with the location and the mechanism of injury. The paramedic arrives, confirms the behavior of the wound, applies a chest seal according to the local protocol, watches the breathing and the circulation while the patient is carried, and reports the dressing and the time to the receiving team. When the worker becomes restless and his neck veins become prominent, the paramedic reassesses the seal at once, considers the authorized intervention for trapped air, and brings the patient to a trauma center where imaging confirms the diagnosis and a surgeon places a drain that controls what the injury has hidden.

To summarize, an open chest wound is a wound of the chest wall that communicates with the pleural space, and it is recognized by the sound and the movement of air at the opening rather than by the size of the wound. The lethal possibility to name first is air trapped under pressure in the chest, and the dressing that stops air from entering can itself create that emergency, so monitoring for worsening breathing, a rising pulse, distended neck veins, and a dulling level of consciousness must follow every dressing as closely as the dressing itself. The lay first aider covers the wound with a protected hand or a clean dressing, calls emergency medical services, keeps the patient warm and in the position that eases breathing, and leaves the advanced interventions to the trained responder, the paramedic, or the hospital team trained and authorized to perform them. Needle decompression, placement of a chest drain, imaging, and surgical repair are named with the level of training and authorization they require, and none of them is a first-aid measure. Emergency evacuation is part of the treatment, because only definitive hospital care can control the bleeding and let the trapped air out under observation. Every time, pressure, temperature, depth, and figure in this chapter is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country where you work.
