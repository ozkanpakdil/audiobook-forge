# Chapter 39 — Spinal Trauma

A rider lying twisted beside a motorcycle, a diver pulled from shallow water, and a driver held by a belt in a car stopped against a wall may all carry the same injury, and none of them may show it. The spinal column is the bony case that protects the spinal cord, and a force that damages the case may bruise, stretch, or divide the cord inside it, so the responder behaves as though the column may be broken rather than trying to prove that it is not. Two dangers are named first: an injury high in the neck can take away the drive to breathe, and an unstable column can convert a partial injury into a complete one during a careless move. The scene is made safe before any hand touches the patient, and the hazards are traffic, fire, electricity, water, animals, violence, falling structures, and the patient's own blood. This chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country where you work.

The column is a stack of vertebrae joined in a curve, and the cord runs inside a canal the vertebrae enclose, so the bone is a helmet the cord wears along its length. The cervical region moves most and has the least bone to spare, and the cord there is wide in proportion to its canal, which is why a small displacement in the neck can produce a large deficit. The thoracic region is stiffened by the ribs and is injured less often, but a fracture there follows a severe transfer of energy and should prompt a search for injury to the chest and the great vessels. The lumbar region carries the weight of the trunk and holds a leash of nerve roots rather than cord in its lower part, so injury there may damage roots and produce weakness less complete than a cord injury.

Cervical injury is predicted by mechanisms that bend, straighten, rotate, compress, or pull the head away from the trunk. A collision from behind throws the head backward and then forward, a head-on collision throws it forward into the restraint, and a side impact bends the neck sideways while the shoulder is held by the belt. A dive into shallow water loads the crown of the head and drives the neck into itself, and the same load happens in a fall onto the head, in a tackle, and when a heavy object strikes the crown. The finding that should fix the responder's attention is often the mechanism itself rather than any wound, together with pain in the neck, tenderness at the back of the neck, a change in the voice, and weakness or numbness in the arms or the legs.

Thoracic and lumbar injury is predicted by compression, by bending forward, and by the shearing a lap belt produces. A fall from a height lands the patient on the feet or the buttocks and drives force upward through the pelvis and the spine, so the heel, the pelvis, and the spine are injured together, and a painful heel in a patient who fell should raise the question of a fracture above it. A lap belt without a shoulder belt folds the patient over the belt while the upper body is thrown forward, which can tear the column at the thoracic and lumbar hinge and can also injure the bowel. The finding that suggests cord or root injury here is a band of pain around the trunk, weakness of the legs, or a loss of the sense of a full bladder.

No responder can exclude a spinal injury at the roadside, and the attempt is not part of the lay first aider's work. Certain findings make the probability high enough that the patient is treated as injured, and they include a high-energy mechanism, pain in the neck or the back, tenderness when the spine is pressed, a wound or deformity over the column, weakness or numbness in a limb, an altered level of consciousness, intoxication, a head injury, and any other injury severe enough to distract the patient from the neck. Where the examination cannot be trusted, because the patient is confused, intoxicated, or unresponsive, the precaution is kept until a clinician with imaging clears it. A patient who is talking normally, moving all four limbs, and denying pain may still have an unstable column, and that is the quietest trap in this chapter.

Spinal injury produces two distinct emergencies, and a responder who confuses them will treat neither well. The first is a column that can no longer hold the cord, which threatens a deficit not yet present and is prevented by handling rather than by any device. The second is a cord injury that has already occurred, which may worsen for reasons inside the patient rather than because of movement, and which is watched rather than repaired in the field. An injury above the middle of the neck can take the drive from the diaphragm, so a patient breathing now may stop when swelling or bleeding rises around the cord, and the warning is breathing that becomes shallow, a voice that weakens, and a cough that fails. A high cord injury also removes the vessel tone that keeps the circulation tight, so the patient may have low blood pressure with a slow pulse instead of the fast pulse of blood loss, which is called neurogenic shock and is a different problem from hemorrhage.

The finding that reveals a cord injury is a change in what the patient can feel or do, and it is sought before the patient is moved. Weakness or paralysis of the arms, the legs, or both, a loss of sensation below a line on the trunk, an inability to feel the bladder filling, and an inability to hold urine or stool are cord findings. The patient may describe tingling, burning, or a band of tightness around the chest or the abdomen, and may also describe nothing at all, because a cord injury can be painless while the column that caused it is broken. Breathing that uses the belly rather than the chest, a weak cough, and a quiet voice suggest that the muscles of respiration are already involved.

The neurological examination is recorded before movement because movement changes what the examination will find, and a baseline never written cannot be reconstructed. The record begins with the time, the patient's level of consciousness, and whether the patient is alert, drowsy, or unresponsive, because an examination performed on an unresponsive patient is a different examination from one performed on a patient who can cooperate. The responder then records what the patient can move on each side, what the patient can feel on each side, and where the change begins. The findings are written on the patient or in the record that travels with the patient, together with the time, because a deficit present at the first contact and one that appears later tell two different stories.

The level of a spinal cord injury is the lowest segment of the cord that still works normally, and everything below that level is described as what remains, which is the phrase the receiving team needs. A sensory level is the lowest place on the skin where the patient can still feel a light touch reliably, and a motor level is the lowest place where a muscle can still move, and the two need not be the same. The responder describes the level in words, such as movement present at the elbows and the wrists but absent at the fingers, rather than naming a vertebra that has not been imaged. What remains below the level is recorded in the same way, because a patient who can move the toes has a different injury from one who cannot, and that difference changes the urgency and the destination. Sensation around the anus and the ability to tighten the anal muscle are the last functions tested and the most important to note, because their preservation predicts a better outcome than a complete loss.

Motor and sensory testing follow a fixed order so that two responders describe the same thing. The patient is asked to shrug the shoulders against the examiner's hands, to bend the elbow, to extend the wrist, to grip two fingers, to lift the knee, to straighten the knee, to pull the foot upward, and to push it downward, and each movement is recorded as present, weak, or absent rather than as a number. Sensation is tested with a light touch on both sides at the same level, working from the top of the body downward, and the patient is asked to say when the feeling changes, because the patient's own report is the finding. The pattern of loss matters more than its depth, because a loss beginning at the chest and sparing the face points to an injury high in the column, while a loss beginning at the groin points lower. The examiner compares the two sides, records limits imposed by a splint or a dressing, and repeats the examination after every move.

Breathing is the function a cervical cord injury threatens first, and it is watched from the first minute rather than assessed once. The diaphragm is driven by nerves that leave the cord high in the neck, and an injury at or above that level can leave a patient using only the muscles between the ribs and the muscles of the abdomen, which is seen as the belly rising while the chest stays still. The airway is opened with the jaw and the chin while the head and the neck are held in line by another pair of hands, and the trained responder uses the adjuncts and the equipment the service carries. The lay first aider watches for noisy breathing, for a voice that fades, for a patient who cannot clear the mouth, and for a patient who is becoming drowsy, and calls emergency medical services at the first of those findings.

Movement precautions are the heart of this chapter, and they are a set of small refusals rather than one maneuver. The head is kept in line with the trunk and the trunk with the pelvis, so the column stays in the position in which it was found, and no responder lifts the head to look at the neck or turns the face to see a wound. The patient is not sat up to drink, is not allowed to twist to look at the responder, and is not rolled onto the side to remove clothing unless the plan requires it and enough hands are present. The neck is not pulled, not massaged, and not tested by asking the patient to look in every direction, because those are the movements an unstable column cannot tolerate. The reason for all of this is that a movement too small to see can be the difference between a bruise and a division.

Immobilization is a sequence that begins with hands and continues with a device, and the device is chosen by the local protocol rather than by preference. The first act is manual stabilization, in which one responder takes the head with both hands and holds it in line with the trunk while the rest of the team works, and that hold is kept until a device takes over the work. The second act is the application of the device, which may be a rigid collar, a head support, a padded board, a vacuum mattress, or a combination the service has adopted, and the collar alone is not immobilization, because it limits some movement and leaves the rest. The finding that tells the responder the device is working is a patient whose head, neck, and trunk move as one piece when the stretcher is lifted, and the finding that it is not working is a head that still turns or a trunk that slides. The device is applied by the trained responder or the paramedic under the protocol, while the lay first aider keeps the head supported and waits.

The device is a means of moving the patient safely and not a treatment, and it is taken away as soon as definitive care can take over. A patient strapped to a rigid board for a long time develops pain and pressure damage over the bones of the back and the hips, breathes less deeply because the chest is held, and grows cold from the hard surface beneath, so the protocol of every service sets a limit and a plan for removing the board. The straps are secured firmly enough that the patient cannot slide, and no strap is placed so tightly across the chest that breathing is restricted. A patient who vomits while immobilized is turned as a unit by a team, or the front of the device is opened where its design allows, because a filling airway must be cleared before any consideration of the column.

The rule that settles every conflict in this chapter is that a spinal precaution must never prevent the treatment of an airway that is closing, because a patient without an airway is dead before the spine matters. When the airway is blocked by the tongue, by blood, by vomit, or by a swelling in the neck, the responder opens it, and the opening is done with the head and neck held in line by a second pair of hands rather than by tilting the head back. A jaw thrust lifts the tongue away from the throat while the neck stays neutral, and the removal of visible material from the mouth clears what blocks it, and the trained responder may use an airway adjunct the protocol authorizes. A collar that blocks the opening of the mouth may be loosened or removed at the front by the trained responder while the head is held, and that decision favors the airway over the device. A patient who is not breathing is given rescue breaths and, where the responder is trained and the protocol allows, cardiopulmonary resuscitation, and whether oxygen is given, in what concentration, and by what route is set by the local protocol and the clinician responsible rather than by this book.

Turning the patient as one unit, which is called a log roll, is a controlled maneuver for a trained team, and it exists to reach what the examination from the front cannot reach. It is used to examine the back and the spine, to place a board or a mattress under the patient, to remove a bulky object from beneath the body, and to clear an airway when vomiting cannot be managed from the front. The team agrees on the side to turn toward and on the number of hands, one responder holds the head and gives the count, and the turn begins and ends on that count so that no part of the trunk lags behind. The lay first aider does not perform this maneuver, because a poorly coordinated roll is worse than no roll at all.

Extrication from a vehicle balances the danger of the scene against the danger of movement, and it is decided by the trained responder or the paramedic under the local protocol. The scene is made safe first, which means holding the traffic, stabilizing the vehicle so that it cannot roll or shift, controlling fire and fuel, and protecting the patient from glass and sharp metal. Where the vehicle is burning, where it may fall, where the airway is blocked, or where a second collision is imminent, the patient is moved quickly with the best spinal hold the time allows, because a perfect precaution in a burning car is no precaution at all. A confined space, such as a wrecked car, a trench, or a collapsed structure, is entered only by those trained and equipped to do so, and inside it the same order holds, with the head held and the device applied where the shape of the space allows. A patient who is fully alert, free of pain in the neck and the back, free of any neurological finding, and free of a distracting injury may sometimes be allowed to walk under the protocol of the service, but that decision belongs to the trained responder and never to the lay first aider, and it is never made for a patient who is intoxicated, confused, or unable to give a clear account.

Handling is the part of the work that most often undoes the precaution, and it depends on hands, on a count, and on a plan spoken before the move. The team decides the route, names who holds the head, and moves on a single command, and the head holder keeps the head in line and does not let go until the device is secured. The patient is lifted rather than dragged and kept level rather than tipped, and is laid on the stretcher without being allowed to roll, and the straps are secured before the stretcher is raised. The transfer into the vehicle is the moment when a slide is most likely, so the patient is fixed to the stretcher and the stretcher is fixed to the vehicle, and the receiving end of the journey is told what has been done.

Children and older patients change the details of this chapter, and both are injured by forces that would leave a fit adult unharmed. A young child's head is large in proportion to the body and the neck muscles are weak, so the same blow bends the neck further and the same restraint may fit poorly. An older patient's column is weakened by osteoporosis and stiffened by arthritis, so a fall from standing height can break the neck or the upper back, and the same patient may describe the pain as a familiar ache. The finding that should raise suspicion in either age group is pain that the patient dismisses, and the responder treats that pain as real.

When the patient is already paralyzed, the work changes from prevention to protection of what remains, and the manner of the care changes with it. The patient who cannot move the legs, cannot feel the bladder, or cannot take a deep breath may know exactly what has happened, so the responder speaks directly to the patient and explains each act before it is performed. The paralyzed patient is moved with the same care as any other, because a partial injury can become complete, and the level of the injury is recorded again after every move so that any change is caught. The patient cannot feel pain below the level and may not report a fracture of a leg or a burn from a hot surface, so the whole body is looked at. The patient's temperature falls quickly because the vessels below the level no longer tighten, so warmth is a clinical need and not a courtesy, and the patient is covered from the first minute.

Hypothermia belongs to every patient in this chapter, because a patient lying still on cold ground, undressed for examination, or wet from water or spilled fuel loses heat faster than a moving person replaces it. The responder removes wet clothing where the plan of movement allows, insulates the patient from the ground, covers the head and the trunk, and keeps the vehicle warm on the way. When the patient is on a device, the covering is placed over the device as well, and the straps are not loosened to do it. A temperature below 35 degrees Celsius is often named as the point at which cold itself becomes dangerous, and that figure is a teaching illustration which must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the threshold differs with the patient and with the way the temperature is measured.

Transport is a continuation of the same care rather than a pause in it, and the observations made at the scene are repeated in the vehicle and compared with what came before. The responder rechecks the breathing, the pulse, the level of consciousness, the movement and the feeling of each limb, and the pain, and every change is written with its time. The destination is chosen before the patient is moved, and it is the nearest facility that can image the spine and provide the care the patient needs, which may be a trauma center with a surgeon and an intensive care unit. The receiving team is told the mechanism of injury, the time of the injury, the neurological findings at each point, what was done, and what the patient was lying on, because a short report that is accurate is worth more than a long report that is vague.

Definitive hospital care owns the diagnosis and the repair of a spinal injury, and it begins with the clinician's examination and with imaging. A plain radiograph shows the bones, computed tomography, called CT after its first mention, shows them in finer detail and is faster in a patient who cannot cooperate, and magnetic resonance imaging, called MRI after its first mention, shows the cord, the discs, and the ligaments that bone images cannot. The decision to remove a collar and to declare the spine clear belongs to the clinician who has examined the patient and seen the images, and no responder in the field makes that decision. Surgery for a broken column, the decompression of a cord that is compressed, and the stabilization of a spine that cannot hold itself are performed in an operating room by a surgeon with an anesthesia team, while traction and the reduction of a displaced vertebra are hospital acts that must never be attempted in the field.

Several acts a tempted responder might attempt are not field procedures, and knowing them is as much a part of competence as knowing the ones that are. No responder reduces a displaced vertebra, applies traction to the neck, manipulates the spine to test it, or tries to straighten a column that looks wrong, because each of those movements can turn a bruised cord into a divided one. No responder removes a fragment or a bullet from the spine, because that is a surgical decision made when it is indicated, and the object is stabilized in place. The decisions about pain relief, about the prevention of infection, and about protection against tetanus belong to the clinician and the local protocol, and the substance, the amount, the route, and the timing are set there rather than here, so a listener cannot take a prescription from these pages.

Some patients combine the spinal question with another emergency, and the combination changes the order of the work without changing the rule. A patient with a head injury may be unresponsive, may be vomiting, and may be unable to cooperate with any examination, and that patient is treated as having a spinal injury until a clinician decides otherwise. A patient with a penetrating wound of the neck may have a cord injury and a bleeding vessel at the same time, and the airway and the bleeding come before the column in the order of the primary survey. The injury that is missed is usually the one that looked harmless at the first contact, and the defender against that is suspicion and repetition, which is why a patient who walked from a car and then sat down with a numb hand is a constructed illustration of a spinal injury that announced itself late rather than a reassuring story.

No manual can teach the hands to hold a head in line while a team turns a body, so these skills are learned on models and on each other under a teacher, and they are certified by an authority that also defines what a responder may do without supervision. Local protocol decides which device is carried, which patient may be allowed to walk, when a helmet may be removed, and which hospital receives the patient, and that protocol takes precedence over anything written here. Every number in this chapter, whether a temperature, a time, a distance, or a depth, is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between services, countries, and editions of the same guideline. The reader is asked to bring these principles to the training, to keep the training current, and to work inside the system that supervises the care.

To summarize, the spinal column is the case that protects the spinal cord, and the responder behaves as though it may be broken rather than trying to prove that it is not, because a movement too small to see can turn a bruise into a division. The scene is made safe before the patient is touched, the lethal possibilities of a high cervical injury and of an unstable column are named early, and the neurological examination is recorded before any movement, including the level of the injury and what remains below it. The head is kept in line with the trunk, the patient is not sat up, turned, or tested by movement, and immobilization begins with the hands and continues with a device chosen by the local protocol. The device is a means of transport and is removed as soon as definitive care can take over, and a strap is never placed so tightly across the chest that breathing is restricted. A spinal precaution must never prevent the treatment of an airway that is closing, so the airway is opened with the head and neck held in line, and that rule settles every conflict in this chapter. Hypothermia travels with every immobilized patient, the paralyzed patient is protected and spoken to, and the observations are repeated until handover. Every temperature, time, distance, and figure here is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country where you work.
