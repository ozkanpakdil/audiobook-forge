# Chapter 24 — Forearm Fractures

A forearm fracture can look dramatic and still be the least of a patient's problems, and it can look almost innocent while a compartment inside the limb is filling with pressure that will kill muscle if nobody recognizes it. Before any of that is considered, the scene must be made safe, and the hazards are the ordinary ones of traffic, fire, live electricity, unstable structures, animals, violence, and the patient's own blood, which carries infection into any break in the responder's skin. The lethal possibility to be named first is an open forearm fracture that has torn the radial or the ulnar artery, and the finding that reveals it is blood that soaks dressing after dressing, a swelling that grows while the responder watches, or a hand that has become pale and cold beyond the wound. The other lethal possibility is bleeding inside the closed space of the forearm, whose early finding is pain far greater than the injury appears to explain, with a limb that feels tight and hard rather than merely swollen. The response begins with personal protective equipment, which means gloves at the least and eye protection where it is available, and it continues with the primary survey in the fixed order remembered as ABCDE, because a fracture of the forearm never outranks an airway that is closing or a chest that is not moving air. The provider level is named for every act in this chapter, since a lay first aider may cover, support, and keep a patient warm, while the trained responder, the emergency medical technician, or the paramedic may splint with equipment and carry the patient toward a hospital, and definitive hospital care owns the reduction, the fixation, and the release of a compartment under pressure. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country where you work.

The mechanism of injury is the first piece of clinical evidence because it predicts what the eye cannot see, and the forearm has a short list of repeating mechanisms. A fall onto the outstretched hand drives force up through the wrist into the radius, and a direct blow across the raised forearm, the kind taken while shielding the head, breaks the ulna alone in what is often described as a nightstick fracture. A vehicle collision, a fall from a height greater than 2 meters, a machinery injury, or a gunshot wound produces the both-bone fracture and also predicts damage to the chest, the head, and the abdomen that must be sought in the same patient. A figure such as that height is offered only as a teaching illustration and must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, because services set their own thresholds for a dangerous fall. A twisting force applied while the hand is fixed, as happens when a sleeve is caught in a rotating machine, produces a fracture with a rotational component that no splint applied in the field can correct. An illustration constructed for teaching, and identified as a construction rather than a real patient or incident, is the cyclist who lands on an extended arm and holds the forearm across the chest, where the visible bend is only part of the story because the same fall may have broken a small wrist bone that will not show for weeks. The mechanism also decides the urgency, since a low-energy fall and a high-energy collision demand different degrees of suspicion.

The forearm is not a single bone but a pair that works as a unit, and understanding that unit separates a useful assessment from a guess. The radius lies on the thumb side and the ulna on the little-finger side, and between them runs the interosseous membrane, a sheet of tough tissue that binds the bones and carries part of the force traveling from the hand to the elbow. Together with the joints at the wrist and at the elbow, the pair of bones and the membrane form a ring, and a ring that breaks at a single point usually breaks somewhere else as well. That is why a fracture of the ulna may be accompanied by a dislocation of the head of the radius at the elbow, and why a fracture of the radius may be accompanied by a dislocation of the joint between the bones at the wrist. The same logic explains the shape of the limb, because the radius rotates around the ulna when the palm is turned up or down, and a fracture that alters the length or the rotation of either bone changes the way the whole forearm moves. The finding that should make the responder suspicious of a second injury is tenderness or deformity far from the visible break, at the elbow or at the wrist, and the responder reports it rather than testing it by moving the joint.

The distinction between a fracture of a single bone and a fracture of both bones is the distinction between an injury that a splint can hold and an injury that changes the shape of the limb. In an isolated fracture of the ulna or of the radius, the other bone acts as an internal splint, so the deformity is often modest and the limb may keep a nearly normal outline. In a both-bone fracture nothing remains to hold the length or the rotation, and the forearm shortens, bends, and twists, so the limb takes on a shape that everyone at the scene can see. The muscles that cross the fracture pull the fragments toward each other and rotate them, which is why a both-bone fracture that looks acceptable on the ground may look unacceptable once the patient relaxes or once the limb is lifted. The responder must never straighten a severely deformed fracture to make it look normal, and the reason is not tidiness but the risk to the vessels and the nerves that lie close to the bone and can be torn by a fragment moved without control. The correct act is to splint the limb in the position found and to let the reduction belong to definitive hospital care, where it is performed with imaging and with preparation for the anesthesia the procedure requires. An isolated fracture is not a minor injury either, because the same fall that breaks a single bone can dislocate the joint at the end of the other.

Assessment begins with the eyes and the hands and never with a probe, because the wound and the swelling are the only evidence the field will offer. The responder looks at the whole limb rather than at the point of pain, compares it with the other side, and notes the contour, the length, the rotation of the hand at rest, and any wound that communicates with the fracture. An open fracture is present when bone is visible or when a wound lies over the line of the bone, and it turns a closed fracture into an emergency of contamination as well as of structure. The responder does not push a protruding fragment back beneath the skin, because the act drives contamination deeper and can tear what is still intact, and instead covers the wound with a clean or sterile dressing and builds the splint around it. Bleeding is controlled with direct pressure over a dressing and never with a finger inside the wound, and an embedded object is stabilized in place and never removed in the field, because removal may release hemorrhage that cannot be controlled or divide a structure the object is currently splinting. Swelling is expected and is not itself the danger, but a swelling that is tight, tense, and growing, with skin that shines and does not pit when pressed, is the finding that changes the whole plan. Every one of these observations is repeated after the limb has been splinted, because a splint that is too tight can create the very problem the responder is trying to prevent.

The neurovascular examination is the part of the assessment that decides how fast the patient must travel, and it is performed before and after every intervention. The responder feels for the pulse at the wrist on the thumb side and on the little-finger side, because the arteries of the forearm supply the hand along separate routes and either can be lost while the other still beats. The color, the warmth, and the refill of the fingertips are compared with the uninjured hand, and the time taken for color to return after the nail is pressed is noted as a rough guide rather than a measurement. Sensation is tested in the territory of each nerve that crosses the forearm, which means the pad of the index finger, the pad of the little finger, and the web space between the thumb and the index finger, and the patient is asked whether feeling is normal, reduced, or absent. Movement is tested by asking the patient to bend and straighten the fingers, to spread them, and to touch the thumb to each fingertip, because a tendon divided at the wrist can leave the fingers limp. The finding that must be reported at once is a hand that has become pale, cold, numb, or weak after a splint was applied, because it means the splint, the swelling, or the fracture itself is compromising the blood supply or the nerves. A pulse that can still be felt does not exclude a compartment syndrome, and a hand without a pulse is a surgical emergency that belongs to definitive hospital care and not to a second attempt at splinting.

Several structures cross the forearm in close company with the bones, and each can be injured by the same force that broke the bone. The radial and the ulnar arteries run along the bones, and a fragment can tear or compress either, while the interosseous membrane between the bones can be torn and can allow either bone to ride over the other. The median, the ulnar, and the radial nerves travel through the forearm and supply the muscles of the fingers and the thumb, so a nerve injury may show itself first as a hand that cannot grip or as a patch of skin that has gone numb. Tendons pass through the same region on their way to the hand, and a laceration that reaches a tendon can disable a finger even when the bone is untouched and the wound looks small. A nerve or a tendon that has been divided cannot be repaired in the field, and a responder who believes that moving the fingers has restored function has learned nothing and risked much. The field contribution is to record what was found before the splint and after it, to immobilize the limb so that torn structures are not pulled further, and to carry the patient to a place where a surgeon can explore and repair. The finding that should raise the priority is a hand that never had normal feeling or movement from the moment of injury.

Compartment syndrome is the complication of a forearm fracture that must be anticipated rather than discovered, and it is a surgical emergency rather than a late observation. The muscles of the forearm are divided into compartments by sheets of tough tissue that do not stretch, and a fracture, a crush, or a tight dressing can raise the pressure inside a compartment until the small vessels feeding the muscle are closed. The earliest and most reliable sign is pain out of proportion to the injury that does not settle with the comfort measures a responder can offer, together with pain that appears when the fingers are gently straightened by someone else. The limb feels tense and hard, the skin may look shiny, and the patient often describes a deep ache different from the pain of the broken bone itself. Numbness in the territory of a nerve and weakness of the fingers come next, and pallor, coolness, and loss of the pulse come last, which means a hand with a good pulse can still be losing its muscle. The responder must never wait for the pulse to disappear before acting, because by then the muscle may already be dead and the patient may be developing the systemic illness that follows the breakdown of muscle. The finding that should trigger escalation is any pain increasing despite splinting and despite the measures the protocol allows, and the response is to remove anything encircling the limb, to keep the limb at the level of the heart, and to move the patient to a hospital without delay. The measurement of pressure inside a compartment, and its release by cutting the tight tissue, belong to definitive hospital care and are never a field procedure.

Elevation is a comfort measure with a narrow place in the care of a forearm fracture, and the rule changes as soon as a compartment is suspected. A closed fracture with swelling but no tense compartment may be supported on a pillow and raised a little to reduce edema, which belongs to the lay first aider as much as to the trained responder, provided the hand stays visible and the fingers keep their color and feeling. Once a compartment is suspected, the limb is kept at the level of the heart rather than raised above it, because raising the limb lowers the pressure that drives blood into the muscle and can make a struggling compartment worse. Nothing that encircles the limb is left in place, so rings are removed from the fingers before swelling makes that impossible, and any bandage tight enough to leave a mark is released by the provider who applied it. A ring that cannot be removed, a splint that has become tight, and a bandage cutting into the skin are findings to report, and each is a reason to look again at the hand. Cold applied to the limb may be used in some services as a comfort measure, but that decision belongs to the trained responder and to the local protocol. The decision about pressure inside a compartment and about the operation that releases it belongs to definitive hospital care, and no amount of elevation substitutes for that operation.

Pain relief for a forearm fracture is a clinical decision and not an instruction that can be carried away from a page. The finding that makes it urgent is a patient in severe pain, a patient whose pain is increasing inside a splint, or a patient whose age or condition makes distress dangerous, and the choice of substance, amount, route, and timing belong to the provider authorized to prescribe and to the local protocol in force. The lay first aider contributes by supporting the limb, keeping the patient still and warm, and asking the trained responder for help with pain rather than offering anything from a bag. Protection against tetanus is decided by the provider authorized to make that decision under the local protocol, because the answer depends on the wound, the contamination, and the patient's vaccination history, and no reader should take that decision from this chapter. Antibiotics are considered when a fracture is open or a wound is deep, contaminated, or caused by a bite, and again the substance and the amount belong to the prescriber and the guideline rather than to the field responder. Oxygen, where available, is given only under the protocol that authorizes it, because that decision rests on the patient's breathing and circulation and on the local rule. Splinting itself is among the most effective comfort measures in the field, because a limb held still hurts less, and that is a contribution the lay first aider can make with a pillow, a board, or a folded magazine.

Immobilization is the central field act for a forearm fracture, and it must hold the elbow, the forearm, and the wrist as a single unit because the bones rotate against each other when the hand turns. The lay first aider begins by leaving the limb in the position found and supporting it with both hands while emergency medical services are called, because a limb left unsupported pulls on the fracture with its own weight. Padding is placed under whatever rigid object is available, which may be a pillow, a rolled magazine, or a folded blanket, and it is placed so that the bony points of the elbow and the wrist are cushioned rather than pressed. The forearm is then bound to that support above and below the fracture, with the bindings firm enough to hold the limb and loose enough that the fingers keep their color and warmth. A sling is added afterward to carry the weight of the forearm from the shoulder, and the hand is left visible so the fingers can be watched for color, warmth, and movement during the journey. The lay first aider does not pull the limb straight, does not push a bone back into place, does not remove an embedded object, and does not give anything by mouth unless the local protocol directs otherwise, because those acts belong to other hands. The finding that tells the lay first aider the splint is too tight is a hand that has become pale, blue, cold, or numb, and the response is to loosen the binding at once and call for the trained responder.

The trained responder or paramedic applies the same principles with equipment the service authorizes, and the aim is a limb held securely enough to travel and lightly enough to keep the hand alive. A rigid splint, a molded splint, or a vacuum splint is padded and applied along the line of the forearm, and where the local protocol allows, the position of function is used so the hand is supported with the wrist slightly extended rather than left to hang. The splint extends from the upper arm to the hand so the elbow cannot bend and the wrist cannot rotate, because a splint that stops at the wrist allows the very movement that displaces the fracture. The bindings are checked by touch and by the appearance of the hand, and the hand is examined again for color, warmth, pulse, and feeling before the patient is moved. The responder records the time the splint was applied and the state of the hand at that moment, because the receiving team must compare what they find with what the field found. A fracture that cannot be held because of extreme deformity, or a limb whose circulation is failing, is a reason to move faster rather than to apply more force, and any adjustment belongs to the protocol and to the clinician who can be reached. Traction applied to the forearm in the field is not a technique of this manual, because pulling on a fractured forearm can damage nerves and vessels and turn a closed injury into an open one.

Definitive care begins with imaging and with the decision about how the fracture will be held while it heals. A radiograph of the forearm is taken from different directions and usually includes the elbow and the wrist, because the second injury at the end of a broken bone is easy to miss. Computed tomography may be used when a fracture reaches a joint, when the pattern is complex, or when the surgeon needs to plan the fixation, and it belongs entirely to the hospital and to the clinician who orders it. A fracture of both bones that is displaced, unstable, or rotated needs an operation to restore the length and alignment of the forearm, and this is usually done with plates and screws or with a rod passed inside the bone, which are techniques of the operating room and never of the field. An isolated fracture of a single bone may heal in a cast when it is stable and undisplaced, while an unstable one, an open one, or one accompanied by a dislocation at the other joint also needs operative fixation. A badly contaminated open fracture may be held first with an external frame that passes pins through the skin above and below the wound, and that frame, like the wound itself, is the responsibility of the surgical team. The decision to operate rests on the pattern of the fracture, the state of the soft tissue, the condition of the nerves and vessels, and the needs of the patient, and none of that can be judged from the roadside. The responder's part is to deliver a patient whose limb is splinted in the position found, whose hand has been examined and documented, and whose wound is covered with a clean dressing, because the cleaning, the removal of dead tissue, the irrigation, and the closure of an open fracture belong to the operating room and never to the field.

Hypothermia is a complication of trauma rather than a detail of comfort, and a patient with a forearm fracture is at risk from the moment of injury. The patient may be lying on cold ground, may have had a sleeve cut away to expose the limb, may be losing blood from an open wound, and may be waiting in wet clothing, and each condition removes heat from a body already using its reserves to answer injury. Cold makes the blood clot less effectively, deepens shock, and makes the heart more likely to develop a dangerous rhythm, so warming is part of the treatment of the fracture and begins before the journey rather than at the hospital door. The lay first aider covers the patient with whatever is available, insulates the patient from the ground, removes wet clothing where that can be done without exposing the wound, and keeps the vehicle warm during transport. The trained responder continues that protection during splinting and repeated examinations, and limits the time the limb and the patient are uncovered. A temperature below 35 degrees Celsius is often named as the point at which cold itself becomes dangerous, and that figure is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the line shifts with age, with illness, and with the way the measurement is taken. A patient who has stopped shivering while still cold is losing the ability to make heat, and that finding should be reported and treated as urgent.

From the first contact to the handover, the patient is watched in a rhythm that repeats the same observations in the same order, because the condition of a patient with a forearm fracture can change within a single journey. The responder returns to the airway, the breathing, and the circulation, then to the level of consciousness and the limbs, and then to the injured forearm, where the findings that matter are the color and warmth of the hand, the pulse at the wrist, the feeling in the fingers, the tightness of the swelling, and the amount of blood on the dressing. The Glasgow coma scale is used where the responder is trained in it, and its value lies in the trend over time rather than in a single score. The record carries the mechanism of injury, the time of the injury, the state of the hand before and after splinting, the time the splint was applied, whether a tourniquet was used and when, and every change observed, because the receiving team continues a story the field began and a gap in it forces them to start again. The destination is chosen before the patient is moved, and for a both-bone fracture, an open fracture, or a hand that has lost its circulation it is a trauma center with orthopedic and vascular surgery, and with an intensive care unit if the patient is unstable. Transport is urgent and gentle at the same time, because speed that throws the limb around adds pain and can displace the fracture further, and because a patient who arrives in worse condition has gained nothing from the hurry. The findings that should send the responder back to the beginning are a falling level of consciousness, breathing that has become faster or noisier, a pulse that has become weak or rapid, and a hand that has become pale, cold, or numb.

To summarize, a forearm fracture is an injury of a pair of bones that work as a ring, so a break at a single point should always raise the question of a second injury at the elbow or the wrist, and the responder treats the limb rather than the picture it makes. Scene safety and the primary survey come first, an open fracture with arterial bleeding is the lethal possibility to name early, and a compartment that is filling announces itself with pain out of proportion to the injury and with pain when the fingers are gently straightened. The limb is splinted in the position found and never straightened to look normal, the splint holds the elbow, the forearm, and the wrist together, an embedded object is stabilized and never removed, and the hand is examined for color, warmth, pulse, and feeling before and after every intervention. The lay first aider covers, supports, warms, and calls for help, the trained responder or paramedic splints with equipment under a protocol, and reduction, fixation, imaging, repair of vessels and nerves, and the release of a compartment belong to definitive hospital care. Analgesia, protection against tetanus, antibiotics, fluids, and oxygen are decisions of the authorized prescriber and the local protocol, and no substance, amount, route, or frequency can be taken from these pages. Hypothermia travels with every injured and exposed patient, so warming begins at the scene, the limb and the patient are watched continuously, and the time of every intervention is written on the patient in a way the receiving team will see. Every time, pressure, temperature, depth, and figure in this chapter is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such values differ between services and editions, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, or the law of the country where you work.
