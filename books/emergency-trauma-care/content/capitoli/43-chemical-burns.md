# Chapter 43 — Chemical Burns

A chemical burn is the one burn that does not stop when the patient is carried away from its source, because the substance that caused it remains on the skin, in the wound, and often inside the tissue, and it continues to destroy cells until it is removed, diluted, or at last spent. This single fact governs the whole chapter, and it is why the first task at a chemical scene is not the dressing of a wound but the removal of the agent and the protection of everyone near it. The lethal possibilities to be named early are an agent that is still burning while the responder watches, an airway that is closing from fumes that were breathed in, and a poisoning that reaches the heart from a substance that was absorbed through the skin. A fourth possibility is quieter and just as real, which is the rescuer who becomes the next patient because the same agent touched unprotected skin or eyes. This chapter follows the chemical burn from the scene, through decontamination and irrigation, to the transfer of information and the surgical assessment that belong to definitive hospital care, and it names the provider level that owns each act. It is educational material, and it does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country where you work.

A thermal burn ends its destruction when the source of heat is removed, so cooling is a limit as much as a comfort, but a chemical burn has no such moment unless the chemical is taken off the body. The agent can sit as a liquid in a skin fold, as a powder inside a boot, or as a residue on a glove, and every minute it stays in contact is another minute of tissue loss, so depth is not fixed at the moment of contact and a wound that looks shallow may become full thickness over the following hours. Acids tend to produce a coagulative injury in which dead protein forms a barrier that slows deeper penetration, while alkalis tend to produce a liquefying injury that dissolves tissue and carries the agent deeper, which is why an alkali burn is usually the more destructive of the two. Concentration, volume, duration of contact, and the nature of the tissue all change the outcome, and a dilute agent left in place for a long time can do more harm than a concentrated one that was washed off at once. For all these reasons the response is measured in how quickly the agent is removed rather than in how quickly a dressing is applied, and every figure offered in this chapter is an illustration rather than a rule, to be checked against the guideline that is current in the responder's service.

No care begins until the scene has been made safe, and a chemical scene is made safe in a different way from a road crash, because the hazard may be invisible, odorless, and heavier than air. The responder approaches from upwind and from higher ground where that is possible, stays out of enclosed spaces where vapor collects, and does not enter a spill or a shed to reach a patient who is lying in it. The substance may be a liquid, a vapor, a gas, a fine powder, or a mixture of several, and the label, the container, the placard on a vehicle, and the smell are the first evidence of what is present. The hazardous materials team is the resource that owns the identification and the removal of the agent, and the lay first aider who has found such a scene calls for that team and does not attempt a rescue into it. The trained responder works within the equipment and the authority the service provides, and nobody enters a contaminated space in ordinary clothing on the assumption that the exposure will be brief. The finding that tells a responder to stop and withdraw is any doubt about what the substance is, any smell that irritates the throat, and any patient who is lying in a pool of liquid, because those are the signs that the scene has not yet been made safe.

Protection of the rescuer is not a courtesy to the responder but a condition of the rescue, because a contaminated responder cannot help anyone and becomes a second patient who consumes the resources sent for the first. Personal protective equipment, or PPE, is chosen for the agent rather than for the patient, and a service that carries only gloves and eye protection must call for the team that carries more when the substance is unknown or is a vapor. Gloves, eye protection or a face shield, a gown or an apron, and boots or covers for the feet are the minimum for a liquid or a powder, and clothing that has absorbed the agent is treated as contaminated material and removed as soon as there is a safe place to do it. The lay first aider uses whatever barrier is at hand, including plastic bags over the hands and a scarf or glasses over the eyes, and never lays a bare hand on a patient who is wet with a chemical. The patient's own clothing is part of the hazard rather than part of the patient, since a soaked shirt, a boot full of powder, and a glove that holds liquid against the skin all continue the injury after the patient has left the source. A responder who is splashed washes the area at once with plenty of water, and a responder whose eyes are splashed begins irrigation without waiting to finish any other task, because the eye is the tissue that tolerates delay least.

Decontamination begins with the removal of the patient from the source and continues with the removal of everything that carries the agent, and the order within that act matters. Contaminated clothing is cut away rather than pulled over the head, because pulling a shirt over the face can drag the agent into the eyes and the mouth, and the scissors are used along the seams so that the fabric never crosses the face. Jewelry, watches, belts, shoes, and anything else that holds liquid against the skin are removed at the same time, because a ring can hold an agent against a finger and a watch can hold it against the wrist long after the arm has been washed. Dry powder is brushed off the skin before water is added, because water can turn a powder into a concentrated solution, can drive the particles into the skin, and can react with some powders to produce heat. An object embedded in the skin, whether a fragment of glass or a piece of metal, is left where it is and padded around, because pulling it out in the field may release bleeding that cannot be controlled outside an operating room. The finding that marks the end of this step is skin that no longer carries visible powder, fabric that is no longer in contact with the patient, and a responder whose own gloves and eyes are still protected. Nothing in this book authorizes the use of a neutralizing chemical on the skin, because neutralization generates heat and can add a thermal burn to a chemical one, and the correct fluid is water in large amounts.

Some agents must be handled in a way that differs from the ordinary rule of water first, and the responder must know the classes even when the exact substance is unknown. Dry lime, cement powder, and other dry alkalis are brushed away before irrigation, because adding water to quicklime produces heat and can burn the skin a second time. The elemental metals, including sodium, potassium, lithium, and magnesium, react with water and may ignite or burst on contact, so they are removed as dry particles and the skin is protected as the local protocol directs rather than washed. White phosphorus ignites when it meets the air and burns until the air is excluded, so the particles are kept under water or under a wet dressing while the patient is moved. Phenol is poorly soluble in water and can be spread by a careless wash, and the particular solvent that is used to remove it belongs to the protocol and to the provider trained in its use. Hydrofluoric acid is the agent that most deserves to be called different, because it passes through the skin, destroys the tissue beneath it, and binds the calcium and magnesium in the blood in a way that can disturb the rhythm of the heart even when the visible burn is small. None of this changes the rule that the responder protects the skin, the eyes, and the airway first, and every decision about a special agent, its removal, and its antidote belongs to the local protocol and to the clinician authorized to make it.

Irrigation is the central act of chemical burn care, and it is performed copiously, gently, and for longer than instinct suggests. Water is applied at low pressure from a clean area toward a contaminated one so that the flow carries the agent away from the eyes, the mouth, and the wound rather than across them, and the fluid is tepid rather than ice cold, because cold water constricts the vessels and can deepen the injury. Irrigation is prolonged, with a period of 20 to 30 minutes as a common teaching figure, and with alkalis, hydrofluoric acid, and concentrated agents often requiring much longer washing; the exact figure is set by the guideline and the local protocol that govern the responder's service. The patient's pain, the visible residue, and the measured acidity or alkalinity of the runoff are the findings that decide whether washing continues, and a trained responder who measures the runoff does so under the protocol that authorizes the measurement. Irrigation is interrupted only for an airway that must be opened, for a cardiac arrest that must be treated, and for the move to the vehicle, and it is resumed during transport rather than abandoned at the door. Ointments, oils, butter, toothpaste, and herbal preparations are never applied to a chemical burn in the field, because they trap the agent, hide the wound, and give the receiving team a surface that must be cleaned before it can be assessed. The working rule is that the wound is washed until the fluid runs clear and the pain has eased, and then washed again, because a burn that looks clean may still hold agent in a fold of skin.

The extent and the depth of a chemical burn are judged in spoken words, and they are judged with caution because the agent may still be spreading. The palm of the patient's own hand, including the fingers, is a rough guide to one per cent of the body surface, while the rule of nines describes the head and each arm as about 9 per cent, each leg as about 18 per cent, and the front and the back of the trunk as about 18 per cent each, and these proportions vary in a child and differ between services and editions, so the local guideline and not this page is the authority. Depth is written as a word rather than as a numeral, with a partial-thickness burn and a full-thickness burn as the two categories that matter. A superficial burn is red and painful and does not blister, a partial-thickness burn blisters or weeps and is intensely painful, and a full-thickness burn may be white, waxy, brown, or black, may look dry and leathery, and may be painless because the nerves within it are gone, so a patient whose pain is far greater than the appearance suggests may be carrying an agent still at work, and the responder describes what is seen rather than guessing at a number of degrees.

An eye that has been exposed to a chemical is an emergency of the first order, and its treatment begins at the scene and continues without pause. Irrigation starts at once with whatever clean water is available, because the damage continues while the responder looks for a better fluid, and a delay of minutes can decide whether the patient keeps the sight of that eye. The lids are held open with the fingers, the patient is asked to look in different directions while the water runs, and the flow is directed from the inner corner outward so that the agent is not washed into the other eye. A contact lens is removed if it can be seen and reached without force, because a lens can hold the agent against the surface of the eye, and the responder does not delay irrigation in order to search for it. If only one eye is affected, the other is protected by positioning the patient with the contaminated side lower, and if the agent was a powder or a vapor, both eyes are assumed to be involved until they are examined. Irrigation continues during the journey, with a period of at least 20 minutes as a common teaching figure whose exact value comes from the current guideline and the local protocol rather than from this page, and it continues until the receiving clinician says that it can stop. No neutralizing solution, no ointment, and no eye drop are placed in a chemically burned eye in the field, and the decision about what is placed in the eye belongs to the clinician and to the protocol. The finding that makes the eye case urgent even when the patient says the eye feels better is the delayed injury of an alkali, which can go on damaging the surface for hours after the pain has settled.

Chemical injury does not always arrive through the skin, and the airway is the route that kills most quickly. Gases and vapors such as ammonia, chlorine, and the products of burning plastic reach the throat and the lungs within a breath, and the finding that predicts trouble is a patient who is hoarse, who is coughing, who has a burning throat, or whose voice has changed since the exposure. Stridor, drooling, and a rising effort to breathe are the findings that mark an airway that is closing, and they may appear after a quiet interval during which the patient felt almost well. The lay first aider moves the patient into fresh air, keeps the patient sitting upright if that makes breathing easier, and calls emergency medical services at once, and the full name is used here because the abbreviation is not spoken in this book. The trained responder or paramedic supports the airway with the equipment and the protocol the service authorizes, and gives oxygen only as that protocol directs, while definitive hospital care owns the examination of the airway and the decision about an artificial airway. Smoke from a fire and fumes in an enclosed space carry the same warning, and any patient who was unconscious in such an atmosphere is treated as an airway emergency until proved otherwise. The finding that moves the responder from watching to acting is any noise in the breathing, any change in the voice, and any fall in the level of consciousness, because each of those says that the airway is losing its width.

The primary survey keeps its order of airway, breathing, circulation, disability, and exposure even at a chemical scene, but decontamination runs alongside it rather than waiting at the end. If the patient is not breathing, the airway and the breathing are treated first, while another pair of hands continues to remove clothing and to wash the skin, so that the agent is not left burning during resuscitation. If the patient is breathing and the agent is still on the skin, decontamination becomes the immediate priority, because the injury continues while the substance remains in contact and because the patient must be clean enough to be touched by the receiving team. Circulation is assessed with the same eye and hand as in any other patient, since a chemical burn can conceal bleeding from a wound made by the same incident, and bleeding that a tourniquet has controlled is not released in the field while the time of its placement is written on the patient. Disability is recorded as the level of consciousness, the pupils, and the movement of the limbs, and a limb that is deformed is splinted in the position found and is never straightened to make it look normal, because movement endangers the vessels and nerves that are still intact. Exposure is where the chemical burn is seen whole, and the responder looks in the skin folds, the armpits, the groin, the scalp, the ears, and the perineum, because a liquid pools in those places and a burn missed there will be found later by someone else. The finding that returns the responder to the start of the survey is any change for the worse, at any point, in any patient.

A chemical burn can poison the patient as well as injure the skin, and the substances that do this are few but lethal. Hydrofluoric acid is absorbed and binds calcium and magnesium in the blood, and the findings that should raise the alarm are muscle twitching, numbness, cramping, an irregular pulse, and a patient who becomes faint or unresponsive, all of which can occur when the visible burn looks small. Phenol is absorbed through intact skin and can produce weakness, sweating, a falling level of consciousness, and a disturbance of the heart rhythm, and the smell of the agent on the patient is itself a piece of information for the receiving team. The elemental metals that react with water and the phosphorus that burns in air add their own systemic hazards, and a patient exposed to any of them is moved to definitive care without waiting for the skin to look worse. The responder's part in these cases is to recognize that the patient is more ill than the wound explains, to keep the patient warm and still, to watch the breathing, the pulse, and the level of consciousness, and to say clearly in the handover which agent was involved. The specific antidote, the blood test, and the decision to give a substance into a vein belong to the clinician, to the poison control center, and to the local protocol, and no reader should take a treatment from these pages. The finding that should make a responder suspect systemic poisoning is a patient whose general condition is worse than the burn can account for, because the skin is not always the whole of a chemical injury.

Cold is a complication of chemical burn care rather than a detail of comfort, and the act of washing a patient makes it worse. Prolonged irrigation wets the skin and the clothing, the patient lies on the ground while the responders work, and burned skin can no longer hold heat or water, so the patient who is being decontaminated is also losing temperature. The responder protects the patient by using tepid water rather than cold water, by placing insulation between the patient and the ground, by working in a sheltered place where that is possible, and by covering every part of the patient that is not being washed at that moment. Wet clothing is cut away and replaced with dry coverings, and the vehicle is warmed before the patient is placed in it, because a warm ambulance is part of the treatment and not a luxury. The patient's temperature is watched during transport, and a patient who has stopped shivering while still cold is a patient whose body has given up the attempt to make heat. A body temperature such as 35 degrees Celsius is often named as the line at which cold itself becomes dangerous, and the accepted threshold varies with age and with the way the temperature is taken, so the local guideline decides the figure that is used. The finding that tells the responder that warming must become active rather than passive is a patient who is cold to the touch, who has stopped shivering, and whose level of consciousness is dulling.

The information that travels with a chemically burned patient is part of the treatment, because the receiving team cannot undo an exposure it does not know about. The name of the agent is the first item, taken from the label, the container, the placard, or the safety data sheet if one is available, and the container itself is sent with the patient when the hazardous materials team says that it is safe to do so. The time of the exposure, the duration of contact, the concentration if it is known, and the parts of the body that were affected are stated next, together with the decontamination already performed and the time at which irrigation began. The patient's allergies, other illnesses and medicines, and tetanus status where it is known are added to the handover, because a chemical burn is a wound and carries the risk of infection. Emergency medical services are told what is coming before the patient arrives, and the poison control center may be consulted for advice about a particular agent. A record of what was done, when it was done, and who did it is written where the receiving team will see it, and anything applied to the skin is named. Reassessment is a rhythm rather than a single act, so the responder repeats the airway, the breathing, the circulation, the level of consciousness, and the look at the skin, and compares each set of findings with the one before. The finding that makes this step urgent rather than routine is a patient whose wound is enlarging, whose breathing is changing, or whose pulse is becoming irregular, because in each of those cases the receiving team needs the name of the agent before the patient reaches the door.

Definitive hospital care owns the assessment and the surgery of every chemical burn that will not heal by itself, and the responder's task is to recognize which patients belong there. A full-thickness burn, a burn that crosses a joint, a burn of the hands, the face, the eyes, or the genitalia, and a burn that encircles a limb or the chest all require surgical assessment, because the swelling that follows can compress vessels, nerves, and the chest wall. A burn that covers a large part of the body, a burn in a child or in an older patient, a burn with an inhalation injury, and a burn in a patient who has other injuries from the same incident are also referred to a center with the staff and the equipment to treat them, and the destination is chosen by the local protocol and the receiving system. The operations that follow belong entirely to the surgeon, and they include the release of a constricting eschar, the removal of dead tissue, the early excision of a deep burn, and the grafting of skin, none of which is a field procedure no matter how urgent the patient appears. The chest is never bound tightly in a patient with a circumferential burn, because the swelling inside the burn is the danger and a tight bandage adds to it rather than relieving it. A chemical burn may continue to deepen after arrival, so the surgeon often waits and reassesses before operating, and the responder who understood that the injury was still active has already served the patient. The patient must also be watched for the complications that follow any serious wound, including infection, fluid loss, hypothermia, and the effects of the agent on the organs, and the handover must carry the history that makes those complications likely. The finding that changes the destination is any burn that meets the criteria for a specialist unit, and the decision about that destination belongs to the local protocol and to the receiving system rather than to the responder's preference.

To summarize, a chemical burn is an injury that continues until the agent is removed, so decontamination comes before everything except scene safety and the protection of the rescuer. The scene is approached from upwind, the hazardous materials team owns the identification and the removal of the substance, and no responder enters a contaminated space without the equipment and the authority to do so. Contaminated clothing is cut away rather than pulled over the face, dry powder is brushed off before water is added, jewelry and anything else holding liquid against the skin is removed, and an embedded object is stabilized in place and never pulled out in the field. Irrigation is copious, tepid, low pressure, and prolonged, directed from a clean area toward a contaminated one, with a period of 20 to 30 minutes as a common teaching figure that the current guideline and the local protocol are the authority on, and an exposed eye is irrigated at once and continuously from the inner corner outward while the patient is moved, with both eyes treated as involved when the agent was a powder or a vapor. Some agents behave differently, and dry alkalis, elemental metals, white phosphorus, phenol, and hydrofluoric acid each require the special handling the local protocol defines, while acids and alkalis differ in the depth they reach and alkalis go on injuring longer. The primary survey keeps its order while decontamination runs beside it, hypothermia is prevented because washing cools the patient, and pain is treated by the provider authorized to prescribe and never by a dose taken from this book. The name of the agent, the time of exposure, the duration of contact, the decontamination performed, and the time irrigation began all travel with the patient, while full-thickness burns, burns across joints, burns of the hands, face, eyes, or genitalia, circumferential burns, large burns, and burns with inhalation injury belong to definitive hospital care, where the surgeon releases, removes, excises, and grafts, and none of that is a field procedure. Every number in this chapter is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between services, countries, and editions, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, or the law of the country where you work.
