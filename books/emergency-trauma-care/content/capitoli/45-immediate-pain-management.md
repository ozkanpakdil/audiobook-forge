# Chapter 45 — Immediate Pain Management

A patient who is badly hurt will often speak about the pain before anything else, and the responder who hears only a complaint and moves past it has already missed a piece of clinical evidence. Pain is not a comfort that can wait until the bleeding has stopped and the journey is over, because pain itself quickens the heart, stiffens the breathing, and raises the demand for oxygen in a body that may already be short of it. This chapter treats pain relief as part of trauma care rather than as an addition to it, and it names the provider level that owns each act, from the lay first aider with bare hands to the trauma surgeon in the operating room. The lethal possibility to name early is pain out of proportion to the injury, because that finding can be the first sign of compartment syndrome, of an expanding collection of blood, or of bleeding inside a cavity. Every substance, amount, and interval mentioned here belongs to the local protocol and the prescriber, so nothing in this chapter can be taken as a prescription. This chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

No measure of comfort may precede the making safe of the scene, because a responder struck while kneeling beside a patient becomes a second casualty and takes the only pair of hands away from the first. The hazards that kill rescuers are moving traffic, fire and smoke, live electrical current, deep or fast water, an animal still loose, a person still violent, a structure that may collapse, and the contaminated material of the patient's blood, vomit, and tissue. The finding that a scene is unsafe often arrives before the injury does, as the smell of fuel, a wire lying in water, or a crowd that has not stopped shouting, and each such finding is a reason to wait at the edge. Personal protective equipment, or PPE, is worn before the first hand is laid on a patient, with gloves at the least and eye and mouth protection where they are available, and it is used for every patient rather than for the ones who look infectious. The lay first aider who cannot make the scene safe stays out and calls for those who can, while the trained responder and the emergency medical technician make it safe within their equipment and their authority. Only then does the work of easing pain begin, because the rescuer must survive the rescue to be of use to anyone.

Untreated pain is a burden on the body as real as the wound, which is why relief belongs to treatment and not to its aftermath. Severe pain quickens the pulse and narrows the vessels, increasing the work of a heart that may already be short of blood to pump. It stiffens the muscles of the chest and the abdomen, so a patient with a rib fracture or a belly injury breathes in small sips, and small sips lead to collapsed lung segments and infection. Pain also keeps the patient from cooperating with the acts that save life, such as lying still while a limb is splinted, keeping a dressing pressed, or turning onto a stretcher without tearing a clot. The finding that pain is doing harm is a patient who breathes in short gasps, refuses to lie flat, or grows restless while the pulse climbs without new bleeding, and that finding is treated as a complication rather than as a complaint.

Pain is also information, and it is collected before any attempt is made to remove it, because the description a patient gives is one of the few pieces of history that only the patient can supply. The site, the character, the moment it began, what makes it worse, and what makes it better are all part of the picture, and the change in that picture over time is more useful than any single answer. A spoken scale on which the patient says the pain is none, mild, moderate, or severe is enough for the field, while the numbers and the wording of any other scale differ between services and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. The assessment is recorded before a medicine is given, because the level of consciousness is judged partly by the response to a painful stimulus. Some patients cannot describe pain at all, so a small child who cries, draws the limbs up, or goes quiet and still is assessed with the help of the parent or the familiar adult, and a patient who does not speak the language of the responder may under-report pain. Grimacing, guarding, and a limb held absolutely still are recorded in the same way as a spoken score, because the body speaks for the patient who cannot.

Pain relief never displaces the order of the primary survey, and it never delays the control of hemorrhage or the journey to definitive care. Airway, breathing, circulation, disability, and exposure are attended to first, in that order, because a patient who is not breathing gains nothing from comfort, and a patient bleeding to death needs pressure and a surgeon before a medicine for pain. The principle that decides the order when two correct acts compete for the same hands is life before limb, and it applies here as life before comfort as well. Analgesia is given while the patient is kept warm, splinted, and moved, not as a reason to remain on scene and not as a substitute for the acts that stop the bleeding. The finding that tells the responder to defer relief is any patient whose airway, breathing, or circulation is still failing. The exception is the patient whose pain is so severe that it prevents the very acts that save life, such as lying still or tolerating a splint, and that balance is struck by the provider the protocol authorizes to make it.

The measure available to every provider, and to the bystander who has nothing at all, is reassurance, and it is not a small thing. A calm voice that explains what is happening, what is about to happen, and what the patient should expect removes the fear that sharpens pain, and a hand held, a face brought to the level of the patient, and a familiar person kept close are care the lay first aider can give while waiting. Silence, unexplained hurry, and a conversation held over the patient as if the patient were not there increase both fear and pain. Positioning is the next measure any provider may use, and its principle is that the body's own position of comfort is usually the safest position for pain. A patient with a chest injury who is struggling to breathe may need to sit up, one with an abdominal injury may draw the knees up, and a patient with a back injury is kept still in the position found. The responder must not force a limb toward the shape it is supposed to have, because movement of a deformed fracture can injure a vessel or a nerve that is still intact, and where the spine may be injured the position of comfort and the need to limit motion are balanced by the trained responder.

Immobilization is pain relief, because a broken bone held still stops tearing at the tissue around it and stops sending new pain with every movement. The rule of this manual is that a severely deformed fracture is splinted in the position found and is never straightened in the field to make it look normal, and the reason is the risk to the vessels and the nerves that lie close to the bone. The lay first aider may support the limb with the hands, a rolled blanket, a board, or anything that holds it against the body, and may bind it loosely enough that the fingers or toes beyond it stay warm and pink. The trained responder or paramedic adds padding over the bony points and a splint that reaches above and below the injury, and then checks what lies beyond it, because a splint that is too tight becomes a new source of pain and a new danger. The finding that a splint is too tight is a limb that has become pale or blue, a pulse that can no longer be felt below it, numbness, or pain that rises rather than falls, and the response is to loosen and reapply rather than to wait. A splint is not a treatment for the fracture, but a way of keeping the patient alive and as comfortable as possible until definitive hospital care.

Cold is a simple measure that belongs to everyone, and it works by narrowing the vessels and reducing the swelling that stretches the tissue around an injury. Cold is never placed directly on the skin, because it can freeze the skin and cause a burn of its own, and it is never placed on an open wound, on tissue that looks pale or dead, or on a limb whose circulation is already in doubt. A cloth, a bandage, or a plastic bag with a little water in it is laid between the cold source and the skin, and a short application is enough, with its length set by the local protocol and confirmed against the current guideline. A period such as 20 minutes is often described as a useful first application, and that number, like every other in this chapter, must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the safe time differs with the part of the body and the age of the patient. Cold is not a substitute for a splint, for a dressing, or for transport, and it must not delay any of them. The finding that cold should be stopped is skin that has become white, hard, or numb, or pain that is now worse than before.

An exposed wound is a painful wound, and much of the pain a responder can remove is removed by covering the injury once and leaving it alone, because air moving across a raw surface, a dressing that dries and sticks, and the repeated lifting of a dressing to check the bleeding all add pain and disturb the clot. An embedded object is stabilized in place and never removed in the field, because removal may release bleeding that cannot be controlled and may injure a structure the object is not yet touching. Cold is also a complication of trauma rather than a detail of comfort, and it makes pain worse at the same time as it makes bleeding worse. The lay first aider removes wet clothing, replaces it with dry covering, insulates the patient from the ground, and covers the head, because much of the heat is lost downward and from the scalp. The finding that warming has failed is a patient who has stopped shivering while still cold, because shivering is the body's attempt to make heat and its disappearance is a warning rather than an improvement. Every number used to describe dangerous cold, such as a body temperature below 35 degrees Celsius, is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the accepted line differs between services and editions.

Medicines that relieve pain are a powerful part of trauma care, and they are also the part of this chapter that carries the greatest risk, which is why the decision to use them belongs to a named provider and to a protocol. This manual names no substance, no amount, and no interval, because those things differ between countries, between services, and between editions of the same guideline, and because a listener must not be able to take a prescription from a book. What the manual describes is the decision: who may make it, what finding makes it urgent, what finding makes it dangerous, and what must be watched while its effect is present. The finding that makes the decision urgent is pain that is severe, that prevents the patient from cooperating with care, or that is harming the breathing and the circulation. The finding that makes it dangerous is a patient whose airway is not protected, whose breathing is already depressed, whose circulation is failing, or whose level of consciousness cannot be followed reliably. Where those findings are present, the decision belongs to the physician or to the provider the protocol authorizes to weigh them, and never to the responder's own estimate of what the patient deserves.

The range of available medicines is wide, and the choice among them belongs to the provider authorized to make it rather than to whoever happens to be first. A simple medicine may be enough for mild pain in a patient who is fully awake and not vomiting, while a stronger medicine is reserved for severe pain, and which medicine is used, and how it is given, are settled by the prescriber and the protocol rather than by the responder. The stronger medicines that dull pain also dull the patient, which is why the choice is coupled to the ability to watch the patient rather than separated from it. A nerve block, which is the deliberate numbing of a nerve to remove pain from a region, is a procedure for a trained clinician and not for the field, and it belongs to definitive care or to a service that specifically authorizes it. Nothing in this paragraph is an instruction, and every substance, amount, and interval remains subject to the local protocol and the prescriber.

The patient who is bleeding is the one in whom pain relief must be weighed most carefully, because a medicine that dulls pain may also lower the blood pressure and hide the signs of worsening shock. A patient in shock may respond differently to a medicine than a patient with a normal circulation, and the amount belongs to the provider who can see the whole patient and knows the protocol. The patient with a head injury is the second such patient, because relief that sedates confuses the examination of the level of consciousness, and the Glasgow coma scale, which is the standard way of recording that level, loses its value when the patient has been made drowsy by a medicine. Relief may still be given, because pain itself raises the pressure inside the head and worsens the injury, and the balance is struck by the physician with the monitoring and the imaging that definitive care provides. The finding that must be passed to the receiving team is the time, the substance, and the effect of any medicine given, so that the next examination of the level of consciousness can be read against it. The rule is not that the injured brain is never given relief, but that relief is given by a provider who can continue to follow the brain afterward.

The patient whose pain is out of proportion to the injury is the most important patient in this chapter, because that disproportion is a diagnostic sign and not a complaint. A limb that is tense, that hurts far more than the wound suggests, that hurts when the fingers or toes are stretched, and that is becoming numb or weak is a limb whose pressure inside may be rising toward compartment syndrome, and the correct acts are to remove anything tight, to keep the limb at the level of the heart unless the protocol says otherwise, and to move the patient toward definitive care without delay. Pain that grows while the patient is watched, or that is not eased by a splint in the correct position, is a finding that demands escalation and a report to the receiving team in plain words. A small wound with pain far beyond its size may also mean a retained fragment, a deep track, or a collection of blood expanding beneath the skin, and the responder does not probe it to find out. The sudden disappearance of pain in a limb that was painful can be as serious as the pain itself, because it may mean that the nerve or the blood supply has been lost. A belly that is rigid and tender, a pain worse when the patient moves, and chest pain that worsens with a deep breath can all be signs of bleeding inside a cavity, and none of them is treated by binding the chest tightly, which this manual forbids because it prevents the breathing the patient needs.

A teaching that must be corrected is the belief that relief must be withheld so that the examination of the abdomen or the chest stays honest. The signs that guide the surgeon, such as a rigid belly, a growing distension, localized tenderness, and a change in the level of consciousness, are not removed by reasonable relief, and the trend of those signs remains readable while the patient is comfortable. Pain also harms the patient in its own right, because a patient in agony cannot cooperate with the examination, cannot breathe deeply, and cannot be moved safely. The decision to give or withhold relief belongs to the physician and the protocol, and the observations continue before and after it in the same order and at the same intervals. The finding that changes the plan is any new or worsening sign, because that finding is reported to the physician whether or not the patient still complains of pain.

Whenever a medicine for pain is given, the monitoring that must accompany it begins at the same moment and continues until the patient is handed over. The level of consciousness, the rate and depth of breathing, the oxygen saturation, the pulse, the blood pressure, the color and warmth of the skin, and the ability to speak and swallow tell the provider whether the medicine is helping or harming. The findings that should stop any further medicine and bring the protocol's reversal plan into action are a patient who is becoming drowsy, a breathing rate that is slowing or becoming shallow, a fall in the blood pressure, and a patient who can no longer be woken, and nausea, vomiting, itching, a rash, or a swollen face or throat are recorded and reported as well. The specific danger is sedation, which is the relief of pain carried one step further into loss of alertness and of the drive to breathe, and the line between comfortable and too drowsy moves fastest in the patient who has lost blood, the patient who is cold, the patient who is old, and the patient given more than one medicine. This is why a patient who has received such a medicine is never left alone, why the observations are repeated rather than assumed, and why the receiving team is told exactly what was given. The equipment and training this monitoring requires are the reason the decision is restricted to the providers a service authorizes, and a service that cannot monitor should not authorize the medicine.

The record of what was given is part of the treatment, because the next provider must be able to read the patient's course rather than guess it. The time, the substance, the effect on the pain, and any adverse effect are written where the receiving team will see them and spoken aloud at the handover, and the same care is taken with the measures that were not medicines. A patient moved with an embedded object or a tourniquet in place carries the times and details of those measures in the same record, because the receiving team must know what is in the wound and how long the limb has been without flow. The finding that the record is incomplete is any gap that forces the next provider to start the story again, and a gap of that kind is itself a harm.

The lay first aider may do far more for pain than most people expect, and may not do the one thing most people reach for. The lay first aider reassures, explains, stays present, positions and supports the injury, covers the wound, applies cold through a barrier, keeps the patient warm, and asks someone to call emergency medical services. The lay first aider does not give any medicine, does not take a medicine from the patient's own pocket without instruction, does not remove an embedded object, does not straighten a deformed limb, and does not loosen a tourniquet that has controlled bleeding. The reason for the last three rules is the same, which is that the act may release bleeding or injure tissue that is still intact, and pain is not a reason to break the rule. The trained first responder and the emergency medical technician work within a service, a kit, and a protocol, and their contribution is broader because they can measure and monitor: they assess and record the pain, splint and immobilize properly, apply cold, keep the patient warm, and repeat the observations that show whether the injury is worsening. Some services authorize them to give a simple pain-relieving medicine under a protocol or the direction of a clinician, while the finding that sends them higher is pain out of proportion, a limb that is swelling or becoming numb, a changing breathing pattern, or a falling level of consciousness.

The paramedic carries a wider set of authorizations and the monitoring equipment they require, and may make decisions about stronger relief within the local protocol. That authority carries the duty to watch the patient continuously, to record the response, and to withhold or stop a medicine when the findings call for it, and the protocol decides the substance, the amount, and the interval, none of which this manual states. The nurse meets the patient at the door of the emergency department, on the ward, or in the ambulance bay, and the nurse's part is assessment, administration, and surveillance: repeating the assessment the field began, giving the medicine the prescriber ordered, and watching for the response and the adverse effects listed here. The nurse is often the person who notices that the pain is out of proportion, that the dressing is soaked, that the limb has changed color, or that the breathing has become shallow, and that finding is reported to the physician at once. The physician decides, weighing the injury, the patient, the circulation, the breathing, and the diagnosis against one another, choosing whether a medicine is given and setting the monitoring that accompanies it, and keeping the diagnostic question open. The finding the physician treats as a surgical emergency rather than as a pain problem is a limb that is tense and painful on stretch with a failing pulse, because that finding may need an operation to release the pressure inside.

The trauma surgeon owns the definitive treatment of the injuries that cause the pain, and many acts that remove pain permanently are surgical acts. The surgeon explores the wound, removes a bullet or a fragment when it is clinically indicated and never because a responder decided it should come out, repairs a torn vessel or tendon or nerve, releases the pressure inside a limb when compartment syndrome is confirmed, and controls bleeding inside a cavity. A nerve block may be placed by a trained clinician as part of definitive care, in a setting where the patient can be monitored and the procedure and its complications can be managed, and nothing in this chapter authorizes any of those procedures in the field. There is also a group of patients for whom relief is not a step toward a cure but the whole of the care that can be offered, and this manual does not turn away from them. A patient who is dying, one beyond the reach of surgery, and one who must wait while others are treated first are still owed warmth, shelter, a position that eases breathing, and the presence of someone who stays. The decision about medicine for such a patient belongs to the physician and the protocol, and while nobody in this manual is authorized to end a life or hasten a death, comfort is part of care even when survival is not possible.

To summarize, pain in trauma is part of the illness rather than a complaint that follows it, and it is treated by every provider at the level that provider's training, equipment, and protocol allow. The scene is made safe and personal protective equipment is worn before any care begins, the lethal possibility of pain out of proportion to the injury is named early, and the primary survey in the order of ABCDE comes before any measure of comfort and is never delayed by it. The measures available to everyone are reassurance, positioning, immobilization, cold applied through a barrier, protection of the wound, warmth, and a calm presence, and each has a finding that shows whether it is working and a finding that shows it is not. Medicines that relieve pain belong to the provider whom the service authorizes, and this chapter names no substance, no amount, and no interval, because those are set by the local protocol and the prescriber; what is described is who makes the decision, what makes it urgent, what makes it dangerous, and what must be monitored. The monitoring that accompanies any such medicine watches the level of consciousness, the breathing, the pulse, the blood pressure, and the skin, and a patient who becomes drowsy, whose breathing slows, or whose pressure falls is one in whom the medicine must stop and help must be called. The patient whose pain is out of proportion, whose limb becomes tense or numb, or whose pain suddenly disappears is a diagnostic emergency and is moved toward definitive care. Every time, temperature, pressure, and number in this chapter is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.
