# Chapter 48 — Surgical Treatment

Definitive surgical treatment is the final stage of trauma care and the stage that decides what survival will mean for the patient, and it begins only after the work of the field has been done correctly or deliberately left undone. The operations described here are performed by surgeons in a hospital, and not one of them is a field procedure, even when the injury looks desperate and the hospital seems far away. They are described so that the first responder can understand what each operation is for, what it achieves, and what the prehospital provider must have done or refused to do to make it possible. That understanding shapes the first hour, because a dressing that is not lifted and a tourniquet whose time is written on the patient become part of the operation that follows. Every procedure in this chapter belongs to definitive hospital care, and the chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country where you work.

The surgical chain begins before the operating room, in the decision not to become a second casualty, because no patient is saved by a rescuer who is injured at the scene. Traffic, fire, live electricity, fast water, an unstable structure, an animal, and violence are the hazards that kill rescuers, and the patient's own blood, vomit, and tissue carry infection through a break in the skin or a splash into the eye. Those hazards belong to the place where the injury happened, and the responder who cannot make that place safe does not enter it, while the trained responder and the paramedic work only within the authority and the equipment their service provides. Inside the hospital the hazards change shape rather than disappear, and the operating team protects itself with personal protective equipment, with safe handling of needles and blades, and with a plan for a patient who is agitated, intoxicated, or violent. The finding that must travel with the patient is any remaining hazard and any exposure that occurred, because the safety of the next person to touch the patient is part of the same treatment.

The first purpose of any trauma operation is hemostasis, which is the stopping of bleeding at its source under direct vision rather than through a dressing. Hemostasis exists because some bleeding cannot be reached by a hand, a bandage, or a tourniquet, and because a vessel that has retracted into a cavity will keep filling that cavity while the surface looks quiet. The surgeon opens the region, finds the torn vessel, and controls it by pressure at the bleeding point, by tying it off where it can be sacrificed, or by repairing it where the tissue beyond it depends on the flow. What this achieves is a circulation that can be sustained and a patient whose remaining injuries can then be treated, and no field measure replaces it. The prehospital acts that make it possible are all acts of restraint, which are pressure held without lifting the dressing, a tourniquet left in place with its time recorded, a foreign body left undisturbed, and a deformed limb left in the position found. The acts that make it harder are the probing finger, the blindly placed clamp, the loosened tourniquet, and the well-meant straightening of a limb that tears the clot.

An operation also depends on the story that arrives with the patient, and that story has a fixed shape the receiving team listens for. The mechanism of injury is given first, because it predicts which structures lie along the path of the energy and which cavity must be examined first. The time of the injury is given next, because every interval after it changes the urgency of imaging and the order of the operating list. What was done is then stated plainly, including direct pressure, a tourniquet and the exact time it was applied, splints, dressings, and any pain relief given before arrival by the provider authorized to give it. What was not done matters just as much, and the team must hear that an embedded object was left in place, that a bullet was not extracted, that a wound was not explored or closed, and that no attempt was made to straighten a severely deformed fracture. Any change observed on the way, such as a patient who became drowsy or a dressing that soaked through, belongs in the same report, because it is strong evidence that bleeding may still be going on.

Debridement is the surgical removal of tissue that is dead, dying, or so heavily contaminated that it cannot heal, and surgical exploration is the examination of the wound's depths that decides what must be removed and what can be repaired. Debridement exists because devitalized muscle, fat, and skin have no blood supply to carry defense or repair, and because such tissue feeds the bacteria that turn a survivable wound into a lethal infection. The surgeon examines the wound in good light, takes away what will not live, washes the remaining tissue, and repeats the process over the following days as the border between living and dead becomes clear. What this achieves is a wound with a blood supply and a clean surface, which is the only condition under which closure, skin grafting, or healing by itself can succeed. Exploration achieves a precise list of what has been divided and what is intact, and that list decides every repair that follows. A first responder must never probe a wound with a finger or an instrument, must never widen it, and must never test a structure by pulling on it, because each of those acts can complete a partial injury and restart bleeding that had stopped.

Vascular repair restores the flow of blood through a torn artery or vein, and its purpose is to save the limb while protecting the life of the patient who owns it. It exists because a limb whose main artery is divided will die within hours, and because the same injury can drain the circulation long before the limb is ever in question. The surgeon removes the damaged segment, rejoins the healthy ends, or replaces the gap with a length of the patient's own vein, and the repair is then protected by stable alignment of the limb and by close watching for bleeding and for swelling. What it achieves is a limb with a pulse and a patient whose circulation has stopped leaking, and the result depends heavily on how long the tissue was without blood. The field makes this operation possible by controlling hemorrhage without destroying the vessels, by splinting in the position found, by keeping the patient warm, and by reaching a surgical center without delay, and it makes the operation impossible by probing, by clamping blindly, or by loosening a tourniquet that has already controlled the bleeding. When flow returns to tissue that has been starved, that tissue may swell, release substances that harm the body, and provoke a dangerous rhythm of the heart, which is why the surgeon may release pressure inside the limb at the same operation. A period of 2 hours is often discussed as the point at which muscle and nerve begin to suffer lasting harm, and that interval, like every figure in this chapter, is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because tolerance differs with temperature, with age, and with the injury itself.

Tendon repair reconnects a muscle to the bone it moves, and nerve repair reconnects the pathway that carries command and feeling, and both are operations of function rather than of survival. A divided tendon leaves a hand or a foot without power, and a divided nerve leaves skin without feeling and muscle without command, and the loss of feeling is itself dangerous because a numb hand will be burned, cut, or crushed without warning. The surgeon brings the tendon ends together with fine sutures and protects the repair with a splint, and for a nerve the surgeon aligns the ends without tension and may need a length of nerve from elsewhere in the body to bridge a gap. The timing is a surgical judgment, because a clean wound may be repaired at once while a heavily contaminated or infected wound may have the repair delayed until the wound is safe. What these operations achieve is the possibility of recovery and never a promise, since a nerve regrows slowly and incompletely and the result is better in a young patient and in a clean wound. The field must not grasp at a tendon, must not pull on it, must not ask the patient to move against resistance, and must not close the wound over it, and instead it covers the wound, splints in the position found, and records the movement and feeling that were lost. Any new loss of feeling or movement after splinting must be passed on at handover, because that points to pressure that has to be relieved.

Fracture reduction is the manipulation or the operation that restores a broken bone to a position in which it can heal and in which the vessels and nerves beside it are not compressed. It exists because a displaced fracture can kink an artery, stretch a nerve, and lock a joint, and because alignment is the first step toward stability and toward the recovery of function. Reduction achieves a limb that can be held, a wound that can be examined, and a circulation that can be preserved, and it is performed where the patient can be monitored and where bleeding can be controlled if a clot is disturbed. That last point is the reason the first responder never straightens a severely deformed fracture to make it look normal, because moving the fragments can release a clot, complete a partly torn vessel, and turn a contained injury into a free hemorrhage. The field splints the limb in the position found, checks and records what lies beyond the injury, and moves the patient toward the place where reduction can be done safely. The finding that makes reduction urgent rather than elective is a limb that is pale, pulseless, or numb, and even then the reduction belongs to a clinician and not to the roadside.

External fixation holds a broken bone with pins that pass through the skin into the bone and are joined by a frame outside the limb, and it exists because some fractures must be stabilized before the soft tissue can accept a permanent implant. It achieves alignment, control of movement, and access to the wound, so that dressings can be changed and dead tissue removed without losing the position of the bone, and it is used for the open fracture with heavy contamination, where metal inside the wound would give bacteria a surface to grow on. Internal fixation holds the fracture with plates, screws, or rods placed on or inside the bone, and it is the definitive stabilization that allows early movement and reliable healing. Its existence rests on the fact that a bone held still heals in the position it is set, and the surgeon chooses the device according to the bone, the pattern of the break, and the condition of the skin and the muscle, which is a judgment no manual can make. Internal fixation is often deferred in a patient who is cold, still bleeding, or infected, and there is nothing wrong with that delay, because the surgeon is choosing the moment that gives the patient the best chance. The field must never remove, loosen, or adjust a frame, must not use it as a handle for lifting the limb, and must not let anything pull on it during transfer, and its task is to immobilize the limb as it lies and to report exactly how it was found.

Damage control is the philosophy that governs the care of a patient who is too ill to survive the definitive operation, and it reorders the surgeon's whole plan around that fact. It exists because a long and technically perfect operation can kill a patient who is cold, whose blood will not clot, and whose tissues are starved of oxygen, and because those three problems worsen one another in a circle that only resuscitation can break. The first operation is therefore short and focused, and it controls the bleeding, contains the contamination, packs the cavity, and closes the skin only enough to protect what lies beneath. What this achieves is a patient who reaches an intensive care unit, or ICU, alive, where warmth, blood, and time restore the conditions under which a longer operation becomes safe. The definitive repair is then performed in a second planned operation and the final closure in a third, and a wound left open or a fracture left in a temporary frame is a sign of correct judgment rather than of neglect. The listener should carry away the sequence that puts life before limb, in which hemorrhage comes first, contamination second, protection third, and only then the perfect restoration of a limb that can wait.

Fasciotomy is the operation that opens the tough covering around a group of muscles to release pressure that has risen inside a limb, and it is performed to prevent the death of muscle and nerve. It exists because bleeding and swelling inside a closed space can raise the pressure until the small vessels that feed the muscle are compressed, and because muscle starved in that way dies and releases substances that threaten the kidneys and the heart. The surgeon makes a long opening through the skin and the fascia so that the muscle can expand, and the wound is left open afterward because closing it would recreate the pressure that was just relieved. What fasciotomy achieves is a limb with living muscle and a patient protected from the consequences of muscle death, and the result depends on how early the pressure is released, since a late operation saves neither muscle nor function. The field must not create the condition in the first place, so it does not bind a limb tightly, it releases a bandage that has become constricting, it splints without compression, and it moves the patient quickly when pain is severe out of proportion to the wound. The finding that must be reported is pain that increases when the muscles are stretched, together with a limb that is tense and a region of skin that has lost feeling, because a pulse that can still be felt does not rule out the problem. A pressure measured inside the compartment may be quoted as the number that makes the diagnosis, and any such figure is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the accepted value changes with the patient and with the service.

Amputation is the removal of a limb or a part of a limb, and in trauma it appears in two forms that must not be confused. A traumatic amputation is an injury in which the part has already been torn away, and the surgeon's work is to complete the debridement, to shape the stump so that it can heal, and to decide whether the part can be rejoined. An elective amputation is a deliberate operation on a limb that cannot be saved or that threatens life, chosen when the vessels, the nerves, and the muscle are destroyed beyond repair, or when infection and muscle death are spreading faster than they can be controlled. What amputation achieves is survival and a stump that can later be fitted, which is a better outcome than a limb that kills its owner. Replantation, which is the reattachment of a severed part, is an operation of vessels, nerves, bone, tendon, and skin performed in a fixed order, and it succeeds best after a clean cut and worst after a crush or a tear. The field must never complete an amputation, must control the stump with pressure and packing and a tourniquet where the protocol permits, and must send the severed part wrapped in clean material, kept cool, and never placed directly on ice or in water. The decision about reattachment belongs to a trauma center, and the time the part has been without blood is one of the figures that guides it, to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

Removal of a foreign body is a surgical decision made when removal serves the patient, and it is never a field procedure. It exists because a retained object such as a bullet, a fragment of metal, a piece of glass, or a splinter of wood can lie in a joint and destroy it, can press on a nerve and cause pain, can sit in a vessel and travel, can enter the eye or the airway, or can slowly release a substance that harms the body. The surgeon removes what is indicated, leaves what is harmless, and searches with imaging and with the track of the wound rather than with a probing finger, and the operation is planned around the structures that lie along the way. What removal achieves is the relief of a specific problem, and the decision must weigh the damage of the search against the harm of leaving the object. A bullet that remains in the body follows a particular rule, and the first of those rules is that a first responder does not extract bullets, because removal is a surgical decision made when there is a clinical reason and because many bullets that cause no problem are left in place and watched. The wound track is not the same as the path of the bullet, since a bullet can be deflected by bone and can end far from where the track appears to point. The field stabilizes an embedded object in place, builds padding around it, moves the patient with it undisturbed, and describes the entry, the exit if there is one, and the object that was left, because removal may release hemorrhage that cannot be controlled and may damage structures that are still intact.

Anesthesia and pain relief are decisions made by the provider authorized to make them under a protocol, and this manual deliberately names no substance, no amount, no route, and no frequency. The surgical point is that an operation cannot proceed safely in a patient whose pain is uncontrolled, whose breathing is impaired, or whose stomach is full, so the anesthesia provider assesses the airway, the lungs, and the patient's recent intake before any procedure. What the field contributes is honesty about what was given, when it was given, and by whom, because the receiving team must not add a second decision on top of an unknown first one. Pain that is severe and out of proportion to the wound is also a clinical finding, because it may be the first sign of compartment syndrome, and it must be reported rather than simply relieved. The finding that makes pain control urgent is a patient whose pain is worsening despite splinting and positioning, and that finding belongs in the handover as a warning rather than as a complaint.

Antibiotics and protection against tetanus belong to the prescriber and to the local protocol, and the surgical decision that surrounds them is a decision about timing and about source control. The reason is that no antibiotic reaches dead tissue or a collection of pus, so the surgeon must open, drain, and remove before the substance can work, and the substance and the amount are chosen by the clinician rather than by this book. The finding that makes the decision urgent is a wound that is deep, heavily contaminated, caused by a bite, or already showing the signs of spreading infection, together with a patient whose vaccination history is unknown or incomplete. What the operation achieves in that setting is source control, in which the infected material is removed, the cavity is drained, and the wound is left open so that it can be watched and cleaned. The field's part is to raise the question early, to describe the wound and the patient accurately, and to refuse to make a prescription out of a manual.

The replacement of blood and the correction of clotting belong to definitive hospital care, and the reason is that they treat a patient whose bleeding has been controlled rather than substitute for controlling it. The surgeon and the resuscitation team decide what is given, in what proportion, and how the patient is warmed, and those decisions are made against the patient's response rather than against a formula printed anywhere. What the field contributes is a careful estimate of blood lost, the number of dressings soaked, the time a tourniquet was applied, and the trend of the patient's level of consciousness and pulse, because these allow the receiving team to judge how much has gone. A patient who is cold, whose blood will not clot, and whose tissues are acidic is a patient in whom surgery must be short, which is the damage-control principle seen from the resuscitation side. The listener should carry away one rule, which is that the operation that stops the bleeding is the treatment, and everything given into a vein supports the patient until that operation is done.

Imaging is the surgeon's map, and it is performed to answer specific questions rather than to look at everything. Computed tomography, or CT, shows the cavities of the chest, the abdomen, and the pelvis and can reveal bleeding, air, and injury to organs that no hand can feel, while ultrasound can be brought to the bedside and repeated, and a chest radiograph shows the lungs, the space around them, and the position of tubes and lines. What imaging achieves is a plan, because the operation that is chosen depends on which structure is injured, and a patient whose circulation is failing may go to the operating room without imaging at all, since the delay would cost more than the information gains. The field's report decides what is imaged and how urgently, because the mechanism of injury and the patient's condition point to the region that must be examined first. The limits of imaging must be understood as well, since a normal image does not exclude an injury, and a picture that does not fit the patient must never overrule the patient.

Closure of a wound is the last step of the surgical sequence and never the first, and the decision to close is made only when the conditions for healing are present. The surgeon closes a clean wound at once, delays closure of a contaminated wound until it has been cleaned and watched, and leaves a wound open when closing it would trap infection inside. What delayed closure achieves is a wound that can be inspected and cleaned each day, and it is the reason a patient may be told that a wound will be closed later rather than now. The field must never close a wound with sutures, staples, adhesive strips, or tissue adhesive, and must never seal a wound that is deep, contaminated, caused by a bite, or holding a foreign body, because closure over such a wound converts a surface problem into a deep infection. The finding that tells the team a wound is ready to close is a wound that is clean, living, and without spreading redness or discharge, and that judgment is made by the clinician who can see it.

Infection that follows trauma is often a surgical problem, because a collection of pus, a piece of dead bone, or a closed space full of contaminated fluid cannot be cured by any substance alone. The surgeon drains the collection, removes the dead tissue, and opens the spaces so that the wound can heal from within, and the operation may need to be repeated until the infection is controlled. What this achieves is the removal of the environment in which bacteria grow, which is the only way some infections are cured. Not every injury that once required an operation now does, and the trauma team may treat an injury to a solid organ such as the liver, the spleen, or a kidney by watching the patient closely instead of opening the abdomen, because an operation on a bleeding organ can itself cause harm. What non-operative management requires is the ability to examine the patient repeatedly, to measure the circulation, and to have imaging and an operating room available within minutes if the patient changes, and the finding that ends that plan is a patient who becomes unstable, whose abdomen becomes more tender and distended, or whose need for blood and fluid keeps rising. The field's part in prevention is surgical in spirit even when no operation is possible, because it removes visible dirt and debris by irrigation, covers the wound, keeps the patient warm, and delivers the patient to care without delay.

The operating room is a place where a team works in a fixed order, and the patient enters it only after the team has confirmed who the patient is, what is known, and what is planned. The surgeon, the anesthesia provider, the nurses, and the technicians each have a role, and the first minutes are spent on the same questions the field asked, which are the airway, the breathing, the circulation, the level of consciousness, and the exposure that reveals hidden injury. What this achieves is that no injury found in the field is lost between the door and the table, and that the operation matches the patient rather than the label on a chart. Documentation is part of the operation, because the tourniquet time, the medicines given before arrival, the wounds covered, and the changes observed are all things the team must have. After the operation the patient is watched as carefully as before it, because the complications of trauma do not end when the bleeding stops, and the patient may return to an intensive care unit, or ICU, for warming, for support of breathing, and for repeated examination. What the team watches for is bleeding that restarts, a limb that becomes tense and painful, a wound that becomes red and foul, a fever, a falling level of consciousness, and difficulty in breathing, since each of these points to a complication that can still be treated if it is found early. The chain from the first hand on the wound to the last dressing in the ward is one treatment, and every link reports to the next.

Hypothermia is a surgical problem and not a detail of comfort, because a cold patient bleeds more, clots poorly, and tolerates both anesthesia and operation worse than a warm one. The patient becomes cold from blood loss, from lying on a cold surface, from undressing for examination, and from fluids that are not warmed, and the operating room must continue the warming that the field began. What warming achieves is a patient in whom the clotting system can work and in whom the heart is less likely to develop a dangerous rhythm, and it is pursued from the scene to the ward without a pause. A body temperature of 35 degrees Celsius is often named as the point at which cold becomes dangerous, and that figure, like every temperature, time, pressure, and depth in this chapter, is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the line differs with the patient and with the method of measurement. The field's part is to cover the patient before the journey, to insulate against the ground, to replace wet clothing, and to keep the vehicle warm, and it is done at the same moment as the bleeding is controlled.

A manual can explain what an operation is for, but it cannot teach the operation, and the difference matters at the moment a reader is tempted to act. Every skill described in this chapter is learned with a teacher, practiced under supervision, and certified by an authority that also defines what may be done without it, while local protocol decides which procedures are authorized, which equipment is carried, and which hospital receives the patient. Nothing here names a drug, an amount, a route, or a frequency, and nothing here should be read as permission to perform a surgical act outside the training and authorization that the reader holds. Where this chapter names a time, a temperature, a depth, or a pressure, it explains the reasoning and never sets a rule, because such figures differ between services, countries, and editions of the same guideline. The reader is asked to bring this understanding to training, to keep that training current, and to work within the system that supervises the care.

The prehospital contribution to a good operation is a set of acts and a set of refusals, and the refusals are as important as the acts. The acts are to control hemorrhage with pressure and, where the protocol allows, with packing or a tourniquet, to keep the airway open and the breathing working, to splint what is broken in the position found, to cover wounds with clean material, to keep the patient warm, to observe and record changes, and to move the patient to the right hospital without unnecessary delay. The refusals are to remove an embedded object, to extract a bullet, to probe or explore a wound, to close a wound, to straighten a severely deformed fracture, to loosen a tourniquet that has controlled bleeding, and to bind the chest tightly. Each refusal has a reason that can be spoken aloud, because an object may be plugging a vessel, a bullet belongs to a surgical decision, a wound closed over contamination becomes a deep infection, a straightened limb can tear a vessel, a released tourniquet can bleed again where no one can restore control, and a bound chest cannot expand. A constructed illustration, offered as a construction and never as a real patient or a real incident, shows how the order decides, when a patient arrives with a crushed leg, a tourniquet that has controlled the bleeding, a pale face, and a pulse that is fast and thin. The leg is the injury everyone can see and the temptation is to take the patient to the operating room for the limb at once, but the team treats the circulation first, warms the patient, replaces blood under its own protocol, and performs a short operation that controls bleeding and contamination, leaving the leg in a temporary frame with its wounds open. Only when the patient is warm and stable does the team return for the vascular repair, the debridement, and the fixation, and if the limb cannot be saved, the amputation is performed to save the patient, because life before limb is a sequence and not a slogan.

To summarize, surgical treatment is the definitive stage of trauma care, and every procedure in it exists for a reason that the first responder can understand and must respect. Hemostasis stops bleeding at its source under vision, and it is made possible by pressure held without lifting, by a tourniquet left in place with its time recorded, and by nothing embedded being disturbed. Debridement and exploration remove dead and contaminated tissue and reveal what is torn, vascular repair restores flow to a limb and a circulation to a patient, tendon and nerve repair restore movement and feeling once survival is secure, reduction and external and internal fixation restore and hold alignment, fasciotomy releases pressure that would kill muscle, amputation is chosen when a limb cannot be saved or threatens life, and removal of a foreign body is a surgical decision made only when it serves the patient. The damage-control philosophy governs the patient who is too ill for the definitive operation, and it puts the control of bleeding first, the containment of contamination second, and the complete repair later, because a cold patient whose blood will not clot cannot survive a long operation. The prehospital provider makes these operations possible by controlling hemorrhage, protecting wounds, splinting in the position found, keeping the patient warm, recording every time and every change, and reaching the right hospital without delay, and makes them impossible by removing embedded objects, extracting bullets, probing or closing wounds, straightening deformed limbs, loosening a tourniquet, or binding a chest. The chain from the first hand on the wound to the last dressing in the ward is a single treatment, and every link depends on the honesty and the restraint of the one before it. Every time, temperature, pressure, and depth named anywhere in this chapter is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between services, countries, and editions. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, or the law of the country in which you work.
