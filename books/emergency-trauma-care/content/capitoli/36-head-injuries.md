# Chapter 36 — Head Injuries

A head injury is the injury that most often kills a patient who is still talking, and the responder who understands that sentence will act differently from the one who does not. The lethal possibility to be named first is bleeding inside the skull, and the finding that reveals it may be nothing more alarming than a patient who is drowsy, who repeats a question, or who vomits once and then seems to recover. No care begins before the scene has been made safe, and the hazards of a head injury scene are the ones that produced it, which are moving traffic on a road, a fall from a height above a surface that may still be unstable, fire, a live electrical cable, deep water, an animal that is still loose, a violent person who may strike again, and a structure that may collapse. The patient's own blood and vomit are hazards as well, so gloves and eye protection, or whatever barrier is actually at hand, come before the first touch. This chapter is educational material and it does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work, and every act described here is named with the provider level that owns it.

The skull is a closed box that holds the brain, the vessels that feed it, and the fluid that cushions it, and the box cannot stretch when something inside it grows. Blood that collects inside that space has nowhere to go, so the pressure within the skull rises and is passed on to the brain, which suffers first at the place where the blood lies and then everywhere as the pressure spreads. The structures that are compressed most easily are the ones that control the level of consciousness and the size of the pupils, which is why those two observations carry so much meaning after a blow to the head. The same pressure can force part of the brain downward through the narrow openings at the base of the skull, and that movement is the final and most lethal step of a hemorrhage that was small when it began. The injury outside the skull is therefore never the whole story, because a scalp wound may be trivial while the bleeding that will kill the patient is entirely hidden.

The mechanism of injury is the first clinical evidence, and it predicts damage that the eye cannot see. A fall from a height of 3 meters, a figure offered only as a teaching illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, predicts a deceleration injury in which the brain moves within the skull and tears where it is tethered. A vehicle collision predicts the same, and the direction of the impact decides where the brain strikes the bone, while an assault with a fist or a weapon predicts a blow that may leave no mark on the scalp. A blast predicts a pressure wave and fragments, and a fall from standing in an older patient, or any blow in a patient who takes a medicine that thins the blood, can produce a hemorrhage that nobody expects from the size of the event. The energy that reached the head is what matters rather than the size of the wound, and the responder who asks how the patient was injured is already thinking about what may be bleeding inside.

Scalp wounds bleed in a way that frightens everyone who sees them and that is rarely dangerous in itself, and the reason lies in the anatomy of the scalp. The scalp carries an unusually rich supply of blood vessels, and those vessels are held open by the firm layer beneath the skin, so a small cut can pour a startling volume over the face and the clothing while the total loss stays modest. The lay first aider controls such bleeding with firm direct pressure over a clean pad, exactly as for bleeding anywhere else, and does not lift the pad to see whether the flow has stopped, adding further layers on top instead if blood shows through. The opposite mistake is to dismiss the scalp wound as a nuisance and to stop watching the patient, because a wound on the scalp is a marker of energy that reached the skull and a fracture may lie beneath it. The trained responder examines the wound gently, without probing it with a finger or an instrument, and records what was seen while continuing to observe the patient.

A fracture of the skull may be open, with a break in the skin over it, or closed, and it may be a simple crack or a depressed segment that has been pushed inward against the brain. The findings that raise the suspicion are a wound or a swelling over the bone, a step or a soft depression felt through the scalp, and bruising that appears behind the ear or around both eyes some hours after the injury. Clear fluid, or blood mixed with clear fluid, escaping from the nose or an ear points to a fracture at the base of the skull, and that fluid is cerebrospinal fluid, which surrounds the brain and has leaked through the covering that should contain it. A fracture at the base of the skull may also produce bleeding from the ear, and the responder must not pack the ear or the nose to stop it, because the blood and the fluid need a way out and packing may push infection inward. When the bone is exposed or the brain itself is visible, the wound is covered with a clean dressing that is laid over it rather than pressed into it, and no attempt is made to push bone fragments back into place. An embedded object is stabilized in place and the patient is moved with it, because removal may release a hemorrhage that cannot be controlled and may destroy tissue that is still intact, and the confirmation of a fracture belongs to definitive hospital care.

Concussion is the most common form of traumatic brain injury, and it is a disturbance of brain function rather than a visible wound, which is why it is described as a diagnosis of exclusion. It is suspected when a patient who has struck the head reports a headache, dizziness, nausea, blurred vision, or a feeling of being slowed down, and when the patient cannot recall the moments before or after the impact. Loss of consciousness is neither required nor a reliable measure of severity, because a patient who never lost consciousness may still be confused, while a patient who was briefly unconscious may recover fully. The trained responder or the physician makes the diagnosis only after the more dangerous causes of the same complaints, above all bleeding inside the skull, have been considered and excluded. A patient suspected of concussion is not returned to sport, to driving, or to work on the day of the injury, and the decision about when any of those may resume belongs to a clinician after the local protocol has been followed.

The level of consciousness is the most important observation the responder repeats, because it is the earliest and most sensitive sign that the brain is losing ground. The assessment begins with speech, asking the patient's name, the place, the day, and what happened, and the answer reveals whether the patient is fully awake and oriented or merely capable of words. If the patient does not answer, the responder calls the patient's name loudly and then applies a painful stimulus in the way the local protocol teaches, and the response to voice and to pain is graded and recorded rather than described vaguely. A change in the level of consciousness carries more meaning than any single measurement, so the responder writes the finding together with the time it was made and compares it with the previous entry. The Glasgow coma scale is the formal tool for this purpose where the responder has been trained in it, and it turns an impression into a number that two people can compare, while the trend across repeated examinations matters more than any one score. A patient who is becoming harder to rouse, or who is roused and then drifts away again, has given the responder the finding that changes the plan.

Bleeding inside the skull is the injury that kills while the patient is talking, and it takes several forms that behave differently and demand the same urgency. Blood may collect between the skull and the tough outer covering of the brain, which is called an epidural hematoma, and this is often the result of a torn artery and expands quickly. Blood may collect beneath that covering and over the surface of the brain, which is called a subdural hematoma, and this is more often venous and may develop over hours or days, especially in an older patient or in one who takes a medicine that thins the blood. Blood may also collect within the brain itself after a severe blow, or in the space filled with cerebrospinal fluid, and every one of these collections raises the pressure inside the closed box. The findings are the same in their meaning whatever the name, and they are a level of consciousness that is falling, a headache that is worsening, vomiting, a seizure, and pupils that have become unequal. The name is a matter for the hospital, and the responder's task is to recognize that the patient is deteriorating and to reach definitive care before the deterioration becomes irreversible.

The lucid interval is the trap that must be taught to every responder, and it is the period in which a patient who has bled inside the skull appears to recover before collapsing. A constructed illustration, offered as a construction rather than as a real patient, is the young person who is struck on the side of the head, is briefly stunned, then stands, speaks normally, and asks to go home while a torn artery continues to bleed beneath the bone. The patient may remain lucid for a period often described as lasting from minutes to several hours, and any such interval is only an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because its length varies with the vessel, the patient, and the injury. When the volume of blood finally exceeds what the skull can accommodate, the level of consciousness falls quickly, one pupil may widen, and the patient may stop breathing. The lesson is not that the responder should wait for the collapse, because waiting is exactly what loses the patient, but that an apparently recovered patient after a significant blow to the head belongs in a place where imaging and a surgeon are available.

A change in the level of consciousness alters the plan more than any other single finding, and it must never be explained away by alcohol, by fatigue, or by the emotion of the scene. A patient who was alert and is now drowsy, who was answering sensibly and is now repeating the same question, or who has become restless and combative without a reason, may be showing the first effect of a rising pressure inside the skull. The same finding may come from a lack of oxygen, from a fall in the circulation caused by bleeding elsewhere, from a low blood sugar, from a seizure that has passed, or from a substance the patient has taken, and the responder cannot tell these apart in the field. The correct act is therefore to treat the change as serious, to correct within the responder's scope only what can be corrected there, and to move the patient toward the assessment that can separate the causes. The lay first aider does not try to arouse a patient with food, drink, or a slap, and does not leave a patient who has become drowsy unattended for any reason.

The pupils are examined together and compared with each other, because a difference between them points to something pressing on the structures that control them. A pupil that has become wider and no longer narrows when a light is shone into it, on the same side as the injury, points to pressure on that side, and it is one of the last findings before the patient is lost. A difference between the pupils may also come from an injury to the eye itself, from a previous eye operation, or from a medicine the patient has used, so the finding is a reason to act and not a diagnosis. The lay first aider may look at the pupils and report what is seen, but the interpretation belongs to the trained responder and to the physician. The finding that changes the plan is a pupil that was equal and has become unequal, or one that was reactive and has stopped responding to light, and it means the patient must reach imaging and a surgeon as fast as is safe.

Vomiting after a head injury is common and it is also a warning, and the responder must decide which of the two it is in the particular patient. A single episode in a patient who is otherwise alert and improving may come from the shock of the injury, while repeated vomiting, or vomiting in a patient whose level of consciousness is falling, points to a rising pressure inside the skull. The immediate danger of vomiting is the airway, because a patient who is drowsy may breathe the stomach contents into the lungs, and that injury can be fatal on its own. The correct response is to turn the patient onto one side, or into the position that lets the vomit drain, while keeping the head and the neck in line with the body when a spinal injury is suspected. The lay first aider clears the mouth with a finger wrapped in cloth only when something is visible, does not put a finger blindly into the throat, and gives nothing by mouth to a patient who is drowsy or vomiting. The trained responder protects the airway with the equipment and the authority of the service, and both levels move the patient toward definitive care without delay.

A seizure after a head injury may occur within moments of the blow or much later, and the two carry different meanings. A seizure at the time of the injury is often the immediate effect of the impact, while a seizure that appears after an interval, in a patient who had seemed stable, suggests bleeding or swelling inside the skull and changes the plan at once. During the seizure the responder protects the patient from injury rather than trying to stop the movement, clears the space around the patient, cushions the head, and neither restrains the limbs nor places anything in the mouth. After the movement stops, the patient is turned into a position that protects the airway, kept warm, and watched closely, because a period of drowsiness and confusion normally follows and must not be mistaken for a new deterioration without comparing it with what came before. The time the seizure began and how long it lasted are recorded, because the receiving team needs both to understand the course of the injury. A seizure that continues, or that repeats without recovery between episodes, is a medical emergency, and the trained responder treats it within the service protocol while the patient is moved to definitive care.

Airway management in a patient with a head injury must assume that the neck is also injured, because the force that injured the head often passed through the neck, and because a patient who cannot protect the airway cannot be left with the tongue blocking it. The lay first aider opens the airway with a jaw thrust or by lifting the chin while keeping the head and the neck as still as the situation allows, and does not twist or extend the neck to make the airway open. The trained responder uses the adjuncts and the techniques the service authorizes, holds the head in a neutral line with the hands or with the equipment carried, and calls for a second responder when the airway and the neck must be managed at the same time. Vomit, blood, and loose teeth are cleared from the mouth, and a patient who is breathing noisily or not at all is treated first, because no injury to the brain can be helped by a patient who is not breathing. Breathing is then watched for its rate, its depth, and its pattern, since the brain controls breathing and an injured brain disturbs it, and a pattern that becomes irregular, with pauses, points to injury at the base of the brain. When the patient stops breathing, cardiopulmonary resuscitation is begun by the lay first aider and continued by the trained responder, and it is performed with the same care for the neck.

The circulation must be assessed in every patient with a head injury, because bleeding elsewhere can imitate and worsen the injury inside the skull, and a patient who has fallen from a height may have a broken pelvis or a torn vessel in the abdomen. The responder examines the skin for its color and warmth, feels the pulse for its rate and strength, and looks for the wound that is bleeding or the abdomen that is becoming rigid. In a patient with a head injury the pressure must be kept high enough to perfuse the brain, so the support of the circulation is decided by the trained responder under the local protocol. Cold is a complication of a head injury and not a detail of comfort, because a patient lying on the ground after a blow to the head loses heat quickly, and a patient who is undressed, wet, or still for a long time is at particular risk. The lay first aider removes wet clothing where that can be done without disturbing the head and the neck, covers the patient with blankets, coats, or foil, and puts insulation between the patient and the ground, because much of the heat is lost downward. A body temperature below 35 degrees Celsius is a figure often used to mark the point at which cold itself becomes dangerous, and that figure is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the accepted line differs between services and editions. The position itself is a treatment, since a patient who is vomiting or cannot protect the airway is turned onto one side with the head and the neck kept in line, while a patient in shock is kept flat and warm.

The rhythm of observation continues from the first contact until the handover, and it is the trend across repeated examinations rather than any single examination that tells the receiving team what is happening inside the skull. The observations are repeated in the same order every time, which is the level of consciousness, the pupils, the breathing, the pulse, the color and the warmth of the skin, the movement of the limbs, and the pain in the head. Each set is written down with the time, and the times matter as much as the findings, because a hemorrhage that took 2 hours to produce a change is understood differently from one that produced it in 20 minutes, and those intervals are illustrations to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. The handover states the mechanism and the time of the injury, the level of consciousness when the patient was first seen and at every change since, the number of times the patient vomited, whether there was a seizure, the pupils on each side, what bleeding was controlled, how warm the patient has been kept, and any medicine the patient takes. The lay first aider contributes the observations and the times, and the trained responder adds the measurements and the record of every intervention. A gap in that story forces the receiving team to begin again.

Definitive hospital care begins with a reassessment of the whole patient and with imaging of the head, and the decision about which patients need imaging is made by the physician according to the local guideline. Computed tomography is the examination that shows blood inside the skull, a fracture of the bone, and the swelling of the brain, and it is available in some hospitals at all hours and in others only after a transfer, which is one reason for the field report to be accurate and early. The criteria that decide who is scanned include the mechanism, the level of consciousness, the findings on examination, the age of the patient, and whether the patient takes a medicine that thins the blood, and those criteria must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol because they differ between services. A patient with a head injury and a normal examination may still be observed in hospital for a period set by the local guideline, and the responder must not tell the family that observation is unnecessary. Definitive care owns the removal of a collection of blood, the placing of a monitor to follow the pressure inside the skull, the intensive care unit, and the operation that repairs the skull, and none of those is a field procedure.

Some patients hide a serious head injury better than others, and the responder must lower the threshold for concern in each group. A young child may be alert and playing after a fall and may still have a fracture, and a child who vomits repeatedly, who is sleepy at the wrong hour, or who is not feeding normally needs assessment. An older patient may develop a subdural hematoma after a fall that looked trivial, and the bleeding may declare itself days later, so the history of a fall matters even when the examination seems reassuring. A patient who takes a medicine that thins the blood, or who has a bleeding disorder, may bleed inside the skull after a very small impact, and the finding that makes the decision urgent is any head injury at all in such a patient. Alcohol and other substances confuse the picture because they produce the same drowsiness and unsteadiness as a brain injury, and the safe rule is to attribute the change to the injury until a clinician has shown otherwise. A further danger belongs to the days after the injury, because a brain that has not yet recovered tolerates a second impact poorly and a second blow during that period can produce swelling out of all proportion to the force, which is why a patient suspected of concussion is removed from sport, from driving, and from work until a clinician permits a return.

Certain acts are named here so that no responder attempts them, since knowing what must not be done is as much a part of competence as knowing what to do. A responder does not give a patient food or drink after a head injury, because vomiting and the risk of aspiration follow, and does not give a medicine to relieve pain or to wake the patient when the substance and the amount belong to a prescriber and to the local protocol. A responder does not press on an open fracture of the skull, does not pack an ear or a nose that is leaking blood or cerebrospinal fluid, does not remove a fragment of bone or a foreign body from a head wound, and does not straighten the neck to inspect the back of the head. A responder does not diagnose concussion from a distance, does not decide that a patient is safe to go home, and does not quiet a patient with a medicine that may hide a falling level of consciousness. Restraint of a combative patient belongs to the trained responder and to the local protocol. The lay first aider is asked to do the few things that help, which are to make the scene safe, to keep the airway open, to control bleeding by pressure, to protect the patient from cold, to watch the level of consciousness, and to send for help early.

To summarize, the head injury that kills is usually the one that is hidden, and the responder reads it in the patient's behavior rather than in the wound. Scene safety comes before care, the mechanism of injury predicts what cannot be seen, and a scalp wound that bleeds alarmingly is controlled by direct pressure while the skull beneath it is treated as possibly fractured. Concussion is a diagnosis of exclusion, made only after bleeding inside the skull has been considered, and no patient suspected of it returns to sport, to driving, or to work on the day of the injury. The level of consciousness is the most important observation and it is repeated with the time of every change, while the pupils, the vomiting, and the seizures are the findings that alter the plan, and the lucid interval is the trap in which a talking patient deteriorates after seeming to recover. The airway is protected with the neck kept in line, breathing and the circulation are watched, and cold is treated as a complication from the first minute. Definitive hospital care owns computed tomography, the monitoring of the pressure inside the skull, the intensive care unit, and the operation that removes the blood, and no field responder explores a head wound, presses on an open fracture, packs a leaking ear or nose, or removes an embedded object. The patient is watched continuously to the handover, the times and the findings are written where the receiving team will see them, and every figure in this chapter is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.
