# Chapter 20 — Immobilization and Splinting

Immobilization holds an injured part still, and its purpose is not to make the limb look normal but to prevent further injury and to control pain while the patient is carried toward definitive care. The scene is made safe before any injured part is touched, because traffic, fire, electricity, water, an animal that is not yet secured, and a person who is still violent are each a lethal possibility, and the patient's own blood is treated as infectious, with personal protective equipment, or PPE, wherever it is available. The lethal finding to be named first is the damage that movement has already caused, revealed by a limb that is pale, cold, and pulseless with a swelling that grows while the responder watches, or by a neck or a back that is painful or deformed with numbness or weakness in the arms or the legs. Splinting is rarely the act that saves a life, but the wrong splint or movement can end one, because sharp bone ends lie beside vessels, nerves, and skin that are still intact. The lay first aider may support, pad, and steady an injured part, the trained responder or paramedic may apply the splints the service authorizes, and the reduction of a fracture, the fixation of a bone, the repair of a vessel or a nerve, and the release of pressure inside a swollen limb belong to definitive hospital care and the surgeon. This chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country where you work.

The reason to hold a fractured part still is that movement of the broken ends causes harm that the fracture itself has not yet caused. A broken bone is no longer a column but a lever with a jagged end, and every jolt of a vehicle, every attempt to sit up, and every pull on the limb drags that end across the tissue beside it. The vessels that run close to the bone can be torn, a clot that has sealed a torn vessel can be dislodged, and a nerve that was only stretched can be cut, so an injury that was survivable becomes one that threatens the limb or the life. Pain is the second reason, and it is not a detail of comfort, because severe pain drives the pulse up, deepens shock, and makes the patient move in ways that add to the damage. A splint is therefore a container and a support rather than a treatment of the fracture, since it neither puts the bone back nor makes it heal, and the listener should hear that distinction plainly. What the splint does is keep the limb in the position found, take the weight off the broken part, and remove movement from the journey. Correction of the position, restoration of the length of the bone, and the holding of the pieces together with metal belong to definitive hospital care.

Recognizing that a part needs immobilization begins with the mechanism of injury rather than with the shape of the limb, because the energy that acted on the body predicts damage that the eye cannot see. A fall from a height, a vehicle collision, a falling object, a twisting injury, and a direct blow each produce patterns that a trained eye expects, and a fall from greater than 3 meters, offered as an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, should raise the question of injury to the spine, the pelvis, the heels, and the long bones. The findings that point to a fracture are sharp and well localized pain, swelling that appears quickly, a deformity or an unnatural angle, an inability to use the limb or to bear weight, a wound over a bone, and a snap the patient heard at the moment of injury. The lay first aider looks and asks rather than tests, and never moves the part deliberately to see whether it is broken, because such a test causes pain, may convert a closed fracture into an open one, and may injure a vessel that is still intact. A limb that is pale, cool, blue, pulseless, or numb below the injury, and a limb whose swelling is tight and growing, make the injury an emergency of its own and demand the fastest safe transport. Older patients and patients whose bones are weakened by illness may have a fracture with very little deformity, so a mechanism severe enough to break a bone justifies immobilization even when the limb looks almost normal.

The first principle of splinting is that the limb is splinted in the position in which it is found, and a severely deformed fracture is never straightened to make it look normal. The reason is anatomical, because the bone ends have come to rest where the vessels and nerves beside them may be compressed but not yet torn, and pulling the limb straight can drag a sharp end through a vessel that was still carrying blood or a nerve that was still working. A clot that has sealed a torn vessel is also fragile, and the movement of straightening can release it and turn a contained injury into a hemorrhage that no dressing will control. The exception is narrow and it belongs to a trained provider working under a protocol, who may apply gentle traction along the line of the limb in a specific situation, usually when the limb beyond the injury has no pulse and the protocol authorizes the attempt, and this is not a maneuver for the lay first aider or for a responder without that authorization. When such a decision is made, the limb is moved once, slowly, and held in the new position, and the circulation, the sensation, and the movement are checked again at once. The patient's pain and the appearance of the limb are not the measure of correct splinting, since the purpose is to prevent further harm rather than to satisfy the eye.

The second principle is that a splint must control the joint above the injury and the joint below it, because a broken bone is only one part of a chain that moves. An unsupported bone can rotate and angulate at the fracture even when the middle of the limb is bound, so a forearm splint holds the elbow and the wrist, a lower leg splint holds the knee and the ankle, and a thigh splint holds the hip and the knee. A bone that lies between two joints, such as the shaft of the thigh bone or of the upper arm bone, cannot be held still by a short splint, however firmly it is tied, and a splint that stops short of a joint allows the movement it was meant to prevent. A broken finger is comfortable when bound to the finger beside it with a small pad of cloth between them, a technique the lay first aider can learn and use. The finding that the joints are controlled is a limb that does not move at either end during a transfer or a turn, and the finding that the splint is inadequate is movement, a new angle, or a return of severe pain. Where a fracture of the thigh is suspected, the device that holds the limb in traction belongs to the trained responder or the paramedic and to the protocol of the service, and the lay first aider supports the limb where it lies and waits.

The third principle is that every bony prominence under the splint is padded, and the padding must be rigid enough, long enough, and secured without becoming a tourniquet. The prominences to protect are the elbow, the wrist, the knuckles, the hip, the knee, the ankle, and the heel, and each needs a layer of soft material between the skin and whatever holds the limb. Padding is made from what is at hand, including folded cloth, a spare shirt, a towel, a sanitary pad, foam from a vehicle seat, a rolled blanket, or a pillow, and it is placed so that the pressure of the ties is spread over a broad area rather than one point. The rigid part must be stiff enough not to bend when the limb is lifted, long enough to cross the joints above and below, and broad enough to spread the force, and the limb is secured at several points with triangular bandages, strips of cloth, tape, or a strap. A narrow cord, a wire, and a cloth rolled into a hard rope concentrate force and can injure the skin and the nerve beneath them even when the fracture is held well. The finding that the ties are too tight is a groove in the skin, a limb that tingles, or a tie that cannot be slid under with a finger, and because swelling grows for hours after an injury, a splint that is comfortable at the scene is checked after every move and loosened when the check has changed.

Commercial splints exist in many forms, and the choice among them belongs to the trained responder, to the paramedic, and to the service that carries them. A rigid padded splint is a stiff board or molded shell lined with foam and held with straps, useful for a forearm, a wrist, or a lower leg when the padding is adequate and the joints are crossed. A malleable splint can be bent to the shape of the limb and then holds that shape, a vacuum splint becomes rigid when the air is drawn out of it and molds itself around the limb, and an inflatable splint surrounds the limb with air pressure but must be watched closely because it can become too tight as the limb swells. A traction splint holds the thigh bone in gentle pull and belongs to the trained responder or paramedic taught and authorized to use it, and it is never improvised by a lay first aider. A cervical collar is a device for a trained provider and it supports the neck but does not immobilize it by itself, so it is used with manual support and with the movement restrictions the local protocol describes. A pelvic binder, a spine board, and a vacuum mattress are also devices of the trained responder and of definitive hospital care, and each has a place, a method, and a limit that training teaches and that no chapter can replace.

Where no manufactured splint is available, a serviceable one is built from the objects that a house, a vehicle, or a clinic already holds, and the listener should be able to picture a few of them in the ear. A wooden board, a rolled newspaper or magazine, stiff cardboard, a broom handle, a walking stick, an umbrella, and a tool handle can all serve as the rigid part of a splint once wrapped in cloth so that the edges do not cut the skin. A rolled blanket, a pillow, a folded jacket, and a stack of cloth can hold a limb in a comfortable position when the injury does not need rigid support, as in a simple sprain or a suspected fracture of a finger. In a clinic without supplies, tongue depressors taped together make a finger splint, plastic pipe padded with gauze makes a forearm splint, and a cardboard carton cut into a strip makes a splint for a lower leg. The uninjured leg is the finest splint for the injured leg, because it is the correct length, it is already padded with muscle, and it moves with the patient, and the chest wall serves the same purpose for an injured arm when the arm is supported against it. Whatever object is chosen, it is padded at every bony point, it crosses the joints above and below, and it is secured with ties that spread their force, because a hard object held against skin without padding causes an injury of its own.

The upper limb is immobilized with a sling that carries the weight of the arm and with a swathe that holds the arm against the body, and both belong to the lay first aider as much as to the trained responder. A triangular bandage, a scarf, or a strip of cloth is passed under the forearm and tied behind the neck so that the hand sits a little higher than the elbow, because an arm that hangs down swells and hurts more. The swathe is a broad band around the chest and the arm, placed low, tightened only until the arm is held, since a band that crosses the chest too high or too tightly restricts breathing, and the danger is greater when the ribs are also injured. The fingertips are left uncovered so that the circulation, the sensation, and the movement can be checked at every stage, and the patient is asked to open and close the hand, to spread the fingers, and to say whether the hand feels numb or cold. A deformed elbow or upper arm is not forced into a sling, and if the arm cannot be brought to the chest without moving the fracture, it is supported in the position found, padded and splinted, and the patient is carried that way. The finding that the sling must be adjusted is a hand that has become pale or blue, a hand that tingles, or a wrist pulse that cannot be felt, and the response is to lower the arm slightly, to loosen the swathe, and to check again.

The lower limb is immobilized with the same principles applied to greater weight, and the patient is not asked to stand or to bear weight on a suspected fracture. A suspected fracture of the thigh is supported in the position found, often with a pillow under the limb and the legs bound together, with binding around the ankles, the calves, and the thighs and padding between the knees. A suspected fracture of the lower leg is held with a rigid splint that crosses the knee and the ankle, or with the injured leg bound to the uninjured one, and the foot is kept at a right angle to the leg so that the ankle does not drop and stretch the tissue at the back of the leg. A suspected fracture of the knee is splinted as it lies, neither forced straight nor forced into a bend, because the deformity is the position in which the vessels behind the knee are least stretched. A suspected fracture of the ankle or foot is supported with a pillow or a soft splint and the shoe is left in place unless it must be cut away to check the circulation, because the shoe supports the foot and removal is difficult once the limb has swollen. The finding that the lower limb splint has failed is a foot that has become cold, white, or numb, a calf that is tight and increasingly painful, or a patient who describes pain far worse than the injury appears to explain.

The spine deserves its own paragraph because a broken neck or back can turn a walking patient into a paralyzed one through movement alone, and the suspicion is raised by the mechanism of injury and the findings rather than by a radiograph at the roadside. A fall from a height, a collision in which the patient was thrown, a dive into shallow water, and a heavy blow to the head are situations in which the spine is treated as injured until proved otherwise. The findings that support the suspicion are pain or tenderness in the neck or back, a step or deformity along the spine, numbness, tingling, or weakness in the arms or legs, and loss of bowel or bladder control, and any of these makes protection of the spine urgent. The lay first aider does not move such a patient at all unless there is immediate danger such as fire or traffic, and if a move is unavoidable, the head and the body are kept in one line and the patient is slid rather than lifted, with as many hands as the scene allows. The trained responder or paramedic may hold the head and neck by hand, apply a properly sized cervical collar, and roll the patient onto a board or vacuum mattress with a team, a skill taught and practiced rather than read. The board is a tool for the move, not a bed, and it is removed as early as definitive hospital care allows, because a hard surface under a patient who cannot feel it causes pressure injury, and the number of hours is a figure to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

An open fracture, in which bone has broken through the skin, combines the fracture with a contaminated wound and changes the priority of care. The wound is covered with a clean or sterile dressing, the bone end is not pushed back under the skin, and an object embedded in the wound is left exactly where it is and stabilized with padding around it, because removal may release hemorrhage that cannot be controlled and may tear a structure that is still intact. The limb is then splinted in the position found, with the dressing protected from the pressure of the splint, and the patient is moved toward definitive hospital care without an attempt to clean the wound beyond removing obvious gross dirt from the skin around it. The decision about protection against tetanus is made by the provider authorized to make it under the local protocol, because the wound, the contamination, and the patient's vaccination history all shape the answer, and the decision about antibiotics follows the same rule, so this chapter names the decisions and never the substance, the amount, the route, or the frequency. The findings that make the open fracture urgent are tissue that has lost its blood supply, dirt or clothing driven deep into the wound, and bleeding that continues despite pressure, and any of these means the fastest safe journey. Bleeding from an open fracture is controlled with direct pressure around the wound and, where the protocol authorizes it and the bleeding is from a limb and cannot be stopped, with a tourniquet whose time is written on the patient in a place the receiving team will see.

Reassessment after splinting is the same examination that was performed before it, repeated until the handover, and it checks the circulation, the sensation, and the movement of the part beyond the splint. The circulation is judged by the color and the warmth of the skin, by the pulse felt at the wrist or the top of the foot, and by the time it takes for the color to return after the nail bed is pressed and released, and a delay longer than 2 seconds is one figure used to describe poor flow, an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. The sensation is judged by asking the patient whether the fingers or the toes feel normal, numb, or tingling, and by comparing the injured side with the uninjured side, because a change the patient reports is more reliable than the impression of the responder. The movement is judged by asking the patient to move the fingers or the toes gently, where pain allows, and by noting any movement that was possible before the splint and is no longer possible after it. The findings are written down and spoken aloud, together with the time of the splint and the material used, because a receiving team that knows what was found before can judge what has changed since. The findings that require immediate action are any loss of pulse, any new numbness or weakness, and pain out of proportion to the injury, and the response is to loosen the splint, reassess, and transport without delay.

A splint that is too tight, a circumferential bandage wound too firmly, and a limb that continues to swell can together produce compartment syndrome, in which the pressure inside a closed space of the limb rises until muscle and nerve lose their blood supply. The earliest and most reliable finding is pain out of proportion to the injury that becomes worse when the fingers or the toes are gently straightened, joined by a limb that feels tense and tight, by numbness, by weakness, and by a pulse that may still be present because the pressure can close the small vessels before it closes the large one. The responder must not wait for the pulse to disappear before acting, because a pulse that can still be felt does not mean that the tissue is receiving blood. The measurement of the pressure inside the limb and the operation that releases it belong to definitive hospital care, and the release is a surgical procedure that is never performed in the field. A limb that has been without adequate flow for a number of hours can suffer damage that cannot be repaired, and that number of hours is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the tolerance of muscle and nerve differs with the temperature and with the patient. The responder's part is to loosen anything that encircles the limb, to keep the limb at the level of the heart, to keep the patient warm, and to move toward the surgeon as quickly as is safe.

Hypothermia is a complication of trauma rather than a detail of comfort, and an immobilized patient is especially exposed because the patient cannot move to keep warm and often lies on cold ground while clothing is cut away. Cold makes the blood clot less effectively, deepens shock, and makes the heart more likely to develop a dangerous rhythm, so every bleeding, wounded, immobilized, or waiting patient is protected against heat loss. The responder covers the patient before the journey rather than at the end, insulates the patient from the ground, replaces wet clothing with dry covering where the wound allows, and keeps the vehicle warm, and the splint and its padding are part of the warmth because cloth and foam insulate as they support. A body temperature below 35 degrees Celsius is one figure for the point at which cold itself becomes dangerous, and that threshold must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because it differs with age, the site of measurement, and the patient's usual state. The finding that cooling has already begun is a patient who has stopped shivering while still cold, with confusion and a slow pulse, and that means warmth and transport without delay. Comfort is part of the treatment, because a patient in severe pain moves, breathes shallowly, and worsens shock, so the patient is kept comfortable without moving the fracture and is asked about pain, numbness, and tingling. The decision about pain relief belongs to the provider authorized to prescribe it and to the local protocol, and the substance, the amount, the route, and the frequency are set there rather than in a chapter, so the listener is given the decision and not a prescription.

The record includes what was immobilized, with which material, the position found, the circulation, sensation, and movement checks before and after the splint, and the time of each entry, because the receiving team continues a story the field began. The handover is spoken in the same order as the record and it names the provider level of every act that was performed, since a receiving team must know what was done and by whom in order to judge what is safe to do next. Emergency medical services are activated early where they exist, and the report states the location, the number of patients, the mechanism of injury, what has been splinted, and what remains hazardous at the scene. The receiving facility is chosen for the care the patient needs, which may be a trauma center with imaging and an operating room, or a hospital that can stabilize the patient and arrange a transfer, and the choice is made before the patient is moved. Transport is urgent and gentle at the same time, because speed that throws the patient around adds injuries and pain, and a patient who arrives in worse condition has gained nothing from the hurry. To summarize, splinting holds an injured part still in order to prevent further injury and to control pain, and its purpose is never to make the limb look normal. Scene safety comes before the patient, the lethal findings of a pulseless limb or a moved spine are sought early, and every intervention is named with the provider level that owns it, so the lay first aider supports and pads while the trained responder or paramedic applies the authorized devices and definitive hospital care performs the reduction, the fixation, and the release of pressure. The joints above and below the injury are controlled, every bony prominence is padded, the limb is supported in the position found, and a severely deformed fracture is not straightened, because the bone ends may lie beside vessels and nerves that are still intact. The circulation, the sensation, and the movement are checked before and after the splint, repeated until the handover, and written down with the time and the material, since an unrecorded change is one the receiving team will not know about. The patient is protected against hypothermia from the first minute, the decision about pain relief belongs to the prescriber and the protocol, and the journey to definitive care is both urgent and gentle. Every time, temperature, distance, and figure in this chapter is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such numbers differ between services, countries, and editions.
