# Chapter 27 — Femur Fractures

A fracture of the femur is dangerous well beyond the limb, because the thigh can conceal a large hemorrhage while the skin above it stays almost unbroken. The femur is the longest and strongest bone in the body, so the energy required to break it in a healthy adult is usually enough to injure the head, the chest, and the abdomen as well. The lethal possibility is bleeding into the thigh followed by shock, and the findings that reveal it are a patient who is pale, restless, thirsty, or drowsy, with a pulse that climbs while the pressure falls. No care begins before the scene is safe, and the hazards to be cleared first are moving traffic, machinery that may still be running, fire, live electricity, water, a structure that can collapse, an animal that is loose, a violent person, and the patient's own blood. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country where you work.

The mechanism of injury is the first prediction a responder makes, and it decides how suspicious to be about everything else. A vehicle collision, a fall from a height, a motorcycle crash, a pedestrian struck by a vehicle, and a crush beneath a heavy weight are the mechanisms that most often break a femur, and each predicts damage elsewhere. The direction of the force matters as much as its size, because a blow to the side of the thigh bends the bone where the force meets it, while a force along the line of the leg drives the shaft toward the hip or the knee. In an older patient with thin bone, a fall from standing is enough to break the hip or the shaft, so a gentle story does not exclude a serious fracture.

The thigh is not a solid column of muscle but a compartment that can hold a large volume before it changes shape, and that is why a femur fracture can bleed without a wound. A closed fracture of the shaft may lose a volume measured in liters into the soft tissue, and a value such as 1 to 2 liters is offered only as an illustration of that capacity, because the real amount depends on the bone, the vessels torn, and the patient, and every such number must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. An open fracture bleeds outward as well as inward, so the visible loss and the hidden loss are added together. The thigh can also swell until the limb is tight and cool, and this hidden loss is why a patient with a broken femur is treated as a bleeding patient from the first minute.

The femur is described in three parts, and the part that is broken changes both the danger and the treatment. A fracture of the neck or the region just below the hip is a proximal fracture, common in older patients after a simple fall, with the leg shortened and turned outward. A fracture of the middle of the bone is a shaft fracture, the one that most often follows high energy and the one a traction splint is designed for. A fracture near the knee is a distal fracture, and it may injure the vessels and nerves that pass close to the joint. Any of these may be open, meaning the bone has pierced the skin and the wound communicates with the fracture, and an open fracture is a different emergency from a closed one.

An open femur fracture is contaminated at the moment it occurs, so the patient needs a facility where the wound can be cleaned and debrided in an operating room. When bone is visible in the wound, or when a wound lies over the fracture line and blood oozes from it, the responder treats the injury as open even if no bone is seen. The responder covers the wound with a clean dressing, controls what external bleeding can be controlled, and never pushes a bone end back under the skin. Pushing the bone back is a form of reduction, it is not a field procedure, and it drives contamination deeper while risking a vessel or a nerve that is still intact.

Shock is the complication a femur fracture most often produces, and it must be sought in every patient with a broken thigh. In the early stage the patient compensates, with a rapid pulse, cool hands, a narrowed pulse pressure, and a measured pressure that still looks acceptable, and that apparent stability is the most dangerous moment. As compensation fails, the patient becomes restless and then drowsy, the breathing becomes rapid and shallow, the skin becomes cold and clammy, and the pressure falls. The responder cannot correct this by pressing on the wound, because the bleeding is inside the thigh, so the work is to keep the patient still and warm, to control external bleeding, and to shorten the time to definitive care. A pressure of 90 millimeters of mercury is sometimes named as a threshold in this reasoning, and it is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the line shifts with age, with pregnancy, and with the patient's usual state.

The deformity of a fractured femur reflects the force involved and tells the responder how much tissue has been injured. The limb may be shortened, because the muscles of the thigh pull the fragments past one another, and it may be rotated or bent at an angle no joint could produce. A severely deformed limb is splinted in the position in which it is found, and it is not straightened to make it look normal, because the movement can tear a vessel or a nerve that had escaped and can release a clot holding back hemorrhage. Reduction belongs to definitive hospital care, where it is performed with anesthesia, imaging, and preparation for what may follow.

The first actions after the scene is safe follow the primary survey, and the broken thigh must not distract from them. The primary survey is performed in the fixed order of ABCDE, which means airway, breathing, circulation, disability, and exposure, and catastrophic external bleeding is controlled first where the protocol places it. External bleeding from the fracture is controlled with direct pressure from a protected hand over a clean dressing, adding further layers if blood soaks through. A tourniquet may be needed when bleeding from the limb cannot be controlled and the service authorizes it, and once it has controlled the bleeding it is not loosened in the field, because the clot behind it may not hold. The time the tourniquet was applied is written on the patient where the receiving team cannot miss it, and a period such as 2 hours is often quoted as the point at which the risk to the limb becomes serious, an interval to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

The neurovascular assessment decides how urgently the limb must be treated, and it is performed before and after every intervention. The word carries both halves of the question, since the nerves below the fracture tell whether sensation and movement are intact, and the vessels tell whether blood is still reaching the foot. The examination is repeated after splinting, after any movement, and at intervals during transport, because a pulse that was present can disappear and a limb that was warm can cool. Everything found is recorded with the time it was found, because the hospital team compares the record with what they find on arrival. The finding that makes the injury a vascular emergency is a foot that is pale, cool, and pulseless, or a patient whose pain and numbness worsen in a limb that is tightly swollen.

The circulation below the fracture is checked in a fixed order that the responder can repeat aloud. The color of the skin is compared with the other foot, and a pale or mottled foot is abnormal, while the warmth is felt with the back of the hand. The pulses at the top of the foot and behind the inner ankle are felt and compared with the uninjured side. The time it takes for the color to return after the skin or the nail bed is pressed is noted, and a delay beyond 2 seconds is a warning that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the value changes with room temperature, with the patient's age, and with the examiner. The finding that matters most is a difference between the two limbs, because a limb that was never normal may mislead the responder.

Sensation and movement are tested gently, and the result is written down rather than remembered. The patient is asked whether the foot and toes feel normal, and whether tingling or numbness has appeared or changed. The patient is then asked to move the toes and the ankle, and the movement is compared with the other side, while the responder watches for pain that limits it. A nerve injured by the fracture may leave a patch of numbness on the top of the foot, between the toes, or along the outer side of the leg, and any such finding raises the urgency of the transfer. The responder does not test sensation by prodding the fracture itself, because that causes pain and adds nothing.

Reassessment is a rhythm rather than a single act, and it follows the same order as the primary survey. The responder returns to the airway, the breathing, and the circulation, then to the level of consciousness and to the limb, comparing each finding with the one before. The findings that mark a patient who is still bleeding are a dressing soaked through, a thigh that has swollen further, a pulse that has risen and softened, and skin that has become cold and clammy. The findings that mark a limb in danger are increasing pain, new numbness, a foot that has cooled, and a pulse that can no longer be felt. Either set of findings means that the next step is the fastest safe journey to definitive care, not another attempt at the same measure.

A traction splint is a device that pulls the lower leg away from the body along the line of the thigh while an anchor presses against the pelvis or the upper thigh, and its purpose is to hold the fracture out to its length. The steady pull opposes the spasm of the thigh muscles, which is what causes the shortening, the overlapping of the fragments, and much of the pain. By holding the fragments apart it can reduce further bleeding and protect the vessels and nerves from being crushed between the bone ends. It also makes the limb easier to secure for transport, and it is the reason a trained responder may choose it when the conditions are right. It is not a treatment that heals the fracture, and it never replaces the operation that fixes the bone.

A traction splint is indicated for a closed fracture of the shaft of the femur in a patient whose circulation and sensation below the injury are intact or can be restored by gentle traction, and it is applied by a trained responder or paramedic working under a protocol. It may also be considered for an open shaft fracture when the wound has been covered and the limb is grossly deformed, provided the local protocol allows it and the application does not delay transport. It is not indicated for a fracture of the hip or the neck of the femur, because the pull is applied below the injury and cannot correct a fracture above it. It is never a task for the lay first aider working alone with improvised material, because a device applied without training can tighten a limb and worsen the fracture.

The contraindications to a traction splint are as important as the indications, and a responder who cannot name them should not apply one. A suspected fracture of the pelvis is the first, because the counter-traction presses against a ring that may already be broken and bleeding. A suspected injury of the knee, including a dislocation or a fracture around the joint, is the second, because the pull passes through the knee and can worsen damage to the vessels behind it. A fracture of the ankle or the foot is the third, because the ankle hitch must grip a part that is itself injured, and a partial or complete amputation of the leg is the fourth, because the remaining stump cannot take the tension. A device that is not sized for a small child is a further reason to choose another method, as is any situation in which applying the splint would take longer than the journey to the hospital.

A fractured pelvis makes the traction splint the wrong choice because the pelvis is the anchor the device must pull against. When the ring of the pelvis is broken, the counter-traction can separate the fragments further, reopen bleeding that had been contained, and add to the internal hemorrhage that already threatens the patient. A pelvis that is painful, unstable, or bruised should be treated as broken until imaging proves otherwise, and the trained responder supports it with the measures the protocol provides rather than loading it. If a traction splint has already been applied and a pelvic injury is then suspected, the responder does not simply remove it in a hurry but reports the suspicion and follows the local protocol for the change. The safest rule for the listener is that a traction splint and an unstable pelvis do not belong together.

An injured knee makes the traction splint the wrong choice because the pull passes through the joint and along the vessels behind it. A dislocated knee is a limb-threatening injury rather than a sporting accident, since the artery behind the knee can be torn or stretched and the foot can lose its supply within a short time. Applying traction to such a knee can complete the injury to that vessel, and the pain and the deformity can be made worse. The limb is therefore splinted in the position in which it was found, the circulation below it is checked and recorded, and the patient is moved to a facility where the vessels can be assessed and the joint realigned under proper conditions. Any knee that cannot be straightened without force, and any knee with a cool or pulseless foot, belongs to that pathway.

When a traction splint is contraindicated or unavailable, the limb is still immobilized, and there are several ways to do it. A rigid splint long enough to span from the hip to beyond the foot, padded and secured without extreme pressure, holds the limb in the position found. The uninjured leg may serve as a natural splint when the patient must be moved and no other device is at hand, with padding between the limbs and broad bands above and below the fracture, though this requires care and is not a substitute for a proper device. A vacuum splint or a padded board, where the service carries one, gives similar support. Whatever is used, the rule is unchanged: the severely deformed limb is splinted as it lies, and it is never straightened to make it look normal.

Padding and gentle handling decide whether a splint protects the limb or injures it. The bony points, including the hip, the knee, the ankle, and the heel, are padded before the splint is secured, because pressure there can cause skin damage within a short time. The bands that hold the splint are tight enough to prevent movement and loose enough that the circulation beyond them is not impaired, and the limb is never bound so tightly that the toes change color or swell. After the splint is in place, the responder checks the pulses, the warmth, the color, and the sensation again, because a splint applied correctly should not change any of them for the worse. A finding that has changed means the splint is released, adjusted, and the examination repeated.

Positioning for transport is chosen to protect the fracture and the patient at the same time. The patient is kept flat and still on the stretcher unless a chest or head injury requires another position, and the fractured limb is supported along its whole length so that its weight does not pull on the fracture. The limb is not propped on an object that leaves the middle of the thigh unsupported, because a sagging fracture angulates and can injure what lies beside it. The patient is secured to the stretcher so that a sudden stop or a rough road cannot throw the body against the injury, and the straps are placed to avoid pressing on the fracture itself. The journey is made as smooth as the urgency allows, because speed that throws the patient about adds pain and can add injury.

Pain relief is a clinical decision rather than a field instruction, and this manual deliberately names no substance, no amount, no route, and no frequency. The lay first aider may keep the limb still, keep the patient warm, speak calmly, and reduce the jolting of the journey, and those acts genuinely reduce pain without any drug. The trained responder and the paramedic may give analgesia only when their service authorizes it and their protocol provides it, and the decision belongs to them and to the medical direction that supervises them. The finding that makes the decision urgent is severe pain that is not relieved by immobilization, or pain that is worsening despite a splint that appears correct, because that finding may mean the circulation is failing. Whatever is given is recorded with the time, so the receiving team knows what the patient has already had.

Hypothermia travels with a femur fracture, because the patient is bleeding, undressed for examination, lying on cold ground, and waiting for transport, and cold makes bleeding worse. The responder removes wet clothing, covers the patient before the journey rather than after it, insulates the patient from the ground, and keeps the vehicle warm. A body temperature below 35 degrees Celsius is often named as the point at which cold itself becomes dangerous, and that value is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the threshold shifts with age and with the way the temperature is measured. A patient who has stopped shivering while still cold is a patient in whom the body has given up making heat, and that finding makes warming urgent rather than optional.

An open femur fracture is a surgical emergency, and the definitive care belongs to the operating room. The surgeon cleans the wound, removes dead and contaminated tissue, and stabilizes the bone, and the timing of that operation is decided by the receiving team with the patient's condition in view. The decision about antibiotics is made by the prescriber under the local guideline, and the decision about protection against tetanus belongs to the provider authorized to make it, with the wound's nature and the patient's vaccination history both taken into account. Nothing in a manual can tell a listener which substance to use or how much, because those choices belong to the protocol and to the clinician who knows the patient. The responder's part is to cover the wound, immobilize the limb, record what was seen and done, and reach the surgeon without delay.

Compartment syndrome is the complication of a broken thigh that is most often missed, and it can develop inside the thigh or in the leg below it. The pressure inside a closed compartment rises as blood and swelling accumulate, and when it rises far enough the muscle and the nerve within it begin to die. The findings are pain that is severe and out of proportion to the injury, pain that worsens when the muscle is stretched, a limb that feels tight and hard, and numbness or weakness below the injury. A pressure of 30 millimeters of mercury is often quoted in this reasoning, and that value must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the decision depends on the whole picture rather than on one number. The field response is to remove anything that is binding, to keep the limb at the level of the heart rather than above it, and to move the patient to definitive care at once.

A femur fracture in a child is not simply a smaller version of the adult injury, and an older patient is a different problem again. The bone of a child bends and springs before it breaks, so the same force may cause a fracture that is incomplete, and the child may walk on a limb an adult could not use. Because a child has less blood to lose, a smaller volume of hemorrhage can produce shock, and a device designed for an adult may be the wrong size for a small limb, so the responder uses the method the local protocol provides for children. In an older patient the bone is thinner, the fall was often gentle, and medicines that make bleeding easier may be in use, so a fracture that looks contained can bleed more than expected.

Definitive hospital care begins with imaging and ends in the operating room, and it is the surgeon who decides which route the patient takes. A radiograph shows the level and the pattern of the fracture, and a computed tomography scan, known as CT, may be used when the fracture reaches the hip or the knee or when other injuries are suspected. The surgeon may apply traction as a temporary measure to hold the bone while the patient is prepared, and the definitive repair is usually internal fixation, in which a nail or a plate holds the fragments until the bone heals. Where the wound is heavily contaminated or the patient is too unstable for a long operation, an external frame may be placed first. The destination is chosen before the patient is moved, and it is the nearest facility that can provide the care the fracture actually needs, with the handover naming the mechanism, the neurovascular findings before and after splinting, what was applied to the limb, and whether a tourniquet was placed and when.

Consider a constructed illustration, offered as a teaching construction and not as a real patient. A young rider is thrown from a motorcycle and is found lying on the road with the left leg shortened and turned outward and a swelling that grows while the responder watches. The responder makes the road safe, sends for emergency medical services, checks the airway, the breathing, and the circulation, and finds a pulse that is fast and a patient who is pale and thirsty. The limb is splinted in the position found, the foot is checked before and after the splint, and the patient is covered and insulated from the cold road. The lesson of the illustration is that the leg was never the only problem, and that the swelling in the thigh was the hemorrhage that mattered most.

Every act in this chapter belongs to a named level of provider, and the level is set by training, equipment, authorization, and protocol rather than by urgency. The lay first aider may make the scene safe, call for help, keep the patient still and warm, cover an open wound with a clean dressing, and support the limb in the position found. The trained responder and the paramedic may assess and record the neurovascular state, apply a splint or a traction splint where the protocol allows, control external bleeding, apply a tourniquet and record its time, and give analgesia under medical direction. Definitive hospital care owns the reduction of the fracture, the cleaning and repair of an open wound, the fixation of the bone, the release of a compartment under pressure, and the removal of an embedded object, and none of those is a field procedure. Knowing where that line lies is as much a part of competence as knowing what to do, because an act performed beyond one's training can turn a survivable injury into a fatal one.

To summarize, a fracture of the femur is a whole-body emergency because the thigh can conceal a large hemorrhage and because the force that broke the bone often injured something else. The lethal possibility is concealed bleeding into the thigh with shock, recognized by a patient who is pale, restless, thirsty, or drowsy, with a rising pulse and a falling pressure, so the patient is treated as a bleeding patient from the first minute. The limb is examined for color, warmth, pulses, sensation, and movement below the fracture, and those findings are recorded with their times and repeated before and after every intervention. A severely deformed limb is splinted in the position found and never straightened, an embedded object is never removed in the field, and a tourniquet that has controlled bleeding is not loosened, with its time written where the receiving team cannot miss it. A traction splint is a tool for the trained responder or paramedic under a protocol, indicated for a closed shaft fracture with intact or recoverable circulation, and contraindicated when the pelvis or the knee is injured, when the ankle or the foot cannot take the hitch, and when the device does not fit the patient. Hypothermia is prevented, pain relief belongs to the authorized prescriber under the local protocol and never to a prescription taken from this book, and the open fracture belongs to the operating room. Every value in this chapter, whether a volume, a distance, a time, a pressure, or a temperature, is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such values differ between services, countries, and editions.
