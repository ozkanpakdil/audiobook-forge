# Chapter 28 — Knee Injuries

The knee is the joint where a weekend sports injury and a limb-threatening emergency can look almost the same, and the difference is often decided by findings that take less than a minute to obtain. A twisted knee that the patient can stand on is usually a ligament injury that will be assessed later, while a knee driven out of place by a dashboard, by a fall from a height, or by the front wheel of a motorcycle may have torn the artery behind it, and that injury can cost the limb and sometimes the life. This chapter treats the kneecap, the ligaments, and the joint itself, and it keeps one question at the front of every paragraph, which is whether the blood supply to the foot is still intact. The provider level is named for every act, because the lay first aider, the trained responder or paramedic, and the trauma team each own a different part of the care, and the acts that belong to the hospital are described as hospital acts so that nobody attempts them in the field. This is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

No hand is laid on a patient with a knee injury before the scene has been made safe, and the knee itself is rarely the hazard that matters. Moving traffic around a crashed vehicle, a vehicle that may still roll or burn, spilled fuel, broken glass and torn metal, a live electrical cable, an animal that is loose, and a person who is still violent are the dangers that injure rescuers, and each is dealt with before the patient is approached. Body fluids are the quiet hazard, because blood and tissue from an open knee wound can carry infection into a break in the responder's skin or a splash into the eye, so gloves and eye protection are used for every patient rather than only for those who look infectious. The lay first aider uses whatever barrier is at hand, including ordinary gloves, a plastic bag over the hands, and glasses or a scarf over the eyes, while the trained responder uses the personal protective equipment issued by the service. A responder who cannot make the scene safe does not enter it, and waiting at the edge for those with the authority to hold the road or isolate the current is a competent act rather than a failure.

Knowing what lies behind the knee explains why the injury is feared, and the anatomy can be spoken in a few sentences that a listener can carry. The knee joins the thigh bone to the shin bone, and the kneecap rides in front of the joint within the tendon of the muscle that straightens the leg, so the kneecap is part of the mechanism of movement rather than a separate bone. Stability comes from the ligaments, with the collateral ligaments on each side resisting a bending force and the cruciate ligaments in the center resisting the shin bone sliding forward or backward, and from the menisci, which are pads of cartilage that spread the load between the bone ends. The popliteal artery passes behind the knee, tethered above by the opening in the tendon of the adductor muscle and below by the fibrous arch of the calf muscle, so a joint driven out of place tears or stretches the artery rather than pushing it aside. The tibial nerve and the common peroneal nerve travel through the same region, and the peroneal nerve wraps around the neck of the fibula where it lies close to the surface, which is why a knee injury can take away the ability to lift the foot.

The mechanism of injury is the first clinical evidence at the knee, because it predicts damage that the surface does not show. A dashboard impact drives the shin bone backward against the thigh bone, a fall onto a bent knee compresses the kneecap against the bone beneath it, a twist with the foot planted tears ligaments and menisci, and a motorcycle crash combines a direct blow with a twisting force and with the patient being thrown. A low-energy twist in a sport and a high-energy collision can produce the same swelling, so the responder should not let a familiar story of sport lower the concern, because the mechanism that matters is the energy and the direction rather than the name of the activity. The findings that demand immediate attention are a knee that is visibly out of place, a knee that is already grossly swollen and tense, an open wound over the joint, a foot that has changed color or temperature, and a patient who cannot feel or move the foot. A constructed illustration, offered as a teaching construction rather than as a real patient, is the rider who stands after a crash, reports a sore knee, and walks a few steps while the pulse at the foot is never checked.

A fractured kneecap is the injury a responder is most likely to see and least likely to over-treat, and its danger lies in the mechanism of the leg rather than in the bone. A direct blow cracks the bone where it lies and usually leaves the surrounding tendon intact, while a sudden powerful contraction of the thigh muscle can pull the kneecap apart and tear the mechanism that straightens the knee. The findings are swelling over the front of the joint, tenderness directly on the bone, a gap that can sometimes be felt between the fragments, and, in the more serious pattern, an inability to straighten the knee against gravity or against gentle resistance. A break in the skin over the kneecap makes the injury an open fracture even when the wound is small, and an open wound that communicates with the joint turns it into a surgical problem for the hospital rather than a dressing problem for the roadside. The lay first aider covers the wound, presses gently around it rather than over the exposed bone, immobilizes the limb in the position found, and calls for help, while the trained responder splints the leg without testing how far the knee will straighten. The kneecap can also slip out of its groove, and when it does the limb is immobilized as it lies while the lay first aider refuses to push it back, because the attempt is painful and can injure the cartilage.

A dislocated knee is the injury in this chapter that threatens the limb and occasionally the life, and it is the one most easily missed because it may have corrected itself before the responder arrives. The thigh bone and the shin bone lose their normal relationship, and the joint may be driven forward, backward, or to the side, depending on the direction of the force, with the backward pattern most often associated with damage to the artery behind the knee. In some patients the joint returns to a normal position on its own or during the movement of the leg, and the knee can then look almost ordinary while the artery behind it is torn or blocked. The dangerous sequence is that the inner lining of the artery is injured and a clot forms over the following hours, so a pulse that was present at the scene can disappear later and a limb that was pink can become pale and cold. Every knee that has been grossly deformed, even briefly, is therefore treated as a vascular emergency until a hospital has proved otherwise.

The reason a dislocated knee is a vascular emergency is that the artery behind the knee is the only main supply to the leg below it, and the small branches around the joint are not reliably enough to keep the foot alive. The hard findings of a failing circulation are a foot that is pale or mottled, a foot that is cold compared with the other side, an absent pulse at the top of the foot or behind the inner ankle bone, and a patient who cannot feel the toes or move the ankle. The softer findings matter just as much, and they include a pulse that is present but weaker than on the other side, pain that is severe and out of proportion to the look of the knee, numbness in the web space between the first two toes, and a swelling behind the knee or in the calf that grows while the responder watches. A pulse that is felt once proves only that the artery was open at that moment, and it does not remove the need for reassessment, because the clot that closes the artery may form while the patient is being moved. The finding that settles the question in hospital is imaging of the blood vessels, which may be an ultrasound study or a computed tomography examination, followed by a repair in the operating room, and none of that can be replaced by a field test.

The time pressure in a knee dislocation is measured against the tolerance of muscle and nerve, and it is the reason the patient is moved rather than observed. Muscle and nerve can survive a period without blood flow that is often quoted as 6 hours, and that figure is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the true limit changes with the temperature of the limb, with the completeness of the blockage, and with the patient. The urgency is not reduced by a pulse that is still present, because the injury to the artery may be a tear in its inner layer that closes gradually rather than a clean division that stops the flow at once. The lay first aider shortens the time by calling for help immediately, by not moving the knee unnecessarily, and by keeping the patient still, while the trained responder reports the time of the injury, the condition of the foot, and every change in the pulse.

The criteria for immediate referral are simple enough to be remembered under pressure, and they begin with the mechanism rather than with the examination. Any knee that was dislocated and has returned to position, any knee that is still deformed, any knee with an absent or diminished pulse at the foot, any limb that is pale, cold, or numb, any knee with an expanding swelling behind the joint or in the calf, and any open wound that reaches the joint all require the fastest possible transfer to a hospital with orthopedic and vascular surgical capability. A patient who can walk does not cancel the referral, because a reduced dislocation can be walked on for a short distance before it declares itself, and a patient whose pain is blunted may look calmer than the injury deserves. The responder should say aloud what has been found and what has changed, including the side that is injured and the state of the pulses, so that the receiving team can prepare the right examination before the patient arrives. The finding that always justifies the transfer is a limb whose circulation is in doubt, because the cost of an unnecessary journey is far smaller than the cost of a limb lost while the patient waits.

Ligament injuries of the knee are common, and their importance in this chapter is that they can be the visible part of a much larger injury. A ligament is torn when the joint is forced beyond its normal range, and the patient often describes a twist, a pop, immediate swelling, and a knee that feels as though it will not hold the weight. An injury of one ligament usually leaves the knee sore but stable enough to bear weight, while an injury of several ligaments leaves a joint that moves in directions it should not, and that pattern of multiple ligaments torn is the signature of a knee that was dislocated and then returned to position. The finding that should raise the concern is a knee that is grossly loose, a knee that locks, or a knee with a numb or pulseless foot, and the lay first aider's part is to immobilize the limb and to avoid any test that moves the joint.

Assessment of the knee follows the same order as every other injury in this manual, and it begins with the eyes and the hands rather than with movement. The responder looks first at the position of the limb, at the swelling, at the color of the skin below the knee, at the wounds and the bleeding, and at whether the patient is holding the knee still or guarding it. The hands then compare the two limbs, feeling the temperature of the skin, the pulses at the top of the foot and behind the inner ankle bone, and the sensation over the top of the foot and between the first two toes, because the nerves that cross the knee supply those areas. The responder asks the patient what happened, whether the knee was ever out of place, whether the foot feels numb or cold, and whether the patient can move the toes. What the responder does not do is test the ligaments by pulling and pushing the joint, because a knee that is already injured can be made worse by the test, and the stability examination belongs to the clinician in the hospital. A comparison with the uninjured side is the most useful single act, because the patient's own normal pulse and normal sensation are the standard against which the injured side is judged.

Reassessment of the pulses and the sensation is not a single act but a rhythm that continues from the first contact to the handover, and it is repeated after every intervention. The two pulses are checked at the top of the foot and behind the inner ankle bone, and the responder writes on the limb or in the record where each pulse was felt, because a mark that two people can find makes the next comparison honest. Sensation is checked by asking the patient whether the touch on the top of the foot and between the first two toes feels the same as on the other side, and movement is checked by asking the patient to bend and straighten the toes and to lift the foot. The pulse and the sensation are checked before a splint is applied and again afterwards, and any change is treated as a reason to loosen the splint and to reassess rather than as a reason to wait. A reasonable interval for repeating the observations when the circulation is in doubt is 15 minutes, and that number is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because a limb with a threatened artery deserves a rhythm set by the receiving team. The finding that must be passed on at once is a pulse that has disappeared or weakened, or a patient who can no longer feel the toes.

Immobilization of the injured knee is done in the position found, and the rule that a deformed joint is never straightened to make it look normal applies here more than anywhere else in the limb. The reason is that the artery and the nerves lie within a short distance of the bone ends, and forcing the joint back into line can tear the vessel that is still intact or release a clot that is holding the bleeding. The lay first aider can immobilize the limb with a board, a folded blanket, a rigid object such as a rolled magazine, or by binding the injured leg to the uninjured one, with padding between the knees and the ankles so that the skin is not pressed against bone. The trained responder uses a padded long splint that extends from the groin to the ankle, applies it without traction, and checks the pulse and the sensation before and after, because a splint that is too tight can itself stop the circulation. A traction splint is not used on a knee injury, because pulling on the leg may worsen a dislocation and a vascular injury, and the knee is named among the injuries that make that device the wrong choice. The finding that tells the responder to loosen the splint is a foot that has become pale, cold, or numb, or pain that has increased rather than settled.

An open wound at the knee changes the problem from a closed injury to a contaminated one, and the responder's task is to protect the wound rather than to inspect it. A wound that communicates with the joint allows bacteria to enter the space between the bones, and that space has little defense against infection, so the injury becomes an urgent surgical problem that needs washing out in an operating room rather than a dressing at the roadside. An object embedded in the wound, whether a fragment of glass, a piece of metal, or a splinter of wood, is stabilized in place and never removed in the field, because removal may cause hemorrhage that cannot be controlled and may injure a structure that is still intact. The correct act is to build padding around the object, to keep it from moving, and to move the patient with it in place, while the trained responder controls any bleeding around it without pressing on the object itself. The decisions about protection against tetanus and about antibiotics belong to the clinician and to the local protocol, and the finding that makes the decision urgent is a wound that is deep, contaminated, or caused by a bite or by soil. Every such wound is described at handover, including the object that is still in place, the time of the injury, and the dressing that has been applied.

Swelling is expected after a knee injury, and a swelling that is tense or that keeps growing is a warning rather than a detail. Blood and fluid collect inside the joint and in the tissues of the thigh and the calf, and the pressure that builds can compress the vessels and the nerves of the leg, which is the beginning of compartment syndrome. The findings that should worry the responder are pain that is severe and out of proportion to the wound, pain that increases when the toes or the ankle are gently stretched, a calf or a thigh that feels hard and tight, numbness or weakness in the foot, and a pulse that is becoming difficult to find. The lay first aider does not bind the limb tightly to reduce the swelling and does not press repeatedly into the swollen tissue, because both acts raise the pressure further. The trained responder removes or loosens anything that encircles the limb, keeps the limb in the position found, and moves the patient toward definitive care, while the measurement of the pressure inside the limb, which is often described at a level of 30 millimeters of mercury, belongs to the hospital and to a clinician who can act on it. That figure, like every other in this chapter, is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the line at which the pressure becomes dangerous depends on the patient and on the injury.

External bleeding from a knee injury is usually modest, and the bleeding that kills is the bleeding that is not seen. A torn artery behind the knee can pour blood into the thigh and the calf, so the limb swells while the skin remains almost dry, and the patient shows shock before anyone sees a wound. The findings of shock are a patient who is anxious, restless, or thirsty, a pulse that is fast and thin, skin that is pale and cool, breathing that has become rapid, and a level of consciousness that is dulling. The lay first aider covers and presses any external wound, keeps the patient flat and still unless another injury forbids it, keeps the patient warm, and sends for help, while the trained responder watches the swelling and shortens the journey. A tourniquet is used only for a limb that is bleeding beyond control and only by a responder authorized and trained to apply it, and once it has controlled the bleeding it is not loosened in the field, and the time it was placed is written on the patient where the receiving team will see it. The reason the time matters is that the receiving team must know how long the limb has been without blood flow in order to judge the risk of releasing the tourniquet, and a time that was never written is a time that is lost.

Cold is a complication of trauma rather than a detail of comfort, and a patient with a knee injury is at risk from the moment of the injury. The patient lies on cold ground, the clothing is often cut away to examine the limb, the injured leg is exposed while a splint is applied, and a patient who is bleeding from a torn vessel loses the volume that carries heat, so the body temperature falls while the responders work. Cold makes the clotting system work less well, deepens shock, and makes the heart more likely to develop a dangerous rhythm, which is why the protection of warmth begins before the journey rather than after it. The responder removes wet clothing and replaces it with dry covering, insulates the patient from the ground, covers the patient including the uninjured limb and the head, and keeps the vehicle or the room warm on the way. A temperature below 35 degrees Celsius is often quoted as the point at which cold becomes dangerous in itself, and that figure must be verified against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the threshold differs with age and with the way the temperature is measured. The finding that warns the responder is a patient who has stopped shivering while still cold, because shivering is the body's attempt to make heat.

Moving a patient with a knee injury is a task of support rather than of speed, because the limb must travel in the position found and the whole patient must be considered. The injured leg is supported above and below the joint, the knee is not allowed to bend or twist during the lift, and the patient is moved as a unit, with the spine protected when the mechanism suggests that the spine may also be injured. The destination is chosen before the patient is moved, and it is the nearest facility that can examine the circulation of the limb and, if necessary, repair the vessels, which may mean a trauma center rather than the closest hospital with an emergency room, and that choice follows the local protocol for the region. The handover carries the time of the injury, the mechanism, whether the knee was ever out of place, the findings of the pulse and the sensation at each check, the swelling, the wounds, the splint, and every change that was observed.

Pain from a knee injury can be severe, and it is real, but the treatment of pain is a decision about a patient rather than a rule that can be carried away from a page. The lay first aider may comfort the patient, support the limb, protect the patient from cold and from unnecessary movement, and explain what is happening, and may not give any substance, because the choice of a medicine and the amount and the route belong to a prescriber. The trained responder and the paramedic may give pain relief only within the protocol of the service, and the finding that makes the decision urgent is pain that is severe, a joint that is deformed, or a limb whose circulation is in doubt, because pain of that kind worsens the circulation and makes the patient harder to assess. The choice of substance, the amount, the way it is given, and how often it is repeated are set by the local protocol and by the clinician, and no amount or interval may be taken from this manual. Definitive hospital care owns everything at the knee that repairs rather than contains, including the reduction of the dislocated joint under controlled conditions, the repair or bypass of a torn artery, the repair of ligaments, the washing out of an opened joint, and the fixation of a broken kneecap, and none of those is a field procedure.

To summarize, the knee is a joint where a common injury and a limb-threatening emergency look alike, and the responder decides between them by the mechanism, the position of the limb, and the condition of the foot. A fractured kneecap, a kneecap out of its groove, and a torn ligament are each painful and each needs immobilization in the position found, while a knee that has been dislocated, even if it has returned to place, is a vascular emergency because the artery behind the joint is the main supply to the leg below. The signs of a failing circulation are a pale or cold foot, an absent or weakened pulse at the top of the foot or behind the inner ankle bone, numbness between the first two toes, and a swelling behind the knee that keeps growing, and a single normal pulse does not cancel the emergency, because the artery can close over the hours that follow. The limb is splinted as it lies, never straightened, with the pulse and the sensation checked before and after and repeated in a rhythm until the handover, and the patient is covered and kept warm because cold is a complication of trauma. Every intervention is named with the provider level that owns it, no definitive procedure is presented as first aid, and the immediate referral criteria are a suspected or reduced dislocation, a doubtful pulse, an open wound into the joint, or a limb that is pale, cold, or numb. Every time, pressure, temperature, and number in this chapter is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol before it is acted on, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.
