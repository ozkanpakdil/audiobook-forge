# Chapter 46 — Imaging

Imaging is a hospital act, and the first responder's part in it is restraint, because no picture taken at the roadside ever stopped bleeding that a hand could not stop. Traffic, fire, electricity, water, animals, violence, structural collapse, and the hazards of the patient's own body fluids are made safe before the first glove is put on. The lethal possibility of this chapter is a patient who bleeds while a team waits for a study. The imaging suite has dangers of its own, which are radiation, the magnetic field of the magnetic resonance scanner, contrast material, and contaminated blood and tissue that make personal protective equipment, or PPE, necessary in every room. This chapter describes what each kind of image answers, what it cannot answer, and who may ask for it, and it is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country where you work.

No image is an answer in itself, because an image replies to a question and the question must exist before the patient is moved. An image ordered without a question produces a report nobody can use, and an image ordered only to reassure delays treatment the patient needs. The clinician who asks for the study owns the question, and the clinician who reads it must know what the patient looked like when it was made. The finding that makes an image useful is not its quality but its power to change what the team does next.

Who owns what in imaging is decided by training, authorization, and equipment rather than by urgency. The lay first aider never orders an image, never reads one, and never delays the journey for one, and the lay first aider's contribution is an account of the mechanism of injury, the time it happened, and every change since. The trained responder, the emergency medical technician, and the paramedic may use a bedside ultrasound device in services that carry one under a written protocol, and even then the finding is reported rather than treated. The nurse manages the patient during the study and recognizes the deterioration that must end it. The physician orders the study, the radiologist interprets it, and the trauma surgeon decides what the operating room must do about it.

Plain radiography is the oldest and most available image, and its strength is bone. A radiograph answers whether a bone is broken, whether a joint is displaced, whether a fracture is aligned, and whether a dense object lies inside the body, and it answers in seconds in almost every hospital. The examination is made in at least two views taken at right angles, because a fracture invisible in one direction is often obvious in the other, and the joints above and below the injury belong in the same study. A physician satisfied by a normal radiograph in a patient with continuing pain makes the mistake this chapter exists to prevent.

What a radiograph cannot do matters as much as what it can, and those limits are why a normal film never ends an assessment. Soft tissue, blood, and clot are shown poorly, so a fracture may be invisible at first, and an interval such as 10 to 14 days is a teaching illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol because the timing differs with the bone and the patient. A rib fracture may be hidden, and a chest radiograph taken with the patient lying flat hides a pneumothorax and flattens the mediastinum that would warn of a torn aorta. The clinician treats the patient and the mechanism of injury and uses the radiograph as one piece of evidence among several.

The radiograph has a second and narrower use, which is the search for a foreign body dense enough to cast a shadow. Metal, most glass, gravel, and many fragments of a bullet are radio-opaque and appear as clear marks that can be located in relation to a fracture or a joint. Wood, plastic, thorns, and some glass are radiolucent and disappear against the tissue, so a clean radiograph never proves that nothing was left in a wound. The responder must not probe a wound in search of an object, because probing pushes contamination deeper and can injure a structure that is still intact. Every embedded object is stabilized in place while the patient is moved with it in place, removal is a surgical decision made when it is clinically indicated, and no first responder extracts a bullet.

The chest and the pelvis are the two places where a plain radiograph still earns its place in the first minutes, because each answers a question that changes the next action. A chest radiograph taken at the bedside answers whether a lung has collapsed, whether blood has collected in the pleural space, and whether the mediastinum has widened, and it does so in a patient too unstable to leave the resuscitation room. A pelvic radiograph answers whether the ring of the pelvis has broken, which predicts bleeding from vessels no hand or dressing can reach. A tension pneumothorax, however, is diagnosed with the eyes and the hands and not with a film, and no image may delay the release of air compressing the heart.

Ultrasound is the image that has moved closest to the bedside, and it answers one question with great power, which is whether fluid has collected where fluid does not belong. A trained clinician passes a probe over the abdomen, the flanks, the lower chest, and the region around the heart, and looks for blood in the peritoneal cavity, the pleural space, and the sac around the heart. The study takes minutes, can be repeated as the patient changes, uses no radiation, and does not require moving the patient out of the resuscitation room. The combination that matters most is free fluid together with an unstable circulation, because that combination sends the patient to the operating room rather than to the scanner. Ultrasound is performed by a physician or by a trained responder or paramedic under a protocol and never by a lay first aider.

Ultrasound has limits as real as its strengths, and a negative study never closes the question. Bowel gas, obesity, a distended stomach, and air that has leaked under the skin block the passage of sound and hide the fluid behind them, and a thin layer of blood can be missed by an experienced hand. The quality of the image depends on the skill of the operator, so a study performed in haste by an untrained hand is worse than no study. The examination is repeated rather than trusted, because fluid absent on arrival may be present an hour later, and the trend over time carries more meaning than a single view. The finding that overrides a negative ultrasound is a patient who keeps deteriorating without explanation, and that patient belongs in the operating room or the scanner according to the judgment of the trauma surgeon.

Beyond fluid, ultrasound answers several other questions at the bedside when the operator is trained and the protocol allows it. It can find a radiolucent foreign body that a radiograph cannot show and can display its depth and its relation to a vessel or a tendon. It can examine a vessel for flow and for a collection of blood around it, and it can guide a needle or a drain into a collection that must be emptied. In a pregnant patient it answers the obstetric question without radiation, which is why ultrasound is the first image in pregnancy whenever it can answer the question at hand. These uses belong to the physician and the trained clinician, and each is a report to the team rather than a treatment.

Prehospital ultrasound exists in some services and not in others, and where it exists it is a tool of the trained responder or paramedic under a written protocol. A service that carries the device uses it to answer a narrow question that changes a decision, which may be fluid in the abdomen, the absence of lung sliding over one chest, or the movement of the wall of the heart. What it never changes is the journey, because the patient is loaded and moving while the study is performed and no pause is taken for a better picture. The finding that ends field imaging is a patient whose airway, breathing, or circulation is failing, and that finding means the device is put away and the vehicle moves. The rule is therefore short and absolute, which is that transport is never delayed for an image.

Computed tomography is the reference image for the injured head and trunk, and its strength is that it shows bone, blood, air, and soft tissue together. The term is spelled out at its first mention in this chapter because computed tomography, or CT, is what a listener will hear named in every trauma room. A CT examination answers whether there is blood inside the skull, whether an organ in the chest or abdomen is torn, whether there is free fluid or free air in the belly, and whether the spine has broken, and it answers within minutes in a hospital that has the machine and the team. A whole-body study is performed in a patient stable enough to lie still, and it is the standard by which the injured trunk and head are assessed in a trauma center.

The CT examination of the head answers a few questions that decide between the operating room and the ward. It shows an epidural collection of blood, a subdural collection, bleeding beneath the arachnoid, bruising of the brain itself, a fracture of the skull, and air that has entered the skull. The finding that most often sends a patient to the operating room is a collection that compresses the brain and pushes the midline aside, and a shift beyond 5 millimeters is a figure quoted as an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the decision depends on the patient and not on the number alone. A patient who is awake and talking and then becomes drowsy may have a collection that is expanding, and that interval of apparent recovery is one of the traps of head injury. A patient whose medicine thins the blood may bleed after a blow that leaves no mark, so a normal CT in that patient does not end the observation.

The CT examination of the trunk answers where the injury is and whether it is still bleeding. It shows free fluid in the abdomen, an injury of the liver, spleen, kidney, or pancreas, a tear of the mesentery or bowel, a collection behind the peritoneum, a fracture of the pelvis, and blood or air in the chest. When contrast material is used according to the protocol, the study can show contrast escaping from a torn vessel, which is the image of active bleeding and the finding that makes the team move faster. The grade of an organ injury informs the decision between observation, an operation, and a radiological procedure, and it is one fact among several the surgeon weighs. The study is performed in a patient stable enough to tolerate the journey to the machine, and not in a patient bleeding faster than the team can replace.

The limits of CT are the limits of any study that requires a stable patient in a room far from the resuscitation area. A patient who is bleeding and unstable can die in the scanner, because the team that must stop the bleeding is separated from the patient by corridors and minutes. Movement to the machine removes hands from the patient and removes the patient from the place where an airway can be secured and a chest opened. The rule that follows is that the operating room is the first destination for a patient who remains unstable after the first measures, and the scan comes after bleeding is controlled when the question requires it. The trauma surgeon owns that decision, and no image is worth a life spent waiting for it.

CT is also the way a broken spine is found in a patient who cannot be examined reliably. A patient who is awake, sober, free of pain in the neck, and free of a distracting injury may have the neck cleared by examination in many protocols, and that decision belongs to the physician. A patient who is unconscious, intoxicated, or unable to answer has the whole spine imaged, because a missed injury of the neck can leave a patient paralyzed after a rescue that appeared successful. CT shows bone with great clarity and finds a fracture, a dislocation, or a misalignment of the spinal column. It does not show the cord, the discs, or the ligaments well, so a patient with a normal CT and an abnormal examination needs a different study. The finding that ends the use of CT for the spine is a neurological abnormality that requires the cord itself to be seen.

Magnetic resonance imaging is the study that shows the spinal cord, and its strength is the soft tissue that CT cannot resolve. The term is spelled out at its first mention in this chapter because magnetic resonance imaging, or MRI, is what a listener will hear named when the cord and the ligaments are in question. MRI shows the cord, the discs between the vertebrae, the ligaments that hold the column together, a collection of blood around the cord, and swelling of the cord itself after a blow. It is the study that finds an injury of the cord when the radiograph and the CT are normal. MRI cannot be performed in a patient who is unstable, who cannot lie still, who has certain metal implants, or who has monitoring incompatible with the magnet, and it takes far longer than CT. A period such as 24 hours is sometimes named as the window in which the study is most useful, and that interval is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol because the timing depends on the patient and the service.

The same study answers questions in the brain that CT answers only partly. MRI shows the fine structure of the brain, areas of bruising scattered through the white matter after a violent movement of the head, and changes that may explain a patient whose level of consciousness does not match the CT report. It is used when the injury is not explained by the earlier images and when recovery is not following the expected course. It is not a study for the acute resuscitation room, and it is never obtained at the cost of a delay in treating a patient who is bleeding or whose brain is compressed. The finding that makes the study urgent is a neurological deficit without an explanation on CT.

Computed tomographic angiography is the study that answers the question of the vessel, and it combines the speed of CT with the ability to show the channel of the blood. It shows the aorta, the vessels of the neck, the vessels of the arms and legs, and the great vessels of the abdomen and chest, and it can show contrast escaping from a torn vessel into the tissue around it. The study is performed with contrast material, and the choice of material, the decision to use it, and the preparation of the patient belong to the physician and the local protocol rather than to any rule taken from this book. It answers whether a vessel is torn, blocked, or bleeding, and it answers in a patient stable enough to lie still. The finding that changes the plan is an injury of a named vessel, because that decides whether the patient needs an operation, a procedure in the radiology suite, or close observation.

The limits of computed tomographic angiography are the limits of every study that depends on timing and on a stable patient. A small vessel may be missed when the contrast and the movement of the scanner do not meet at the right instant, and a vessel that has gone into spasm after injury may look normal while the tissue it supplies is dying. A patient who cannot lie still produces an image that cannot be read, and a patient whose kidneys are already damaged may be harmed by the contrast material, which is a decision the physician must weigh. The study also takes time, and a patient with hard signs of a vascular injury, which are bleeding that will not stop, a pulse that has disappeared, or a limb that is cold and pale, may need the operating room before any image is obtained. A normal study therefore does not exclude a vascular injury when the examination says otherwise.

Conventional angiography is the older study of the vessel, and it is the place where the image and the treatment are the same act. A thin tube is passed into a vessel and contrast is injected at the point where the question lies, so the physician sees the bleeding vessel and can then block it from the inside with material placed through the same tube. This is the procedure of choice for bleeding from the pelvis after a fracture, for bleeding from the liver or spleen in a patient stable enough to be moved, and for bleeding from the neck or from a vessel that an operation cannot reach. It is performed by an interventional radiologist, a physician trained in procedures guided by imaging, and it requires a circulation that can tolerate lying on the table for the time the procedure takes. The procedure is both diagnosis and treatment, and it is definitive hospital care in the fullest sense. The finding that makes it possible is a patient responding enough to the first measures to be moved, because a patient who is exsanguinating belongs in the operating room instead.

Conventional angiography has limits the team must respect, because it is a powerful tool in the wrong order. It is not a procedure for a patient bleeding faster than blood can be given, because the team would be away from the operating room while the patient empties. It is used after an operation when bleeding continues from a site the surgeon packed rather than repaired, and before an operation when the site of bleeding is known to be reachable only from inside a vessel. It requires a specialist, a suite, and equipment many hospitals do not have, so the local protocol decides who is sent where and by what route. The finding that tells the team to abandon the procedure is a patient who becomes unstable on the table, because the operation must then take priority.

For the prehospital provider the rule about imaging is the rule about transport, and it is written to be remembered under pressure. Transport is never delayed for an image, no image is a reason to wait, and the only images taken before arrival are those the service has trained the responder to obtain under a protocol that authorizes them. A study that changes the destination or the warning given to the receiving hospital is useful, and a study that merely satisfies curiosity is a delay that costs blood. When the patient has already been imaged at another hospital, the receiving team must be told what was done, what was found, and what has changed since, and the images and the report must travel with the patient rather than follow later. The finding that ends every field discussion of imaging is a patient whose condition is worsening, because at that moment the only useful action is to move.

The hospital uses imaging to decide between the operating room and the ward, but the decision is made by the patient's physiology and not by the picture alone. The patient who remains hypotensive after the first measures, whose abdomen is rigid and tender, whose chest is filling with blood, or who needs continuous replacement of blood belongs in the operating room whatever the image shows. The patient whose scan shows a contained injury of the liver or spleen, whose circulation is stable, and whose pain can be managed belongs on a ward or in a monitored bed where the examination can be repeated. The patient between those two belongs in an intensive care unit, or ICU, where the airway can be watched and the circulation supported while the team decides. The image informs the decision by showing where the injury is and whether it is still bleeding, and it never makes the decision by itself. The finding that moves a patient from the ward toward the operating room is any change for the worse, because observation is a treatment only while the patient is improving.

The limits of a normal image are the heart of this chapter, because the normal report is the one that most often misleads. An image records a single moment in an injury that continues to evolve, and a study performed in the first hour may be normal while a collection of blood is still forming. A small pneumothorax, a tear of the bowel, a bruise of the pancreas, and an injury behind the peritoneum may all be invisible at first and reveal themselves only on a repeated examination. The clinician therefore treats the patient and repeats the examination, and a normal image in a patient who is deteriorating is a reason to look again rather than to relax. A constructed illustration is the patient whose first scan is reported as normal, whose pulse rises through the night, and whose repeated scan the next morning shows the injury that was there all along.

Some patients need a different approach because their bodies or their circumstances change the risk. A pregnant patient is imaged only when the question cannot be answered by ultrasound, and the decision about which study to use and how to protect the unborn child belongs to the physician and the local protocol. A child is more sensitive to radiation than an adult, so the physician chooses the smallest study that answers the question rather than the most complete one. An older patient whose medicine thins the blood may bleed into the skull after a fall that seemed minor, so a normal scan in that patient is followed by observation and a repeated examination. Every threshold and interval in this chapter is an illustration to be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, because accepted practice differs between services and editions.

Imaging takes place in a cold room with an undressed patient on a hard table, and it is therefore a place where hypothermia begins if nobody prevents it. A patient who is bleeding, burned, exposed, or immobilized loses heat quickly, and the loss is increased by wet sheets, contrast material, a cold suite, and the time spent waiting for a report. The team keeps the patient covered between images, insulates the patient from the table where possible, keeps the room and blankets warm, and remembers that a cold patient clots poorly and tolerates blood loss badly. The monitor stays on the patient throughout the study, and the provider who owns the airway and the circulation stays in the room rather than watching from a window. A temperature such as 35 degrees Celsius is often quoted as the point at which cold becomes dangerous, and that figure, like every other in this manual, must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol because the threshold shifts with the patient and the way it is measured. The finding that stops the study is a patient becoming more unstable rather than less.

The image must be communicated as carefully as it is obtained, because a finding that never reaches the team was never made. Every study has a time, a name, and a report, and the clinician who ordered it is responsible for reading the report and acting on it rather than filing it. The handover between the emergency team and the ward, the operating room, or the intensive care unit includes what was imaged, what was found, and what the images do not explain, and it names the provider who will follow the result. The record of the imaging is part of the clinical record and is written with the same care as the record of an operation. The finding that demands a spoken conversation rather than a written note is a result that changes the plan before the next shift arrives.

To summarize, imaging answers questions and never treats patients, so the question comes before the study and the patient's condition comes before both. Plain radiography answers questions about bone, alignment, and a radio-opaque foreign body, and it cannot show soft tissue, a radiolucent object, or a fracture that has not yet become visible. Ultrasound answers at the bedside whether fluid has collected where it should not, and it depends on the operator, the body, and repetition rather than on a single view. Computed tomography is the reference for the injured head and trunk and shows bone, blood, and organ injury together, while it requires a patient stable enough to travel to the machine. Magnetic resonance imaging shows the spinal cord, the discs, and the ligaments, while computed tomographic angiography and conventional angiography show the vessel, the last of these being both the diagnosis and the treatment. The prehospital provider never delays transport for an image, the hospital decides between the operating room and the ward by the patient's physiology and not by the picture, and a normal image never overrides a patient who is deteriorating. Every time, depth, and figure in this chapter is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, or the law of the country where you work.
