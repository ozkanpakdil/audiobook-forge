# Chapter 30 — Ankle Injuries

The ankle is a small joint asked to carry the whole weight of the body, and it fails in ordinary moments as well as violent ones. No hand is laid on such a patient until the scene is safe, so traffic is held, an unstable vehicle is left alone or made stable, spilled fuel is kept from any source of ignition, and a live wire, deep water, a loose animal, a violent person, or a wall that may still fall is handled by someone with authority to handle it. Personal protective equipment, or PPE, with gloves at the least, shields the responder from the patient's blood at the same moment that it shields the patient from the responder's hands. Three possibilities here can kill or cripple if they are missed: an open fracture that bleeds and becomes contaminated, a dislocated ankle that has shut off the blood supply to the foot, and an injury elsewhere that the swollen joint has hidden. The ankle is rarely the wound that kills, and that is why it is dangerous, because attention is limited and a dramatic joint can spend all of it; this chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

Knowing what can break begins with knowing what the joint is made of. The shin bone, the tibia, carries the weight and ends in the inner knob of the ankle, the slender fibula runs down the outer side and ends in the outer knob, and these knobs are the malleoli that can be felt on either side of the joint. Between them sits the talus, the bone of the foot that fits into the arch the other two form, and ligaments hold the whole arrangement together. On the outer side a complex of ligaments resists the foot rolling inward, on the inner side a broad and powerful ligament resists the foot rolling outward, and above the joint a ligament binds the tibia and the fibula so the arch does not spread under weight. The same force produces different damage at different ages, because a young ligament tears while an older bone gives way first, so tenderness over bone is never assumed to be a sprain.

The mechanism of injury is read as a prediction rather than as a story. A foot rolling inward drives the talus against the outer malleolus, so the outer ligaments tear, or the outer bone breaks, or both happen together, and this is the commonest mechanism of all. A foot rolling outward presses the talus against the inner malleolus and opens the space between the tibia and the fibula, so the inner bone may break while the ligament above the joint tears as well, and a joint that loses that binding is unstable even when the bones look almost intact. A foot planted while the leg rotates produces a long crack that may climb the fibula far above the ankle, which is why tenderness high on the outer calf changes the whole assessment. A fall from a height drives the talus up into the lower tibia and can shatter the bone above the joint, while a wheel or a heavy weight tears skin from within and contaminates everything it opens; a wound over the joint makes the injury an open injury until a surgeon has looked at it.

A malleolar fracture is a break of one or both of the bony knobs, and the number broken predicts how stable the joint will remain. One broken malleolus with the ligaments on the other side intact is often a joint that still holds, while two broken malleoli, or a break that includes the back lip of the tibia where the talus rests, usually means a joint that can shift under the least load. What matters at the roadside is not the label but whether movement will drive the bones apart and stretch the thin skin over them. The findings of an unstable joint are deformity, an ankle that feels loose when a trained responder compares it gently with the other side, and an inability to bear any weight. The skin over the malleoli lies directly on bone, so a fracture that displaces even a little can tear it from within, and a wound over a broken ankle may have been made by the bone end; a puncture no larger than a fingertip over a malleolus is therefore treated as an open fracture, and its care begins with covering it and not with inspecting it.

An ankle dislocation is the displacement of the talus from under the tibia, and it is one of the few limb injuries in which minutes decide whether the foot survives. The joint looks wrong in a way that is hard to mistake: the foot is turned or shifted, the skin over one malleolus is stretched tight and pale or shiny, and the patient is in severe pain. The vessels that feed the foot run close to the bones behind the inner ankle and across the front of the joint, so a displaced joint can kink or compress them, and the finding that reveals this is a pale, cool foot with no pulse that can be felt, together with numbness or an inability to move the toes. The lay first aider must not pull the joint back into place, because the attempt may drive a bone end through skin or through a vessel and turn a threatened foot into a dead one, so the limb is left exactly as it lies, any wound is covered, the patient is kept warm, and help is sent for at once. The trained responder or paramedic may reduce the joint only where the local protocol authorizes it and only with the training and monitoring that make the attempt safe, and definitive hospital care confirms the position with radiography, checks the vessels and nerves again, and holds the joint with the fixation the injury requires.

A sprain is a torn ligament, and most ankle sprains are painful but forgiving, which is precisely why the serious variant is missed. The ordinary injury is a tear of the outer ligament complex after the foot rolls inward, and it produces swelling, bruising, and pain that settles over days with protection and gradual use. The dangerous variant is the high sprain in which the ligament binding the tibia and the fibula is torn, because that injury changes the width of the arch the talus sits in, and the joint behaves like a fracture even when the image shows no break; its signs are tenderness above the joint between the two bones, pain when the lower leg is gently squeezed so the bones press together, and an inability to bear weight. A sprain and a fracture can coexist, and a sprain never protects the bones from a second injury, so every ankle swollen enough to distrust is treated as broken until someone with imaging has said otherwise.

The difference between a joint that is swollen and a joint whose blood supply is failing is the most important judgment in this chapter, and it is made with the eyes and the hands. A swollen ankle is expected after any injury: the skin is soft, the foot beyond it stays warm and keeps its color, the pulses can be felt, the toes can be moved, and the pain is in proportion to what happened. A joint in trouble behaves differently, because the swelling becomes tense and shiny and keeps growing while the responder watches, and the pain becomes far greater than the injury would explain, often a deep ache or a burning that does not ease. The toes may go pale or blue, the skin may feel cool compared with the other limb, and a pulse that was easy to find earlier may become faint or disappear. This is not a reason to wait and watch, because these findings are the language of a foot being starved of blood, so whatever compresses it is removed, the limb is kept in the position found, and the patient is moved toward definitive care without delay; a swollen ankle inside a tight boot or a tightly wound bandage is the classic setting for this.

The neurovascular assessment is a short examination performed in the same order every time so that two people can compare what they find, and its parts that need only eyes and hands belong to the lay first aider while the whole of it belongs to the trained responder and the clinician. The first comparison is with the other limb, because what is normal for one patient is not a fixed standard and the uninjured side is the best reference available in the field. Color and warmth are looked at and felt, and the time taken for color to return after the tip of a toe is pressed is noted, since a slow or absent return warns that the smallest vessels are not filling; a delay of about 2 seconds is often taught as the line between normal and worrying, and that figure is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. The pulse is felt behind the inner ankle and on the top of the foot, and the responder notes not only whether it is present but how easily it is found. Sensation is tested by asking whether the toes feel normal, whether there is tingling, and whether light touch is felt on both sides of the foot, while movement is tested by asking the patient to spread the toes and bend the foot, and any deterioration in these findings is an emergency.

One assessment is a photograph and a series of assessments is a film, and it is the film that tells the receiving team what is happening to the foot. The first check is made on contact, the second after any movement of the limb, the third after the splint or dressing is applied, and then at the intervals the local protocol sets until handover. The reason for checking after splinting is that a splint applied correctly can still become too tight as the limb swells inside it, and a bandage comfortable a few minutes ago can become a tourniquet without anyone intending it. The findings are written down with the time they were found, on the patient or on the record that travels with the patient, because a finding that is only remembered will be lost in the noise of a handover; a pulse that was present and has gone demands an immediate review of everything wrapped around the limb and the fastest safe journey onward.

Whether a painful ankle needs a radiograph is decided by criteria written to spare patients from images that cannot show what is feared, and the criteria are simple enough to hold in the ear. The assessment begins with where it hurts: if there is pain near either malleolus, the examiner looks for tenderness over bone along the back edge or the tip of that malleolus, or along the lower part of the bone just above it, which is where the tenderness of a break most often sits. If such tenderness is found, or if the patient cannot walk four steps, radiography is indicated, and those two tests are described here as they are commonly taught, to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol because the accepted form of the rule differs between services. When the pain is in the middle of the foot, the examiner looks for tenderness over the base of the fifth metatarsal bone or over the navicular bone, because those points mark fractures that are easily missed. The criteria belong to the trained responder and the clinician who have been taught them and are used with the examination rather than instead of it, while the lay first aider treats every painful ankle as though a bone were broken, keeps it still, and lets the hospital decide with an image and a hands-on examination.

The most common failure with an ankle is not a technical error but an early surrender of suspicion. A patient who can stand, who has no deformity, and whose swelling is modest can still have a fracture, a tear of the ligament above the joint, or a dislocation that has already returned to place on its own. A constructed illustration, offered as a construction and never as a real patient or a real incident, is the cyclist who stands after a fall, walks a few steps to prove that nothing is broken, declines an ambulance, and is found hours later with an ankle swollen inside a boot and a foot that has lost its pulse. The lesson is not that every ankle must be treated as catastrophic, but that a few steps are weak evidence and a repeated examination is stronger; the responder who hears a patient refuse help records the refusal, keeps the patient warm and still, and reports what happened and when, because the decision to decline belongs to the patient.

The field sequence for an injured ankle begins not at the ankle but at the start of the primary survey, because a swollen joint is never a reason to skip the search for the injuries that kill. The airway, the breathing, and the circulation are checked first, catastrophic bleeding anywhere on the body is controlled, and only then is the ankle examined, which is the principle of life before limb applied to a patient who is usually stable. Bleeding from an open ankle wound is controlled with direct pressure over a clean dressing, with further layers added if blood soaks through and the first dressing left undisturbed, while a foreign body is stabilized in place and packed around rather than pulled out, because it may plug a torn vessel and its removal may release bleeding that cannot be controlled in the field. The wound is covered with the cleanest material available, and it is not scrubbed, not filled with anything the responder happens to carry, and not closed, since closure over contamination traps infection inside a joint and turns a treatable injury into a destructive one. The patient is kept flat, still, and warm, the ankle is left in the position found until the splint is ready, and the trained responder repeats the neurovascular check and decides whether the limb can be moved before transport begins.

Footwear must be a deliberate decision, because a boot can be a splint and a boot can also be a vise. A firm boot already on the foot and not compressing it holds the ankle better than most field splints and may be left in place while the patient is moved, which spares pain and spares the joint movement. A boot that is tight because the foot is swelling inside it is a different problem, because a foot can lose its blood supply inside a boot without any wound being present, and the sign that calls for action is a foot becoming numb, pale, or painful beyond what the injury explains. The safe way to remove such footwear is to cut the laces and open the boot along its length, working from the top downward while the other hand supports the ankle, and never to pull the boot off by the heel, because that movement levers the foot and can displace a fracture; the decision belongs to the trained responder, and the lay first aider removes only what clearly compresses the foot and can be taken away without moving the joint.

Immobilization of the ankle has one goal that governs every detail, which is to hold the joint in the position in which it will function best while preventing any movement that could worsen the injury. That position is the functional one, with the foot at approximately a right angle to the leg, neither pointed down as it hangs nor pulled up toward the shin, because this is the position in which the foot can later bear weight and in which tendons and skin are under the least tension. A severely deformed ankle is the exception, and the rule for it is absolute: it is splinted exactly as it lies, with padding built up around the deformity, and it is never straightened to make it look normal, because the force needed to straighten it can crush a vessel or stretch a nerve that is still intact. The splint is padded along its whole length, placed so that it supports the foot and the lower leg together, and secured with straps that hold without pinching, and a pillow, a folded blanket, or a commercial splint can serve if each is shaped and padded properly. The foot is supported as well as the leg because the ankle cannot be held still if the foot swings, and the padding is generous because every strap transfers its pressure to the skin beneath it; the splint is right when the joint does not move as the patient is lifted and the neurovascular check is unchanged after it is on.

Pressure injury is the quiet complication of splinting, and it happens where a hard surface meets skin over bone. The heel, the malleoli, the head of the fibula below the knee, and the outer edge of the foot have the least padding, and these are the places where a careless strap or an unpadded splint edge will cause damage over the hours of transport. A bandage wound in circles around the ankle is a particular danger, because as the limb swells the circles tighten together and behave like a tourniquet, so the trained responder prefers a wrap that crosses the joint rather than encircles it, applied evenly and checked at once by looking at the toes. The check that follows every application is the same neurovascular assessment performed before it, and any change in color, warmth, sensation, movement, or pulse means the wrap is released and reapplied more loosely; a splint that is comfortable when applied is not proof that it will be comfortable an hour later, since swelling inside it continues.

Pain relief is part of the care of an ankle injury, and it is also a clinical decision this manual deliberately does not make. The finding that makes relief urgent is severe pain that immobilization has not eased, and the finding that makes it more than a comfort problem is pain growing out of proportion to the injury, because that kind of pain may be the first voice of a compartment syndrome or of a foot losing its supply. The choice of substance, the amount, the route, and the interval belong to the prescriber and the local protocol, so the trained responder gives only what the protocol authorizes, the lay first aider gives nothing by mouth except what a clinician has directed, and nobody leaves a patient to wait in silence, because untreated pain worsens the circulation and deepens shock. Cold is a complication of trauma and not a detail of comfort, and the patient with an ankle injury is unusually exposed to it. The limb is often bare while it is examined and splinted, the patient lies on the ground, clothing may have been cut away to reach the injury, and the journey may be long; a patient losing heat in those conditions clots poorly, tolerates shock badly, and is more likely to develop a dangerous heart rhythm. A body temperature below 35 degrees Celsius is often named as the point at which these effects become serious, and that figure is an illustration which must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the line differs between services, between countries, and between editions of the same guidance. The response is to cover the patient before the journey rather than after, to insulate the patient from the ground, to replace wet clothing with dry covering, and to keep the injured limb under the same blanket as the rest of the body, checking it briefly and covering it again; a patient who has stopped shivering while still cold is telling the responder that the body has given up trying to make heat.

The ankle does not exist on its own, and the mechanism that injured it often injured other parts of the body at the same time. A fall from a height that broke an ankle may also have fractured the heel, the pelvis, or the spine, a crash may also have produced a chest injury or a fracture of the thigh, and the swollen joint announces none of them; tenderness high on the outer calf, well above the ankle, suggests that the crack in the fibula climbs far up the leg. The trained responder therefore examines both limbs, the pelvis, the chest, and the spine before deciding where the patient should be taken, and any injury more dangerous than the ankle changes the destination, because the patient belongs at the facility that can treat the most serious problem. Definitive hospital care begins with imaging and continues with decisions no responder can make in the field. Radiography shows the bones and the position of the joint, computed tomography, after which CT is allowed, shows the complex fractures of the lower tibia where the plain image leaves doubt, and magnetic resonance imaging, after which MRI is allowed, shows the ligaments and tendons where the question is soft tissue rather than bone. Reduction of a dislocated joint belongs to the clinician who can monitor the patient and act at once if the circulation changes, and holding the reduction may require a splint, an external frame, or an operation, while fixation of a malleolus with plates and screws and repair of the ligament between the tibia and the fibula are operations performed in an operating room by a surgeon. An open ankle fracture is taken to the operating room for irrigation and debridement of every contaminated and dead fragment, and the wound may be left open where the surgeon judges that closing it would trap infection, which is exactly why the field responder must never close it first.

Certain patients break the ordinary rules of reassurance, and naming them keeps the responder from being misled by a calm appearance. A child whose bones are still growing has a growth plate near the lower end of the tibia and the fibula, and an injury through that plate may look like a sprain while it threatens the future length and shape of the leg, so a child with a painful ankle who cannot bear weight deserves the same gravity as an adult with a deformity. An older patient whose bones are thin may fracture a malleolus with a force that would only bruise a younger limb, a patient whose blood is thinned by medicine may bleed far more than the injury suggests, and a patient who cannot feel the feet may not feel a splint that is too tight and must be checked by eye; each of these patients lowers the threshold for calling emergency medical services. The handover carries the story of the ankle in a fixed order so that nothing important is lost, and it begins with the mechanism and the time of the injury. The position in which the limb was found, whether the skin was broken, whether a foreign body remains in the wound, whether footwear was removed or left in place, and what was applied to the limb are reported with the neurovascular findings and the times they were found; a pulse present shortly before arrival is different information from a pulse found on first contact much earlier. Any tourniquet or tight bandage is named with the time of its application, the receiving team is told whether pain has been increasing, because that observation may be the reason for a rapid move to the operating room, and what was given for pain is reported with the provider authorized to give it, which is a statement about the system and not a prescription.

To summarize, the ankle is a small joint that carries a large load and hides several serious injuries behind an ordinary appearance. The scene is made safe before the patient is touched, and the primary survey precedes the limb, because the ankle is rarely the injury that kills but often the one that distracts. Malleolar fractures, a dislocated ankle, a severe sprain, and an open injury are distinguished by the mechanism and by the examination rather than by the patient's ability to walk, and the difference between a swollen joint and a foot losing its blood supply is read in the color, the warmth, the pulses, the sensation, and the movement of the toes. The neurovascular assessment is repeated on contact, after movement, after splinting, and at every interval until handover, and any change in it is an emergency. The ankle is immobilized in the functional position with the foot at a right angle to the leg, a deformity is splinted as it lies and never straightened, the padding protects the heel and the malleoli, and no bandage is allowed to become a tourniquet. An open ankle wound is covered and never closed in the field, an embedded object is stabilized and never removed, footwear is cut away when it compresses the foot, and the patient is kept warm and moved toward definitive care at the pace the whole body demands. Radiography and the criteria that call for it belong to the trained responder and the clinician, while reduction, fixation, repair of ligaments, and debridement of an open joint belong to the surgeon, and every distance, count, temperature, and interval named here is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between services, countries, and editions.
