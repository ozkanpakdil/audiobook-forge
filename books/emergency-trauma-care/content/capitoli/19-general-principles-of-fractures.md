# Chapter 19 — General Principles of Fractures

A fracture is a break in the continuity of a bone, and the person who first reaches a patient with one faces a decision that has nothing to do with the broken bone itself. Before any hand is laid on the patient, the scene must be made safe, because moving traffic, a live electrical wire, a fire, unstable ground, an animal still loose, or a person still violent can create a second casualty in the seconds it takes to kneel down. The lethal possibility to be named first is not the fracture but the bleeding and the damaged vessels that a broken bone can produce, and the finding that reveals it is a limb that is swelling inside its skin while the patient becomes pale, restless, and cool, or a wound that pours blood where the bone has torn through the surface. The responder also protects against hypothermia from the first minute, because a patient who is bleeding, exposed for examination, or lying on cold ground loses heat quickly, and cold worsens clotting, deepens shock, and makes the heart more likely to develop a dangerous rhythm. This chapter sets out the vocabulary of fractures, the examination that finds them, and the levels of care that own each response, and it is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country where you work.

The vocabulary of fractures begins with the skin, because the state of the skin decides the urgency of everything that follows. A closed fracture is one in which the bone is broken and the skin over it remains intact, so the break has not been opened to the outside world, but closed does not mean harmless, since the bone ends can still have torn a vessel or a nerve and the limb can still be bleeding into its own tissues. An open fracture is one in which the broken bone communicates with a wound in the skin, whether the bone is visible in the wound or the wound is small enough to be missed, and the opening may have been made from the inside by a bone end that pushed out and then withdrew. The distinction matters to the patient because an open fracture carries contamination into the depth of the limb, where bacteria find warm, dead, and poorly supplied tissue, and because the wound and the fracture must be treated together rather than as two separate problems. The finding that makes a fracture open is any break in the skin near the break in the bone, including a puncture, a laceration, or a raw graze, and a responder who cannot tell whether a wound reaches the bone treats the fracture as open until definitive hospital care decides otherwise.

The next pair of terms describes where the broken ends have come to rest, and it is the pair that most changes what the first responder must do. A nondisplaced fracture is one in which the ends remain in their normal alignment, so the limb looks straight and the injury may be mistaken for a bad bruise, while a displaced fracture is one in which the ends have shifted, angulated, or shortened, so the limb is deformed and the normal line of the bone is lost. Displacement is dangerous not because of its appearance but because of what lies beside the bone, since the same movement that shifted the ends can have stretched, torn, or trapped the artery, the vein, and the nerve that run close to it. A severely deformed limb therefore tells the responder that the blood supply and the sensation beyond the injury are at risk, and the rule that follows is absolute, in that a severely deformed fracture is never straightened in the field to make it look normal. The reason is that the position in which the limb was found may be the position in which the vessels and the nerves are least damaged, and an attempt to restore a normal shape can convert a limb with a marginal blood supply into a limb with none.

The shape of the break adds a second layer of meaning, and each pattern predicts a different behavior in the patient. A complete fracture is one in which the bone is broken across its full width, so the two parts can move independently, while an incomplete fracture is one in which part of the circumference remains intact, so the bone is cracked rather than divided and may bend instead of separating. A comminuted fracture is one in which the bone has broken into several fragments, and it speaks of a large transfer of energy, so the responder should expect damage to the soft tissue around it and should look for other injuries produced by the same force. A spiral fracture is one in which the break runs around the shaft in a curve, and it is produced by a twisting force, which is a pattern that should raise the question of how the force was applied, particularly in a child or in a patient who cannot explain the injury. A greenstick fracture is an incomplete break in a child, whose bone is softer and bends before it snaps, so the limb may be bent without a clear break and the injury may be underestimated. A stress fracture is a fine crack produced by repeated loading rather than by one event, and it may cause pain with activity and no deformity at all, so it belongs to the slower assessment of a clinic rather than to the roadside.

An open fracture is the emergency that this chapter returns to again and again, because the wound turns a mechanical injury into an infective one. The wound admits dirt, clothing, road surface, and fragments of the environment into the same space as the broken bone, and the tissue around the break is often bruised and deprived of blood, which is exactly the ground on which infection grows. The finding that should raise the alarm is a wound over or near a fracture, a wound that continues to ooze, a wound with visible bone or marrow, or a wound that the patient received when a limb was crushed or dragged. The lay first aider does not push a protruding bone end back into the limb, does not probe the wound with a finger or an instrument, and does not pour anything into it, because each of those acts pushes contamination deeper and may injure a vessel or a nerve that is still intact. Instead the wound is covered with a clean dressing, the limb is supported in the position found, the patient is kept warm, and the patient is moved quickly toward a facility where the wound can be cleaned and the fracture can be assessed. The decisions about protection against tetanus and about antibiotics belong to the provider authorized to make them under the local protocol, and the finding that makes the decision urgent is a wound that is deep, contaminated, or produced by a bite or by soil, while the substance and the amount belong to the prescriber and never to this book. Definitive hospital care opens the wound, removes dead and foreign material, washes the tissues, and stabilizes the bone, and none of those steps is a field procedure.

Assessment of a suspected fracture begins before the limb is touched, with the mechanism of injury and with a look at the patient as a whole. The mechanism of injury is the story of the energy that acted on the body, and it predicts damage that the eye cannot see, so a fall from a height greater than 3 meters, a figure offered only as a teaching illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, should raise the suspicion of fractures in the spine, the pelvis, the heels, and the long bones at the same time. A vehicle collision, a crushing weight, a blast, and a direct blow each produce their own pattern, and the responder who understands the pattern examines with a purpose instead of searching at random. Only when the scene is safe and the primary survey has been completed does the examiner turn to the limb, and the order of the limb examination is fixed because each finding changes the meaning of the next. The first questions are whether the patient is in pain and whether the limb looks different from its fellow, and only then does the hand come down to feel for warmth, swelling, tenderness, and the integrity of the skin.

The examination of the limb itself follows a fixed sequence, and the sequence is what keeps a responder from missing the finding that matters. Pain is asked about and observed, because pain that is severe, that worsens with the smallest movement, or that is out of proportion to the visible injury is a warning about the tissue inside the limb rather than a complaint to be soothed. Deformity is looked for next, and it means any departure from the normal line, length, or rotation of the limb, including a limb that is shortened, angulated, or turned so that the foot or the hand points in the wrong direction. Swelling is compared with the other side, and a limb that is swelling while the responder watches, or that feels tense and firm rather than soft, is telling the examiner that pressure is rising inside a closed space. Abnormal movement is the next question, and it means movement at a place where no joint exists, but the responder does not deliberately bend a limb to look for it, because the search itself can complete a partial break and can injure the vessels and nerves beside the bone. The skin is examined last in this part of the sequence, because a wound changes everything that came before it, and the finding of any break in the skin turns a closed fracture into an open one.

Swelling deserves its own warning because a fracture can kill a limb slowly while the patient's general condition still looks reassuring. Blood and fluid collect inside the limb, the tissues around the broken bone become tight, and the pressure inside the closed space of a fascial compartment can rise until it exceeds the pressure that fills the small vessels, so the muscle and the nerve within that space begin to die. The findings that reveal this emergency are pain that is severe and increasing, pain that is made worse by stretching the muscles of the compartment, a limb that feels tense and hard, numbness or tingling in the skin beyond the injury, and weakness of the muscles that the nerve supplies. A pulse that can still be felt does not exclude the problem, because the pressure that starves the muscle can be present while the main artery still carries blood, and waiting for a pulse to disappear is waiting too long. The lay first aider does not loosen, tighten, or remove a splint to test the limb, and does not massage the swelling, and instead keeps the limb in the position found, keeps the patient warm, and reports every change to the receiving team. The trained responder reassesses the limb, releases a bandage that has become too tight where the protocol allows, and moves the patient urgently toward definitive care, where the pressure can be measured and the compartment can be released by the surgeon. A pressure figure such as the reading taken inside the compartment in millimeters of mercury is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the threshold used differs between services and between editions.

Skin integrity is examined with the eyes and with a gloved hand, and it is examined over the whole limb rather than only at the point of greatest pain. A wound may sit some distance from the break, a bone end may have pierced the skin and returned, and a small puncture may be the only evidence that the fracture is open, so the responder looks at the front, the back, the inner, and the outer surfaces of the limb. An embedded object in the wound, whether glass, metal, wood, or a fragment of the environment, is stabilized in place and never removed in the field, because removal may release a hemorrhage that cannot be controlled or may injure a structure that the object is currently holding apart. The correct act is to build padding around the object so that it cannot move, to cover the surrounding wound, and to move the patient with the object still in place and with the receiving team told what is there. Where an object is protruding from a limb and cannot be accommodated, the trained responder and the paramedic manage it within their protocol and their equipment, and the lay first aider improvises support rather than extraction. Personal protective equipment, or PPE, is worn for this examination, because the wound of a fracture can carry blood and tissue that transmit infection to the responder's skin and eyes.

The neurovascular examination is the part of the assessment that decides how fast the patient must travel, and it is performed in a fixed order at the far end of the limb. Circulation is checked by the color and the warmth of the skin beyond the injury, by the time it takes for the color to return after the skin is pressed and released, and by the pulse that can be felt at the wrist or at the foot when the responder is trained to find it. Sensation is checked by asking the patient what can be felt, by touching the skin lightly on each side of the limb, and by comparing the injured side with the uninjured one, since a patient who cannot feel a light touch may have a nerve injury that will not recover without prompt care. Movement is checked by asking the patient to move the fingers or the toes, and where the patient cannot, the responder notes whether the failure is caused by pain, by weakness, or by an obvious deformity. A limb that is pale, cool, pulseless, or numb below the fracture is a surgical emergency, and the finding must be passed to the receiving team at once, because the time between the injury and the restoration of blood flow decides whether the limb survives. The lay first aider reports what the patient says and what the skin looks like, and does not attempt to test the pulse or the sensation beyond what can be seen and heard.

The examination performed before the limb is immobilized and the examination performed after it are the same examination, and this is the single most important habit in the care of a fracture. Immobilization changes the patient, because a splint, a sling, a bandage, or a traction device can press on a vessel, a nerve, or a swelling, and because a limb that was straight and perfused may become bent and starved once it is secured. The purpose of repeating the same questions in the same order is that the responder is not looking for a new injury but for a change caused by the treatment, and a change that appears after immobilization is a signal to adjust the splint rather than to wait and watch. Immobilization is therefore never the end of the assessment, and the finding that the limb is now cooler, paler, more painful, or more numb than it was before the splint means the splint must be examined and the patient must move faster toward definitive care. The same rule applies after any movement of the patient, any change of position, any new dressing, and any part of the journey, because each of those events can alter the pressure inside the limb. The record of both examinations is written and passed on, since the receiving team needs to know what was found before and after, and a limb whose condition changed in transit is a different patient from the one described at the scene.

The three levels of care divide the response to a fracture, and the division follows training, equipment, authorization, and protocol rather than the urgency of the moment. The lay first aider may cover an open wound with a clean dressing, support the limb in the position found, keep the patient still and warm, and call emergency medical services early, and those acts are enough to protect the patient while help is coming. The lay first aider does not straighten a deformed limb, does not push a bone end back into the skin, does not pull on the limb to make it longer, does not remove an embedded object, and does not try to test the limb by bending it. Where a limb is bleeding heavily, the lay first aider applies firm direct pressure over a dressing and adds further layers rather than lifting the first, and the decision to place a manufactured tourniquet belongs to emergency medical services, to the trained responder, and to the protocol that authorizes it. Warmth is part of the lay first aider's work from the first minute, because a patient with a fracture is often exposed, immobile, and lying on cold ground, and cold deepens shock and worsens the outcome.

The trained responder and the paramedic may splint and immobilize within the procedures their service authorizes, and their work is defined by doing that one task well rather than by doing more. Immobilization of a fracture belongs to this level because it requires a device, a technique, and the judgment to know which position the limb should be held in, and because the device itself must be checked against the circulation and the nerves before and after it is applied. The paramedic may also decide about pain relief, and the finding that makes relief urgent is a patient whose pain is severe enough to prevent movement, to prevent sleep, or to worsen the circulation, while the choice of substance, the amount, the route, and the timing belong to the local protocol and to the prescriber. Reduction of a fracture, which means restoring the broken ends to their normal alignment, is not a field procedure for the trained responder either, and it is attempted outside the hospital only in the narrow circumstances that the service protocol defines and with the authorization that the protocol requires. Where a limb has lost its blood supply, the trained responder recognizes the emergency, protects the limb in the position found, and shortens the time to definitive care rather than attempting a reduction to restore a pulse.

Definitive hospital care owns everything that repairs, aligns, and fixes, and knowing where that line lies is as much a part of competence as knowing how to splint. A plain radiograph, a computed tomography scan, and where it is available an MRI are used to define the pattern of the break and the position of the fragments, and those images are read by the clinician who will decide the treatment. Reduction of a fracture, whether it is done by closed manipulation or through an open operation, and fixation, whether by a cast, by pins, by plates and screws, or by an external frame, belong to the physician and the surgeon in a place where imaging, anesthesia, and sterile conditions exist. The surgeon also cleans an open fracture, removes dead and contaminated tissue, stabilizes the bone, and closes or leaves open the wound according to the state of the tissues, and the release of a compartment under pressure is performed by the same hands. The decisions about antibiotics, about protection against tetanus, and about fluids and analgesia are made by the providers authorized to make them under the local protocol and with the patient in front of them, because a manual cannot carry a prescription. A patient with a suspected fracture of the hip, the pelvis, or the femur belongs in a trauma center where the operating room and the intensive care unit are available, and the field responder's task is to immobilize, to keep the patient warm, and to hand over a clear account of what was found and what was done.

A fracture is also a marker of the energy that produced it, and that is why the examination of the limb is never finished until the whole patient has been examined. The same force that broke a femur, a pelvis, or a rib can have injured the head, the chest, the abdomen, and the spine, and the finding that should send the responder back to the beginning of the primary survey is any change in the level of consciousness, the breathing, or the circulation. A patient with a fracture of the pelvis can lose a large volume of blood into the space behind the peritoneum, and a patient with a fracture of the femur can lose as much as 2 liters into the thigh, a teaching figure that must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol in force, so the limb that looks dramatic may be the quieter emergency of the two. Children need a special mention because their bones bend and crack in patterns that adults do not share, and because a child who is frightened may not report pain or may report it in a place that does not match the injury. The record that travels with the patient includes the mechanism of injury, the time it happened, the appearance and the neurovascular findings of the limb before and after splinting, the time of any intervention, and the changes observed along the way, because the receiving team needs the trend and not only the latest reading.

Two failures run through the care of fractures, and both are failures of attention rather than of skill. The first is the fracture that is missed because a more dramatic injury holds the eye, which is the wrist that is not examined in a patient with a bleeding thigh, the rib that is not counted in a patient with a head injury, and the second fracture of the same limb that is hidden above or below the one that is found. The second is the fracture that is treated as the emergency while the patient is dying of something else, because a limb can wait for minutes that an airway, a breathing, and a circulation cannot. Life comes before limb when two correct actions compete for the same pair of hands, and that principle decides the order rather than the importance of the limb, so the patient who is not breathing is treated before the patient's deformed leg. The finding that settles the order is not the appearance of the limb but whether the patient can currently breathe, circulate, and respond.

Everything in this chapter points toward one purpose, which is to prevent further harm while the patient is moved toward the care that can repair the break. Immobilization serves that purpose by holding the broken ends still, by controlling pain, by protecting the vessels and the nerves that run beside the bone, and by preventing the sharp ends from moving inside the skin, so the limb is splinted in the position found rather than in the position that looks best. Pain is treated as a clinical problem rather than as an unavoidable companion of injury, and the responder who is authorized to relieve it does so within the protocol, because pain that is severe enough to prevent movement or to disturb breathing adds to the patient's risk. The hypothermia that accompanies a fracture is prevented by covering the patient before the journey, by insulating the patient from the ground, and by removing wet clothing and replacing it with dry coverings without exposing the wound, and a body temperature below 35 degrees Celsius is a threshold to be verified against the current guideline and the local protocol rather than taken from these pages. The patient is watched continuously from the first contact to the handover, and the observations that matter are repeated in the same order so that the trend can be compared rather than guessed. The handover carries the mechanism of injury, the state of the skin, the neurovascular findings before and after immobilization, the time of every intervention, and every change seen, because the receiving team must be able to continue without starting again.

To summarize, a fracture is defined first by the skin, since an open break is an emergency of contamination and infection while a closed break can still have torn a vessel or a nerve, and it is defined next by displacement, completeness, and pattern, because each of those terms predicts what the patient will do and what the responder must avoid. The severely deformed limb is never straightened in the field, a protruding bone end is never pushed back, an embedded object is never removed, and abnormal movement is a finding rather than a test. The examination of pain, deformity, swelling, abnormal movement, skin integrity, and neurovascular status follows a fixed order, the limb beyond the fracture is examined before and after immobilization with the same questions, and a limb that becomes cooler, paler, more painful, or more numb after splinting is a signal to adjust and to hurry. The lay first aider covers, supports, warms, and calls for help; the trained responder and the paramedic splint and immobilize within the protocols that authorize them; and reduction, fixation, cleaning of an open fracture, and release of a compartment belong to definitive hospital care. Scene safety comes before the patient, the lethal possibilities are named early, cold is treated as a complication, and life comes before limb when two correct actions compete. Every time, pressure, temperature, depth, and figure in this chapter is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country where you work.
