# Chapter 34 — Neurovascular Injuries

When a limb is injured, the vessels that carry its blood and the nerves that give it feeling and movement are hurt together far more often than the surface of the wound suggests, and the responder's work is to suspect that damage early and to reach the care that can repair it. The scene is made safe before the patient is touched, because traffic, machinery that is still running, live electricity, fire, deep water, a loose animal, a violent person, and the patient's own blood are the hazards that kill rescuers and patients alike. The lethal possibility named first is an artery that has been torn where no dressing can press on it, and the harm that follows close behind is a limb whose blood supply or nerve supply has been lost while the wound itself looks modest. This chapter follows arterial injury, venous injury, and nerve injury in the limbs and at the places where the limb joins the body, and it gives the four observations that must be recorded before and after every dressing, every splint, and every move. Every act is named with the provider level that owns it, because the lay first aider, the trained responder or paramedic, and definitive hospital care do different things with the same wound, and a manual that blurs them teaches dangerous habits. This is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country where you work.

The reason vessels and nerves are injured together is that they travel together. In an arm the main artery and the nerves of the hand run through the armpit and then along the inner side of the upper arm in one bundle beside the bone, and they divide near the elbow into the branches that reach the wrist and the fingers. In a leg the great artery and the great nerve run down the back of the thigh and pass behind the knee, where the vessel lies tethered against the bone and is stretched rather than cut when the joint is displaced, and below the knee they divide again toward the ankle and the foot. Because the bundle is anchored where it passes a joint, a dislocation can tear a vessel while the bone stays whole, and the knee is the joint where this is most feared. A sharp edge may divide a vessel and a nerve cleanly while the skin shows only a small wound, and a blunt blow may burst the same vessel while the skin stays closed, so the size of the opening is a poor guide to the damage beneath it. The responder therefore treats the mechanism, the position of the wound, and the state of the limb as a single question rather than three separate ones.

Arterial injury announces itself in several ways, and the first is bleeding that keeps time with the heartbeat. Bright blood may leave the wound in jets, or it may travel into the tissues so that the limb swells while the skin looks almost dry, and a swelling that grows while the responder watches is as serious as a wound that pours. When an artery and a vein are torn beside each other the two may connect, and the finding is a humming sensation under the fingers and a sound heard through a stethoscope over the wound, which speaks of blood moving where it should not. The artery of a limb may also be torn with no bleeding visible at all when a clot seals the opening for a time, and that pause is one of the traps of early assessment, because the vessel can open again when the pressure rises or when a dressing is lifted. Bleeding from the armpit, the groin, or the base of the neck cannot be controlled by a tourniquet, so it is packed where the provider is trained and the protocol allows, held under pressure, and carried to a surgeon. A jet of arterial blood from a limb that direct pressure does not stop is the clearest indication for a tourniquet, and the tourniquet belongs to the trained responder or paramedic acting under a protocol, not to the lay first aider.

The findings that demand immediate hospital care are grouped as hard signs, and any one of them is enough. They are bleeding that pulses from the wound, a swelling that enlarges while it is watched, a pulse that can no longer be felt beyond the injury, a limb that has turned pale or cold, pain that is severe and out of proportion to what is seen, numbness or tingling that was not there before, a part of the body that can no longer be moved, and a humming or a sound over the wound. A deep wound that lies over the track of a major vessel, a fracture beside the same track, and a joint that has been displaced are not hard signs by themselves, but they place the patient in the group that must be examined where imaging and a surgeon exist. The field response to a hard sign is to stop the external bleeding with direct pressure, to add a pressure dressing or to pack an appropriate deep wound where the provider is trained and the protocol allows, and to apply a tourniquet when the limb continues to bleed despite those measures. The patient is then splinted in the position found, kept warm, and carried to a facility that can repair a vessel, because no field act restores an artery that has been divided. A figure such as a journey of 1 hour is sometimes described as the window in which a warm limb can still be saved, and that figure is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the tolerance of muscle and nerve differs with temperature, with age, and with the injury.

Between the obvious arterial wound and the untouched limb lies a group of findings that prove nothing and are still important. A wound whose track passes close to a major vessel or nerve, a hematoma that is small but present, a history of brisk bleeding that stopped before the responder arrived, and a fracture of a long bone are each called a proximity finding, and the word exists because the vessel may be bruised, stretched, or partly torn without the signs of a complete rupture. The responder records these findings and passes them on at handover, because the receiving team decides what examination and what imaging the region needs, and that decision is not made at the roadside. A distance quoted as a proximity threshold, such as a wound within 2 centimeters of the vessel, is a teaching illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since services define the zone differently. The correct behavior is to treat the patient rather than the number, to complete the four observations, and to arrange a destination where the vessels can be assessed rather than to wait and watch at the scene.

Venous injury behaves differently from arterial injury and is no less dangerous. The blood is darker and leaves the wound in a steady flow that does not spurt, and the pressure inside the vessel is low enough that a firm dressing often holds it. The size of the vein decides the danger, because a torn vein in the groin, the armpit, or the neck can empty the circulation in minutes without ever spurting, and the wound may appear far less dramatic than the loss it is causing. A vein in the neck or the upper chest that is open to the air carries a second hazard, because air can be drawn into it with each breath, and the finding is a wound that bubbles or sucks at the moment the chest expands. The lay first aider covers the wound and presses firmly around it without probing and without lifting a dressing to look, adding layers when blood shows through, while the trained responder may pack an appropriate deep wound under a protocol and moves the patient without delay. Venous congestion in a limb, with swelling, duskiness, and a limb that feels tense, is another sign that the return of blood has been obstructed, and it belongs to the same urgent pathway as an arterial wound.

Nerve injury may be hidden by the bleeding that commands attention, and it is often found only when the responder tests the limb. A nerve that has been bruised and stunned stops working for a time but may recover on its own; a nerve whose fibers are torn while its outer sheath stays whole may regrow slowly over months; and a nerve divided across will not recover without an operation. The signs are a patch of skin that has lost its feeling, tingling or pins and needles in a territory that follows the nerve rather than the wound, weakness of a movement, and a limb that feels heavy or useless to the patient. Sensation and movement are tested in the four observations that follow, and the test is done on both sides so that the patient's own normal hand or foot becomes the reference. A nerve injury that appears after a splint, a bandage, or a move is the most serious version of the finding, because it means that the intervention itself has caused the harm, and the response is to relieve the pressure at once under the local protocol and to send the patient onward. Repair of a nerve, whether by joining it, by grafting a piece, or by transferring a tendon, is a surgical decision and never a field procedure, and its timing depends on the wound and on the patient.

The four observations that must be recorded before and after every immobilization, dressing, and movement are the distal pulses, the capillary refill, the sensation, and the motor function, and they are performed in that order on both limbs. The pulses are felt where the vessel lies near the surface, which for the arm means the thumb side and the little finger side of the wrist and for the leg means the top of the foot and behind the inner ankle, and the responder compares their strength with the other side rather than judging them alone. The capillary refill is tested by pressing the nail bed or the pad of a finger or toe until the skin blanches, releasing it, and watching how quickly the color returns, and a slow return speaks of a limb that is not being perfused. The sensation is tested with a light touch on the skin of each territory with the patient's eyes closed, and the motor function is tested by asking the patient to spread the fingers, to make a fist, to bend the wrist upward and downward, to lift the toes, and to push the foot down against the responder's hand. The responder records the four findings with the time at which they were made, because a finding without a time cannot be compared with the next one. Where the patient cannot cooperate, as with a drowsy patient, a child, or a patient whose language the responder does not share, the observations are made by watching for movement, by testing the response to a gentle pinch, and by asking a companion to interpret.

For the four observations to mean anything, they must be comparable from one examination to the next, so the same hand, the same site, and the same questions are used each time, and the uninjured limb is examined first so that the responder learns what is normal for this patient. Cold, shock, pain, fear, and the pressure of a tight bandage all change the way a limb behaves, so the responder notes the conditions along with the findings and does not blame the limb for the weather. An older patient with narrowing of the arteries may have a weak pulse in the foot before any injury, and a patient whose limb has been cold for hours may show a slow refill caused by cold rather than by a torn vessel, and both possibilities are reasons to record the state of the other side. A pulse that is present does not exclude a vascular injury, because a partly torn artery, an artery in spasm, an artery whose inner lining has lifted and is collecting clot, and an artery whose flow is maintained by smaller vessels around a block can each produce a pulse that the fingers can feel. The pulse also says nothing at all about the nerves, and a limb can have a strong pulse with a hand that cannot feel and cannot move.

This is why the deterioration between two examinations, rather than any single examination, is the finding that matters. A pulse that was strong and is now faint, a capillary refill that was brisk and is now slow, a numbness that has spread from one finger to the whole hand, a limb that was pink and is now pale or mottled, a pain that is climbing despite a splint, and a swelling that has grown by the width of a finger are each a change, and a change is evidence that something is still happening inside the limb. The responder does not answer such a change by looking under the dressing, by loosening a tourniquet that has controlled bleeding, or by tightening a bandage in the hope that pressure will help, because each of those acts can make the injury worse. The response is to stop the journey or the movement, to repeat the four observations and the general survey, to correct what can be corrected without harming the patient, and to reach the receiving team sooner with a clear account of what changed and when. Definitive hospital care is where the pressure inside the limb is measured, where the vessels are imaged, and where the decision to operate is made, and the field record of a falling examination is what tells the surgeon that the limb is losing its supply.

When the blood supply of a limb fails, the signs appear in a known order, and the order is the lesson. Pain comes first and is often severe, and it is followed by pallor as the skin loses its color, by coolness as the surface temperature falls, by numbness and tingling as the nerves feel the lack of blood, and by weakness or paralysis as the muscles follow. The loss of the pulse is one of the last signs and not the first, which is the reason that waiting for an absent pulse before acting is a way to lose a limb. The responder who finds pain, pallor, and a change in feeling below an injury has found an ischemic limb even while a pulse can still be felt, and that combination is a hard sign in practice whatever the pulse says. The limb is kept at the level of the heart or a little below it where the local protocol advises that position favors flow, it is protected from cold and from heat, and no dressing is wound tightly around it. A period such as 6 hours of warm ischemia is often quoted as the point past which a limb is unlikely to be saved, and that interval is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the tolerance of muscle and nerve changes with temperature, with the patient, and with the injury.

Every intervention that touches the limb is an opportunity to make the injury worse, which is why the four observations bracket each one. A bandage that is wound too tightly, a splint that presses over the inner side of the elbow or behind the knee, a traction device that pulls against a vessel, and a plaster that is applied while the limb is swelling can each convert a limb with a pulse into a limb without one, and the harm is found only if the observations are repeated. The rule is that the observations are taken before the dressing, before the splint, and before the move, and again after each of them, and that the two sets are compared and both are recorded. A severely deformed limb is splinted in the position found and is never straightened to make it look normal, because the movement can release a clot, stretch a vessel, or tear a nerve that was still intact, and the appearance of the limb is not the goal of first aid. Where a limb is pulseless and deformed, any attempt to bring it into line is a decision for a provider authorized by the local protocol and is not a technique taught by this manual, and even then it is done only with the observations recorded before and after. The lay first aider keeps the limb still, covers the wound, records what can be felt and seen, and hands those findings to the next provider.

A tourniquet may be the act that saves a life when a limb will not stop bleeding, and it carries its own cost to the nerves and the muscle beneath it. The nerve is among the tissues most sensitive to pressure and to the loss of blood, so a tourniquet is placed as close to the wound as the anatomy allows and never over a joint when a length of limb above the joint is free, and it is tightened until the bleeding stops rather than until a particular number of turns has been made. Once the tourniquet has controlled the bleeding it is not loosened in the field, because the clot behind it may not hold and the patient may bleed again where no one can restore control, and the time of application is written on the patient where the receiving team will see it. The reason the time matters is that the limb below the tourniquet is without flow, and the team that receives the patient must know how long that has been true in order to judge the risk of releasing it and the urgency of restoring the circulation. Because the tourniquet removes the distal pulse and changes the color of the limb, the observations during transport shift toward the tourniquet itself, the bleeding, the general circulation, and the level of consciousness, while the limb is checked for swelling and for the appearance of the skin. A limb that has been without flow for a long period and is then released can also set free substances that harm the whole patient, and that possibility is one more reason why release is a hospital decision made with preparation.

Certain injuries deserve to be named because their anatomy makes neurovascular damage likely, and the responder who recognizes them can act before the signs become obvious. A dislocated knee is the most feared, because the artery behind the knee is tethered and the foot may lose its supply, and the finding is a deformed knee with a foot that is cold or pulseless. A break of the upper arm bone may injure the nerve that lifts the wrist, and the finding is a wrist that drops when the patient tries to raise the hand, while a dislocated shoulder may leave a patch of numbness over the outer shoulder. A break of the smaller bone of the lower leg near the knee may injure the nerve that lifts the foot, and the finding is a foot that drags, while a broken thigh bone may bleed heavily into the thigh and also lie near the great vessel that runs behind it. A displaced elbow, and the break above the elbow that is common in children, can injure the vessel at the elbow and the nerves that serve the hand, and the swelling that follows may compress them further. Wounds of the palm, the armpit, the groin, and the back of the knee each cross a bundle that lies close to the skin, and they are treated with pressure, protection, and the shortest safe journey rather than with exploration.

Definitive hospital care owns everything that repairs rather than contains, and the line between the two is part of what a responder must know. Examination with ultrasound, a computed tomography scan after which the short form CT is used, an X-ray study of the vessels with contrast, and the operating room are the tools that decide whether a vessel can be repaired, whether a graft or a patch is needed, and whether the limb can be saved. Repair of an artery or a vein, repair or grafting of a nerve, transfer of a tendon, release of the pressure inside a swollen compartment, and the decision to amputate all belong to the surgeon, and none of them is a field procedure no matter how urgent the patient appears. A nerve injury may be watched for a time before it is repaired while a vessel injury is treated as an emergency, and that difference in timing is a decision made with the whole patient in view rather than from a single wound. Pain relief, protection against tetanus, and antibiotics for a contaminated wound are clinical decisions whose substance, amount, route, and timing belong to the prescriber and to the local protocol, and no reader should take them from a manual. The receiving team also needs the field record, the times of the four observations, the time any tourniquet was placed, and the changes that were seen, because a story with a gap in it forces the team to start again.

Cold travels with every serious limb injury, because the patient is exposed, often lying on the ground, and sometimes bleeding, and cold makes clotting poor and shock deeper. The responder covers the patient before the journey rather than after it, insulates the patient from the ground, replaces wet clothing with dry coverings where that can be done without disturbing the wound, and keeps the vehicle warm, while the injured limb is protected from both cold and heat. A constructed illustration, offered as a construction and not as a real patient, is a rider thrown from a motorcycle who arrives with a deformed knee and a foot that is pale and numb, where the visible injury is the knee and the emergency is the artery behind it, and where the observations taken at the scene and repeated in the ambulance are what tell the surgeon that the foot is losing its supply. The lesson of that construction is that the most dangerous finding is often the quietest one, that a pulse felt at the ankle at the first examination proves nothing about the next one, and that the record of both examinations is the most useful thing the responder carries into the hospital. Warmth, gentleness in handling, and the shortest safe journey are part of the treatment of a neurovascular injury and not additions to it.

Transport is the last field act, and it is done with the observations repeated rather than with the injury assumed to be unchanged. Before the patient is moved the destination is chosen, which is the nearest facility able to examine and repair vessels and nerves, and the receiving team is told what has been found, what has been done, and what is still changing. During the journey the responder watches the limb, the bleeding, the level of consciousness, the breathing, and the warmth of the patient, and repeats the four observations at the interval the local protocol sets, which may be more often when a finding is abnormal. A period such as every 15 minutes is often used for a stable patient and a shorter interval for one whose limb is threatened, and those intervals are illustrations that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because services and patient ages differ. Any deterioration during the journey is reported ahead of arrival where the service can do so, because the surgeon who knows that a limb is failing can prepare while the patient is still moving. The record is finished at handover with the times, the findings, and the interventions, and the patient is handed over in person rather than left with the paperwork alone.

To summarize, neurovascular injury means damage to the arteries, the veins, and the nerves of a limb, and it must be suspected from the mechanism and the position of the wound long before the signs are severe. Arterial injury may jet, may swell the limb, may hum or make a sound, or may hide behind a clot for a time; venous injury may flow quietly and still empty the circulation, and may draw air into an open vein in the neck or the chest; nerve injury may leave numbness, tingling, or weakness that no bleeding reveals. The four observations are the distal pulses, the capillary refill, the sensation, and the motor function, and they are recorded on both limbs before and after every dressing, every splint, and every move, with the time at which each set was taken. A pulse that is present does not exclude a vascular injury, because a partly torn or narrowed vessel, a vessel in spasm, and a limb supplied by smaller vessels around a block can each keep a pulse while the tissue beyond it is starving, and the pulse says nothing about the nerves. The finding that matters is the deterioration between two examinations, and the hard signs of pulsatile bleeding, a growing swelling, an absent pulse, a pale or cold limb, spreading numbness, new weakness, and a hum over the wound each demand immediate hospital care. A deformed limb is splinted in the position found and is never straightened to make it look normal, a tourniquet that has controlled bleeding is not loosened in the field and its time is written on the patient, and the observations continue during transport until handover. Warmth, gentleness, and the shortest safe journey to a facility that can repair a vessel or a nerve are part of the treatment, and every time, distance, and interval named here is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.
