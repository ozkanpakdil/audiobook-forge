# Chapter 4 — Understanding Wounds

The wound is a mechanical event before it is anything a responder can see, and the surface of the skin is the least reliable guide to what lies beneath it. Energy delivered to the body cuts, stretches, crushes, or tears whatever tissue lies in its path, and the mark on the skin is only the place where that energy entered or left. A blade divides structures in a line, a blow spreads force across a wide area, and a projectile carries energy far beyond the track it makes. Reading a wound therefore begins with the mechanism of injury, because the mechanism describes the forces that acted and the structures they may have reached. This chapter describes the categories of wounds, the assessment that follows them, and the level of care that owns each act, and it is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country where you work.

Before any wound is examined, the scene must be made safe, because a responder who becomes a second patient helps no one. Traffic, unstable vehicles, fire, electricity, water, animals, violence, structural collapse, and the hazards of the patient's own body fluids all take precedence, and personal protective equipment, or PPE, with gloves at the least, protects both the responder and the patient. The lethal possibility that must be named first is hemorrhage that cannot be controlled by direct pressure, and the finding that reveals it is blood that spurts with each beat of the heart, blood that soaks through dressing after dressing, or a swelling that grows while the responder watches. The second lethal possibility is a wound that has taken the airway, and the finding is a patient who coughs blood, whose voice has changed, or who struggles to breathe after an injury to the face or the neck. The third is a penetrating wound of the chest that has opened the pleural space, and the finding is air that moves in and out of the wound with each breath. A patient who is bleeding, burned, or lying exposed also loses heat, so protection against hypothermia begins with hemorrhage control and is not delayed until the journey.

With the scene safe and catastrophic bleeding addressed, the wound can be assessed without being probed, and probing is the first temptation to resist. Beneath the skin lie fat, then a tough sheet of fascia, then muscle, and within and between those layers run the vessels that carry blood, the nerves that carry feeling and movement, and the tendons that transmit the pull of muscle to bone. A wound that reaches only skin and fat damages little that cannot heal, while a wound of the same length that passes through fascia may have divided a tendon, a nerve, or an artery. The lay first aider covers the wound and does not probe it, because a finger or an instrument introduced into a wound can push contamination deeper, dislodge the clot that is holding back hemorrhage, and injure a structure that was intact. The trained responder may inspect and clean within the limits of training, equipment, and protocol, and exploration and repair belong to definitive hospital care.

An abrasion is a wound of friction, in which the surface of the skin is scraped away while the deeper layers stay intact. It is the wound of the road and of the sliding fall, and its importance lies less in the tissue lost than in what has been ground into the raw surface. Dirt, grit, glass, and the material of the road are driven into the skin, so a wound that looks shallow can carry a heavy load of contamination. An abrasion that covers a large area weeps fluid and loses heat, and a patient with extensive abrasions from a road crash is at risk of hypothermia as well as infection. Cleaning belongs to the trained responder or to hospital care according to the protocol in force, protection against tetanus is decided at the first contact, and the lay first aider covers the wound, keeps the patient warm, and does not scrub it.

A laceration is a wound produced by blunt force that splits the skin, and its edges are irregular because the tissue has been torn rather than cut. The force that opens the skin also bruises and crushes the tissue around the tear, so the visible wound sits inside a zone of injury that may not survive. A laceration over a bone may be the only sign of a fracture beneath it, and a laceration over a joint may communicate with the joint space, which turns a skin wound into an orthopedic emergency. Scalp lacerations bleed heavily because the scalp is richly supplied with blood, so closure of a possibly contaminated wound is not a field decision but one made by the trained responder under protocol and by definitive hospital care.

An incision is a wound made by a sharp edge that divides tissue cleanly, and its innocent appearance is its danger. The edges are neat, the tissue around them is undamaged, and the wound may close on its own if nothing important has been cut, which is why an incision is the wound most easily underestimated. The same sharp edge that produces a clean skin wound passes without resistance through a tendon, a nerve, or a vessel, and a patient may have lost the use of a hand while the skin wound looks trivial. The depth of an incision cannot be judged from the surface, and the finding that reveals a deep injury is a loss of movement, a loss of sensation, or a pulse that cannot be felt beyond the wound. The lay first aider controls bleeding with direct pressure, covers the wound, and keeps the limb still, while definitive hospital care explores the wound and repairs what has been divided.

A puncture wound is produced by a narrow object that passes through the skin and leaves a small opening, and the small opening is exactly what makes it deceptive. The surface may be 4 millimeters across, and any figure for such a size is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the importance of a wound lies in its depth and its track rather than its width. The object may have carried clothing, dirt, or metal into the depths, and it may have passed through a vessel, a nerve, the bowel, or the lung while leaving almost no external mark. A puncture over the abdomen may have perforated bowel, and the finding that raises suspicion is pain out of proportion to the wound, tenderness away from the wound, or a rigid abdomen, while a puncture over the chest may have entered the pleural space and produced breathlessness, chest pain, or a pulse that rises while the pressure falls. A puncture of a limb may have divided an artery that has sealed itself temporarily, and the wound may bleed again when the clot is disturbed, which is one reason a puncture is never probed in the field.

A crush injury is produced when a heavy force squeezes tissue between two surfaces, and the damage extends far beyond the skin. The skin may be intact while the muscle beneath has been compressed beyond the point where it can survive, so the visible wound is only one part of a much larger zone of injury. Crushed muscle releases its contents into the circulation, and the consequences include a dangerous rise in the substances the kidney must clear and swelling that can increase for hours after the patient is freed. Compartment syndrome is the complication to fear in a crushed limb, and the finding that reveals it is pain that is severe, out of proportion to the injury, and worse when the fingers or the toes are moved. Crush syndrome is the complication to fear in the patient as a whole, and the finding is a patient who was trapped, who may look stable at first, and whose pulse and pressure change as the effects of the crushed muscle appear. The lay first aider keeps the patient warm and does not attempt to release a long-trapped limb alone, while the trained responder follows the protocol and definitive hospital care manages the fluids and the surgical decisions.

An avulsion is a wound in which tissue is torn away or left hanging as a flap, and a degloving injury is the extreme form in which skin and fat are stripped from the fascia beneath them. The avulsed skin loses its blood supply even when it remains attached, because the vessels that fed it have been torn, and a flap that looks pink at the edges may be dead over most of its surface. A degloving injury of a limb or a hand is a severely contaminated wound with a large area of dead tissue, and it belongs to definitive hospital care from the first contact. The lay first aider controls bleeding with pressure that does not further tear the flap, covers the wound, keeps the patient warm, and sends any detached piece of tissue with the patient in the manner the local service directs. The trained responder does not cut, trim, or replace the flap, because the decision about which tissue can survive belongs to the surgeon who can see it. The finding that demands immediate transport is a limb or a hand stripped over a wide area, with bleeding that continues and a patient who is becoming cold and pale.

Every wound is contaminated to some degree, and the risk of infection is decided by what entered it, how much entered, and what the tissue has left to defend itself with. The organisms that matter most come from soil, manure, saliva, stagnant water, and the skin and clothing of the patient, and each source carries a different set of threats. The wound with the greatest risk combines heavy contamination with dead tissue and a poor blood supply, because antibiotics and the body's own defenses reach living, well-perfused tissue and not the dead tissue at the center of a wound. A wound left untreated for 6 hours or more has accumulated organisms and has lost the chance of clean closure, and that interval is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the safe period differs between services. A bite carries the organisms of the mouth that made it, and a human bite is especially aggressive, while a bite over a joint or the hand is the most dangerous of all because the joint space and the tendon sheaths lie close to the surface. The assessment therefore records the source of contamination, the time that has passed, and the state of the tissue at the base and the edges of the wound, and the decision about protection against infection is made by the prescriber under the local protocol.

An open fracture is a broken bone that communicates with the outside through a wound, and it is a surgical emergency rather than a wound to be dressed and forgotten. The bone end may be visible, or the wound may be small and the fracture discovered only when the limb is examined, so a fracture with a skin wound anywhere near it is treated as open until definitive care decides otherwise. The exposed bone and deep tissues are contaminated at the moment of injury, and the risk of infection is far greater than in a closed fracture because the organisms have direct access to the bone. The lay first aider covers the wound with a clean dressing, controls bleeding with pressure around the wound rather than on the bone end, and immobilizes the limb in the position found. The trained responder splints the limb, records the pulses and the feeling beyond the fracture, and does not push the bone end back into the wound. A severely deformed limb is never straightened to make it look normal, because of the risk of further damage to vessels and nerves, and the cleaning, the removal of dead tissue, and the stabilization of the bone all belong to definitive hospital care.

The assessment begins with the history that the scene provides, and the mechanism of injury is the most important single piece of information. The responder asks what object produced the wound, in what direction the force traveled, how much energy was involved, and how far the patient fell or was thrown. The answers describe the forces that acted on the body and therefore the structures that may have been injured, and they are more reliable than the appearance of the wound. The patient's own history matters, and the responder asks about diabetes, about medicines that suppress the immune system, and about protection against tetanus in the past.

The examination of a wound follows the same order every time, so that nothing is omitted when the scene is loud and the patient is frightened. The patient is first assessed by the primary survey, and only when the airway, the breathing, the circulation, the level of consciousness, and the exposure have been addressed does the wound itself receive attention. The wound is then exposed fully, which may require cutting away clothing rather than pulling it over the wound, and it is examined in good light with the limb in the position found. The responder looks at the size, the shape, the edges, the color of the tissue, the presence of foreign material, and the direction of the wound, and notes whether blood is flowing, oozing, or absent. The responder feels the limb beyond the wound for warmth, swelling, tenderness, and deformity, compares it with the uninjured side, records the pulse beyond the wound, tests the feeling in the skin, and asks the patient to move the part if movement is safe. The finding that a pulse is absent, that feeling is lost, or that movement is impossible sends the patient to definitive hospital care without delay.

The contrast between the small wound and the dramatic one is the lesson this chapter exists to teach. A small puncture can hide a deep injury because the surface closes behind the object, because the track is narrow, and because the structures that were divided may bleed slowly or not at all until the clot that sealed them is disturbed. A spectacular laceration can be superficial because the skin and the fat beneath it are the only tissues the force reached, and the bleeding from a richly supplied area makes the wound look worse than it is. The responder therefore judges a wound by the mechanism and by what lies beneath it, and never by its size or by the amount of blood on the dressing alone. A wound of 3 millimeters over the abdomen, the chest, the neck, or a major vessel is as serious as a large open wound, and the number itself is a teaching illustration whose value must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol.

Certain findings mark a wound as one that needs surgical evaluation rather than a dressing, and the responder who holds them in the order of the examination will not miss them. The first is bleeding that continues despite sustained direct pressure, and the second is a swelling that expands while the responder watches, which suggests a vessel is bleeding into the tissue. The third is a wound in which bone, tendon, muscle, or bowel is visible, because those structures must not be handled in the field. The fourth is a wound with an embedded object, which is stabilized in place and never removed in the field, because removal may release hemorrhage that cannot be controlled or cause further damage to the tissue around it. The fifth is a wound that crosses a joint, a wound over the palm or the sole, a wound of the face, and a wound of the genital area, and the sixth is any wound with a pulse, feeling, or movement lost beyond it. The seventh is gross contamination with dead tissue at the edges or the base, and a wound present for many hours, a wound in a patient whose immune defenses are impaired, and a wound with a foul discharge or crackling in the tissues all belong on the same list.

Location decides the meaning of a wound as much as its size, and some regions tolerate no delay. A wound of the neck may have divided the great vessels, the airway, or the esophagus, and the finding that reveals danger is a swelling that enlarges, a voice that changes, or air that moves in the tissues of the neck. A wound of the chest may have entered the pleural space or the heart, and the finding is breathlessness, a rising pulse, a falling pressure, or a wound that bubbles with each breath, while a wound of the abdomen may have entered the peritoneal cavity and produced tenderness, rigidity, or pain that spreads away from the wound. A wound of the back may have entered the spine, and the finding is a loss of feeling or movement below the level of the wound. A wound of a limb may have injured a vessel or a nerve, and the findings beyond it are the pulse, the feeling, and the movement that the responder must record before and after any splinting.

The tetanus question must be raised at the first contact, because the decision is most effective when it is made early and the patient may not be seen again. Tetanus is a disease caused by a toxin produced by organisms that live in soil and manure, and it follows contamination of a wound, especially a wound with dead tissue, a deep puncture, or a wound that was not cleaned. The responder asks whether the patient has been protected against tetanus in the past and when the last protection was given, and asks in the same breath about the nature of the wound and the material that contaminated it. The finding that makes the decision urgent is a wound that is deep, that contains dead tissue, or that was contaminated with soil or manure, while a clean superficial wound in a patient with documented past protection makes it less urgent. The decision about whether protection is needed, and with what, belongs to the trained responder or the prescriber acting under the local protocol, and the lay first aider asks the question and passes it on without giving any medicine.

Antibiotics are a decision and never a dose in this book, and the decision belongs to the prescriber who knows the wound, the patient, and the local resistance of organisms. The finding that makes the decision urgent is a wound with heavy contamination, dead tissue, a deep track, or a delay in treatment, together with a patient whose defenses are impaired. The finding that makes it less clear is a clean wound of recent onset in a healthy patient, and the prescriber weighs the benefit of protection against the harm of unnecessary medicine. The lay first aider never gives an antibiotic and never carries one, and the trained responder gives one only where the protocol and the authority to do so exist. The whole decision is governed by the local protocol and the judgment of the clinician responsible for the patient.

Pain is a wound in its own right, and its relief is part of care rather than an afterthought. The finding that makes relief urgent is pain that is severe, pain that prevents the patient from moving or breathing deeply, and pain accompanied by fear and a rising pulse. The decision about pain relief belongs to the trained responder or the physician acting under the local protocol, and the substance, the amount, the route, and the interval are the business of the prescriber and are never learned from a manual. The lay first aider reduces pain by splinting, by positioning, by covering the wound, by keeping the patient warm, and by speaking calmly, while the patient in shock, with a head injury, or a child requires particular caution and the most senior clinician available. A patient whose pain is out of proportion to the wound, or whose pain increases after an initial improvement, must be re-examined, because that change may be the first sign of a compartment syndrome or of a developing infection.

The three levels of care run through this chapter because the same wound asks a different act from each pair of hands. The lay first aider makes the scene safe, calls for help, controls bleeding with direct pressure, covers the wound with a clean dressing, keeps the patient warm, does not probe, does not remove an embedded object, does not straighten a deformed limb, and does not give any medicine. The trained responder or paramedic adds the recording of the pulse, the feeling, and the movement beyond the wound, the splinting of the limb, the control of hemorrhage with the devices the service authorizes, and the decision to move the patient rapidly toward definitive care. The nurse and the physician in a district hospital add the cleaning of the wound, the assessment of the deep structures, the decision about tetanus protection and about antibiotics, and the referral of the patient who needs more than the hospital can provide. Definitive hospital care, with the operating theater, the imaging, and the surgeon, owns exploration, the removal of dead tissue, the repair of vessels, tendons, and nerves, the stabilization of fractures, and the removal of a retained foreign body when that removal is clinically indicated. No manual can transfer those acts to the field, and a responder who attempts them does harm that the patient did not have before.

Documentation is part of treatment, because the receiving team acts on what the responder has recorded and cannot act on what has been forgotten. The record carries the mechanism of injury, the time of the injury, the time any tourniquet was applied, the wounds that were found, the contamination that was seen, the pulses and the feeling and the movement that were present, and the protection against tetanus that was discussed. The time a tourniquet is applied is written on the patient, in a place the receiving team will see, because the duration of its use changes the decisions made in the hospital and because the tourniquet itself is not loosened in the field once it has controlled the bleeding. The record also carries what was not done, including the dressing applied and the object left in place, and the handover is spoken in the same order as the primary survey. A constructed illustration may help here: a responder who writes the time of a tourniquet on the skin of the patient's forehead, where no dressing will cover it, has given the hospital a fact it could not otherwise recover, and that example is invented for teaching and describes no real patient or incident.

Warmth and continuous watching are the duties that continue after the wound is covered and before the patient reaches care. A patient who is bleeding loses heat with the blood, a patient who is exposed loses heat to the air, and a patient who is immobilized on a cold surface loses heat to the ground, and cold makes bleeding worse because it interferes with the clotting the body is trying to achieve. The responder removes wet clothing where that is possible, covers the patient with blankets or whatever is available, insulates the patient from the ground, and keeps the head covered, all without delaying transport. The observations are repeated rather than taken once, because the trend of the pulse, the pressure, the breathing, and the level of consciousness is more informative than any single measurement. A temperature of 38 degrees Celsius, or a pulse of 110 beats per minute, is a change to report, and both figures are illustrations that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. A wound that becomes more painful, that begins to smell, that discharges, or that shows darkening tissue around its edges has changed, and the responder who looks again and says out loud what has changed is doing the most valuable thing that can be done at the roadside.

To summarize, a wound is a mechanical event whose surface appearance is the least reliable guide to the injury beneath it, and the responder reads it by the mechanism of injury, by the structures at risk, and by the findings beyond the wound. Scene safety and hemorrhage control come before every other act, an embedded object is left in place, a deformed limb is not straightened, and no wound is probed in the field. Abrasions, lacerations, incisions, punctures, crush injuries, avulsions, degloving injuries, open fractures, and bites each destroy a different tissue and each carry a different risk of contamination, and the small puncture may hide more than the spectacular laceration. The findings that demand surgical evaluation rather than a dressing are bleeding that will not stop, an expanding swelling, exposed deep structures, a wound that crosses a joint, a lost pulse, lost feeling, lost movement, and gross contamination with dead tissue. The tetanus question is asked at the first contact, and the decisions about protection against tetanus, about antibiotics, about pain relief, about fluids, and about oxygen belong to the trained responder or the prescriber under the local protocol. Every number in this chapter is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between services, countries, and editions. The lay first aider covers, warms, and sends, the trained responder assesses, splints, and records, and definitive hospital care explores, repairs, and removes, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, or the law of the country where you work.
