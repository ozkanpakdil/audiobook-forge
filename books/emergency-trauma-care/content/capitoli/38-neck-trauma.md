# Chapter 38 — Neck Trauma

The neck is the narrowest column in the body to carry so much that cannot be given up, and a wound there is measured in minutes. Within a space a hand can nearly encircle lie the airway, the great vessels that supply the brain, the spinal cord, the esophagus, and the nerves that move the shoulder, the tongue, and the vocal cords. A knife, a bullet, a shard of glass, or a fragment of metal may open several of those structures with a single track, and a track entering the base of the neck may continue into the chest, where its damage is invisible from outside. This chapter follows neck trauma from injury to the operating room and names at each step who may do what, and it is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country where you work.

Penetrating injury is the form usually meant when the neck is called dangerous, and it arrives as a knife in an assault, a bullet or a fragment, a fall through glass, a machinery accident, a spike on a building site, or the wire that strikes a rider across the throat. Blunt injury is quieter and just as lethal, and it comes from a seat belt, a punch or a choke hold, a hanging or a strangulation, a blow from a bar, or a fall onto a handlebar. The mechanism of injury is therefore the first prediction the responder makes, because it tells the examiner where to look and what to expect. In the neck more than anywhere else, the size of the opening says almost nothing about the structures beneath it.

The first danger in neck trauma is the airway, the second is a major blood vessel, and the two can arrive together. Airway compromise may be direct, when the trachea or the larynx is cut or crushed, or indirect, when blood, vomit, or swelling fills the space around it and closes it from outside. A major vessel may bleed outward through the wound, inward into the tissues where it forms a swelling that finally presses the airway shut, or into the chest, where the loss is entirely hidden. The spinal cord lies behind the airway and between the vessels, so a wound across the middle of the neck may leave a patient talking while the cord below it has stopped working. A wound at the base of the neck may open vessels and the lung without any swelling appearing outside.

No care begins until the scene is safe, and a stabbing or a shooting carries hazards an ordinary road crash does not. The assailant may still be present, a crowd may still be fighting, a weapon may lie on the ground, and a responder who kneels without looking up may become the next casualty. Traffic, fire, live electricity, water, a collapsing structure, an animal, and machinery that has not been isolated are the other hazards of the scene, and each is a lethal possibility rather than a nuisance. The patient's own blood is the quieter hazard, because a neck wound can spurt some distance, so personal protective equipment, or PPE, with gloves, eye protection, and a barrier for the face is worn before the first hand is laid on the wound. The finding that ends the pause is a scene made safe by someone with the authority to make it safe, and until then the responder waits at the edge and calls for help.

The finding that announces an airway in danger is most often a change in the voice, because the structures that make sound lie in the path of any injury to the middle of the neck. Hoarseness, a muffled or lost voice, a barking cough, noisy breathing on inspiration, and a patient who speaks a few words before pausing for air are each an airway that is narrowing while the responder watches. Blood in the mouth, a wound that bubbles with each breath, and a crackling under the skin that feels like pressed snow are signs that air or blood has escaped into tissue where it does not belong, and that crackling is called subcutaneous emphysema. A swelling on one side of the neck that grows, a windpipe that has shifted from the midline, difficulty in swallowing, and increasing restlessness in a patient who was calm are the same warning spoken in another language. Any of those findings turns a small-looking wound into an emergency that requires an airway plan at once and transport without delay.

The vascular finding that must never be missed is bleeding that does not stop, whether it pours from the wound, pulses with the heartbeat, or collects in the neck as a hematoma that enlarges while the responder holds it. A hematoma that expands is an artery bleeding into a closed space, and the calm that follows the first minutes may mean only that the pressure inside the swelling has matched the pressure inside the vessel. A pulse that cannot be felt on one side of the neck, a cool and pale limb, or a loss of movement or feeling on one side of the body points to a vessel that supplies the brain or a limb, and such a finding demands a surgical center as fast as the journey allows. A vibration felt over the swelling, which clinicians call a thrill or a bruit, is another sign of an abnormal connection between an artery and a vein. The responder does not probe the wound, does not press into the swelling to test it, and does not lift a dressing to look, because each act can release bleeding that was contained.

The order of action at the scene is fixed by what kills first, and in the neck that order is difficult because the airway and the bleeding are neighbors. The lay first aider presses on the bleeding point with a clean pad while keeping the middle of the throat free, and the trained responder adds suction, an airway device, and packing where a protocol authorizes it. Definitive hospital care owns the control of a vessel that cannot be reached from outside, the repair of the airway and the esophagus, and the removal of a foreign body. The finding that tells the responder which danger is winning is the patient's ability to speak and to breathe, and it is repeated at every stage.

The primary survey is performed in the order remembered as ABCDE, which stands for airway, breathing, circulation, disability, and exposure, and each letter is a question whose answer decides whether the responder moves on or stops to treat what has just been found. In most patients the airway is examined first, but in a patient with catastrophic bleeding from a limb or the neck the control of that bleeding may be placed ahead of everything else, with the two acts performed together by two pairs of hands where they exist. Breathing follows and asks whether air is moving on both sides of the chest, whether the chest is rising evenly, and whether a wound at the base of the neck has entered the chest. Circulation follows, asking about the pulse, the color and warmth of the skin, and any bleeding that is ongoing, while disability asks about the level of consciousness, the pupils, and the movement and feeling of all four limbs. Exposure comes last, because the neck cannot be assessed in isolation, and the whole body is examined for other wounds before the patient is covered again and kept warm.

Opening an airway in a patient with a suspected neck injury requires a technique that does not bend the neck, so the trained responder holds the head neutral with both hands while lifting the jaw forward. The lay first aider who has been taught the same maneuver may use it, and the habit of tilting the head back is avoided here because it moves a spine that may already be unstable. The cervical spine is at risk in these injuries, and those precautions must never become the reason an airway is left closed, because a patient who is not breathing has no use for an intact spine. Blood, vomit, and loose teeth are cleared from the mouth only when they can be seen and reached, and a patient is turned onto the side as a unit only when the turn can be done without twisting the neck. Movement is kept to what is necessary, the head stays in line with the body, and the turn onto a board or stretcher is performed as one movement by a team that has practiced it, with any change in breathing, voice, or limb function recorded at once.

The trained responder or paramedic has tools the lay first aider does not, and each is used within training, authorization, and a protocol. Suction removes blood and vomit, a curved or straight plastic airway holds the tongue from the back of the throat in an unconscious patient, and a mask with a bag lets two hands hold the mask and jaw while an assistant squeezes the bag. A device sitting above the opening of the larynx may be used by a trained provider, and placing a tube through the vocal cords belongs to a provider authorized for that skill, most often a physician trained in anesthesia or a paramedic under a protocol naming it. Where the airway cannot be opened from above, a surgical opening through the front of the neck is made only by a provider trained and authorized for that procedure. Oxygen and the decision to give it belong to the protocol and the prescriber, and this manual names neither an amount nor a device setting.

Control of bleeding from the neck changes the usual ladder of pressure, because the same hand that stops the bleeding can also stop the breathing. The lay first aider presses with the fingertips on the bleeding point at the edge of the wound, or presses the wound closed from both sides, keeping the middle of the throat free so the windpipe is not compressed. Nothing is ever wound completely around the neck, because a band that encircles it can close the airway, press on the veins that drain the brain, and hide a swelling growing beneath it. The dressing must not compress the airway: a flat pad is held by hand or fixed to the side of the neck and the shoulder with tape that does not cross the front of the throat, and if blood soaks through, further pads are laid on top without lifting the first. A dressing that has become tight, a voice that has changed since it was applied, or a patient struggling for air shows that it is doing harm, and it is loosened or removed and the airway reassessed at once.

Packing a wound of the neck is a skill for the trained responder or the paramedic working under a protocol, and it is used when pressure on the surface cannot reach a bleeding point deep in the tissue. The gauze is directed along the track of the wound toward the source and held firmly, while the responder watches the breathing and keeps the packing away from the windpipe. The neck is a junctional site, the name for places where a limb joins the body and a tourniquet cannot be placed, and junctional bleeding is controlled by packing and pressure rather than by any encircling device. A hemostatic dressing, a gauze carrying an agent that helps blood clot, is carried in some services and not others, and its use requires training, an appropriate wound, and continued pressure. Nothing here names an agent, an amount, or a duration, because those belong to the local protocol and the authorized provider, and no dressing is a reason to delay the operating room.

A wound of a large vein in the neck carries a hazard that no other wound in this book carries in quite the same way, which is the entry of air into the circulation. When such a vein is open and the pressure inside the chest falls during a breath, air can be drawn into the vessel, and the finding is a wound that sucks or bubbles at the moment of inspiration, or a sudden deterioration in a patient whose wound looked controlled. The first response is to cover the wound with a dressing that does not allow air to pass, to press on it, and to keep it covered while the patient is moved, and the position that lowers the wound relative to the heart is used where it can be achieved without closing the airway or twisting the neck. A patient who becomes suddenly breathless, restless, or unresponsive after a neck wound is treated as having air in the circulation until proven otherwise. That is a hospital emergency from its first moment, and the field contribution is the occlusive dressing, the pressure, and the shortest safe journey.

The neck contains the spinal cord and the nerves that leave it for the arms, the diaphragm, the tongue, and the shoulder, so a neurologic examination belongs to the first minutes and not to the hospital alone. The trained responder records the level of consciousness, the size and reaction of the pupils, and, for each limb in turn, whether it can be moved against resistance, whether it can be moved at all, and whether sensation is present. A patient with a stab wound of the neck and weakness of one arm, or with numbness in a band across the chest, has a neurologic injury that changes the destination and the urgency even when the wound itself is small and dry. The level at which feeling or movement is lost is written down with the time, because the receiving team uses that level and its trend to judge whether the cord is being compressed. The lay first aider does not perform this examination but contributes by noticing and reporting any change and by keeping the patient still until trained help takes over.

Behind the airway and beside the spine run the esophagus and the pharynx, and injury to them is the one most often missed at the first examination because it announces itself late. The findings that raise the suspicion are pain on swallowing, blood mixed with saliva, air in the tissue of the neck, a wound near the midline or tracking down behind the windpipe, and a fever that appears after a delay. Those are not findings a responder confirms or excludes in the field, and no test performed at the roadside can settle the question, so a wound whose track could have reached those structures is treated as though it had. A missed injury of the esophagus or the pharynx leads to infection in the deep spaces of the neck and the chest, and that infection can kill a patient days after a wound that seemed minor has closed. Such a patient is therefore referred for hospital assessment even when the bleeding has stopped and the airway is quiet.

Blunt injury to the neck deserves its own warning, because it leaves no wound to remind anyone that something inside may be broken. A blow from a bar or a fist, a choke hold or a strangulation, a seat belt across the throat, a wire across the road, and a fall onto a bicycle handlebar can fracture the larynx or tear the tissues around the airway, and the patient may walk and talk at first. The findings are hoarseness, a change in the pitch of the voice, pain when the neck is touched, a loss of the usual landmarks at the front of the neck, crackling under the skin, and difficulty in breathing or swallowing that worsens over the first hours. A patient who has been strangled may have a neck that looks entirely normal and still develop a closing airway, so a normal appearance is never used to leave such a patient unobserved. Where a patient is still suspended, the trained responder supports the body before the ligature is released so that the weight does not tear the neck further, and a changed voice after any such injury is treated as an emergency.

Cold is a complication of neck trauma and not a detail of the patient's comfort, and a patient with a neck wound is at risk from the first minute. The neck is often left uncovered because the wound must be watched, blood and water soak the clothing, and a patient who is kept flat and still on the ground loses heat into the earth beneath. Cold makes the blood clot poorly, deepens shock, and makes the heart more likely to develop a dangerous rhythm, so a neck wound that is being watched cannot be an excuse for leaving the patient exposed. The lay first aider removes wet clothing where that can be done without disturbing the wound, covers the patient with whatever is at hand, and insulates the patient from the ground, while the trained responder continues that protection during transport. A body temperature below 35 degrees Celsius is often named as the point at which cold itself becomes dangerous, and that value is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

Shock follows neck trauma for the same reasons it follows any other injury, and it is harder to recognize because much of the lost blood may be somewhere the responder cannot see. A wound at the base of the neck can open a vessel in the chest, and the blood then collects around the lung, compressing it and reducing the oxygen that reaches the blood still circulating. The findings are breathing that has become rapid and shallow, a chest that moves unevenly, a pulse rising and softening, pale cool skin, and a level of consciousness dulling while the neck wound appears quiet. A pressure such as 90 millimeters of mercury is sometimes quoted as a threshold for concern, and it is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol rather than a rule taken from these pages. Fluids, blood products, and medicines for pain are decisions that belong to the prescriber and the local protocol, and this manual names no substance, no amount, no route, and no frequency.

A constructed illustration, offered as an illustration and not as a description of any real patient, is the young man who walks into a clinic holding the side of his neck with a bloodstained cloth over a wound below the angle of the jaw. He is talking, his voice is slightly hoarse, and the wound is small and has almost stopped bleeding, which is exactly the picture that invites a responder to be reassured. While the wound is examined, the neck on that side becomes fuller than the other, the hoarseness deepens, and the patient says that swallowing is painful. Those findings are why he is sent immediately to a surgical center rather than asked to wait, and nothing was probed, nothing was packed blindly, and no dressing was tied around the neck. The case shows only how a quiet wound and a loud neck can belong to the same patient.

The neck cannot be examined in isolation, because the injury that made the neck wound may have made others, and an object still embedded in the neck is left exactly where it is while the patient is moved with it in place. Removal in the field can release bleeding that cannot be controlled and can injure a structure the object is holding apart, and in the neck the object may be plugging a torn artery, supporting a crushed airway, or lying against the spinal cord. The correct act is to build bulky padding around it, press on the bleeding points beside it rather than on the object, and send the patient to a surgical team. The trained responder examines the front and back of the neck, the mouth, and the base of the skull, and undresses the patient enough to find every wound, because a bullet may enter the neck and leave elsewhere. Each wound is described by its position, its size, whether it bleeds, and whether it bubbles or sucks, and that description is repeated at handover.

The destination for a patient with a serious neck wound is a facility with a surgeon, an operating room, and the imaging needed to plan the operation, and immediate surgical referral is the rule for every wound that could have crossed the deep structures. Computed tomography, called CT after its first mention, is the main way of judging which structures are injured when the patient is stable enough to pass through the scanner, and it may be combined with an injection that makes the vessels stand out on the image. Ultrasound at the bedside can show blood around the lung in a patient too unstable to leave the resuscitation area, and catheter angiography, in which a dye is placed within a vessel and followed on an image, examines vessels that are difficult to reach. Definitive hospital care owns the exploration of the neck, the repair of a divided artery or vein, the repair of the larynx or the trachea, the repair of the esophagus, and the removal of a foreign body. None of that is a field procedure, and the transfer is not delayed by the appearance of the wound or by the hope that a quiet neck stays quiet.

The handover is part of the treatment, and in neck trauma it carries facts that no arriving team can reconstruct for itself. It states the mechanism of injury and its time, what caused the wound, how many wounds were found, whether the voice changed or the swelling grew, and what the patient could move and feel before and after movement. Reassessment continues in a rhythm from the scene to the operating room, returning in order to the airway, breathing, circulation, level of consciousness, and limbs, and a patient who was talking and has become silent has given more information than any single look at the wound. The most dangerous patient is the one who looks well, because a small wound under the jaw and a hoarse voice can precede a closing airway before any other sign appears, so the concern is named aloud and a patient who must wait is placed where the airway can be watched.

The position in which the patient is carried weighs the airway, the spine, and the circulation against one another. A patient who breathes with difficulty may sit up where the spine and circulation permit it, a patient in shock is kept flat, and an unconscious or vomiting patient is turned to the side as a unit so that blood drains rather than being inhaled. A patient with an unstable cervical spine is kept in line with the body, and movement of the neck during transport is avoided because it can add a spinal injury to the wound. The patient is often terrified, and terror makes the breathing faster and the movement greater, so the responder speaks in short sentences, says what is being done, and asks the patient to keep the head and body still, without calling the wound minor or removing the dressing. The finding that changes the position is a patient who can no longer breathe in the position chosen, and the position is changed at once.

Protection against infection begins at the first contact and is completed in the hospital. The field contribution is to cover the wound with a clean dressing, avoid touching the inside of the wound, and keep the dressing in place until the receiving team examines it, while cleaning and the removal of dead tissue belong to definitive care under good light and with anesthesia. The tetanus question is raised at the first contact and answered by the provider authorized under the local protocol, and antibiotics follow the same rule, in that a deep, contaminated, or bite wound makes the decision urgent while the substance and the amount belong to the prescriber and the local guideline. The anatomy of the neck also forbids two techniques that work elsewhere, because a tourniquet cannot be placed around the neck and a pressure dressing that binds it tightly would compress the airway and both carotid arteries. The skills in this chapter are learned with a teacher and certified by an authority that defines what may be done without supervision, and local protocol decides what equipment is carried and which hospital receives the patient.

To summarize, the neck packs the airway, the great vessels, the spinal cord, the esophagus, and the nerves into a small space, so a wound there can injure several vital structures with one track and kill while the opening looks small. Airway compromise is the first danger and major-vessel injury the second, and the bleeding may hide inside the neck as a growing swelling or inside the chest as blood around the lung. The scene is made safe first, because violence, traffic, machinery, and the patient's own blood can claim the responder as well. The order of action is the primary survey remembered as ABCDE, with the airway opened in a way that keeps the neck in line, with the cervical spine protected but never at the expense of a closing airway, and with a changed voice, noisy breathing, crackling under the skin, or a growing swelling treated as an emergency. Bleeding is controlled by localized pressure that spares the windpipe, by packing where the provider is trained and authorized, and never by a band around the neck, because the dressing must not compress the airway or hide a hematoma. An embedded object is stabilized and never removed in the field, a patient who is bleeding, exposed, or waiting is kept warm, and every patient whose wound could have crossed the deep structures is referred for immediate surgical assessment. Every number, time, and threshold here is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, or the law of the country where you work.
