# Chapter 14 — Gunshot Wounds to the Limbs

A gunshot wound to a limb is the most common and the most survivable firearm injury. Its ordinary appearance is why it is underestimated, because the wound is small while the injury that kills is a torn vessel deep inside. The lethal possibility is hemorrhage from a major artery of the limb, and its finding is blood that spurts with the heartbeat. The same warning is a swelling that grows while the responder watches, or blood that soaks through dressing after dressing. The scene is made safe first, because a firearm scene may hold a weapon, a person who may fire again, traffic, or broken glass. Personal protective equipment, or PPE, with gloves at the least, is worn before contact, and protection against hypothermia begins at once, because a bleeding patient on cold ground loses heat quickly. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country where you work.

Making a shooting scene safe is a sequence, and it begins before the patient is approached. The first question is whether the person who fired is still present and whether any authority controls the area. The trained responder or the paramedic waits for that control, and the lay first aider waits with them, because a rescuer who is shot adds a second patient to a scene with too few hands. The other hazards are traffic, unstable glass, fire, and a structure that may fall. The finding that tells the responder to hold back is a hostile crowd, an unsecured weapon, or a scene no authority has declared safe.

The injury inside a limb threatens the limb first and the life second, and the order is not fixed. A torn artery in the thigh can threaten both at once, because the thigh holds a large volume of tissue and blood. The structures at risk are the artery, the vein beside it, the nerve that runs with them, the muscle, and the bone. Muscle tolerates lost blood flow far less well than skin, and nerve tolerates it least of all. The principle of life before limb decides the order when the two compete, because a patient who is breathing and circulating can be given a limb later. The finding that settles the order is not the size of the wound but whether the patient can breathe and circulate.

The mechanism of injury is the first prediction the responder makes. With a bullet, the prediction is that the track is longer and wider than the openings suggest. A projectile deposits energy in the tissue it crosses, so muscle some distance from the wound may be dead. The entrance and the exit, if there is one, show direction of travel and never depth, so one small opening may hide a shattered bone and a torn vessel. A wound that crosses a joint is especially dangerous, because the joint may be contaminated and the vessels behind the knee are tethered. A constructed illustration, offered as a construction and not as a real patient or a real incident, is a person struck in the calf who later feels the leg tighten and swell.

Arterial injury announces itself in a small number of ways, and each is taught in the examination. The clearest finding is blood that leaves the wound in jets that keep time with the heartbeat. The next is a swelling beneath the skin that grows while the responder's hand rests on it. A limb beyond the wound that has become pale, cool, and numb, or a pulse at the wrist or foot that cannot be felt, points to a vessel no longer carrying flow. A wound that stopped bleeding may resume when the circulation rises or a dressing is lifted, because a divided artery can retract and clot for a time. The trap is a wound that looks dry while the limb is still warm, because a torn artery may bleed into deep tissue.

Venous injury is quieter and can be just as dangerous, because the large veins of the thigh, the groin, and the armpit return blood to the heart. The bleeding is a steady flow of dark blood rather than a jet, and it may stop with firm pressure and restart when pressure is released. A torn vein in the groin or the armpit lies where no tourniquet can be placed, so pressure must reach the exact bleeding point. A wound near a large vein in the upper chest may draw air into the open vessel with each breath, and the finding is a wound that sucks at the moment of inspiration. Arterial and venous injury often occur together, and blood escaping into a closed compartment has nowhere to go but into the tissue. The finding is a limb that is tense, larger than its partner, and painful beyond what the wound explains, while the pulse may still be present.

Nerve injury is the part of a limb wound most easily missed, because it makes no blood and no swelling. A nerve that runs beside a major vessel is often injured with it, so numbness, weakness, and an inability to move the fingers or the toes belong to the examination. The responder asks the patient to move each part of the limb and to say what can be felt along the skin. A nerve deficit appearing after an examination that was first normal is a finding of the greatest importance. It suggests that swelling or a dressing is now pressing on the nerve rather than that the nerve was cut by the bullet. Repair of a divided nerve is a surgical decision made later, and it is never a field procedure. The lay first aider does not test sensation with a needle or a pin, because the test itself can injure tissue that is already at risk.

An open fracture is present when bone is exposed in the wound or when the wound connects with a broken bone that cannot be seen. A gunshot wound to a limb is treated as an open fracture until imaging shows otherwise. The bone is not straightened and the limb is not pulled into a better shape, because that movement can tear a vessel or a nerve and can release a clot holding back hemorrhage. The limb is splinted in the position found, with the wound covered and the joints above and below supported, and that work belongs to the trained responder or the paramedic. Distal circulation is then assessed by looking, feeling, and asking, on the injured limb and on the uninjured limb for comparison. The responder checks the color and warmth of the skin, seeks the pulse at the wrist or the foot, and asks about movement and feeling. A pulse that is present does not exclude an arterial injury, so each element is recorded separately.

The neurovascular examination is not performed once and then trusted, because a limb can deteriorate quietly between the first normal examination and the handover. It is repeated at the points the local protocol defines, which may be at the scene and again at the receiving facility, and each result is written with the time it was taken. A patient whose first examination was normal can develop an absent pulse, a new numbness, or a limb that has become tense and painful. The recorded trend is the gift the field gives the hospital, because a normal examination followed by a worsening one says more than a single reassuring note. The trained responder and the paramedic own this repeated examination. The lay first aider reports what the patient says about pain, movement, and feeling and does not remove a dressing to look underneath it.

Compartment syndrome is the delayed complication that follows a limb which was saved. The limb is divided by tough sheets of tissue into compartments holding muscle, vessels, and nerves. When bleeding or swelling raises the pressure in one of those closed spaces, the smallest vessels are compressed and the muscle begins to die. The earliest and most reliable finding is pain far greater than the wound appears to justify, together with pain when the muscles are gently stretched. The compartment becomes tense and firm, and numbness appears in the territory of the nerve passing through it, while the pulse is often still present. Weakness and an absent pulse are late findings, so the responder escalates on pain and tension rather than on the pulse. Release of the pressure is a surgical procedure called fasciotomy, and it is never attempted in the field. The pressure inside a compartment is measured in the hospital, and any figure quoted for it must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the threshold differs with the patient and with the way the measurement is taken.

Restoring flow to a limb that has been without blood can itself cause harm, a paradox the responder must understand without managing it in the field. When the surgeon reopens a vessel, the starved muscle receives blood again and swells, and compartment syndrome can appear in a limb whose pulse has just returned. The finding that should raise the alarm is new and severe pain together with a limb that has become tense and hard. A limb without flow for a long period may also release substances into the circulation that affect the heart and the kidneys. The responder's part is to keep the limb in the position found, leave every dressing and splint as it was applied, record the times, and move the patient quickly.

What the lay first aider may do for a bleeding limb is narrow, effective, and worth doing well. The first action is direct pressure on the wound through a clean cloth or dressing, applied with gloved hands and held without interruption while help is called. If blood shows through, a further layer is placed on top and the hand returns to the same place, because lifting the dressing breaks the clot that is forming. A foreign body such as a fragment of metal or glass is not removed, and it is stabilized in place with padding built around it. The patient is covered and insulated from the ground at the same time, because cold is a complication of trauma and not a detail of comfort. The lay first aider does not probe the wound, pour anything into it, or straighten a deformity, and hands over when the trained responder arrives.

The tourniquet is the next rung of the ladder and is owned by the trained responder or the paramedic working under a protocol. The indication is life-threatening bleeding from a limb that direct pressure and packing have not controlled. The place is between the wound and the heart, as close to the wound as the anatomy allows and away from a joint when a length of limb above it is available. The tourniquet is tightened until the bleeding stops rather than until a particular number of turns has been made, and it is not loosened in the field. The time it was applied is written on the patient where the receiving team cannot miss it and spoken aloud at handover. A duration such as 2 hours is often quoted as the point at which the risk to muscle and nerve becomes serious, and that interval is an illustration which must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. The reason it is not loosened is that the clot behind it may not hold and the patient may hemorrhage again where control cannot be restored.

A wound in the groin, the armpit, the shoulder, or the base of the neck lies where no tourniquet can be placed, and bleeding there is among the most lethal of limb wounds. The trained responder answers it with wound packing, pushing gauze to the bleeding point and packing it firmly back along the track until the cavity is filled. Pressure is then held over the packed wound with the flat of the hand while the patient is moved toward definitive care. This is a technique for the trained responder or the paramedic under a protocol, and it is not taught to the lay first aider. A foreign body embedded in a junctional wound is stabilized in place and packed around, and it is never removed in the field. The finding that packing has worked is gauze that stops filling with blood while pressure is held.

Splinting is the trained responder's work, and its one purpose is to hold the limb still so that movement does not tear what the bullet left intact. The limb is splinted in the position found, and a severely deformed fracture is not straightened to make it look normal. The movement that improves the shape can injure a vessel or a nerve and can release a clot that was holding back hemorrhage. A splint supports the joints above and below the wound and is applied over a dressing that is already controlling the bleeding. The neurovascular examination is performed before the splint is applied and repeated after it is secured, because a splint that is too tight can starve the limb beyond it. The finding that the splint must be adjusted is a limb that has become pale, cold, or numb, and the answer is to loosen and reapply rather than to wait.

The destination of a patient with a limb wound is chosen with the injuries in mind rather than the distance. It is the nearest facility that can assess the vessels, image the bone, and provide an operating room. A trauma center is preferred for a limb with an absent pulse, an expanding swelling, an open fracture, or a worsening examination, because the time to restore flow decides whether the limb survives. Transport is urgent and gentle at once, because speed that throws the patient around adds pain and can move a fracture whose ends lie near a vessel. Emergency medical services are activated early with a report naming the wound, the bleeding, what has been done, and the times. The finding that changes the destination is a patient whose condition is worsening, because a patient in shock needs the closest facility that can begin resuscitation.

Pain after a limb wound is severe and must be acknowledged rather than endured. The provider authorized to prescribe decides which substance is given, by which route, and how often, and those decisions belong to the local protocol and the prescriber rather than to this manual. The finding that makes the decision urgent is pain that is severe, worsening, or out of proportion to the wound, because that finding may also point to compartment syndrome. The scope of practice forbids the lay first aider from giving any medicine and forbids any responder from acting outside the local protocol. The decision about whether a nerve block is appropriate belongs to a physician or a trauma surgeon in definitive care.

Protection against tetanus is raised at the first contact with a limb wound, because the projectile carries cloth, soil, and metal into deep tissue. The question of whether the patient is protected is answered by the immunization history and the nature of the wound, and the decision belongs to the provider authorized to make it under the local protocol. Antibiotics are considered in the same way, as a decision made by a prescriber rather than as a field treatment. The finding that makes it urgent is a wound that is deep, heavily contaminated, or associated with an open fracture or dead muscle. Nothing about the substance, the amount, the route, the timing, or the interval may be taken from this chapter. The responder's part is to describe the wound accurately, to record what was done and when, and to keep the patient warm and moving toward the place where that decision can be made well.

Fluids and blood products are decisions about a patient and a local system, and they belong to the paramedic under a protocol and to definitive hospital care. The finding that makes them urgent is a patient whose pulse is climbing while the pressure is falling and whose skin is pale and cool. The choice of what is given into a vein, the amount, and the rate are set by the protocol and the prescriber, and none may be taken from these pages. Oxygen is a decision about a patient rather than a routine act, and whether it is given, how, and to what target belong to the local protocol. The lay first aider does not administer oxygen, and the responder's own task is to stop the bleeding, keep the patient warm and still, and shorten the time to the surgeon.

Hypothermia is a companion of limb trauma rather than a detail of comfort, and it begins when the patient bleeds, lies on cold ground, or is exposed. Blood loss reduces the volume that carries heat, and cold makes the clotting system work less well, so a cold patient bleeds more. The responder breaks that circle by covering the patient before the journey, insulating the patient from the ground, and keeping the vehicle warm. The limb itself is covered with the rest of the patient, because tissue short of blood tolerates cold poorly. A body temperature below 35 degrees Celsius is often named as the point at which cold becomes dangerous, and that figure must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the threshold differs with age and with the way the measurement is taken. The finding that warns the responder that cooling has begun is a patient who has stopped shivering while still cold.

Definitive hospital care begins with imaging, and the choice of study is made by the physician or the trauma surgeon. A plain radiograph shows the fracture and the position of any retained projectile, and it is the first study for most limb wounds. Ultrasound can look for blood around a vessel, and computed tomography, or CT, with dye in the circulation can show whether an artery is torn or blocked. Magnetic resonance imaging, or MRI, is used later and more selectively, chiefly when a nerve injury must be defined after the patient is stable. The finding that decides which study is needed is the neurovascular examination itself, so the record of that examination travels with the patient. None of these studies is a field procedure, and a responder who delays transport to obtain one has reversed the order of care.

Restoring the flow to a limb is surgical work, and it includes the repair of the artery and the vein, the release of the compartments, the cleaning away of dead muscle, and the stabilization of the bone. Some limbs are held open with a temporary tube that carries blood across the injured segment until the patient is stable, and that is a decision made in the operating room. A limb that cannot be saved is amputated in definitive care, and the decision rests on the injury, the time without flow, and the state of the patient. The handover is part of the treatment, and it must carry the mechanism of injury, the times of the injury and of every dressing and tourniquet, and the trend of the examinations. The responder states plainly what was not done and why, because an omission that is known is safer than one that is discovered. The finding that should prompt a written note at the scene is any intervention, any change in the patient, and the moment at which either occurred.

Certain limb sites deserve their own warning because their anatomy changes the danger. The thigh holds the largest bone in the body and the large vessels beside it, and bleeding there can dull the patient's level of consciousness while the wound looks small. A wound near the knee threatens the artery behind it, which is tethered and easily torn, so the pulse at the foot and the movement of the toes are examined. The shoulder and the armpit lie in the junctional zone discussed above, and a wound there may injure the vessels and the nerves of the whole arm. The forearm and the lower leg each contain a pair of bones and tight compartments, so a fracture there can raise the pressure inside a compartment. The finding that must be carried to the receiving team is the site of the wound with the examination of the limb beyond it.

A constructed illustration may help to fix the sequence, and it is offered as a construction and never as a real patient or a real incident. A person is struck in the outer thigh by a single projectile, and at first the wound is small and the pulse at the ankle is easy to feel. The responder controls the bleeding, covers the wound, splints the limb in the position found, keeps the person warm, and repeats the examination. On the second assessment the thigh is larger and firmer, and the person reports pain that the wound does not explain. That change, rather than the wound itself, must be reported at handover, because it suggests bleeding into the tissue or pressure building inside a compartment.

The mistakes that cost a limb or a life in this kind of wound are few and they repeat. The first is to straighten a deformed limb so that it looks better, and the second is to lift a dressing to see whether the bleeding has stopped. The third is to loosen a tourniquet in the field, which can release hemorrhage that no one can control again. The fourth is to wait for the pulse to disappear before taking compartment syndrome seriously, because by then the muscle may already be dying. The fifth is to treat the wound rather than the patient, which means missing shock and missing a second wound on the same limb. The patient is watched continuously until the handover, with the observations repeated in the same order, and the findings that should send the responder back to the beginning are a falling level of consciousness, a pulse that has become weak or rapid, skin that has become colder or paler, and bleeding that has restarted beneath a dressing.

To summarize, a gunshot wound to a limb is the most common and the most survivable of firearm injuries, and it threatens the limb first while still being capable of killing through hemorrhage. The scene is made safe before the patient is touched, personal protective equipment is worn, and protection against hypothermia begins in the first minute. The lethal possibility is hemorrhage from a major vessel of the limb, whose finding is blood that spurts with the heartbeat, a swelling that grows while the responder watches, or blood that soaks through dressing after dressing. The first aid for it is direct pressure, while the trained responder may pack a junctional wound and apply a tourniquet that is not loosened in the field. Arterial, venous, and nerve injuries often travel together, an open fracture is treated as contaminated, and the distal circulation, movement, and sensation are recorded and repeated because a first normal examination can be followed by a worsening one. Compartment syndrome is the delayed complication that follows a limb that was saved, it is suspected on pain and tension rather than on an absent pulse, and its release is a surgical procedure. Definitive care owns imaging, the repair of vessels and nerves, and the cleaning and stabilization of bone, the provider level is named for every intervention, and every time, pressure, temperature, and figure in this chapter is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.
