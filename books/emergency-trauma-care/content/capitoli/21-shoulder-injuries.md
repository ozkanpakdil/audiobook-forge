# Chapter 21 — Shoulder Injuries

No injury of the shoulder may be examined before the scene has been made safe, because a responder struck by traffic, burned, electrocuted, or attacked becomes a second casualty on a scene that already has too few hands. Traffic is held, power is isolated by a person authorized to do so, and a violent person is left to those trained to control violence before any hand is laid on the patient. Personal protective equipment, or PPE, is worn for every patient rather than only for the ones who look infectious, because blood and body fluids carry infection that enters through a break in the skin or a splash into the eye. The lethal possibility to be named first is not the deformed shoulder but the injury that arrived with it, since the force that dislocates a shoulder or breaks a collarbone can also tear the great vessels beneath it, collapse a lung, and fracture the neck. The findings that reveal neurovascular danger are an arm that is pale, cool, and pulseless or a hand that has gone numb or weak, while the hidden chest injury shows itself as breathing that has become fast, shallow, and painful. Warmth belongs to the treatment from this first minute, because a patient who is bleeding, exposed, or lying on cold ground loses heat quickly and cold makes bleeding, shock, and clotting worse. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country where you work.

Why shoulder injuries behave so differently from one another begins with the anatomy of the shoulder girdle, which is a small set of bones and joints rather than a single structure. The collarbone is the only bony strut connecting the arm to the trunk, and it lies directly under the skin, so it breaks easily and shows its breaks easily. The shoulder blade is a broad plate of bone buried under heavy muscle, so it is protected from ordinary blows and shattered by extraordinary ones. The ball and socket between the head of the upper arm bone and the shallow cup of the shoulder blade gives the arm the greatest range of motion in the body and pays for that freedom with the least stability, which is why it dislocates more often than any other joint. The nerves and vessels that supply the whole arm pass through a narrow corridor beneath the collarbone and close to the socket, so an injury that moves bone can also stretch, bruise, or tear what lies beside it.

The mechanism of injury is the first evidence at the shoulder, and it predicts damage before the examination confirms it. A fall onto the outstretched hand drives force up the arm into the collarbone and the socket, and it is the classic way in which a collarbone breaks or a shoulder dislocates. A fall onto the point of the shoulder disrupts the joint between collarbone and shoulder blade, while a blow to the back of the shoulder can push the head of the arm bone out of the socket toward the back and produce a dislocation that is quiet and easily missed. A heavy direct blow, a fall from a height, a vehicle collision, or a crush carries the force required to break the shoulder blade, and that mechanism should raise the expectation of chest, neck, and head injury rather than of the shoulder alone. A seizure, an electric shock, and a violent wrench of the arm are also mechanisms of dislocation, and they deserve to be asked about directly rather than assumed away.

The shoulder never comes first, however dramatic it looks, because a shoulder cannot kill a patient while an airway, a breathing chest, or a torn vessel can. The primary survey is completed in its fixed order of airway, breathing, circulation, disability, and exposure before the shoulder is examined in detail, and the arm is left in the position found while that survey is performed. Life comes before limb whenever two correct actions compete, so a patient with an open shoulder wound, a closed airway, and a fast weak pulse needs the airway secured and the hidden bleeding sought before any splint is considered. A patient who holds the shoulder protectively may be holding it still because the neck is injured, so the head is not allowed to roll, and any change in the level of consciousness, the breathing, or the pulse sends the responder back to the beginning. The lay first aider keeps the head still, supports the arm as found, keeps the patient warm, and calls emergency medical services early.

The neurovascular assessment is a short examination repeated before any handling of the shoulder and again after every handling, including after the sling has been tied, and it belongs to the trained responder, the paramedic, and definitive hospital care. The pulse is felt at the wrist on the thumb side and compared with the uninjured side, and the color and warmth of the hand are compared in the same way. Sensation is tested over the outer side of the shoulder, where the nerve that winds around the neck of the arm bone supplies the skin, and then over the thumb, the little finger, and the palm, because each area belongs to a different nerve. Movement is tested by asking the patient to lift the arm away from the side, bend the elbow, make a fist, and spread the fingers, and any movement the patient cannot perform is recorded rather than argued with. The finding that matters most is a difference between the two arms, since a limb that is pale, cool, pulseless, numb, or weak is a surgical emergency and must not be made worse by pulling on the arm.

A fracture of the collarbone is the most common injury of the shoulder girdle and one of the most forgiving, because the bone lies under the skin where it can be seen and felt without deep probing. The patient usually supports the injured arm with the other hand, keeps the shoulder still, and reports pain when the arm is moved or the bone is pressed, while swelling, tenderness over the bone, and an obvious step in the contour are the usual findings. Most of these fractures heal well with support of the arm and time, which is why the lay first aider may apply a simple sling for comfort while making no attempt to line the bone up. A small group is dangerous and is recognized by findings rather than by worry, namely bone pressing so hard that the skin is white or broken, a fracture near the inner end of the collarbone where the bone can press backward onto the great vessels, the windpipe, and the food pipe, and any fracture with numbness, weakness, or a lost pulse in the arm. A fracture with any of those findings belongs to definitive hospital care and demands immediate transport rather than a longer stay at the scene.

Support of the arm is the whole of field treatment for an uncomplicated fracture of the collarbone, and it is a lay first aid measure that requires nothing more than a triangular bandage, a strip of cloth, or a shirt used as a sling. The shoulder is never pulled, pushed, or rotated to make the bone look straight, because the bone ends lie beside vessels and nerves and because movement of a fragment can turn a closed fracture into an open one through the skin. A figure of eight bandage pulled tight around both shoulders is not the treatment of choice and can compress the armpit and the nerves within it, so where such a bandage is used at all it must be applied by a trained responder under a protocol and checked repeatedly. The fingers are examined after any binding is tied, because a bandage that crosses the wrist can interfere with the circulation even when it was meant only to hold the forearm. Pain relief is a decision made by the provider authorized to prescribe it under the local protocol, and the substance, the amount, and the way it is given are never taken from a manual such as this one.

A dislocation of the shoulder is the displacement of the head of the upper arm bone out of the socket of the shoulder blade, and the direction of that displacement decides both the appearance of the patient and the structures at risk. The most common direction is forward and downward, and the patient holds the arm slightly away from the body and rotated outward, refuses to bring the elbow to the chest, and cannot touch the opposite shoulder with the hand of the injured side. The outline of the shoulder is squared off rather than rounded, the head of the bone can sometimes be felt in front of the socket, and the muscles around the joint are in spasm. A dislocation toward the back is far less common and far more easily missed, because the arm is held close to the body and rotated inward, the front of the shoulder looks almost normal, and the patient cannot turn the palm outward. Because that appearance is subtle, a responder who does not test outward rotation will not find it, a radiograph in a single view can miss it, and the suspicion is stated at handover so that the imaging includes the view that reveals it. The finding that must always be sought is the neurovascular state of the arm, because a dislocated shoulder can injure the nerves and vessels before anyone touches it and can injure them further during any attempt to put it back.

The structures at risk in a shoulder dislocation are specific, and each announces itself through a finding that a trained responder can test without moving the joint. The nerve that winds around the neck of the arm bone supplies the skin over the outer shoulder and helps to lift the arm, so numbness in that patch or weakness when the arm is lifted away from the side points to it. The bundle of nerves that passes from the neck into the arm can be stretched by the displaced head, and the finding is numbness or weakness in the hand or the forearm rather than in the shoulder itself. The artery that runs through the armpit can be torn or blocked by the displaced head, and although this is uncommon it is catastrophic, with a hand that is pale, cold, and pulseless, and it is seen most often in older patients whose vessels are stiff. Because nerve and vessel injury may exist before the joint is put back, the examination is recorded before any handling and repeated afterwards, any worsening is reported in the same breath as the dislocation itself, and the lay first aider never attempts to put a shoulder back into its socket while the trained responder does so only where training, authorization, and a protocol allow.

Putting a dislocated shoulder back into its socket is a procedure with a defined owner, and in most circumstances that owner is definitive hospital care, where imaging can be performed before and after the reduction and where a clinician can decide whether the reduction is safe. The trained responder or paramedic may reduce a dislocation in the field only where the local protocol authorizes it, where the patient has no fracture and no neurovascular deficit, where monitoring is available, and where the attempt will not delay transport to a place that can manage failure. Pain relief and sedation for the procedure are decisions made by the provider authorized to prescribe them under the local protocol, and nothing in this chapter names a substance, an amount, a route, or a frequency, because a manual is not a prescription. The reduction is performed gently with the technique the provider has been trained and certified to use, it is abandoned at once if the pain becomes uncontrollable or the neurovascular examination worsens, and the joint that goes back into place is not proof of success until the pulse, the sensation, and the movement have been checked again.

A fracture of the shoulder blade is unusual in ordinary life and common in the patient who has been through enormous force, and its presence is a message about the rest of the body rather than about the shoulder. The shoulder blade is wrapped in heavy muscle and lies against the rib cage, so the energy required to break it is the energy of a vehicle collision, a fall from a height, a crush, or a blast. The findings are pain over the flat of the shoulder blade, swelling and bruising that may appear late and low on the back, a shoulder the patient holds rigidly still, and tenderness that can be localized to the bone rather than to the joint. Because the same force strikes the ribs, the lung, the spine, and the head, the responder who finds a fractured shoulder blade stops looking at the shoulder and begins a deliberate search that includes the breathing rate and the effort of breathing, the movement of the chest on each side, the tenderness of the ribs and the breastbone, the tenderness of the neck and the back, and the level of consciousness.

The chest injuries that accompany a fractured shoulder blade and a severe shoulder blow are the ones that kill, and they announce themselves through breathing rather than through the shoulder. Broken ribs are felt as tenderness or as a grinding sensation when the chest wall is pressed, and the patient guards the chest and breathes shallowly to avoid the pain. Air that leaks from a torn lung into the space around it collapses that lung, and the findings are increasing breathlessness, reduced breath sounds on the injured side, and a chest that moves less on that side than on the other. Blood that collects in the same space adds a loss of circulating volume, with the same breathlessness together with a pulse that is climbing and a patient becoming pale and restless, while bruising of the lung makes it stiff and shows itself as a patient who works harder for every breath. The responder does not bind the chest tightly to ease the pain of broken ribs, because a tightly bound chest prevents the deep breathing and coughing that keep the lung open, and that restriction can kill a patient whose ribs would have healed.

Because the force that injures the shoulder often passes through the neck, the cervical spine is treated as injured until it has been cleared by the appropriate examination and imaging. The findings that raise suspicion are pain or tenderness in the neck, an injury above the collarbone, an altered level of consciousness, an unreliable patient, and a mechanism of injury that included a fall from a height or a high-speed collision. The lay first aider supports the head in the position found and does not turn the head to look at the shoulder, while the trained responder applies manual stabilization and then the collar and the board or split stretcher that the local protocol requires. An unconscious patient is moved with the head, the neck, and the trunk kept in line, and any turn of the patient is a coordinated act performed by several trained hands rather than by one person rolling a patient alone, because a patient who walked away from a collision and then complains of neck pain is not cleared by having walked.

The joint between the collarbone and the shoulder blade is injured by a fall onto the point of the shoulder, and the patient reports pain at the top of the shoulder, tenderness directly over the joint, and a small step in the contour where the end of the collarbone has risen above the shoulder blade. That injury is painful and disabling but rarely threatens the limb, and its field treatment is support of the arm in a sling with the joint left as it lies and no attempt to push the collarbone down. The joint between the collarbone and the breastbone is injured far less often, usually by a heavy blow to the front of the chest or by a vehicle collision, and its danger depends on direction, because an inner end driven backward can press on the windpipe, the food pipe, and the great vessels at the top of the chest. The findings of such a backward displacement are difficulty in breathing or swallowing, a hoarse voice, swelling at the base of the neck, and a sense of pressure in the throat, and a suspected case is a hospital emergency requiring imaging and specialist care, so no one at the scene pushes on the inner end of a collarbone to test it.

Immobilization of the shoulder is performed for comfort, for protection of the injured structures, and for safe transport, and it follows a fixed order that can be carried out with simple materials. The first action is to leave the arm in the position in which it was found and to support it there with a hand or a knee so that it is not hanging by its own weight. The second action, only when the arm is supported, is to slide a triangular bandage, a wide strip of cloth, or a folded shirt beneath the forearm without lifting or rotating the shoulder. The third action is to bring the ends of the sling over the shoulder of the uninjured side and tie them so that the hand rests a little above the level of the elbow. The fourth action is to secure the arm against the body with a broad band passed around the chest and the arm, tied loosely enough that the patient can still take a full deep breath, since a band that restricts breathing is more dangerous than the injury it supports. The fifth action is to recheck the fingers and the wrist for color, warmth, pulse, and movement and to loosen anything that has changed them for the worse. A simple sling belongs to the lay first aider, while a splint that includes the chest and the arm together belongs to the trained responder or paramedic working within a protocol.

Certain positions and movements must never be forced on an injured shoulder, and the reason is always the same, that the bone ends and the displaced head lie beside structures that cannot be repaired at the roadside. The arm is never pulled lengthwise to compare its length with the other side, because traction can stretch a nerve that is already bruised and can pull a clot from a torn vessel, and it is never lifted to the horizontal or above the head to inspect the armpit, because that movement increases pain and can convert a stable fracture into a displaced one. The elbow is never forced toward the chest and the hand is never forced onto the opposite shoulder to prove that a joint is dislocated, because a test that hurts is a test that can harm. A sling is never tied so tightly that the wrist is compressed, and the patient is never lifted by the injured arm or moved with the arm used as a handle. Where a position cannot be achieved without force the correct response is to leave the limb as found and to move the patient toward definitive care.

Transport of the patient with a shoulder injury is arranged around the whole patient rather than around the arm, and the position is chosen to protect breathing and circulation first. A patient who is fully alert, breathing comfortably, and not in shock may travel sitting up with the arm supported in the sling, because the upright position eases the work of breathing and reduces the pull on the injured shoulder. A patient who is drowsy, pale, or short of breath travels lying flat or in whatever position best supports breathing, with the arm supported on a pillow beside the body rather than folded tightly across the chest, and a patient with a suspected spinal injury travels in the position and on the device that the local protocol requires. Cold is prevented by covering the patient before departure, insulating from the floor of the vehicle, and removing wet clothing, because a patient who is bleeding, exposed, or immobilized loses heat and cold worsens shock and clotting.

The findings that should stop a responder from being reassured are the ones that describe a limb losing its blood supply or a patient losing the ability to compensate. Pain that is severe, increasing, and out of proportion to the injury, a limb that feels tense and tight when it is touched, pain that becomes worse when the fingers are stretched, and numbness or weakness that was not there before all point to rising pressure inside the arm, which is compartment syndrome and belongs to immediate surgical assessment. A hand that has become pale or blue, cool to the touch, and pulseless points to the artery rather than to the compartment and is equally urgent, while a pulse that has become faster and thinner, breathing that has become rapid, skin that has become cold and clammy, and a level of consciousness that is dulling point to bleeding or to shock somewhere in the body beyond the shoulder. A record is written for every intervention and every change, including the time the sling was applied and the neurovascular findings before and after, and it is spoken aloud at handover together with the mechanism of injury and the treatments given, because the receiving team continues a story that the field began.

Definitive hospital care owns everything that restores the shoulder rather than merely supporting it, and the first of those acts is imaging. A radiograph confirms the fracture or the dislocation, shows the direction of the dislocation, and reveals fractures around the socket that change the plan, while computed tomography, after which CT is allowed, is used where the plain images leave a question about the socket or the shoulder blade. Reduction of the dislocation, internal fixation of a fracture, repair of a torn vessel, and release of a nerve under pressure are operations performed by a surgeon in an operating room with anesthesia, and none of them is a field procedure, so the lay first aider supports and transports while the trained responder works only within the protocol that authorizes a reduction. The decision about pain relief belongs to the provider authorized to prescribe it under the local protocol, and the decision about protection against tetanus and about antibiotics where they are indicated follows the same rule, with the finding that makes the decision urgent being a wound that is open, deep, or contaminated. The patient who needs surgery is moved to a trauma center where imaging, an operating room, and an intensive care unit are available, and after the acute treatment rehabilitation restores movement and strength over weeks and months, so the patient who leaves with a stiff shoulder is told that the stiffness is part of the injury and not a sign of failure.

A child with a broken collarbone may have a fracture that bends rather than breaks, and the bone may heal with remarkable speed, but the same finding in a child who cannot yet walk or whose mechanism of injury does not fit the injury should raise the question of how the injury happened at all, and that question belongs to the clinicians and to the authorities who protect children rather than to a manual. An older patient may fracture the upper end of the arm bone rather than dislocate the joint, so the arm may look deformed while the socket is intact, and the examination is recorded without forcing the joint while the imaging decides. A patient whose shoulder pain appears after a fall but whose abdomen is tender and swelling may be describing blood under the diaphragm, which irritates the nerve that refers pain to the shoulder tip, so the shoulder is examined and the abdomen is examined too. A constructed illustration, never a real patient or incident, is the young rider who lands on the point of the shoulder and is found with a painful step and no other complaint, in whom the responder supports the arm, checks the hand, checks the neck, and transports for imaging rather than pushing the collarbone flat.

To summarize, the shoulder is a girdle of small bones and shallow joints in which the position of the bone matters far less than the state of the nerves and vessels beside it. Scene safety comes first, the primary survey comes before the shoulder, and the lethal possibilities to be named are the chest injury, the spinal injury, and the neurovascular injury that accompanied the blow rather than the deformity itself. A fracture of the collarbone is common and usually forgiving, and it is treated by support of the arm in a sling with the limb left as found, while a fracture with skin pressed white or broken, a backward push of the inner end, or a lost pulse belongs to definitive hospital care at once. A dislocation is recognized by the position in which the patient holds the arm and by the inability to move it, and the nerves and vessels are examined before and after every handling because they may already be injured. A fracture of the shoulder blade is a marker of enormous force, so its discovery turns the examination toward the chest, the neck, and the head, and the same force may have injured the spine. Immobilization is performed gently in the order that supports rather than corrects, no position is ever forced, no lay first aider puts a joint back into place, and no drug, amount, route, or frequency is taken from these pages. Every number, time, pressure, temperature, and depth mentioned here is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, or the law of the country where you work.
