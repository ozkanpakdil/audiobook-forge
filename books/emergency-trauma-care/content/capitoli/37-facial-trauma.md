# Chapter 37 — Facial Trauma

The face is the first thing a rescuer sees and the last thing that should hold the attention, because the injuries that kill a patient with facial trauma hide behind the appearance of the face. A face can look destroyed and the patient can still be saved, while a face with one small wound can be closing an airway that nobody is watching. Two dangers run through this chapter, which are the airway that fills with blood or swells shut, and the eye that is blinded by a hand that meant to help. A third is bleeding from the rich vessels of the face, dangerous less for its volume at the roadside than for where the blood goes. No clinical act begins before the scene is made safe, so traffic, fire, electricity, water, animals, violence, structural collapse, and the patient's own body fluids are dealt with first. Personal protective equipment, or PPE, with gloves and eye protection, is worn before any hand is laid on a wound, because blood and saliva carry infection into a break in the skin or a splash into the eye. Every act is named with the provider level that owns it, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country where you work.

The mechanism of injury is the first clinical evidence, and in the face it predicts damage that no eye can see from outside. A punch, a fall onto the face, a steering wheel, an animal kick, a bat, a windshield, a blast wave, and a bullet break the face in different patterns, each with its own likely companions. The force that breaks the face has traveled through the skull and the neck, so such a patient is treated as having a head injury and a cervical spine injury until those are excluded. The finding that keeps the spine protected is any injury above the shoulders, any fall, and any patient who cannot be relied on to hold the neck still. The mouth and the nose open directly into the throat, so a fracture of the facial bones is a fracture of the walls of the passage for air. A blow to the face in a vehicle collision should also raise the question of the chest, the abdomen, and the limbs, because the face explains nothing about the rest of the body. A mechanism that seems trivial, such as a single punch, can still produce swelling that will close the airway over the following hours.

The reason a broken face bleeds into the airway is anatomical, and it explains every rule that follows. The facial bones are not solid blocks but thin shells around air-filled spaces, lined inside by a membrane with an exceptionally rich blood supply. When a fracture tears that lining, blood runs along the fracture lines into the sinuses, the nose, the mouth, and the loose tissue of the throat, none of which can be seen from outside. The tongue lies in a floor of soft tissue that can swell within minutes, so a fracture can both bleed and swell into the exact passage the patient needs for air. A patient lying flat cannot spit, swallow, or cough, and the tongue falls backward when consciousness is dulled, so blood and saliva collect at the back of the throat. The finding that reveals this is not the wound but the sound of the breathing and the change in the voice, which is why position matters as much as any dressing. Blood is an irritant to the stomach, so a patient who has swallowed a large amount may vomit, and vomit in a damaged face is another obstruction.

The primary survey is performed in its fixed order, and in facial trauma the airway decides everything else. A patient speaking in a clear voice has an airway that is open at that moment, and that observation is worth more than any field instrument. The findings of a failing airway are the ones a listener can hear, which are gurgling, snoring, a hoarse or muffled voice, a voice changed since the injury, noisy breathing on inspiration, and drooling because the patient cannot swallow. Swelling growing under the tongue, in the floor of the mouth, or in the soft tissue of the neck turns a stable patient into an emergency within minutes and can continue to enlarge after the examination. Loose teeth, bone fragments, pieces of denture, chewing gum, and vomit can be cleared, and a conscious patient who can be asked to spit is the easiest to protect. The trained responder may use suction and airway adjuncts under a protocol, while the lay first aider keeps the head in a position that lets blood run out. A blind finger sweep is never performed in an unconscious patient, because a finger can push a loose tooth or a fragment deeper into the throat, and only an object that is visible is removed.

The positioning of a patient with facial trauma is a treatment in itself, and it belongs to whoever has hands on the patient. A conscious patient who is breathing and not in shock sits up and leans forward, so that gravity carries blood and saliva out of the mouth instead of back into the throat. A patient who must lie down, because of a spinal injury, a low level of consciousness, or the requirements of the journey, is turned onto one side with the mouth as the lowest part of the drainage path. Where a cervical spine injury is suspected, the turn is a log roll with the head and the neck held in a straight line by a second person, so that the airway and the spine are protected together. No pillow is placed under the head of a patient who is bleeding into the mouth, because a pillow lets the blood pool at the back of the throat. A patient who vomits is turned at once, and the mouth is cleared of what can be seen. The finding that the position has failed is noisier breathing, a worsened voice, or repeated swallowing and coughing, and the position is maintained for the whole journey.

Bleeding from the face is dramatic and rarely fatal by volume alone, and the responder treats it as an airway problem rather than a race against blood loss. A cut lip, a torn nose, and the raw surface of a fractured cheek can produce a frightening flow, but the vessels are small enough that firm pressure around the wound usually controls them. Pressure is applied around the wound and along the line of bleeding, never in a way that pushes the tongue back, blocks the nose, or compresses the neck. A facial dressing is held rather than bound, because a bandage around the head can tighten the airway it is meant to protect. The lay first aider presses with a clean cloth, adds another layer instead of lifting the first, and keeps the patient upright and leaning forward. An object embedded in the face is stabilized with padding around it and never removed in the field, because it may be plugging a vessel or lying against an intact structure, and removal can release hemorrhage that no field measure can control. The trained responder may pack an appropriate wound under a protocol, and definitive hospital care repairs what pressure cannot reach.

The nose deserves its own examination, because it is the most commonly broken bone in the face and two of its injuries are easy to miss. A nasal fracture causes swelling, bruising under the eyes, bleeding, and a change of shape, and the responder does not straighten it, because manipulation worsens the bleeding and cannot be judged without imaging. A traumatic nosebleed is controlled by pinching the soft part of the nose and leaning the patient forward, so that blood runs out instead of down the throat, and a patient who is swallowing blood is watched for vomiting. A dark, tense bulge on the wall between the nostrils is a collection of blood under the lining and not a bruise, and a nose blocked on one side by a smooth, painless swelling needs review, because it can destroy the cartilage if it is left. Clear fluid from the nose, or a salty taste in the mouth, warns of a fracture at the base of the skull, and it changes the plan at once, because nothing is then packed into the nose and nothing is passed through it. Bruising around both eyes without a direct blow, bruising behind the ear, and clear fluid from the ear belong to the same family of findings, and a loose clean dressing over a leaking ear is all that is needed, because packing it introduces infection. The trained responder avoids any tube through the nose and protects the cervical spine, while definitive care makes the diagnosis with computed tomography.

Fractures of the lower jaw are dangerous because the lower jaw carries the tongue, and the tongue carries the airway. A break in the body of the jaw causes pain on opening, a change in the way the teeth meet, bleeding, and swelling, and a break on both sides can let the front of the jaw fall backward with the tongue. The findings are a patient who cannot close the mouth properly, cannot open it at all, cannot bite, a step or a gap in the line of the teeth, drooling, and numbness of the lower lip or the chin. The responder does not test the jaw by forcing it, does not put a displaced fragment back into place, and does not ask the patient to demonstrate movement, because the examination itself can move the tongue into the airway. Definitive care stabilizes and repairs the bone, and the field never does. In an unconscious patient whose tongue has fallen backward, the trained responder may lift the front of the jaw forward under a protocol, and in the conscious patient the drainage position is the correct act. A patient with a broken jaw who is bleeding and drooling is never left lying on the back, even for a short examination.

Dental injuries are easy to dismiss as cosmetic, and they are among the few injuries in this book where a small, fast act in the field decides whether a structure is saved. A tooth knocked completely out of its socket can survive, but only if it is handled correctly and replaced within a short time, and the handling belongs to the lay first aider. The tooth is picked up by the crown, the white part that shows in the mouth, and never by the root, where the living surface that must be preserved sits. If it is dirty, it is rinsed briefly in clean water, saline, or the patient's own saliva, and it is not scrubbed, dried, or wrapped in a dry cloth. The best transport keeps it moist and beyond the patient's reach, which for a fully conscious patient is between the cheek and the gum, and otherwise is a clean container of milk, saline, or the patient's own saliva. A tooth allowed to dry has lost most of its chance, and the time since it left the mouth decides whether it can be saved, with figures such as 30 minutes being an illustration to confirm against the current guideline and the local protocol. A baby tooth is not reimplanted, because that can damage the permanent tooth beneath it, while a broken piece of a permanent tooth is kept and sent, because it may be used in the repair.

A tooth that is loose but still in place is left exactly where it is, and the patient is warned not to bite on it, test it with the tongue, or swallow it, because a free tooth becomes a foreign body that can be inhaled. The responder does not pull a loose tooth, tie it to a neighbor, or pack its socket, because the socket is a wound that definitive care will assess. Replacement of a tooth is performed by the clinician who owns that decision, and where the local protocol authorizes a trained responder to do it, that act is performed under the protocol and not from a manual. The trained responder records which tooth was lost, where it was placed, and the time it left the mouth, because the receiving team needs that history. Definitive hospital care owns the reimplantation, the splinting of a loose tooth, the repair of a fractured crown, and the treatment of the socket. Dental injury is also a marker of force, because a patient with several broken teeth has usually taken a blow that injured the jaw and the neck as well.

The eye is the structure in this chapter that can be destroyed by the most natural human gesture, which is a hand laid on it to see whether it is all right. The eyeball is a globe of fluid held at a pressure slightly above the air around it, and when its wall has been cut or burst, any pressure from outside pushes the contents out through the wound. What has been pushed out cannot be replaced, and the eye is lost. A patient who has been struck in the eye, or who has a wound near the eye, or who has blood in the front of the eye, must never be pressed, massaged, or have the lids forced open, and never has a pressure patch applied. The findings of a possibly burst globe are a pupil that is not round, a tear in the colored part of the eye, blood filling the front chamber, tissue at the edge of the eye, or an eye sitting lower or more prominent than its neighbor. Vision is tested before the lids swell and close and is tested again later, because failing vision is an emergency. The lay first aider shields the eye with something that rests on the bones of the face and does nothing else.

A fragment of metal, glass, a thorn, or a splinter on or in the eye is stabilized and never removed in the field, because it may be the only thing sealing a wound in the globe. The shield is a rigid object such as the base of a clean paper cup, taped to the brow and the cheek so that nothing rests on the eye, and the trained responder covers both eyes where the protocol allows, because that reduces the movement of the injured one. A chemical injury is the exception that demands immediate action, because when a substance has entered the eye the responder irrigates at once and keeps irrigating with clean water or saline, holding the lids open gently and removing a contact lens if it comes away easily. The lay first aider may irrigate a chemical eye injury, and it is one of the few acts in this chapter that begins before anyone asks whether it is allowed, because the harm continues every second the chemical remains. The irrigation is continued on the way to definitive care rather than stopped once the patient is in the vehicle. No attempt is made to neutralize one chemical with another, because the heat and injury of that reaction are worse than the exposure itself.

Fractures of the bones around the eye may leave the globe untouched and still threaten sight and appearance, and their findings are quiet. A patient who sees double in a particular direction, cannot look up or down on one side, whose cheek is numb, or whose eye has sunk compared with the other may have a broken orbit with muscle or fat caught in the fracture. The responder asks about double vision, numbness of the cheek and upper lip, and pain on looking, and records the answers, because those findings may fade once swelling takes over the face. Definitive hospital care uses CT to define the fracture and decides whether the eye must be repositioned and the bone repaired, and none of that belongs to the field. The finding that must be passed on at handover is any change in vision, because a patient who could see at the roadside and cannot see on arrival has had something happen in between.

The examination of the face belongs to the secondary survey, and it is performed in a fixed order so that nothing is missed while the eye is held by the worst part. The scalp and forehead are examined first, then the eyes, the nose, the cheekbones, the upper jaw and mouth, the lower jaw and chin, and finally the neck, with the front and the back of the patient both seen. The responder looks for swelling that is growing, bruising that has appeared since the last look, a shape that does not match the other side, and blood or clear fluid coming from an opening. The skin is felt gently with the flat of the fingers, comparing the two sides, without pressing into a wound and without springing the facial bones, because that adds pain and may worsen bleeding. The inside of the mouth is inspected where it is safe, because a torn lip, a broken tooth, a swelling in the floor of the mouth, and a piece of bone pointing into the tongue are found there and nowhere else. Function is tested by asking whether the patient can bite, open, swallow, see, feel the upper lip, and speak clearly, because each answer describes a bone, a muscle, or a nerve. The lay first aider keeps the patient in the drainage position, keeps the patient warm, and reports what has changed, and the examination is repeated, because the face at the scene is not the face that arrives at the hospital.

Protection of the injured face is mostly covering without compressing, and keeping the patient warm while the examination is done. A facial wound is covered with a clean, dry dressing held in place rather than bound around the head, an eye is shielded without being touched, and an embedded object is stabilized with padding on both sides. The face is the most exposed part of the body, and a patient sitting upright to protect an airway loses heat from the head and chest, so the trunk is covered, wet clothing is replaced, the patient is insulated from the ground, and the vehicle is kept warm. Cold makes blood clot poorly, deepens shock, and makes a dangerous heart rhythm more likely, and a body temperature below 35 degrees Celsius is the figure often used for the point at which those effects become serious, an illustration to confirm against the current guideline and the local protocol. The wound is not scrubbed, painted, or closed in the field, because cleaning and closure are decisions made in hospital where the depth of the injury can be seen. Tetanus protection is raised at the first contact, because a facial wound from soil, an animal bite, or a dirty fragment carries a risk, while the decision belongs to the clinician and the local protocol, and the substance, the amount, the route, and the timing belong to the prescriber and never to this book. Pain is decided in the same way by the provider authorized to prescribe, and the responder's own contribution to comfort is position, warmth, calm speech, and the shortest possible time to care.

Burns of the face deserve a separate warning, because the airway can close after the patient has looked well. A fire, a flash, a steam injury, or a chemical splash can burn the lining of the mouth and throat as surely as the skin, and the swelling of that lining grows over the hours after the injury rather than at the moment of it. The findings are soot around the mouth and the nose, singed hairs inside the nose, a burned or blistered mouth, a hoarse or changed voice, a cough, and a history of being in a closed space with smoke. Each of these is a reason to move the patient to a place where the airway can be controlled rather than to watch and wait. The lay first aider cools a facial burn with clean running water where the protocol of the service advises it, covers the face with a clean dry sheet, and applies no ointment or grease that will have to be peeled away. Swelling in the first hours can make a patient with a facial burn difficult to intubate later, which is why the decision about the airway is made early by the clinician who owns it. The finding that changes the plan is any noise in the breathing, and it changes the plan immediately.

The most important sentence in this chapter is that the appearance of the face must never delay the transfer. A swollen, bloodied, distorted face draws every eye on the scene, and the temptation is to clean it, straighten it, and cover it neatly before leaving, and every one of those acts spends time that the airway and the eye do not have. A patient who needs an operating room for a facial fracture, a torn eye, or a wound that will not stop bleeding needs it as surely as a patient with a bleeding limb, and the journey is the treatment. The destination is chosen before the patient is moved, and it is the nearest facility that can provide the needed care, which for a severe facial injury with an eye injury or an airway concern may be a trauma center with imaging, an operating room, and an intensive care unit. The responder reports what was done and what changed, including the times, the position used, the tooth preserved, the eye shielded, and the object left in place, because the receiving team continues a story the field began. Definitive hospital care owns the repair of the bones, the repair of the eye, the removal of retained fragments, the closure of the wounds, and the decision about reconstruction, and none of those is a field procedure. Every time, distance, temperature, and figure in this chapter is an illustration to confirm against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such numbers differ between services, countries, and editions.

Reassessment continues from the first contact to the handover, because the face changes while the responder watches it. The same observations are repeated in the same order, asking whether the patient is speaking clearly, whether the breathing has become noisy, whether the swelling is larger, whether the eye can still see, and whether bleeding has restarted under the dressing. A patient who was talking and has become quiet, one who has begun to drool, one whose voice has become hoarse, and one who swallows repeatedly have all said that the airway is narrowing, and the response is to return to the beginning of the primary survey. The record is part of the treatment, so the time a dressing was placed, the position the patient was carried in, and the tooth that was sent are written where the receiving team will see them. The finding that ends the observation of a facial injury is the arrival of the team that can see inside the face.

Knowing what must not be done to a face is as much a part of competence as knowing what must be done. The lay first aider positions the patient to drain, presses around a wound without pressing on an eye, preserves a tooth, shields an eye, irrigates a chemical exposure, covers and keeps the patient warm, and sends for help. The trained responder and the paramedic add suction, airway adjuncts, packing under a protocol, cervical spine protection during a log roll, and the monitoring that recognizes a failing airway. Definitive hospital care owns the CT, the repair of the facial bones, the repair of the globe and the eyelids, the removal of retained fragments and embedded objects, the reimplantation of a tooth, the release of a swollen airway, and the reconstruction that follows. Definitive care also owns the decisions about pain relief, tetanus protection, and antibiotics, with the substance, the amount, the route, and the timing set by the prescriber and the local protocol. The line between those levels is a matter of training, authorization, and protocol, and a responder who crosses it with good intentions can convert a survivable injury into a fatal or a blinding one.

To summarize, facial trauma kills through the airway and blinds through the eye, and it almost never kills through the appearance that frightens the scene. A broken face bleeds and swells into the throat because the facial bones are hollow shells lined with a membrane of enormous blood supply, so the patient who cannot sit up and lean forward is placed on one side, with the head low enough that blood drains out of the mouth rather than pooling at the back of it, and the neck is protected during the turn where a spinal injury is possible. The airway is the first step of the primary survey, its failure is heard in gurgling, snoring, a hoarse or changed voice, drooling, and growing swelling, and the lay first aider removes only what is visible, never performs a blind finger sweep, and never binds the face in a way that presses on the airway. The nose, the jaw, the teeth, and the eyes are each examined in their turn, the tooth that has been knocked out is picked up by the crown and kept moist and sent with the patient, and the eye that may have burst is shielded and never pressed, while a chemical exposure is irrigated at once and continuously. Embedded objects are stabilized in place and never removed in the field, the procedures of repair and reimplantation belong to hospital care, and the provider level is named for every act. Every time and temperature in this chapter is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, and the appearance of the face must never delay the transfer.
