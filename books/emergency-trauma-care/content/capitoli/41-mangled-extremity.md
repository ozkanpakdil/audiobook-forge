# Chapter 41 — Mangled Extremity

A mangled extremity is a limb in which severe soft-tissue destruction, vascular injury, exposed bone, nerve damage, and the threat of compartment syndrome occur together. Machinery, a wheel or heavy weight passing over a limb, a blast, or a large animal can produce it, and the energy far exceeds that of a bone broken cleanly. The limb no longer has the shape of a limb, with skin stripped away, muscle torn, bone exposed, and bleeding that may be enormous or hidden inside the swelling. The lethal possibility spoken first is hemorrhage that cannot be controlled, because such a limb can hold enough blood to kill the patient while the wound looks merely terrible. The scene is made safe before any hand is laid on the patient, and the choice between rebuilding and removing the limb belongs to a surgical team in a hospital. Nothing here replaces training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

These injuries share one feature, which is that they deliver energy to a broad area rather than a single point. A limb caught in a rotating shaft is pulled in and crushed along its length, a limb run over by a vehicle is compressed between a tire and the road, and an explosion drives fragments and pressure through the tissue. Because the force is spread, the vessels, nerves, muscles, and skin may each be injured at different distances from the visible opening. Muscle that looks pink at one moment may be dead and unable to contract, and tissue that appears attached may have lost its supply. The bone may be broken into many pieces with an end open to the air, the vessels may be divided, torn, in spasm, or blocked by clot, and the swelling that follows raises the pressure inside closed compartments.

A mangled extremity threatens the patient's life first and the limb only afterwards, and that order must survive every other consideration. The torn vessels of a thigh, a groin, or an armpit can empty the circulation in minutes, and the same amount can vanish into the soft tissues while the skin stays almost dry. A patient who is pale, restless, thirsty, and confused with a fast thin pulse is losing blood whether or not the wound pours. Crushed muscle also releases its contents into the circulation, and that load can injure the kidneys, disturb the heart rhythm, and deepen shock after bleeding is controlled. A fully detached limb is a traumatic amputation and belongs to the previous chapter, while a mangled limb is still attached, sometimes by a bridge of skin or one vessel. The honest statement of this manual is that a mangled limb kills when the bleeding is not controlled, and the priority remains the life.

The scene is more dangerous than most, because the machine that caused the injury may still be running and the structure that fell may still be moving. Machinery is shut down and locked out by a person authorized to do it, and until that isolation is confirmed nobody reaches into it, because a jammed roller or a raised load can move without warning. A limb trapped under a vehicle or a heavy object is not freed by a bystander, since the weight can shift onto the rescuer, the release can restart bleeding, and the returning blood can poison the patient. Live cables, spilled fuel, fire, a loose animal, and a violent person are each answered by withdrawal and by waiting for those trained to control them. A responder who cannot make the scene safe does not enter it, and gloves and eye protection, or whatever barrier is available, go on before the first contact.

Once the scene is held, the first act at the limb is the control of hemorrhage, before any attempt to examine, clean, or straighten it. The lay first aider presses firmly with a clean cloth over the bleeding point, adds layers when blood shows through, and never lifts the dressing to look. The trained responder or paramedic packs an appropriate deep wound in the groin, the armpit, or the base of the neck where a tourniquet cannot reach, and uses a hemostatic dressing where the service carries one. Those junctional wounds kill fastest, because the vessel is large, the bone is deep, and no band can circle the shoulder or the hip to compress it. A limb that keeps bleeding through pressure and packing needs a tourniquet, and that decision belongs to the trained responder or paramedic under a protocol. The principle that orders these acts is life before limb, which chooses survival over the limb even when the limb is the more dramatic problem.

A tourniquet is placed between the wound and the heart, as close to the wound as anatomy allows, and never over a joint when a length of limb above the joint is free. It is tightened until the bleeding stops rather than until a set number of turns has been made, and the wound judges the tightening. Once it has controlled the bleeding it is not loosened in the field, because the clot behind it may not hold and the patient may bleed again with nobody able to restore control. The time of application is written on the patient where the receiving team cannot miss it, and it is spoken aloud at the handover. The time matters because everything below the tourniquet is without blood, and the surgeon who may release it must know how long that has been true. An interval such as 2 hours is often quoted as the point at which the danger to the limb becomes serious, and that figure must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

A mangled limb often remains attached by a narrow bridge of skin, tendon, or one vessel, and that bridge is the most valuable tissue in the wound. Nobody in the field completes an amputation or pulls on a partially detached part, because the bridge may carry the only blood still reaching the tissue and tearing it turns a contained injury into free hemorrhage. The part is supported in the position found, never left to hang by its own weight. A piece of tissue that has already come away is not discarded, because a surgeon may use skin, bone, nerve, or vessel from it to rebuild the limb, so it is wrapped, kept cool, and sent with the patient. That part is never placed directly on ice, because freezing destroys the tissue the surgeon is trying to save, and every fragment travels to the same hospital as the patient.

An object embedded in a mangled limb is stabilized in place and never removed in the field, whether it is metal, glass, a spike, a blade, or part of the machine. Removal may release hemorrhage that cannot be controlled at the scene, may tear a vessel, a nerve, or a tendon the object is holding together, and may open a wound that was sealed. The correct act is to build padding around the object, to hold it steady, and to travel with it still in place. The object is not shortened, cut, or twisted, because those movements travel into the tissue, and the surgeon decides when and how it is removed. The same rule applies to an exposed bone end, which is covered and protected rather than pushed back under the skin, since pushing it back carries contamination deep into the limb.

Protecting the wound means covering it with a clean dressing that keeps dirt out, does not press on exposed bone or torn muscle, and can be removed by the surgeon without a second injury. The wound of a mangled limb is not cleaned, not scrubbed, and not irrigated in the field, because that work belongs to the operating room, where dead tissue is cut away under anesthesia. A dressing that circles the limb is applied with care rather than force, since a bandage wound tightly around a swelling limb can become a tourniquet by accident. The finding that a bandage has become too tight is skin beyond it that has turned pale or blue, a pulse that can no longer be felt, and pain that climbs rather than settles. The response is to release the bandage and reapply it with less tension under the authority of the local protocol, and a limb that swells inside a dressing needs the surgeon sooner rather than a tighter band.

A severely deformed limb is splinted in the position found and never straightened in the field to make it look normal. Straightening can release a clot that sealed a torn vessel, stretch or tear an intact artery, divide a nerve that was only bruised, and force blood into a compartment whose pressure was already rising. The appearance of the limb is not the goal of first aid, and a limb left crooked but perfused is worth more than one that looks straight and has no pulse. The limb is supported where it lies, with a splint built to hold the position rather than to change it. An authorized provider may attempt alignment in a few situations, but only with the distal observations recorded before and after, and never as a task for the lay first aider.

Immobilization is built around the position found and around the rule that support is given above and below the injury rather than across it. A rigid support is placed along the limb on the side away from the wound where possible, padded so that no hard edge presses on skin, and secured with straps that are snug but do not circle the wound. Where the limb cannot rest on a board without pressing the wound, the padding is built up first and the limb rests on it, so the support touches only intact tissue. A fractured thigh is supported with the pelvis and the joint below it, and a mangled forearm is supported with the elbow and the wrist, because a splint ending at the fracture lets the broken ends move. The straps go above and below the wound rather than over it, and the wound is examined after each strap is tightened, because even a well meant splint can become a constricting band.

The state of a mangled limb is recorded through four observations, which are the pulses beyond the injury, the refill of the capillaries, the sensation on the skin, and the movement the patient can produce. They are taken on both limbs before and after every dressing, every splint, and every move, because a finding without a comparison is almost useless. The pulses are felt at the thumb side and the little finger side of the wrist and at the top of the foot and behind the inner ankle, and they are compared with the other side. The refill is tested by pressing the nail bed until it blanches, the sensation by a light touch with the eyes closed, and the movement by asking the patient to bend the wrist, spread the fingers, lift the toes, and push the foot down. A finding without a time cannot be compared with the next, so the observations are written with the hour, and where some cannot be obtained the responder records what was found and states what could not be tested.

What matters is the difference between two sets of observations, and a limb whose pulse was strong and is now faint, whose color was pink and is now pale, or whose pain climbs despite a splint is losing its supply. The signs of a failing limb appear as pain, then pallor, then coolness, then numbness, then weakness, and the loss of the pulse comes late, so waiting for it is a way to lose the limb. The response is not to lift the dressing, loosen a tourniquet, or tighten a bandage, but to repeat the general survey, to warn the receiving team, and to shorten the journey. A period such as 6 hours of warm ischemia is often quoted as the point past which a limb is unlikely to be saved, and that interval is an illustration whose exact value belongs to the current guideline and the local protocol.

Compartment syndrome is not a separate possibility in a mangled limb but one of its expected consequences, because the same force that tears tissue adds blood and edema to closed spaces that cannot stretch. The fascia that wraps the muscles holds each compartment at a fixed volume, and as the contents increase the pressure rises until the small vessels are compressed and the muscle starves. The early findings are pain out of proportion to the injury, pain when the muscles are gently stretched, a compartment that is tense and tender, and a change in sensation over the territory of the nerve. The pulse below the limb may still be strong and the limb may still look well, which is why a pulse that is present does not exclude the condition. The late findings of pallor, pulselessness, paralysis, and a limb that has stopped hurting describe tissue already dead, and the responder who waits for them has waited too long. The patient who cannot describe pain, because of a head injury, alcohol, sedation, or youth, is the one in whom the condition is most often missed.

The immediate measures in the field follow an order, and the first is to remove everything that circles the injured limb, including rings, watches, bangles, and tight clothing. A ring that will not come off a swollen finger is never forced over the knuckle, because the finger may later need to be cut free, and the receiving team is told. Every bandage, splint strap, and cast is then examined for tightness, and whatever the local protocol permits to be released is released, because a dressing that has become a tourniquet is a limb being lost. The limb is kept at the level of the heart rather than raised above it, since elevation lowers the arterial pressure that feeds the compartment, and this is one of the few places in trauma where the instinct to elevate is wrong. The limb is not massaged, not packed in ice, and not bound tightly, and the patient is kept warm, kept still, and moved toward the surgeon.

Every patient with a mangled limb is watched for shock from the first minute, because the wound may have taken blood the eye has not seen and crushed muscle adds its own insult to the circulation. The early patient compensates with a fast pulse, cool hands, and a pressure that still looks acceptable, and that stability is the most dangerous moment, because the loss is already large while the numbers reassure. The patient who can no longer compensate becomes restless, then confused, then drowsy, with rapid shallow breathing and a falling pressure, and by then the remaining time is short. Fluid replacement, the choice of what is given, and the decision to give blood products belong to the paramedic under a protocol and to definitive hospital care, and nothing in this chapter names a substance, an amount, a route, or a frequency. The responder's own contribution is to stop the bleeding, to keep the patient warm and still, and to shorten the time to the operating room.

Cold is a complication of trauma rather than a detail of comfort, and it travels with a mangled limb because the patient is exposed, often on the ground, and losing heat through the wound. A cold patient clots poorly, tolerates blood loss badly, and is more likely to develop a dangerous rhythm of the heart, so cold deepens the shock the wound began. The responder removes wet clothing and replaces it with dry covering without exposing the wound, insulates the patient from the ground, covers the patient before the journey, and keeps the vehicle warm. The injured limb is protected from cold and from direct heat, because a limb that has lost its supply must not be warmed aggressively at the scene. A body temperature below 35 degrees Celsius is often named as the line at which the effects of cold become serious, and that figure is an illustration that each service sets in its own guideline and protocol.

Pain from a mangled limb is severe, and treating it is part of treating the patient rather than a courtesy at the end. The finding that makes relief urgent is pain not controlled by splinting and positioning, together with a pulse and a breathing rate that climb because of it. The decision belongs to the provider authorized to prescribe under the local protocol, and the substance, the amount, the route, and the timing are decisions of that protocol and that prescriber. The examination is recorded before relief is given, including the tension of the limb, the sensation, and the movement, so the effect of the treatment can be judged. Relief must never be used to make a patient quiet enough to be transported without an examination, because a quiet patient is not a safe one and a limb that has stopped hurting may have stopped living.

The wound of a mangled limb is contaminated by definition, since it has met soil, machinery, clothing, and the patient's own skin, and protection against tetanus is part of its care. The question is raised at the first contact, and the answer depends on the vaccination history and the nature of the wound, which is why it belongs to the provider authorized under the local protocol. Antibiotics follow the same rule, and the finding that makes the decision urgent is a wound that is deep, devitalized, heavily contaminated, or joined to a fracture opened to the air. The substance and the amount belong to the prescriber and the local guideline, and nothing in this chapter names either. The field responder helps that decision by describing the wound, reporting the mechanism and the time, and giving the patient's account of previous protection where the patient can, and the record of those times allows the receiving team to decide properly.

The decision between salvaging a mangled limb and amputating it is one of the hardest in surgery, and it belongs to a surgical team in a hospital with the imaging, instruments, blood bank, and intensive care it requires. Scoring systems weigh the injury to skin, bone, vessels, and nerves against the patient's age and condition, and they aid judgment at the hospital rather than serve as a roadside rule. No first responder and no paramedic completes an amputation, pronounces a limb unsalvageable, or tells the patient the limb will be lost. The decision depends on the whole patient, and a patient already in shock, cold, or injured elsewhere may be better served by rapid amputation than by hours of reconstruction, while a stable patient with a single injury may be offered a staged attempt to rebuild. The team weighs the chance the limb will function, the risk of infection and kidney injury, the patient's wishes, and the resources available, and the field provider's task is not to decide but to keep the decision possible.

What the prehospital provider must preserve is easy to list and demanding to carry out. The first thing preserved is the patient's life, because the limb can wait for minutes that a brain and a heart cannot. The second is the tissue, which means no fragment is discarded, no bone end is pushed back, no tissue is pulled away, and no wound is explored in the field. The third is the circulation, which means the tourniquet is not loosened once it has controlled bleeding and its time is written where the receiving team will see it. The fourth is the position of the limb, splinted as found, and the fifth is the record, which carries the mechanism, the injury time, the times of every intervention, and the four observations with the hour each set was taken. The sixth is warmth, because a cold patient tolerates the long operation that salvage requires far worse than a warm one.

Transport is urgent and gentle at the same time, and the destination is chosen before the patient is moved. The receiving facility is the nearest one that can examine and repair vessels, open a compartment, and provide an operating room and a surgeon, which usually means a trauma center. Where the nearest hospital cannot provide that care, the local system decides whether the patient is stabilized there and transferred or carried further at once. A call ahead names the limb, the mechanism, the time of the injury, the tourniquet and its time, what has been done, and what is changing, so the team and the operating room can be ready. The journey avoids unnecessary speed that throws the patient around, because a patient who arrives with more injuries has gained nothing from the hurry. During the journey the limb, the bleeding, the level of consciousness, the breathing, and the warmth of the patient are watched continuously.

The handover is a treatment in itself, because the receiving team continues a story the field began, and a story with a gap forces them to start again. The report follows a fixed order, beginning with the mechanism and the time, then the patient's condition and level of consciousness, then the injuries found, then the interventions with their times, then the observations with the hour they were made, and finally the allergies, medicines, and protection against tetanus where known. The record is written as carefully as the treatment is given, because a tourniquet time never written is a time that will be lost, and a finding never recorded cannot be compared with the next. The limb and every separated fragment are handed over in person rather than left with the paperwork, and the responder says plainly what could not be tested and why. The receiving team is also told what the responder learned about the machine, animal, weapon, or weight involved, because that changes what the surgeon expects inside the wound.

Some patients deserve a word of their own because their mangled limb must be treated within a larger emergency. Children lose heat faster and become shocked on a smaller volume of blood, while an older patient with narrowed arteries or a medicine that thins the blood may lose a limb a younger patient would keep. A patient with a mangled limb and a head, chest, or abdominal injury has more than one threat, and the primary survey in the order of airway, breathing, circulation, disability, and exposure is repeated whenever the condition changes. A patient with two mangled limbs, or with a mangled limb and a pelvic injury, is the patient in whom life before limb decides the whole sequence, because the number of hands is fixed and the bleeding points are several.

Definitive hospital care owns everything in this injury that repairs rather than contains, and the boundary is part of what every provider must know. On arrival the limb is examined again, because the findings may have changed, and the patient is assessed for shock and cold before the limb is addressed in detail. Plain radiographs, ultrasound, and computed tomography study the bone and the vessels, and the surgeon may explore the wound in the operating room to judge which tissue is alive. Repair of a torn vessel, joining or grafting of a nerve, transfer of a tendon, an external frame to hold the bone, the cutting away of dead tissue, the opening of a compartment, and the decision to amputate are all operations and none is a field procedure. Salvage is usually staged, since the first operation controls bleeding and stabilizes the bone, while later operations clean the wound and reconstruct what can be reconstructed. Amputation, when chosen, is a treatment and not a failure, and it is chosen when the limb cannot be made to function or when the attempt would endanger the patient's life.

From the first contact to the handover the patient is watched continuously, because the condition of a mangled limb and its owner can change within a single journey. Watching means repeating the same observations in the same order and comparing them with what was found before. The findings that send the responder back to the beginning are a falling level of consciousness, breathing that has become faster, slower, or noisier, a pulse that has become weak or rapid, skin that has become colder or paler, and a limb that has changed color or feeling. The principle of life before limb is not a slogan but the rule that decides the order of the acts when two correct actions compete for the same pair of hands. The honest statement of this chapter is that a mangled limb is a life-threatening injury whenever the bleeding is not controlled, and that the priority remains the life. The responder who holds to that order, protects the limb without making it the only goal, and moves the patient to a surgeon without delay has done everything the field can do.

To summarize, a mangled extremity is a limb with severe soft-tissue destruction, vascular injury, exposed bone, nerve injury, and the threat of compartment syndrome at once, and it threatens the life before it threatens the limb. The scene is made safe first, and hemorrhage is controlled by pressure, by packing where the provider is trained, and by a tourniquet for a limb that will not stop. The tourniquet is not loosened in the field once it has controlled the bleeding and its time is written on the patient, a deformed limb is splinted as found and never straightened, an embedded object is stabilized, and a partially detached part is never completed. The wound is covered without tight binding, exposed bone is protected rather than pushed back, the limb is immobilized above and below the injury, and the four observations are recorded with their times. The choice between salvage and amputation belongs to a surgical team, and the field provider keeps that choice possible by preserving the life, the tissue, the circulation, the position of the limb, the warmth, and the record. Transport is urgent and gentle to a facility that can repair vessels and open compartments, and every number here is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. Nothing here replaces training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work, and the priority remains the life.
