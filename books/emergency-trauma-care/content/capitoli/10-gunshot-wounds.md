# Chapter 10 — Gunshot Wounds

A firearm wound is never only a hole, and the responder who treats it as one will miss the injury that kills. Before the patient is touched, the scene must be made safe, because a weapon that has already fired may fire again and the person who fired it may still be present, so the possibility of a second shot is the first lethal possibility to name, together with traffic, broken glass, and the patient's own blood as a hazard to the rescuer. The finding that tells the responder to hold back, to call the emergency medical services, and to wait rather than to move in is a scene that has not been secured, a crowd that is hostile, or a weapon still within reach of anyone. Personal protective equipment, or PPE, with gloves at the least, is put on before there is any contact with blood or tissue. The lethal possibility inside the patient is hemorrhage that cannot be controlled, and the finding that reveals it is blood that soaks through dressing after dressing, a swelling that grows while the responder watches, or a level of consciousness that is becoming dull while the pulse turns fast and thin. Because a patient who is bleeding and exposed loses heat quickly, protection against hypothermia begins in the first minute, with the patient covered and the surface beneath insulated, since cold worsens bleeding and shock rather than causing only discomfort. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country where you work.

What a projectile does inside a body is decided less by the projectile than by the energy it carries and by the tissue it crosses, and that single idea explains most of what a responder sees. A firearm turns the chemical energy of a propellant into the motion of a small mass, and the wound is made when that moving mass gives its energy to the tissue in its path. The energy of a moving projectile rises with its mass and rises far more steeply with its speed, so a light projectile that travels fast can transfer more energy than a heavy one that travels slowly, and the shape of the projectile decides how much of that energy is given up and how quickly. A stable, pointed projectile passes through with less transfer, while one that tumbles, flattens, or breaks apart gives more of its energy to the tissue and produces a wider and more irregular injury. Distance changes the speed at which the projectile arrives, because it slows as it travels through air, so a wound from a distant shot and a wound from a shot at contact can differ greatly even when the same weapon was used. The responder therefore treats the visible wound as the opening of a question about energy and about the structures beneath, and reconstructs the mechanism of injury from the scene, from the weapon if it is known, and from the pattern of the wounds, rather than from the wound alone.

A slow projectile produces a track rather than a cavity, and its injury is close to what a blade of the same width would cause. Because the projectile is moving slowly, the tissue ahead of it is pushed aside and cut rather than stretched, and the damage is largely confined to the narrow path the projectile has traveled. Such a wound can still be lethal when the track crosses the heart, a great vessel, or a major nerve, and its quiet external appearance is exactly what makes it dangerous. A projectile that has passed through an intermediate object, or that has traveled a long distance, may arrive with little energy left and produce a wound that a responder is tempted to dismiss. The teaching is that speed decides the pattern and not the seriousness, because a slow projectile that crosses a vital structure kills as surely as a fast one. The responder reads the track as an unknown corridor through the body and asks what lies along it.

A fast projectile stretches the tissue around its path and creates a temporary cavity that opens and closes in a fraction of a second. That cavity is far wider than the projectile itself, and the structures at its wall are pulled, torn, and bruised even though the projectile never touched them. The finding that reveals this remote injury is damage that does not line up with the visible wound, such as a fracture of a bone the projectile did not strike or a nerve that has stopped working far from the track. Tissue that is elastic, such as lung, muscle, and skin, tolerates the stretch reasonably well, while tissue that is dense and inelastic, such as liver, spleen, kidney, brain, and fat, tears under the same force. A cavity described as spanning 10 to 15 centimeters is offered only to make the mechanism imaginable, and any such measurement is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the size depends on the projectile, the tissue, and the distance fired. The responder who understands the temporary cavity stops expecting the wound to explain the patient and starts examining the patient to explain the wound.

Entry wounds are the most reliable part of the picture and the easiest to misread. A typical entry wound is a small, circular or oval defect whose edge is bruised and slightly abraded, and it may carry a ring of dirt or a rim of dried tissue where the projectile rubbed the skin as it entered. When the shot was fired at close range, the skin around the wound may show soot, unburned powder, or a fine speckling that cannot be wiped away, and a contact shot may leave a star-shaped tear or a mark of the muzzle pressed against the skin. These findings speak to the distance of the shot, and the distance is worth recording for the team that receives the patient, although it never replaces the assessment of the patient. The finding that matters more than the appearance is the location of the entry, because the location names the structures that may lie in the path. A small, neat entry wound in the upper abdomen or the chest can sit above an injury that will kill within minutes.

Exit wounds are more variable than entry wounds and are not a measure of the damage done. An exit wound is often larger, more ragged, and more irregular than the entry, with edges that are turned outward and without soot or powder, because the projectile has already given up part of its energy and often carries fragments of tissue with it. Some projectiles do not leave the body at all, and the absence of an exit wound is a normal finding rather than a sign that the injury is minor. When a projectile has crossed the body and left, the responder should not assume that the track was straight, because a projectile can be deflected by bone and can arrive somewhere far from the line between the two wounds. The trap is to treat the number of holes as a count of injuries, when two wounds may belong to one track and several tracks may be hidden entirely. The responder marks and describes each wound, records its position in words, and leaves the interpretation of the track to the team with imaging and an operating theater available to it.

Counting holes is the most common error at the scene, because an even number of wounds suggests one track and an odd number suggests that a projectile is still inside, and neither inference can be trusted. A projectile can leave by a route that seems impossible, can be swallowed or lost in clothing, or can remain in the body after entering and leaving the tissues in a way that produces wounds which look unrelated. More important, a single small entry wound can hide a wound channel through several organs, so the number of holes says very little about the number of injuries. The responder therefore searches the whole patient rather than the visible wound, including the scalp under the hair, the armpits, the groin, the perineum, the mouth, and the back, and rolls the patient to look at what has been lying against the ground. Clothing is cut away rather than pulled over the wound, because pulling can move a projectile, dislodge a clot, or hide a wound that was not yet seen. The search continues until the responder is satisfied that every wound has been found, and any wound discovered later is reported to the receiving team as a new finding.

The principle that organizes this chapter is that the wound is the visible part of an unknown internal injury, and the structures in the path matter more than the number of wounds. A wound of a few millimeters on the surface can sit above a torn artery, a perforated intestine, or a divided spinal cord, and no examination at the scene can exclude any of them. What the responder can do is reason from the location, from the direction the projectile seems to have traveled, and from the patient's behavior, and then act on the worst plausible injury rather than on the most comfortable one. A wound that crosses the midline of the trunk, a wound above the collarbone, and a wound in the groin or the armpit all deserve special concern, because these regions contain vessels and organs that cannot be compressed from outside. The finding that changes the plan is never the size of the wound but a change in the patient, such as a falling level of consciousness, a rising pulse, or a breathing pattern that is becoming labored. The responder who keeps this principle in view will treat the patient rather than the photograph of the injury.

Bone struck by a projectile becomes a source of secondary projectiles, and the fragments travel outward from the fracture with their own energy. A long bone that is shattered in this way sends pieces through the surrounding muscle, and each fragment can cut a vessel or a nerve that the projectile itself had passed by. The finding that suggests this mechanism is a limb that is deformed, that is swelling rapidly, or that has lost its pulse or its movement after a wound near a major bone. A joint crossed by the track is a special concern, because the fragments and the projectile can carry contamination deep into a space that is difficult to clean, and the injury then threatens the limb long after the bleeding has stopped. The trained responder splints the limb in the position found, without attempting to straighten it, because movement of the fragments can turn a contained injury into a free hemorrhage or can divide a vessel that was only bruised. Definitive hospital care is where the bone is examined by radiograph, where the joint is assessed, and where the decision is made about cleaning and repair.

Neurovascular injury is the complication that decides whether a limb survives and whether a patient keeps the use of a hand or a foot. The findings that must be sought below every wound of a limb are the pulse, the color and warmth of the skin, the ability to move the part, and the ability to feel light touch, and each is compared with the same part on the other side. An expanding swelling, blood that continues to spurt despite pressure, a pulse that cannot be felt, a part that is pale or cool, and a loss of feeling or movement are all findings that demand immediate movement toward definitive care. A pulse that is present does not exclude a serious arterial injury, because a torn artery can spasm or can be partly blocked while still transmitting a weak pulse. The trained responder repeats the examination after every intervention, because a pulse that disappears after splinting or after a dressing is applied reports a new problem rather than a stable one. The responder records what was found and when, so that the receiving team can tell whether the deficit is old or new.

The region of the body that the track crosses determines which lethal possibility is named first. A wound of the chest may have crossed the lung, the heart, or a great vessel, and the findings that reveal these are a breathing pattern that is becoming labored, breath sounds that are absent on one side, distended neck veins, a falling blood pressure, or a wound that sucks air. A wound of the abdomen or the pelvis may have crossed the liver, the spleen, the kidney, the intestine, or the large vessels, and the abdomen may look almost normal while the patient is losing blood inside it. A wound of the neck may have crossed the airway, the esophagus, or the vessels that carry blood to the brain, and a wound of the head may have crossed the brain even when consciousness is still intact. A wound of a limb may have crossed a major vessel, a nerve, or a bone, and a wound of the groin, the armpit, or the buttock is a junctional wound that sits where a tourniquet cannot reach. Naming the region and the structures at risk is the first act of reasoning, and it is done before any dressing is placed.

Hemorrhage control follows the ladder of pressure, and each step belongs to a named provider level. The lay first aider presses firmly on the wound with a clean dressing and does not lift the dressing to look beneath it, adding further layers if blood shows through, because each look breaks the forming clot. When bleeding continues from a wound of a limb despite firm pressure, a trained responder or paramedic may apply a tourniquet under a protocol that authorizes it, placing it above the wound and tightening it until the bleeding stops. Once a tourniquet has controlled the bleeding it is not loosened in the field, and the time it was applied is written on the patient where the receiving team will see it, because the duration of application changes what the hospital team must do first. A trained responder may pack a deep wound that pressure cannot control, and may use a hemostatic dressing where the local protocol provides one, while a lay first aider is not asked to pack a wound that requires instruments to reach the bleeding point. The finding that tells the responder to move to the next step is bleeding that continues despite the step just taken, and the finding that tells the responder the step is working is a dressing that stays dry and a patient who is becoming calmer and warmer. Definitive hospital care is where the vessel is found, repaired, or bypassed, and no field measure replaces that.

An embedded object, and a projectile that can be seen or felt, are left exactly where they are and are stabilized in place. The reason is not caution but mechanics, because the object may be pressing on a torn vessel and removal can release a hemorrhage that cannot be controlled at the scene, and because a projectile lodged near a nerve or a vessel can cause further damage as it is pulled. The responder builds padding around the object so that it cannot move, secures the padding without pressing the object deeper, and transports the patient with the object in place. The lay first aider never pulls a projectile from a wound and never probes the wound with a finger or an instrument to find out how deep it goes. First responders do not extract projectiles, because removal is a surgical decision made when it is clinically indicated, and the section of this manual that deals with retained projectiles says the same. The finding that changes this rule is an object in the airway or one that is obstructing the breathing, and even then the maneuver belongs to a trained responder or paramedic working to a protocol, never to a lay first aider.

Breathing and the airway come before the wound once hemorrhage that is immediately lethal has been controlled, and the sequence is the same primary survey that governs every injury in this manual. The responder follows the ABCDE order, which stands for airway, breathing, circulation, disability, and exposure, and each letter is completed before the next is given full attention. The airway is opened and kept open, the chest is examined for a wound that sucks air or for breath sounds that are absent on one side, and the circulation is judged from the pulse, the skin, and the level of consciousness. Exposure means that the whole patient is uncovered long enough to find every wound, and it is done while protecting the patient from cold with blankets and a warm surface. The Glasgow coma scale is recorded at first contact and repeated, because the trend in the level of consciousness is more useful than a single figure, and any value taken from this manual must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. The finding that moves the responder onward is a patient who is breathing, has a pulse, and has been examined from head to foot, and the finding that stops the sequence is a patient who needs cardiopulmonary resuscitation, or CPR, at once.

Cold is a complication of trauma and not a detail of comfort, and a patient who is bleeding, exposed, or waiting for transport loses heat in every part of the body. The responder covers the patient with whatever is available, insulates the surface beneath from the ground, removes wet clothing in a way that does not expose the patient for longer than necessary, and keeps the vehicle or the room warm. A core temperature below 35 degrees Celsius is a teaching illustration of the point at which cold becomes dangerous, and the exact figure at which a service acts must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because cold impairs clotting, worsens shock, and changes the heart's rhythm. A patient who is shivering is using energy that the body needs for other purposes, and a patient who has stopped shivering while remaining cold is a greater concern rather than a lesser one. The trained responder moves the patient with attention to the neck and the back when spinal injury is possible, using a coordinated roll rather than pulling on the arms or the head. Definitive hospital care includes active warming, and the receiving team is told how long the patient has been cold and how the patient was covered.

Pain relief is a decision rather than a routine, and it belongs to the provider whose authorization covers it. A lay first aider offers comfort, calm speech, a warm cover, and careful handling, and does not give any substance for pain. A trained responder or paramedic may give a medicine for pain only under a protocol that names the substance, the amount, and the route, and none of those details belongs in this manual, because a listener must never be able to take a prescription from these pages. The finding that makes pain relief urgent is pain that is severe, that is worsening, or that is preventing the patient from being moved or examined, and the decision is then made by the clinician responsible for the patient under the local protocol. The same reasoning applies to antibiotics, to protection against tetanus, to fluids, and to oxygen, each of which is a clinical decision whose urgency is set by the patient's condition and whose details belong to the prescriber and the local protocol. The responder records what was given and when, so that the receiving team can continue the plan, and the responder never gives a substance that the protocol does not authorize.

Definitive hospital care is where the unknown becomes visible, and the pathway starts with the questions the assessment has already framed. A computed tomography, or CT, examination answers questions about the track and about structures that the examination could not reach, a plain radiograph answers questions about bones and about retained projectiles, and ultrasound answers questions about fluid in the abdomen or around the heart. Surgical exploration of a wound, repair of a vessel, repair of a tendon or a nerve, removal of a projectile, debridement of deep tissue, and fasciotomy for compartment syndrome all belong to the operating theater and never to the field, and a listener who is tempted to perform them at the scene should hear this plainly. A trauma surgeon and an anesthetist lead this phase, with imaging, blood products, and an intensive care unit, or ICU, available for the patient who needs them. The receiving team needs the time of the injury, the time any tourniquet was applied, the wounds that were found and those that were searched for, the treatment given, and the trend in the patient's condition. The most valuable single message at handover is that the patient has changed in a particular way, because a change is what turns a stable patient into one who needs the operating theater now.

A constructed illustration may make the reasoning concrete without describing any real patient or incident. Imagine a person wounded by a single shot in the upper thigh, with a small entry wound, no exit wound, and a thigh that is swelling while the pulse at the ankle becomes difficult to feel. The wound itself looks modest, and a responder who counted holes would count one wound and move on, yet the structures in the path include the large artery and the vein of the thigh and the bone beneath them. The correct acts are to control the bleeding with firm pressure, to move to a tourniquet only if a trained provider is present and the protocol allows it, to splint the limb in the position found, to keep the patient warm, and to travel toward a trauma center without delay. The illustration is constructed for teaching, and the numbers, distances, and times that would be attached to it in a real case must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. The point of the example is that the visible wound and the actual injury are two different things, and the responder who treats the second rather than the first gives the patient the only real chance.

Children and pregnant patients deserve a specific word, because the same wound behaves differently in each. A child has a smaller body, so a track that would cross one structure in an adult may cross several, and a child's blood volume is small, so a loss that looks moderate can be a large fraction of the circulation. A pregnant patient may have the womb wounded, and the findings that matter include abdominal pain, vaginal bleeding, a uterus that is tense or tender, and any change in the baby's movement, all of which demand immediate transport to a center with obstetric and surgical capability. In both patients the responder protects against hypothermia with the same care as in any other patient, and the responder keeps the patient flat and still when spinal injury is possible. The provider level does not change with the age of the patient, because a lay first aider still controls what can be controlled with hands and dressings, and definitive hospital care still owns the surgery. The decisions about medicine, about fluids, and about oxygen remain with the clinician and the local protocol, and the details of those decisions are not taught here.

To summarize, a firearm wound is the visible part of an unknown internal injury, and the responder reasons from the energy of the projectile and from the structures in its path rather than from the number of holes. A slow projectile cuts a track and a fast one stretches a temporary cavity that injures tissue it never touched, bone struck by a projectile becomes a source of secondary projectiles, and a pulse that is present never excludes a serious vascular injury. Scene safety and the possibility of a second shot come before the patient, hemorrhage control follows the ladder of pressure with each step owned by a named provider level, and an embedded object or a projectile is stabilized in place and never removed in the field. The whole patient is searched for wounds that have not yet been found, hypothermia is prevented from the first minute, and the patient is moved toward definitive hospital care where the imaging, the surgical exploration, and the repair belong. Pain relief, antibiotics, protection against tetanus, fluids, and oxygen are clinical decisions whose substance and amount belong to the prescriber and the local protocol, and no such detail can be taken from this chapter. Every figure in this chapter, whether a speed, a distance, a cavity size, a temperature, or a time, is an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such values differ between services, countries, and editions.
