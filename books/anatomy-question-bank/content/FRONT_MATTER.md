# Anatomy — A Narrated Question and Answer Bank

This is a bank of eleven thousand six hundred anatomy questions, written to be answered out loud. It is not a textbook read aloud. Every item follows the same three steps: the question, spoken with its options inside the sentence when it has options; then the words "The answer is" and the correct answer; then two to four sentences saying why that answer is right and, where it helps, why the tempting wrong answer is wrong. The answer always follows its question immediately, so nothing has to be held in mind and no answer key has to be waited for.

It is made for revision by ear: walking, driving, or between patients. Nothing can be re-read, no option can be scanned, and no diagram can be consulted, so the bank contains no figures and no image references. The one hundred and sixteen chapters follow twenty categories in order, beginning with anatomical terminology and the body cavities, then the skeleton, the joints and the muscles, then the heart and the vessels, the lymphatic system, and the nervous system, then the respiratory, digestive, urinary, reproductive and endocrine systems and the special senses. Regional and surface anatomy follow, then cross-sectional and imaging anatomy, embryology, anatomical relationships, and the clinical anatomy that connects a structure to the examination that finds it. The last category integrates everything: structure to function, structure to injury, structure to symptoms, and structure to the clinical examination.

The questions are of nine kinds. Three thousand four hundred and eighty are multiple choice, two thousand three hundred and twenty ask for a structure by name, one thousand seven hundred and forty describe what a radiograph or a section would show, one thousand one hundred and sixty turn on the relations between structures, one thousand one hundred and sixty are clinical anatomy, five hundred and eighty are true or false, five hundred and eighty are matching, three hundred and forty-eight are short answer, and two hundred and thirty-two test three-dimensional reasoning. They range from first-year recall to expert questions whose wrong answers are all defensible until the final step.

The image-based questions of the source blueprint have been rewritten as descriptions, because a voice cannot show a radiograph. Each one describes what the film, the section or the specimen would show and asks which structure or which interpretation fits. A note on the count: the blueprint this bank was built from is titled as a ten-thousand-question bank, but its category table asks for eleven thousand six hundred questions and its subsections were followed as written. Where one category's subsections added up to more than its own heading allowed, they were scaled back proportionally to that heading, so the total stands exactly as the category table gives it.

Every item is anatomically correct, including the wrong options, which are wrong for the stated reason rather than merely unlikely. Where a structure has a common variant, the variant is named as a variant and not taught as the rule. Clinical items are constructed illustrations, never real patients, and they are described as illustrations. The bank gives no drug, no dose, no route and no treatment: where a clinical correlation touches management, it names the structure involved and leaves the management to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

This is educational material. It does not replace an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher, and it is no substitute for examining a real body or a real patient. Used as a companion to proper teaching, it is a way to keep anatomy alive in the ear, one question at a time.
