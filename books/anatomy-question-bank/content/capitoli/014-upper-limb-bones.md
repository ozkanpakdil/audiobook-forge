# Chapter 14 — Upper Limb Bones

The bones of the upper limb carry the hand wherever the mind sends it, and this chapter works through the four groups that make that possible: the humerus of the arm, and the radius, the ulna and the carpal bones of the forearm and the wrist. It holds one hundred questions, and each one gives its answer at once and then explains why, so nothing has to be carried in mind. The items begin at the shoulder and move outward to the elbow and the wrist, testing the parts, the attachments and the relations of each bone in turn. The landmarks that can be felt through the skin are given particular attention, because a listener will meet them again at the bedside. This chapter is educational material and supports, rather than replaces, an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 1301. Which bone of the upper limb is the single long bone of the arm, running from the shoulder joint above to the elbow below? The answer is the humerus. Its head fits the shallow socket of the scapula, and its widened lower end meets the radius and the ulna. Every muscle that moves the shoulder or the elbow pulls on this one bone.

Question 1302. Which rounded part at the upper end of the humerus fits into the glenoid cavity of the scapula: the capitulum, the greater tubercle, the head, or the trochlea? The answer is the head. It is a smooth hemisphere directed upward and medially, and it forms one half of the shoulder joint. The capitulum and the trochlea belong to the lower end of the bone.

Question 1303. Which roughened elevation on the outer surface of the middle of the humeral shaft gives attachment to the deltoid muscle? The answer is the deltoid tuberosity. It lies about halfway down the shaft on the lateral side. A muscle that raises the arm therefore pulls on the middle of the bone rather than on either end.

Question 1304. At the upper end of the humerus, which of the two tubercles lies more laterally, and which three muscles of the shoulder attach to it? The answer is the greater tubercle, which receives the supraspinatus, the infraspinatus and the teres minor. The lesser tubercle lies in front of it and more medially and receives the subscapularis. The groove between the two is the intertubercular sulcus.

Question 1305. A described radiograph of the shoulder shows two rounded eminences at the upper end of the humerus, separated by a deep groove that runs down the front of the shaft. Which eminences are shown, and what runs between them? The answer is the greater and the lesser tubercles, with the tendon of the long head of the biceps in the intertubercular sulcus.

Question 1306. A constructed illustration: a person falls onto the point of the shoulder, and afterward the shoulder looks flattened, the arm cannot be lifted away from the side, and the skin over the outer upper arm is numb. Which structure is injured? The answer is the axillary nerve, which winds around the surgical neck of the humerus and supplies the deltoid.

Question 1307. The intertubercular sulcus of the humerus transmits the tendon of which muscle: the long head of the biceps brachii, the short head of the biceps brachii, the coracobrachialis, or the subscapularis? The answer is the long head of the biceps brachii. That tendon leaves the shoulder joint, runs down the groove, and is held there by a fibrous band. The short head lies medial to it.

Question 1308. True or false: the head of the humerus is larger than the glenoid cavity into which it fits. The answer is true. The head is about a third of a sphere, while the socket of the scapula is shallow and small. That mismatch is what gives the shoulder its wide range of movement and its tendency to dislocate.

Question 1309. Which set of three muscles attaches to the greater tubercle of the humerus: the supraspinatus, the infraspinatus and the teres minor; the subscapularis, the teres major and the latissimus dorsi; the deltoid, the coracobrachialis and the pectoralis major; or the biceps, the brachialis and the triceps? The answer is the supraspinatus, the infraspinatus and the teres minor. Those three with the subscapularis form the rotator cuff.

Question 1310. A described radiograph of the middle of the arm shows a groove running down the back of the humeral shaft, and the bone is broken across it. Which nerve and artery lie in that groove, and what does the nerve supply? The answer is the radial nerve and the profunda brachii artery. The nerve supplies the triceps and the extensor muscles of the forearm.

Question 1311. A constructed illustration: after a fracture in the middle of the arm, the wrist hangs limp, the fingers cannot be straightened, and the back of the hand between the thumb and the index finger is numb. Which nerve is injured? The answer is the radial nerve, pressed against the bone in the radial groove.

Question 1312. Which smooth, rounded articular surface at the lower end of the humerus lies on the lateral side and receives the head of the radius? The answer is the capitulum. It is a rounded knob that meets the disc-shaped head of the radius. The pulley-shaped trochlea lies medial to it and articulates with the ulna.

Question 1313. Which prominent bony process can be felt at the inner side of the elbow, and which nerve passes behind it? The answer is the medial epicondyle, with the ulnar nerve running behind it. The nerve is superficial there, so a knock at the elbow gives the tingling of the funny bone.

Question 1314. Which hollow on the back of the lower humerus receives the olecranon when the elbow is straightened: the coronoid fossa, the radial fossa, the olecranon fossa, or the intercondylar groove? The answer is the olecranon fossa. It is a deep depression above the trochlea on the posterior surface. The coronoid and radial fossae lie on the front of the bone instead.

Question 1315. Which of these pairs belongs together: the capitulum with the ulna, the trochlea with the radius, the capitulum with the head of the radius, or the trochlea with the head of the radius? The answer is the capitulum with the head of the radius. The rounded capitulum meets the disc-shaped radial head, while the trochlea meets the trochlear notch of the ulna.

Question 1316. A constructed illustration: a child falls on the outstretched hand, and the described radiograph shows a break just above the condyles of the humerus with the lower fragment displaced backward. Which structures lying in front of the elbow are most at risk? The answer is the brachial artery and the median nerve, which pass in front of the joint. The deformity can also press on them.

Question 1317. A described radiograph of a straight elbow shows a beak of bone lying inside a hollow at the back of the lower humerus. Which process and which hollow are shown? The answer is the olecranon of the ulna, sitting in the olecranon fossa of the humerus. When the elbow bends, the process swings out of the fossa and the hollow is empty.

Question 1318. Which pulley-shaped articular surface at the lower end of the humerus articulates with the trochlear notch of the ulna? The answer is the trochlea. It is shaped like a spool with a groove in the middle, and it lets the elbow bend and straighten like a hinge. It lies medial to the capitulum.

Question 1319. Which of these groups belongs together: the greater tubercle with the subscapularis, the lesser tubercle with the subscapularis, the deltoid tuberosity with the triceps, or the radial groove with the ulnar nerve? The answer is the lesser tubercle with the subscapularis. The other rotator cuff muscles attach to the greater tubercle, and the radial groove carries the radial nerve.

Question 1320. Which muscles take origin from the front of the medial epicondyle of the humerus, and which from the lateral epicondyle? The answer is the flexor muscles of the forearm from the medial epicondyle and the extensor muscles from the lateral epicondyle. The two epicondyles therefore give a simple rule at the bedside: the inner one bends the wrist and the fingers, the outer one straightens them.

Question 1321. Which statement about the anatomical neck of the humerus is correct: the capsule of the shoulder is attached along it, it is the commonest site of fracture, it lies below the two tubercles, or it closes the medullary cavity? The answer is that the capsule is attached along it. The surgical neck below the tubercles is the usual fracture site.

Question 1322. True or false: the deltoid is the only muscle that attaches to the deltoid tuberosity of the humerus. The answer is true. The tuberosity is a raised area on the lateral surface of the shaft, and it exists for that muscle alone. The other muscles of the region attach instead to the tubercles at the upper end or to the epicondyles below.

Question 1323. Which of these statements about the intertubercular sulcus is correct: it lies on the front of the upper humerus and carries the tendon of the long head of the biceps, it lies on the back of the shaft and carries the radial nerve, it lies below the deltoid tuberosity, or it separates the epicondyles? The answer is the first.

Question 1324. A described radiograph of the elbow shows two rounded prominences on either side of the joint at the lower end of the humerus. Which prominence is medial and which is lateral, and what lies behind the medial one? The answer is the medial epicondyle on the inner side and the lateral epicondyle on the outer side, with the ulnar nerve passing behind the medial one.

Question 1325. Which groove on the back of the humerus runs obliquely downward and laterally, carrying the radial nerve against the bone? The answer is the radial groove. It begins near the surgical neck and runs toward the lateral side of the shaft. Because the nerve lies directly on bone there, a fracture at that level is likely to injure it.

Question 1326. Which of these muscles does not attach to the humerus at all: the deltoid, the coracobrachialis, the subscapularis, or the serratus anterior? The answer is the serratus anterior. It arises from the outer surfaces of the upper ribs and inserts on the medial border of the scapula. The other three all have an attachment on the humerus.

Question 1327. A constructed illustration: after a fall, a person cannot grip with the ring and little fingers, the little finger is numb, and the described radiograph shows a fragment of bone at the inner side of the elbow. Which nerve is injured, and why not the median nerve? The answer is the ulnar nerve, behind the medial epicondyle.

Question 1328. Which part of the upper humerus lies inside the capsule of the shoulder joint and is covered by articular cartilage rather than by periosteum? The answer is the head of the humerus. The capsule is attached around the anatomical neck, so the head sits within the joint cavity. The tubercles lie outside the capsule and are covered by periosteum.

Question 1329. A supracondylar fracture in a child heals with the lower fragment tilted so that the forearm points inward when the arm is extended. What is this deformity called: cubitus varus, cubitus valgus, a dislocation of the radial head, or an olecranon spur? The answer is cubitus varus. Cubitus valgus is the opposite deviation, with the forearm carried outward.

Question 1330. Which structure winds around the surgical neck of the humerus, passing between the bone and the muscles that cover it? The answer is the axillary nerve, with the posterior circumflex humeral artery. It travels through a space bounded by the teres minor above, the teres major below and the long head of the triceps medially. A fracture at the surgical neck threatens it.

Question 1331. Which small prominence on the outer side of the elbow gives origin to the muscles that straighten the wrist and the fingers? The answer is the lateral epicondyle of the humerus. It lies at the lateral edge of the lower end, above the capitulum. Its counterpart on the inner side gives origin to the flexor muscles.

Question 1332. Which of these statements about the lower end of the humerus is correct: the capitulum lies lateral to the trochlea, the trochlea lies lateral to the capitulum, the epicondyles lie on the front of the bone, or the olecranon fossa lies on the front? The answer is that the capitulum lies lateral to the trochlea and meets the head of the radius.

Question 1333. A described radiograph of a child's elbow shows a small rounded ossification center at the medial side of the joint, separated from the shaft by cartilage. Which center is it, and why must it not be mistaken for a fragment? The answer is the center for the medial epicondyle, which appears later than the others and joins the shaft only toward the end of growth.

Question 1334. Name the two articular surfaces at the lower end of the humerus, spoken as a pair from lateral to medial. The answer is the capitulum and the trochlea. The capitulum receives the head of the radius, and the trochlea receives the trochlear notch of the ulna. Together they form the condyle of the humerus.

Question 1335. Why does the shoulder dislocate forward and downward more often than in any other direction: because the capsule is weak below and in front, because the glenoid cavity is deeper below than above, because the acromion blocks upward displacement, or because the biceps tendon holds the head back? The answer is the weak capsule below and in front.

Question 1336. Which bone of the forearm lies on the thumb side when the body is in the anatomical position? The answer is the radius. It is the lateral bone of the forearm and the broader of the two at the wrist. The ulna lies medial to it on the little finger side.

Question 1337. Which part of the radius takes part in the elbow joint: the head, the neck, the radial tuberosity, or the styloid process? The answer is the head. It is a disc-shaped upper end whose upper surface glides against the capitulum of the humerus. The neck below it is encircled by a ligament rather than by cartilage.

Question 1338. Which roughened prominence on the upper radius lies just below the neck, faces medially, and gives insertion to the biceps brachii? The answer is the radial tuberosity. Its position on the medial side of the bone is a useful reminder of how the tendon of the biceps twists as it reaches the forearm.

Question 1339. Which band of fibrous tissue encircles the head of the radius and holds it against the ulna, allowing it to spin but not to slide away? The answer is the annular ligament. It is attached to the front and the back of the radial notch of the ulna and forms about four fifths of a ring around the radial head.

Question 1340. A described radiograph of the wrist shows that the bony point reaching farthest down on the outer side is longer than the corresponding point on the inner side. Which bone carries the longer point, and what is that point called? The answer is the radius, and the point is its styloid process.

Question 1341. Which part of the lower radius is grooved to hold the tendon of the extensor pollicis longus: the dorsal tubercle, the ulnar notch, the styloid process, or the radial tuberosity? The answer is the dorsal tubercle, a small prominence on the back of the lower end around which the tendon runs. The ulnar notch receives the head of the ulna.

Question 1342. A constructed illustration: an older person falls onto the outstretched hand, and afterward there is a painful swelling at the wrist with the hand displaced backward, so that the wrist looks like a fork. Which bone is broken, and in which direction is the lower fragment displaced? The answer is the lower end of the radius, displaced backward and upward.

Question 1343. True or false: the shafts of the radius and the ulna are joined along their length by a fibrous membrane. The answer is true. The interosseous membrane stretches between the two interosseous borders. It gives attachment to muscles, keeps the bones apart, and carries part of the force that the hand receives up to the ulna.

Question 1344. Which of these pairs belongs together: the head of the radius with the radial notch, the head of the ulna with the radial notch, the head of the radius with the ulnar notch, or the head of the ulna with the trochlear notch? The answer is the head of the radius with the radial notch of the ulna, at the upper joint between the bones.

Question 1345. Which movement carries the lower end of the radius across the front of the ulna: supination, pronation, flexion, or abduction? The answer is pronation. The radius rotates around the ulna so that its lower end crosses to the medial side, and the palm turns to face the floor. Supination untwists the two bones and returns them to parallel.

Question 1346. A described radiograph of a forearm held with the palm facing forward shows two bones lying side by side, each with a narrow shaft and a wider end. Which bone lies lateral, and which bone lies medial? The answer is the radius laterally and the ulna medially. With the palm forward, the two bones are parallel and neither crosses the other.

Question 1347. Which pointed projection at the lower end of the radius forms the outer boundary of the wrist and can be felt as a bony knob just above the base of the thumb? The answer is the styloid process of the radius. It reaches farther down than the styloid process of the ulna on the other side of the wrist.

Question 1348. A constructed illustration: a fracture crosses the lower third of the radius, and the described radiograph shows that the head of the ulna no longer sits in its notch at the wrist. Which two injuries are present? The answer is a fracture of the distal radius with dislocation of the distal radioulnar joint.

Question 1349. The radius rotates about the ulna during pronation and supination. Which structure forms the pivot at its upper end, and which at its lower end? The answer is the head of the radius turning within the ring of the radial notch and the annular ligament above, and the head of the ulna turning within the ulnar notch of the radius below.

Question 1350. Which two carpal bones articulate with the carpal surface of the radius: the scaphoid and the lunate, the lunate and the triquetrum, the scaphoid and the trapezium, or the pisiform and the lunate? The answer is the scaphoid and the lunate. The radius carries the hand at the wrist, and the ulna is separated from the carpus by a disc.

Question 1351. A described cross section through the lower end of the radius shows a broad bone with a concave surface below, divided into two facets, and a small notch on its medial edge. Which bone is this, and what does the notch receive? The answer is the radius, and the notch is the ulnar notch, which receives the head of the ulna.

Question 1352. Name the two bones of the forearm in the order in which they lie from lateral to medial in the anatomical position. The answer is the radius and then the ulna. The radius lies on the thumb side and the ulna on the little finger side. The order reverses when the forearm is fully pronated.

Question 1353. Which muscle inserts on the radial tuberosity: the biceps brachii, the triceps brachii, the brachialis, or the pronator teres? The answer is the biceps brachii. Its tendon crosses the front of the elbow and twists as it reaches the medial side of the bone. The brachialis inserts on the ulna instead.

Question 1354. Which muscle inserts into the lateral surface of the middle of the radial shaft and turns the forearm so that the palm faces downward? The answer is the pronator teres. It arises from the medial epicondyle of the humerus and the coronoid process of the ulna and sweeps across to the radius. Its pull rotates the radius around the ulna.

Question 1355. When the forearm turns from the position with the palm forward to the position with the palm backward, the radius rotates about a longitudinal axis. In which direction does its lower end travel, and which bone stays still? The answer is that the lower end of the radius travels forward and medially across the front of the ulna, while the ulna stays in place.

Question 1356. A described radiograph shows a break through the lower end of the radius with the hand and the fragment displaced forward rather than backward. Which pattern is this: a backward displacement fracture, a forward displacement fracture, a fracture of the radial head, or a fracture of the ulnar styloid? The answer is a forward displacement fracture, the reverse of the commoner backward pattern.

Question 1357. A constructed illustration: a young child is lifted by the hand and afterward holds the arm still and will not use it, because the head of the radius has slipped partly out from under the ring that holds it. Which structure has given way, and at which end of the bone? The answer is the annular ligament at the upper end of the radius.

Question 1358. Which bone of the forearm forms the point of the elbow and carries the notch that grips the humerus? The answer is the ulna. Its upper end is thick and its lower end is small, the reverse of the radius. It lies on the little finger side of the forearm.

Question 1359. Which process of the ulna projects forward below the trochlear notch and forms the lower lip of that notch? The answer is the coronoid process. The olecranon forms the upper lip and the point of the elbow. The two are joined by the notch that receives the trochlea of the humerus.

Question 1360. The trochlear notch of the ulna articulates with which part of the humerus: the capitulum, the trochlea, the head, or the greater tubercle? The answer is the trochlea. It is a hinge joint, so the ulna can only bend and straighten on the humerus. The head of the radius meets the capitulum instead.

Question 1361. Which muscle inserts on the olecranon of the ulna, and what movement does its contraction produce? The answer is the triceps brachii, which straightens the elbow. It is the only muscle that inserts on that process, and the process gives it a lever arm behind the joint. Its long head also crosses the shoulder joint.

Question 1362. A described radiograph of the elbow taken from the side shows a broad notch on the upper ulna gripping the lower humerus, with a beak above it and a smaller projection below. Which three parts of the ulna are shown? The answer is the trochlear notch, with the olecranon above it and the coronoid process below it.

Question 1363. True or false: the ulna takes part in the wrist joint. The answer is false. The lower end of the ulna is separated from the carpal bones by a disc of fibrocartilage, so the ulna has no direct articular surface at the wrist. The radius alone carries the hand at that joint.

Question 1364. Which structure lies between the head of the ulna and the carpal bones and keeps the ulna from bearing weight at the wrist: the annular ligament, the articular disc, the interosseous membrane, or the flexor retinaculum? The answer is the articular disc. It is part of the triangular fibrocartilage complex and completes the socket for the carpus.

Question 1365. A constructed illustration: a person falls onto the bent elbow, and the described radiograph shows a break through the beak of bone at the back of the joint with the fragment pulled upward; the elbow can no longer be straightened against resistance. Which structure is broken, and which muscle has pulled the fragment? The answer is the olecranon, pulled by the triceps brachii.

Question 1366. Which of these pairs belongs together: the olecranon with the biceps brachii, the coronoid process with the brachialis, the radial notch with the trochlea, or the ulnar styloid with the olecranon fossa? The answer is the coronoid process with the brachialis. The brachialis inserts on the front of the ulna just below the coronoid process, and the triceps inserts on the olecranon.

Question 1367. Which shallow depression on the lateral side of the coronoid process of the ulna receives the head of the radius? The answer is the radial notch. It forms the lateral wall of the upper radioulnar joint, and the annular ligament is attached to its edges. The radius spins against it during pronation and supination.

Question 1368. A described radiograph of the forearm after a fall shows a break through the upper part of the ulna together with displacement of the radial head at the elbow. Which two injuries are present, and which structure has given way? The answer is a fracture of the ulna with dislocation of the radial head, and the annular ligament no longer holds the head in place.

Question 1369. Which statement about the ulnar styloid process is correct: it receives the attachment of the articular disc but does not reach the carpal bones, it articulates directly with the triquetrum, it is longer than the radial styloid, or it lies at the upper end of the ulna? The answer is the first. It is a small projection at the lower end of the ulna.

Question 1370. A constructed illustration: a person who leans the inner side of the elbow on a desk for long periods develops tingling in the little finger and wasting of the small muscles of the hand. Which nerve is compressed, and where? The answer is the ulnar nerve in the cubital tunnel behind the medial epicondyle, covered only by skin and fascia.

Question 1371. Which border of the ulna gives attachment to the interosseous membrane, and toward which bone does that border face? The answer is the interosseous border, which faces laterally toward the radius. It is the sharp lateral crest of the shaft, and the membrane that runs from it to the radius divides the forearm into front and back compartments.

Question 1372. Which statement about the head of the ulna is correct: it lies at the lower end of the bone, it lies at the upper end, it articulates with the humerus, or it gives insertion to the biceps? The answer is that it lies at the lower end, where it fits the ulnar notch of the radius.

Question 1373. Which ridge on the upper part of the ulna runs downward from the radial notch and gives origin to part of the supinator muscle? The answer is the supinator crest. It lies on the lateral surface of the upper shaft, blending with the radial notch above. The muscle wraps around the upper radius from it.

Question 1374. Which part of the ulna lies directly beneath the skin along the back of the forearm and is therefore the sharp edge that can be felt with a finger? The answer is the posterior border of the shaft. It is subcutaneous throughout its length, with no muscle covering it. That is why the ulna is so easily exposed to injury from behind.

Question 1375. A described cross section through the lower forearm shows two bones, one broad and one small, with a disc of fibrocartilage extending from the smaller bone to the carpus. Which bones and which structure are shown? The answer is the radius and the ulna at the wrist, with the articular disc separating the head of the ulna from the carpal bones.

Question 1376. Name two muscles that have an attachment on the ulna, spoken as a pair. The answer is the brachialis and the triceps brachii. The brachialis inserts on the coronoid process below the joint, and the triceps inserts on the olecranon above it. Many other muscles also arise from the shaft of the bone.

Question 1377. Which statement best explains why the elbow bends and straightens without the forearm rotating sideways: the ulna is a stable hinge on the trochlea while the radius rotates about it, the radius is fixed while the ulna rotates, the two rotate together, or the joint is a ball and socket? The answer is the first.

Question 1378. A transverse section is taken across the forearm just above the wrist. Which of the two bones lies lateral in that section, and which bone has a rounded head seen on the medial side? The answer is the radius laterally and the head of the ulna medially. At that level the radius is broad and the ulna is small.

Question 1379. The force of the hand passes through the radius at the wrist, yet a break in the ulnar shaft can still disturb that joint. Why: because the two bones are joined at both ends, because the ulna reaches the carpal bones, because the disc is ulnar, or because the ulnar styloid carries the load? The answer is that the two bones are joined at both ends.

Question 1380. How many carpal bones form one wrist, and how are they arranged? The answer is eight bones in two rows of four. The proximal row lies next to the radius and the distal row next to the metacarpal bones. The row of bones forms an arch whose concavity faces the palm.

Question 1381. Which carpal bone lies at the outer side of the proximal row, next to the radius and above the thumb: the lunate, the scaphoid, the triquetrum, or the pisiform? The answer is the scaphoid. It is the largest bone of the proximal row and takes the weight of the hand against the radius. The lunate lies medial to it.

Question 1382. Which carpal bone lies in the floor of the hollow at the base of the thumb, the hollow that is bounded by two tendons and is known as the anatomical snuffbox? The answer is the scaphoid. Its tubercle can be felt on the palm side of the wrist as a small rounded prominence.

Question 1383. A described radiograph of the wrist shows a crack running across the middle of one carpal bone in the proximal row, with no displacement. Which bone is most likely broken, and why is the break often missed? The answer is the scaphoid. The crack may be invisible at first, and the pain is felt in the hollow at the base of the thumb.

Question 1384. A constructed illustration: a person falls onto the outstretched hand, and afterward there is tenderness in the hollow at the base of the thumb when the wrist is moved, though the described radiograph looks normal. Which bone is most likely broken? The answer is the scaphoid, the carpal bone that breaks most often.

Question 1385. Which carpal bone lies immediately distal to the scaphoid and the lunate and receives the two of them side by side? The answer is the capitate. It sits in the middle of the distal row and is the largest carpal bone. Its rounded head fits into the hollow formed by the scaphoid and the lunate.

Question 1386. With which carpal bone does the first metacarpal form the saddle joint that gives the thumb its wide movement: the trapezoid, the trapezium, the capitate, or the hamate? The answer is the trapezium. The joint between the two is a saddle, which allows the thumb to swing across the palm and to oppose the fingers.

Question 1387. Which carpal bone carries a curved hook-shaped process that can be felt on the front of the wrist near the base of the little finger? The answer is the hamate. The hook projects forward and gives attachment to the flexor retinaculum and to the small muscles of the little finger.

Question 1388. A described radiograph of the wrist shows the carpal bones in two rows; the largest bone lies at the center of the distal row, with the third metacarpal above it. Which bone is it, and which bone lies between it and the radius? The answer is the capitate, with the lunate between it and the radius.

Question 1389. Which of these pairs belongs together: the scaphoid with the first metacarpal, the trapezium with the first metacarpal, the hamate with the radius, or the pisiform with the radius? The answer is the trapezium with the first metacarpal. Those two form the saddle joint of the thumb, while the radius meets the scaphoid and the lunate.

Question 1390. A constructed illustration: a person has numbness of the thumb, the index finger and the middle finger, weakness of the muscles at the base of the thumb, and normal sensation over the little finger. Which nerve is compressed, and beneath which band? The answer is the median nerve, compressed beneath the flexor retinaculum in the carpal tunnel at the wrist.

Question 1391. The flexor retinaculum stretches across the front of the wrist and forms the roof of the carpal tunnel. To which two bones is it attached on the lateral side? The answer is the tubercle of the scaphoid and the ridge of the trapezium. On the medial side it attaches to the pisiform and the hook of the hamate.

Question 1392. True or false: the pisiform is a sesamoid bone that develops within the tendon of the flexor carpi ulnaris. The answer is true. It is the smallest carpal bone and lies in the proximal row on the medial side. It gives attachment to the flexor retinaculum and to a small muscle of the little finger.

Question 1393. Which carpal bone is the keystone between the radius above and the capitate below, and most often dislocates in a fall on the hand: the scaphoid, the lunate, the capitate, or the trapezium? The answer is the lunate. It sits in the middle of the proximal row and takes the capitate into its lower cup, and a fall on the hand can drive it out of the socket of the radius.

Question 1394. A described radiograph of the wrist shows the lunate still in its place against the radius while all the other carpal bones have shifted backward around it. Which injury is shown, and which bone has stayed behind? The answer is a dislocation of the carpus around the lunate, with the lunate still in the socket of the radius.

Question 1395. Which carpal bone lies in the distal row between the trapezium on one side and the capitate on the other? The answer is the trapezoid. It is smaller than both of its neighbors and articulates below with the second metacarpal bone. It has no movement of its own and moves only as the rest of the wrist moves.

Question 1396. Which of these pairs belongs together: the fifth metacarpal with the hamate, the fifth metacarpal with the trapezium, the second metacarpal with the pisiform, or the first metacarpal with the lunate? The answer is the fifth metacarpal with the hamate. The first metacarpal meets the trapezium, and the second meets the trapezoid.

Question 1397. A break across the waist of the scaphoid often heals badly because the blood supply enters at one end and runs backward along the bone. Which end receives the blood, and which end is left without it? The answer is that the vessels enter the distal part near the tubercle and the proximal pole is nourished in a backward direction, so that pole may die.

Question 1398. A described section across the wrist shows a tunnel bounded by an arch of carpal bones below and a tough band above, with tendons and a nerve packed inside. Which structure forms the floor, and which forms the roof? The answer is the carpal bones forming the floor and the flexor retinaculum forming the roof, with the median nerve between them.

Question 1399. Which of these structures does not pass through the carpal tunnel: the median nerve, the tendon of the flexor pollicis longus, the ulnar nerve, or the tendons of the flexor digitorum superficialis? The answer is the ulnar nerve. It enters the hand through a separate channel between the pisiform and the hook of the hamate.

Question 1400. Why does compression within the carpal tunnel spare the sensation of the little finger: because the ulnar nerve supplies that finger and does not pass through the tunnel, because the little finger has no nerve supply, because the median nerve spares it, or because the radial nerve supplies it? The answer is that the ulnar nerve supplies the little finger and runs outside the tunnel.

To summarize, the humerus is the single bone of the arm, and each of its parts carries a name worth saying aloud. The greater tubercle takes the supraspinatus, the infraspinatus and the teres minor, the lesser tubercle takes the subscapularis, the radial groove carries the radial nerve against bone, the medial epicondyle shelters the ulnar nerve, and the lateral epicondyle gives origin to the extensor muscles. In the forearm the radius lies on the thumb side, with its head turning against the capitulum and its lower end carrying the hand, while the ulna forms the point of the elbow and is separated from the carpus by the articular disc. The eight carpal bones lie in two rows of four, with the scaphoid in the floor of the anatomical snuffbox, the trapezium at the saddle joint of the thumb, the capitate at the center and the hook of the hamate beside the ulnar nerve.
