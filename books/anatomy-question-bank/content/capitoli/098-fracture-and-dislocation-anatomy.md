# Chapter 98 — Fracture and Dislocation Anatomy

This chapter examines the anatomy that decides where a bone breaks and what a wound divides. It covers the fracture patterns that carry names, the bones and levels they involve, the dislocations that take a named nerve or vessel with them, and then the anatomy of wounds: the layers of the skin, the way a wound heals, and the structures that lie in the track of a penetrating injury at each region of the surface. It holds one hundred questions, and each question is answered immediately after it is asked and then explained, so nothing has to be held in mind until the end. This is educational material, and it supports rather than replaces an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 9701. Which word names a break in which the bone is shattered into several separate pieces: transverse, comminuted, spiral, or impacted? The answer is comminuted. Comminuted means broken into fragments rather than into two clean parts. A transverse break runs straight across the shaft, and a spiral break twists around it.

Question 9702. A described radiograph shows a break of the lower end of the radius, with the hand and the distal fragment carried backward into a dinner fork shape. Which fracture is described? The answer is a Colles fracture. The break lies in the last centimeter or two of the radius, and the backward tilt of the fragment gives the deformity its name.

Question 9703. Which term describes the incomplete break of a child's long bone, in which one cortex cracks while the other side merely bends: greenstick, comminuted, segmental, or pathological? The answer is greenstick. A child's bone is softer and more flexible than an adult's, so it bends before it breaks and gives way on one side only.

Question 9704. Which carpal bone lies in the floor of the hollow at the base of the thumb, and which part of it is famous for losing its blood supply after a break? The answer is the scaphoid bone, and its proximal part is the part at risk. Blood enters the scaphoid near its far end and flows backward toward the wrist.

Question 9705. Which bone is broken by a punch that lands on a wall, producing a break through the neck of the bone with the knuckle sunk toward the palm? The answer is the fifth metacarpal bone. Its neck is the weakest part, just behind the head. The fourth metacarpal neck can break in the same way, but far less often.

Question 9706. Which injury combines a break of the upper third of the ulna with a dislocation of the head of the radius at the elbow: a Colles fracture, a Monteggia injury, a Galeazzi injury, or a Barton fracture? The answer is a Monteggia injury. The dislocated radial head is the part easily missed, so the elbow must be examined whenever the ulnar shaft is broken.

Question 9707. Which injury pairs a break of the lower third of the radius with a dislocation of the joint between the two forearm bones at the wrist? The answer is a Galeazzi injury. The head of the ulna springs out of its notch on the radius. It is the mirror image of the Monteggia injury, which pairs an ulnar break with a radial head dislocation.

Question 9708. Which nerve lies in the groove that spirals around the back of the shaft of the humerus, and which movement is lost when a break there injures it? The answer is the radial nerve, and the loss is extension of the wrist and the fingers. The nerve then passes forward between the brachialis and the brachioradialis.

Question 9709. A constructed illustration: a person falls and afterward cannot lift the arm, and the outer part of the shoulder is numb. Which nerve is injured, and at which part of the humerus? The answer is the axillary nerve, injured as it winds around the surgical neck. It supplies the deltoid and the skin over the lower part of the shoulder.

Question 9710. A described radiograph of a child's elbow shows a break across the lower humerus just above the joint, with the distal fragment carried backward. Which fracture is shown, and which line drawn down the front of the humerus helps to detect it? The answer is a supracondylar fracture, and the line along the front of the shaft should meet the capitellum.

Question 9711. In a supracondylar fracture of the humerus in a child, which two structures lie immediately in front of the lower end of the bone and are therefore at risk? The answer is the brachial artery and the median nerve. Both pass down the front of the elbow in the hollow beside the biceps tendon.

Question 9712. A constructed illustration: after an elbow injury a person cannot pinch the thumb and the index finger together, although the fingers can still be closed into the palm. Which nerve branch is most likely injured: the ulnar nerve, the deep branch of the median nerve in the forearm, the radial nerve, or the axillary nerve? The answer is the deep branch of the median nerve in the forearm.

Question 9713. A described radiograph of an injured child shows a break that runs along the growth plate and then out through a corner of the shaft, carrying a small triangular piece of bone with it. Which pattern of growth plate injury is this? The answer is an injury through the plate and the shaft together. That corner of bone is its mark, and the plate itself is usually only partly damaged.

Question 9714. Which zone of the growth plate is the weakest, and therefore the plane along which a child's bone usually separates: the reserve zone, the proliferative zone, the hypertrophic zone, or the zone of calcified cartilage? The answer is the hypertrophic zone. Its cells are large and the matrix between them is scanty, so it gives way before the bone around it.

Question 9715. Which statement about a bridge of bone that forms across a growth plate is correct: it slows or stops growth at that point, it has no effect on growth, it makes the bone longer than the other side, or it disappears with age? The answer is that it slows or stops growth at that point. The bridge is a bar of bone tethering the two sides of the plate together.

Question 9716. Which break of the upper femur lies inside the capsule of the hip joint, so that the blood supply to the head of the bone is threatened? The answer is a break of the femoral neck. The vessels that feed the head run up along the neck beneath the capsule, and a break at that level can tear them.

Question 9717. Which vessel is the main supply to the head of the femur in an adult: the artery of the round ligament, the medial circumflex femoral artery, the lateral circumflex femoral artery, or the obturator artery? The answer is the medial circumflex femoral artery, by way of the vessels that climb the neck. The artery of the round ligament is small and reaches only a little of the head.

Question 9718. A constructed illustration: after a dashboard injury the leg is shortened, turned inward, and drawn toward the other leg. Which injury is this, and which nerve lies against the back of the joint and may be stretched? The answer is a posterior dislocation of the hip, with the sciatic nerve at risk behind it.

Question 9719. Which long bone of the leg has a sharp front border lying directly beneath the skin, so that a break there readily opens through the skin? The answer is the tibia. Its subcutaneous surface has almost no muscle over it. The fibula lies lateral to it and carries little of the weight of the body.

Question 9720. Which injury is a break of the upper part of the fibula with a tear of the ligaments between the two leg bones at the ankle: a Maisonneuve injury, a Pilon fracture, a Jones fracture, or a Colles fracture? The answer is a Maisonneuve injury. The force travels up the fibula and springs the ankle joint apart at its far end.

Question 9721. A described radiograph of an ankle shows a break of the inner bony prominence and a break of the outer bony prominence, with the ankle joint tilted. Which injury is shown, and which ligament is torn on the inner side? The answer is a bimalleolar fracture, with the deltoid ligament torn on the inner side.

Question 9722. Which ligament is attached to the medial malleolus, and which ligaments are attached to the lateral malleolus? The answer is the deltoid ligament on the inner side and the lateral collateral ligaments on the outer side. The deltoid is a strong triangular band, while the lateral ligaments are three separate cords.

Question 9723. A described radiograph shows a break of the lower end of the tibia that splits the joint surface into several pieces and drives one of them upward into the shaft. Which fracture is shown, and what is the upper surface of that bone called? The answer is a Pilon fracture, which involves the roof of the ankle, the tibial plafond.

Question 9724. True or false: the talus is covered with cartilage over most of its surface and its blood supply enters from behind and below, so a break of its neck can starve the body of the bone. The answer is true. Most of the surface of the talus is articular, so few vessels can enter it, and a break at the neck cuts the supply that runs backward through the bone.

Question 9725. Which vessel is the main supply to the body of the talus: the artery of the tarsal canal, the dorsalis pedis artery, the peroneal artery, or the medial plantar artery? The answer is the artery of the tarsal canal, a branch of the artery behind the inner ankle. It enters the bone through a sling of vessels beneath the neck.

Question 9726. Which bone is broken when a person lands on the heels from a height, and which angle of that bone is flattened by the break: the talus, the calcaneus, the navicular, or the cuboid? The answer is the calcaneus, and the angle between its upper surface and its back is flattened.

Question 9727. Which break lies at the junction of the shaft of the fifth metatarsal bone with its base, in a zone where the blood supply is poor: a Jones fracture, an avulsion of the base, a stress fracture of the second metatarsal, or a break of the great toe? The answer is a Jones fracture. It lies beyond the point where the tendon of the peroneus brevis is attached.

Question 9728. A described radiograph of the foot of a person who has recently taken up long-distance running shows a fine crack across the shaft of the second metatarsal bone with a small collar of new bone around it. What kind of break is this? The answer is a stress fracture. Repeated loading of otherwise normal bone cracks it without any single injury.

Question 9729. Which joint of the foot lies between the midfoot and the forefoot, and which bone is its keystone because its base is recessed and locked between two others? The answer is the tarsometatarsal joint, and the keystone is the base of the second metatarsal bone. Disruption there lets the forefoot shift sideways.

Question 9730. Name two structures that must always be examined when the shaft of the ulna is broken, spoken as a list. The answer is the head of the radius and the joint between the two forearm bones at the wrist. A break of the ulna may be part of an injury in which either of those has also come apart.

Question 9731. A described radiograph of the shoulder shows a break in the middle of the clavicle, with the inner fragment drawn upward and the outer fragment dropping. Which bone is broken, and why do the fragments part in that way? The answer is the clavicle. The sternocleidomastoid muscle pulls the inner fragment upward, and the weight of the arm pulls the outer fragment down.

Question 9732. Which structures lie immediately behind and below the middle of the clavicle, and may be injured by a break there: the trachea, the brachial plexus and the great vessels of the arm, the phrenic nerve alone, or the thoracic duct? The answer is the brachial plexus and the great vessels of the arm. They pass between the clavicle and the first rib.

Question 9733. Which joint is injured when a fall onto the point of the shoulder separates the outer end of the clavicle from the acromion, and which ligament above the joint is torn first? The answer is the acromioclavicular joint, and the acromioclavicular ligament tears before the stronger ligament between the coracoid and the clavicle.

Question 9734. A constructed illustration: a person falls with the arm forced outward and upward, and afterward the shoulder looks flattened and the outer side of the arm is numb. Which injury is this, and which nerve is most likely stretched? The answer is an anterior dislocation of the shoulder, with the axillary nerve stretched around the neck of the humerus.

Question 9735. A described radiograph shows the head of the humerus lying below and in front of the socket of the scapula, with a small notch in the back of the head. Which injury is shown, and what is the notch called? The answer is an anterior dislocation of the shoulder, and the notch in the back of the head is a Hill-Sachs lesion.

Question 9736. Which structure is torn from the front and lower rim of the glenoid socket when the shoulder dislocates forward: the coracoacromial ligament, the glenoid labrum, the acromioclavicular ligament, or the tendon of the long head of the biceps? The answer is the glenoid labrum, and the tear is called a Bankart lesion.

Question 9737. Which dislocation of the elbow is the commonest in adults, and which large vessel lies immediately in front of the joint and is therefore at risk? The answer is the posterior dislocation, with the brachial artery in front. The ulna and the radius both pass backward behind the humerus.

Question 9738. True or false: the ulnar nerve lies in a groove behind the medial epicondyle of the humerus and is therefore easily injured in a child's elbow injury. The answer is true. The nerve runs in a shallow bony groove with only skin and fascia over it, which is why a knock there is felt as a tingling shock.

Question 9739. Which of these pairs belongs together: a Colles fracture with the ulna, a Jones fracture with the first metatarsal bone, a Bennett fracture with the base of the thumb metacarpal, or a Jefferson fracture with the axis? The answer is a Bennett fracture with the base of the thumb metacarpal. A Colles fracture is of the radius, a Jones fracture of the fifth metatarsal, and a Jefferson fracture of the atlas.

Question 9740. Which injury is a burst of the first cervical vertebra into several pieces after a blow on the top of the head, and which ligament must be checked with it: a hangman fracture, a Jefferson fracture, a teardrop fracture, or a Chance fracture? The answer is a Jefferson fracture, and the transverse ligament of the atlas is the structure to check, because it holds the odontoid process in place.

Question 9741. Which fracture passes through the pedicles of the second cervical vertebra on both sides, and what is that vertebra called? The answer is a hangman fracture, and the vertebra is the axis. The break lies between the upper and the lower articular surfaces, so the body of the axis can slip forward on the third cervical vertebra.

Question 9742. A described section of the upper cervical spine shows a small peg of bone rising from the body of the second cervical vertebra into the ring of the first. Which ligament holds that peg against the front of the ring, and what lies immediately behind the peg? The answer is the transverse ligament of the atlas, with the spinal cord behind.

Question 9743. A described radiograph of the spine shows a horizontal split running through the body of a vertebra and continuing backward through its pedicles and its spinous process, so that the upper half has moved forward. Which injury is shown, and what kind of force produces it? The answer is a Chance fracture, produced when the body is bent forward over a fixed lap belt.

Question 9744. A constructed illustration: after a fall from a height, a person has a break of a vertebral body with a fragment driven backward into the spinal canal. Which part of the vertebra has the fragment come from, and why is the cord at particular risk in the thoracic region? The answer is the back of the vertebral body, and the thoracic canal is narrow and already well filled by the cord.

Question 9745. Which statement distinguishes a wedge compression fracture from a burst fracture of a vertebral body: the wedge fracture involves only the back of the body, the burst fracture drives bone backward into the canal, the burst fracture spares the posterior wall, or the wedge fracture always injures the cord? The answer is that the burst fracture drives bone backward into the canal, while the wedge fracture spares the posterior wall.

Question 9746. One described illustration shows a leg shortened and turned outward after a fall in an older person, and another shows a leg shortened and turned inward after a dashboard injury. In which direction has the limb rotated in each, and why does the direction differ? The answer is outward in the break of the femoral neck and inward in the posterior dislocation of the hip.

Question 9747. Which defect is a break in the part of a vertebra that joins its upper and lower articular processes, and what is the forward slip of that vertebra on the one below called? The answer is spondylolysis, and the slip is spondylolisthesis. The defect lies in the pars interarticularis, between the two articular processes.

Question 9748. A constructed illustration: after a severe injury to the lower back a person has loss of feeling around the saddle area and weakness in both legs. Which structure is compressed, and why does compression there produce these findings on both sides? The answer is the cauda equina, the bundle of lumbar and sacral nerve roots that descends below the end of the spinal cord.

Question 9749. Which of these pairs belongs together: the radial nerve with a break of the shaft of the humerus, the axillary nerve with a break of the shaft of the femur, the common peroneal nerve with a dislocation of the shoulder, or the sciatic nerve with a break of the clavicle? The answer is the radial nerve with a break of the shaft of the humerus, because the nerve lies in the spiral groove against the bone.

Question 9750. A constructed illustration: a knee is dislocated backward in a road traffic injury, and afterward the foot cannot be lifted at the ankle. Which two structures behind the knee are most at risk? The answer is the popliteal artery and the common peroneal nerve. The artery is tethered where it passes through the adductor magnus and beneath the arch of the soleus.

Question 9751. Which layer of the skin lies immediately deep to the epidermis, carries the blood vessels, the nerves, the hair follicles and the sweat glands, and gives the skin its strength? The answer is the dermis. The epidermis above it has no vessels of its own, and it is fed by diffusion from the dermis.

Question 9752. Which layer contains the hair follicles, the sweat glands and the vessels that bleed when the skin is cut: the epidermis, the dermis, the fatty layer beneath the skin, or the deep fascia? The answer is the dermis. The fatty layer beneath the skin holds fat and larger vessels, but the fine supply to the skin runs in the dermis.

Question 9753. A described cross section of skin shows a thin outer layer of flattened cells with no vessels, a thicker fibrous layer beneath it, and a layer of fat below that. Which three layers are these, named from outside inward? The answer is the epidermis, the dermis, and the subcutaneous fat.

Question 9754. Which word names a wound made by a sharp edge, with clean, straight margins and little bruising: an abrasion, a laceration, an incised wound, or a puncture? The answer is an incised wound. A laceration has ragged margins where the skin has been torn, and an abrasion has lost only its surface.

Question 9755. Which wound is a narrow track that passes deeply through the skin while leaving only a small opening at the surface? The answer is a puncture wound. Because the opening is small, the depth of the track and the structures it divides are easily underestimated.

Question 9756. A cut made across the lines of tension of the skin gapes widely, while one made along those lines closes neatly. In which direction do those lines run on the front of the neck, and why does the direction matter? The answer is across the neck, roughly horizontal, because the fibers of the dermis are arranged along the lines and a cut between them pulls open.

Question 9757. What is the name of the new tissue that fills a healing wound, made of budding capillaries, fibroblasts and a loose matrix, and giving the surface a red, granular look? The answer is granulation tissue. It is the bed over which new epidermis migrates to close the defect.

Question 9758. Which statement describes healing by secondary intention: the wound edges are held together and heal with a fine scar, the wound is left open and fills from its base, the wound heals with no new tissue at all, or the wound heals only by contraction? The answer is that the wound is left open and fills from its base, with more granulation tissue and a larger scar.

Question 9759. True or false: in a wound healing by secondary intention, specialized fibroblasts grip the edges of the defect and pull them together, so that the wound grows smaller. The answer is true. These myofibroblasts contain bundles of contractile filaments, and their pull is called wound contraction.

Question 9760. What is the name of the modified fibroblast that contains contractile filaments and draws the edges of an open wound together? The answer is the myofibroblast. It appears in the wound some days after the injury and disappears once the defect has closed.

Question 9761. A described microscopic section of a wound four days old shows neutrophils around the margins, macrophages clearing debris, and a loose mesh of fibroblasts and new capillaries. Which process is shown, and what tissue will fill the defect? The answer is the formation of granulation tissue.

Question 9762. Which cell is chiefly responsible for laying down the collagen that gives a healing wound its strength: the neutrophil, the macrophage, the fibroblast, or the platelet? The answer is the fibroblast. The macrophage clears debris and signals, and the platelet forms the first plug, but neither of them builds the scar.

Question 9763. Which type of collagen is laid down first in a healing wound and is later replaced by a stronger type, and what are the two types called? The answer is that type three is laid down first and is replaced by type one. Type three forms thin, weak fibers, and type one forms the thick, strong fibers of mature scar.

Question 9764. Which statement about a keloid scar is correct: it stays within the bounds of the original wound, it grows beyond the wound into normal skin, it is always painful, or it fades within a few weeks? The answer is that it grows beyond the wound into normal skin. A hypertrophic scar stays within the wound and often settles with time.

Question 9765. A constructed illustration: a burn is red, blistered and painful, and it blanches when it is pressed. Which depth of burn is this, and which layer has been injured? The answer is a superficial partial thickness burn, with injury reaching into the upper part of the dermis while the deeper layers and the skin appendages are spared.

Question 9766. Which described burn is white and leathery, with no blisters, a dry surface, and no pain at all: a superficial burn, a superficial partial thickness burn, a deep partial thickness burn, or a full thickness burn? The answer is a full thickness burn, in which every layer of the skin has been destroyed.

Question 9767. Which of these names the zone at the center of a burn, where the tissue is destroyed beyond recovery: the zone of hyperemia, the zone of stasis, the zone of coagulation, or the zone of repair? The answer is the zone of coagulation. Outside it lies the zone of stasis, which may yet survive, and outside that the red zone of hyperemia.

Question 9768. A described illustration of a burn shows a pale dead center, a ring around it that is sluggish and mottled, and an outer rim that is red and warm. What are those three zones called, named from the center outward? The answer is the zone of coagulation, the zone of stasis, and the zone of hyperemia.

Question 9769. A described dissection of the scalp shows a wound that has opened widely, with blood and pus spreading far beyond its edges over the vault of the skull. Which layer of the scalp is that, and why does material spread so freely there? The answer is the loose areolar layer between the galea and the pericranium, which contains little to hold the two together.

Question 9770. A constructed illustration: a narrow wound enters the chest wall just below the lower border of a rib. Which structure lies in the groove of that rib and is likely divided, and what lies immediately deep to the ribs? The answer is the intercostal nerve and vessels, with the pleura and the lung immediately deep to the ribs.

Question 9771. Where in an intercostal space do the intercostal nerve, artery and vein lie, and in what order do they lie from above downward? The answer is in the groove along the lower border of the rib above, in the order of vein, artery and nerve from above downward.

Question 9772. Which statement about the diaphragm is correct: its dome rises as high as the fourth intercostal space in the midclavicular line, it lies level with the first lumbar vertebra at every point, it stays below the twelfth rib, or it lies only on the left side? The answer is that its dome rises as high as the fourth intercostal space in the midclavicular line, so a wound below the nipple may still enter the abdomen.

Question 9773. Which avascular fibrous band lies in the midline of the anterior abdominal wall between the two rectus sheaths, and what lies immediately deep to it? The answer is the linea alba, and deep to it lie the peritoneum and then the abdominal cavity.

Question 9774. A described cross section of the anterior abdominal wall shows, from outside inward, skin, fat, three flat muscles with their fibrous sheets, a thin membrane, and a smooth serous layer. Which structure lies between the muscle sheets and the serous layer? The answer is the transversalis fascia, with the peritoneum deepest of all.

Question 9775. A described wound in the upper outer quadrant of the buttock passes deeply between the gluteus maximus and the bone. Which two structures lie at greatest risk: the femoral nerve and artery, the sciatic nerve and the inferior gluteal artery, the obturator nerve, or the great saphenous vein? The answer is the sciatic nerve and the inferior gluteal artery, which leave the pelvis below the piriformis muscle.

Question 9776. In the femoral triangle, in what order do the femoral nerve, artery and vein lie from lateral to medial, and which muscle forms the lateral border of the triangle? The answer is nerve, artery and vein from lateral to medial, with the medial border of the sartorius forming the lateral side.

Question 9777. In the popliteal fossa, which structure lies deepest, against the bone and the capsule of the knee: the popliteal artery, the popliteal vein, the tibial nerve, or the common peroneal nerve? The answer is the popliteal artery. The vein lies superficial to it, and the tibial nerve most superficial of the three, just under the deep fascia.

Question 9778. Which artery crosses the floor of the hollow at the base of the thumb, where it can be torn by a wound in that hollow or by a break of the bone beneath it? The answer is the radial artery, which passes between the tendons of the abductor pollicis longus and the extensor pollicis longus.

Question 9779. A constructed illustration: a deep wound crosses the middle of the palm and the little finger cannot be bent. Which tendon sheath has most likely been opened, and with which space does that sheath communicate? The answer is the sheath of the little finger, which communicates with the ulnar bursa in the palm.

Question 9780. Which of these is a closed space of the palm bounded by the palmar aponeurosis in front and the metacarpal bones behind, through which pus from an infected tendon may spread: the thenar space, the midpalmar space, the carpal tunnel, or the cubital fossa? The answer is the midpalmar space. The thenar space lies along the outer side of the palm.

Question 9781. A described dissection of the palm shows a narrow track passing from a small skin wound down to lie beneath the thick fibrous sheet in the middle of the palm, with tendon sheaths on either side of the track. Beneath which sheet has the track passed? The answer is the palmar aponeurosis.

Question 9782. Name the three nerves whose function should be tested in any wound of the hand, spoken as a list. The answer is the median nerve, the ulnar nerve, and the radial nerve. The median nerve supplies the muscles that oppose the thumb, the ulnar nerve the small muscles of the hand, and the radial nerve the skin of the back of the hand.

Question 9783. Which nerve supplies the muscles of the ball of the thumb that draw the thumb across the palm, and through which tunnel does it pass at the wrist? The answer is the median nerve, which passes through the carpal tunnel beneath the flexor retinaculum.

Question 9784. At the wrist, does the ulnar nerve lie medial or lateral to the ulnar artery, and through which canal do the two pass? The answer is that the nerve lies medial to the artery, and both pass through the canal at the wrist beside the pisiform bone.

Question 9785. Which statement about the synovial sheaths of the flexor tendons of the fingers is correct: every finger has its own separate sheath, the sheath of the little finger communicates with the ulnar bursa, the sheath of the index finger communicates with the radial bursa, or the sheaths end at the wrist? The answer is that the sheath of the little finger communicates with the ulnar bursa, which is why infection there can spread into the palm.

Question 9786. Which of these pairs belongs together: the median nerve with the muscles that spread the fingers apart, the ulnar nerve with the muscles of the ball of the thumb, the radial nerve with the skin of the back of the hand, or the axillary nerve with the skin of the little finger? The answer is the radial nerve with the skin of the back of the hand. The ulnar nerve supplies the muscles that spread the fingers.

Question 9787. A deep wound in the armpit opens the space between the chest wall and the arm. Which large vessel lies there, and how are the cords of the brachial plexus arranged around it? The answer is the axillary artery, with the cords named by their position, lateral, medial and posterior to it.

Question 9788. A described longitudinal section of a finger shows a glistening cord running to the base of the last bone, wrapped in a thin sleeve of synovial membrane and held against the bone by a series of fibrous bands. Which structures are shown? The answer is the flexor tendon, its synovial sheath, and the fibrous pulleys that hold the tendon in place.

Question 9789. True or false: skin that has healed after a deep wound contains no hair follicles and no sweat glands, because those structures were destroyed and cannot grow again. The answer is true. New epidermis spreads from the wound margins, but it cannot rebuild the appendages that lay in the dermis.

Question 9790. True or false: the epidermis contains no blood vessels of its own and is nourished by fluid that diffuses from the dermis beneath it. The answer is true. That is why a very superficial cut does not bleed, while a cut that reaches the dermis does.

Question 9791. Which statement about the strength of a healed wound is correct: it regains its original strength within a week, it reaches its greatest strength at about three weeks, it never quite regains the strength of uninjured skin, or it becomes stronger than bone? The answer is that it never quite regains the strength of uninjured skin, although it settles at about 80 per cent of it.

Question 9792. Which of these pairs belongs together: a wound of the popliteal fossa with the facial nerve, a wound of the posterior triangle of the neck with the phrenic nerve, a wound of the anterior triangle of the neck with the accessory nerve, or a wound of the root of the neck with the brachial plexus? The answer is a wound of the root of the neck with the brachial plexus, which emerges between the scalene muscles there.

Question 9793. A wound on the medial side of the knee divides a vein running just beneath the skin. Which vein is that, and which deeper vessel lies behind it in the canal above the knee? The answer is the great saphenous vein, with the femoral artery lying deep in the adductor canal.

Question 9794. Name two structures that lie at risk in a wound that crosses the popliteal fossa, spoken as a list. The answer is the popliteal artery and the common peroneal nerve. The artery lies deepest against the bone, and the nerve runs along the inner edge of the biceps tendon before winding around the neck of the fibula.

Question 9795. A described dissection of the side of the neck shows a wound in the triangle behind the sternocleidomastoid muscle, and afterward the shoulder on that side droops. Which nerve has been divided, and which muscle has lost its supply? The answer is the accessory nerve, and the trapezius has lost its supply.

Question 9796. A constructed illustration: a wound crosses the front of the neck at the level of the larynx, between the hyoid bone above and the cricoid cartilage below. Which sheath lies in that zone, and which structures does it contain? The answer is the carotid sheath, containing the common carotid artery, the internal jugular vein and the vagus nerve.

Question 9797. Which statement about wounds of the neck is correct: a wound above the angle of the mandible may reach the base of the skull, a wound between the angle of the mandible and the cricoid cartilage spares the great vessels, a wound below the cricoid cartilage cannot reach the chest, or every wound of the neck spares the airway? The answer is that a wound above the angle of the mandible may reach the base of the skull, where the internal carotid artery and the internal jugular vein pass upward.

Question 9798. A described wound lies over the head of a metacarpal bone, and the person says that it was made by a tooth. Which structures may lie in the depth of that small wound, and why is its true depth often not seen at first? The answer is the extensor tendon and the joint beneath it. With the fist clenched the tendon and the joint lay in the wound, and they drew back when the hand opened.

Question 9799. Which of these pairs belongs together: an abrasion with a sharp blade, a laceration with a blow from a blunt object, a puncture with a fall onto rough ground, or an incised wound with a bite? The answer is a laceration with a blow from a blunt object, which tears the skin against the bone beneath and leaves ragged, bruised margins.

Question 9800. Which tough fibrous band stretches across the front of the wrist, attached to the pisiform bone and the hook of the hamate on one side and to the tubercle of the scaphoid and the trapezium on the other, and which nerve passes beneath it? The answer is the flexor retinaculum, with the median nerve and the flexor tendons passing beneath it.

To summarize, a fracture is described by the bone, the level and the pattern of the break, and the named patterns such as the Colles, Monteggia, Galeazzi, Bennett, Jones, Jefferson and Chance injuries each fix a particular bone and a particular displacement. The blood supply of the scaphoid, the head of the femur and the body of the talus enters each bone from one end and runs backward, so a break across the neck endangers the part beyond it. A dislocation carries its named neighbors with it: the axillary nerve at the shoulder, the sciatic nerve at the hip, the brachial artery in front of the elbow, and the popliteal artery and common peroneal nerve behind the knee. A wound divides whatever lies along its track, and the track is read from the surface inward through the epidermis, the dermis, the fat, the fascia and the muscle, with the intercostal bundle in the groove of the rib above the space, the ulnar nerve medial to the ulnar artery at the wrist, and the femoral nerve lateral to the femoral artery below the groin. Healing passes through inflammation, granulation tissue, collagen of type three replaced by type one, and contraction by myofibroblasts, and the scar that remains has neither hair follicles nor sweat glands.
