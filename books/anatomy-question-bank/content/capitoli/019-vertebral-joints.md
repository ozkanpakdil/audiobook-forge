# Chapter 19 — Vertebral Joints

This chapter takes the joints of the skull and the joints of the vertebral column together, because the two are built on different plans. It begins with the sutures of the vault and the fontanelles of the newborn, then the cartilaginous joints of the skull base and the one synovial joint of the head. It goes on to the joints between the vertebrae, from the discs and the facet joints to the ligaments that steady them. The chapter holds one hundred questions, and each one gives its answer immediately and then explains why, so nothing has to be held in mind. This is educational material, and it supports rather than replaces an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 1801. Which suture runs down the middle of the skull vault between the two parietal bones? The answer is the sagittal suture. It extends from the bregma in front to the lambda behind, and its interlocking edges make it a serrate suture. Its fibers are continuous with the pericranium outside and the dura mater within.

Question 1802. Which suture separates the frontal bone from the two parietal bones: the sagittal suture, the coronal suture, the lambdoid suture, or the squamous suture? The answer is the coronal suture. It crosses the vault from temple to temple, and behind it lies the pair of parietal bones. The sagittal suture lies farther back, in the midline.

Question 1803. Name the suture that separates the occipital bone from the two parietal bones at the back of the skull. The answer is the lambdoid suture. It slopes downward and forward on each side from the lambda, and its shape suggests the Greek letter lambda. The occipitomastoid suture continues the same line toward the base.

Question 1804. Which suture separates the parietal bone above from the squamous part of the temporal bone below? The answer is the squamous suture. Its edges are beveled, so the parietal bone overlaps the temporal bone rather like a roof tile. It lies above the ear on the side of the vault.

Question 1805. The metopic suture divides which bone into two halves in the newborn: the temporal bone, the frontal bone, the occipital bone, or the parietal bone? The answer is the frontal bone. The suture runs down the middle of the forehead and usually closes during the first few years of life. A trace of it in an adult is a normal variant.

Question 1806. Which of these pairs belongs together: the coronal suture with the frontal and the parietal bones, the sagittal suture with the occipital and the temporal bones, the lambdoid suture with the two temporal bones, or the squamous suture with the two parietal bones? The answer is the coronal suture with the frontal and the parietal bones. The sagittal suture joins the two parietal bones to each other.

Question 1807. What is the name of the point on top of the skull where the coronal suture meets the sagittal suture? The answer is the bregma. In the infant the same spot is felt as the anterior fontanelle, the soft diamond-shaped gap between the bones. It lies in the midline toward the front of the vault.

Question 1808. Which point marks the meeting of the sagittal suture with the lambdoid suture at the back of the vault? The answer is the lambda. It lies in the midline behind the bregma and is shaped like an inverted Y. In the newborn the same spot holds the small posterior fontanelle.

Question 1809. A described specimen of a newborn skull shows a diamond-shaped membrane-covered gap where the coronal and the sagittal sutures meet, with the frontal and the parietal bones around it. Which structure is described, and when does it usually close? The answer is the anterior fontanelle, which closes during the second year of life. It is the largest of the fontanelles.

Question 1810. Which fontanelle lies at the meeting of the sagittal and the lambdoid sutures: the anterior fontanelle, the posterior fontanelle, the sphenoidal fontanelle, or the mastoid fontanelle? The answer is the posterior fontanelle. It is triangular, much smaller than the anterior one, and it closes within the first few months after birth. The mastoid and sphenoidal fontanelles lie lower on the side of the skull.

Question 1811. True or false: the anterior fontanelle stays open longer than the posterior fontanelle. The answer is true. The posterior fontanelle closes within the first few months of life, while the anterior one is still open at the end of the first year. The anterior fontanelle is also the larger of the two.

Question 1812. What is the name of the H-shaped junction on the side of the skull where the frontal, the parietal, the temporal and the sphenoid bones come together? The answer is the pterion. It lies a little above and in front of the ear, in a thin part of the vault. It is the weakest point of the skull and an important surgical landmark.

Question 1813. Name the four bones that meet at the pterion, spoken as a list. The answer is the frontal bone, the parietal bone, the temporal bone, and the greater wing of the sphenoid. The anterior branch of the middle meningeal artery grooves the inner surface deep to this point, so a blow here is dangerous. The junction varies in shape from one skull to another.

Question 1814. Which artery lies immediately deep to the pterion, grooving the inner surface of the skull? The answer is the middle meningeal artery. It enters the skull through the foramen spinosum and divides into an anterior and a posterior branch. Its anterior branch runs upward and backward beneath the pterion.

Question 1815. A constructed illustration: a blow to the temple is followed by a brief recovery and then a deepening drowsiness, and imaging shows a lens-shaped collection between the skull and the dura mater. Which vessel has most likely been torn? The answer is the middle meningeal artery, torn where it crosses deep to the pterion. Blood then collects outside the dura mater, and the management belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 1816. A described dried skull shows a star-shaped point at the back of the side wall where the parietal, the occipital and the mastoid part of the temporal bone meet. Which landmark is described? The answer is the asterion. It lies behind and below the mastoid process and marks the site of the mastoid fontanelle in the newborn. The transverse and sigmoid venous sinuses meet deep to it.

Question 1817. Which venous channel lies immediately deep to the asterion, at the junction of the transverse and the sigmoid sinuses? The answer is the sigmoid sinus where it becomes the transverse sinus. The point is marked on the outside of the skull by the asterion. A surgeon placing a burr hole at this landmark must know the vein beneath it.

Question 1818. Which kind of suture has edges that interlock like the teeth of a saw: a squamous suture, a serrate suture, a plane suture, or a schindylesis? The answer is a serrate suture. The coronal, the sagittal and the lambdoid sutures are serrate, and their interlocking edges resist pulling forces. A squamous suture is beveled instead, and a plane suture has smooth edges.

Question 1819. What is the name of the joint in which a thin plate of one bone fits into a groove in another, as the rostrum of the sphenoid does with the vomer: a gomphosis, a syndesmosis, a schindylesis, or a synchondrosis? The answer is a schindylesis, a fibrous joint of the skull base. A gomphosis is the peg-in-socket joint of a tooth.

Question 1820. Which cartilaginous joint joins the body of the sphenoid bone to the basilar part of the occipital bone? The answer is the spheno-occipital synchondrosis. It lies in the midline of the skull base and is the chief growth center of the cranial base. It closes around the time of puberty, later than most growth plates of the body.

Question 1821. A described magnetic resonance imaging scan of a child's skull base shows a band of cartilage in the midline between the sphenoid body and the basilar part of the occipital bone. Which joint is shown, and what happens to it? The answer is the spheno-occipital synchondrosis, which ossifies around puberty. Until then it allows growth in length of the skull base.

Question 1822. Which cartilage-filled fissure of the skull base lies between the petrous part of the temporal bone and the occipital bone, and which foramen lies at its posterior end? The answer is the petro-occipital fissure, with the jugular foramen at its posterior end. The fissure remains as a synchondrosis in the adult. The carotid canal opens in front of it.

Question 1823. The jugular foramen lies at the posterior end of which fissure of the skull base? The answer is the petro-occipital fissure. The fissure runs between the petrous temporal bone and the occipital bone and is filled with cartilage. The jugular foramen at its end carries the internal jugular vein and several cranial nerves.

Question 1824. True or false: the temporomandibular joint is the only synovial joint in the skull. The answer is true. Every other joint of the skull is fibrous or cartilaginous, and none of them has a joint cavity. The jaw joint alone has a cavity, a synovial membrane and synovial fluid.

Question 1825. The temporomandibular joint is best described as which kind of joint: a ball and socket joint, a saddle joint, a synovial joint of the modified hinge type, or a fibrous joint? The answer is a synovial joint of the modified hinge type. It is a hinge because the bones move on each other in one plane, and modified because it also glides forward.

Question 1826. Which two bones form the temporomandibular joint? The answer is the temporal bone and the mandible. The head of the mandible meets the mandibular fossa and the articular tubercle of the temporal bone. The joint lies just in front of the external acoustic meatus.

Question 1827. A described specimen shows a joint of the head opened from the side, with a fibrocartilaginous disc dividing its cavity into an upper and a lower compartment. Which joint is shown? The answer is the temporomandibular joint. The disc is the only fibrocartilaginous disc in the head, and it travels forward with the head of the mandible.

Question 1828. In which compartment of the temporomandibular joint does the gliding movement take place: the upper compartment, the lower compartment, both compartments equally, or neither compartment? The answer is the upper compartment, between the disc and the temporal bone. The lower compartment, between the disc and the head of the mandible, allows rotation. The two movements together open the mouth.

Question 1829. Which muscle attaches to the articular disc of the temporomandibular joint and to the neck of the mandible? The answer is the lateral pterygoid muscle. It pulls the disc and the head of the mandible forward and is the chief muscle of protrusion. Because of it, the disc travels with the bone when the mouth opens.

Question 1830. A constructed illustration: after a wide yawn the jaw stays open and cannot be closed, and the chin is pushed forward. Where has the head of the mandible moved? The answer is anterior to the articular tubercle of the temporal bone. Both heads are pulled forward by the lateral pterygoid muscles and slip over the summit of the tubercle.

Question 1831. Besides the lateral ligament that thickens its capsule, which two ligaments are associated with the temporomandibular joint: the sphenomandibular and the stylomandibular, the alar and the apical, the anterior and the posterior longitudinal, or the transverse and the cruciform? The answer is the sphenomandibular and the stylomandibular ligaments. Neither strengthens the capsule, and the sphenomandibular ligament is the pivot about which the mandible swings.

Question 1832. A constructed illustration: a fracture crosses the neck of the mandible, and the fragment carrying the head is drawn forward and medially. Which muscle is responsible for that displacement? The answer is the lateral pterygoid muscle. It attaches to the neck of the mandible and to the disc and pulls both forward and medially. The rest of the mandible is pulled upward by the muscles of closure.

Question 1833. When the mandible is thrust forward in protrusion, in which plane does the head of the mandible move? The answer is the sagittal plane. Both heads glide forward and downward on the articular tubercles in that plane. Protrusion is therefore a movement in one plane, and it is checked by the posterior fibers of the temporalis muscle.

Question 1834. Which of these pairs belongs together: the sagittal suture with a fibrous joint, the spheno-occipital junction with a synovial joint, the temporomandibular joint with a cartilaginous joint, or a tooth in its socket with a synovial joint? The answer is the sagittal suture with a fibrous joint. The spheno-occipital junction is cartilaginous, the temporomandibular joint is synovial, and a tooth in its socket is fixed by a fibrous gomphosis.

Question 1835. Which statement about the sutures of the growing skull is correct: they are the main sites of growth in the vault, they fuse before birth, they prevent all movement, or they are synovial joints? The answer is that they are the main sites of growth in the vault, where bone is added at the sutural edges as the brain enlarges. A suture that fuses too early therefore stops growth in that direction.

Question 1836. A described radiograph of a young child's skull shows a raised ridge crossing the vault above one ear, with flattening of the forehead on that same side. Which suture has fused too early, and what has the fusion prevented? The answer is the coronal suture on that side, whose early fusion has stopped growth of the vault from front to back. The appearance is an illustration.

Question 1837. The atlanto-occipital joint is formed between the occipital condyles and which surfaces of the atlas: the superior articular facets, the inferior articular facets, the anterior arch, or the posterior arch? The answer is the superior articular facets. They are concave and receive the convex condyles of the occipital bone. The joint allows the head to nod.

Question 1838. Which statement about the atlanto-occipital joint is correct: it is a synovial ellipsoid joint and allows mainly nodding, it is a pivot joint and allows rotation, it is a fibrous joint, or it is a cartilaginous joint? The answer is that it is a synovial ellipsoid joint and allows mainly nodding. Flexion and extension of the head happen here, while most rotation happens at the joints below.

Question 1839. A described magnetic resonance imaging scan of the upper neck, in a constructed illustration, shows the dens of the axis displaced backward against the spinal cord, with a torn band that normally holds it against the anterior arch of the atlas. Which joint has been disrupted? The answer is the median atlantoaxial joint. The band is the transverse ligament of the atlas, whose rupture lets the dens move backward into the canal.

Question 1840. Which class of joint unites two vertebral bodies, with a pad of fibrocartilage between them? The answer is a secondary cartilaginous joint, or symphysis. The two bone surfaces are covered by thin plates of hyaline cartilage, and the disc between them is fibrocartilage. The joint allows slight movement in every direction.

Question 1841. What structure lies between the bodies of two adjacent vertebrae: a synovial cavity, an intervertebral disc, a bursa, or a layer of elastic cartilage? The answer is an intervertebral disc. It is built of an outer ring of fibrous tissue and a soft inner core. It cushions the bodies and allows the column to bend.

Question 1842. A described cross section through an intervertebral disc shows concentric rings of tough fibrous tissue around a soft, glistening center. What is the name of the outer ring? The answer is the annulus fibrosus. Its fibers cross from one lamella to the next and run obliquely between the vertebral bodies. The soft center within it is the nucleus pulposus.

Question 1843. What is the name of the soft, gel-like core of the intervertebral disc? The answer is the nucleus pulposus. It lies a little behind the center of the disc, nearer the back than the front. It is rich in water and in substances that hold water, and it acts as a cushion.

Question 1844. Which part of the annulus fibrosus is thinnest and therefore most likely to give way: the anterior part, the posterior and posterolateral part, the upper part, or the lower part? The answer is the posterior and posterolateral part. The ring is thickest in front and thinnest behind and behind to the side. That is why a disc usually herniates backward and to one side.

Question 1845. A constructed illustration: a person has pain shooting down one leg, and imaging shows disc material protruding backward and to the side into an intervertebral foramen. Which structure is being compressed? The answer is the spinal nerve as it leaves the canal through the foramen. A posterolateral protrusion meets the nerve where the foramen holds it.

Question 1846. A described sagittal magnetic resonance imaging scan of the lower back shows a disc bulging backward and indenting the sac of the meninges. Which structure has moved backward, and from where has it come? The answer is part of the disc, pushed out of its place between two vertebral bodies. The bulge is usually made of nucleus pulposus that has escaped through a tear in the annulus.

Question 1847. The posterior longitudinal ligament lies in which position relative to the intervertebral discs? The answer is immediately behind them, within the vertebral canal. It runs down the back of the vertebral bodies from the axis to the sacrum. Because of it, the discs lie between that ligament and the front of the canal.

Question 1848. Which ligament is narrow and weakest at its sides, so that it fails to check a disc that herniates backward and to one side: the anterior longitudinal ligament, the posterior longitudinal ligament, or the ligamentum flavum? The answer is the posterior longitudinal ligament. It is broad over the middle of each disc and narrow at its edges, so a posterolateral disc escapes beside it.

Question 1849. Which long ligament runs down the front of the vertebral bodies from the base of the skull to the sacrum? The answer is the anterior longitudinal ligament. It is broad and firmly attached to the front of each body and to each disc. It checks extension of the vertebral column.

Question 1850. Which ligament limits hyperextension of the vertebral column: the anterior longitudinal ligament, the posterior longitudinal ligament, the supraspinous ligament, or the ligamentum flavum? The answer is the anterior longitudinal ligament. It lies in front of the bodies, so throwing the head and trunk backward stretches it. The ligaments behind the canal check flexion instead.

Question 1851. The ligamentum flavum connects which parts of two adjacent vertebrae? The answer is their laminae. It runs from the lower edge of one lamina to the upper edge of the one below, on each side of the midline. It is yellow and elastic, and it forms part of the back wall of the vertebral canal.

Question 1852. Why is the elasticity of the ligamentum flavum important when the vertebral column bends forward: it pulls the vertebral bodies together, it helps the column straighten again, it prevents rotation, or it carries the spinal nerves? The answer is that it helps the column straighten again and keeps the elastic ligament from folding into the canal. It stores energy as it is stretched.

Question 1853. Which of these pairs belongs together: the anterior longitudinal ligament with limiting extension, the ligamentum flavum with connecting the spinous processes, the supraspinous ligament with connecting the vertebral bodies, or the posterior longitudinal ligament with limiting rotation? The answer is the anterior longitudinal ligament with limiting extension. The ligamentum flavum joins the laminae, and the supraspinous ligament joins the tips of the spinous processes.

Question 1854. Which structures form the boundaries of an intervertebral foramen? The answer is the bodies and the disc in front, the facet joints behind, and the pedicles of the two adjacent vertebrae above and below. The spinal nerve and its accompanying vessels pass through the opening. Narrowing of the foramen therefore traps the nerve.

Question 1855. Bending forward and backward at the waist happens in which plane and about which axis? The answer is the sagittal plane, about a transverse axis. Flexion carries the trunk forward and extension carries it back in that same plane. Lateral flexion, by contrast, happens in the coronal plane.

Question 1856. Turning the head to look over one shoulder happens about which axis: a transverse axis, an anteroposterior axis, a longitudinal axis, or an oblique axis? The answer is a longitudinal axis. The movement is rotation, and most of it happens between the atlas and the axis. It takes place in the transverse plane.

Question 1857. What kind of joint is a zygapophyseal joint, the joint between the superior and the inferior articular processes of two adjacent vertebrae? The answer is a synovial plane joint. It has a cavity, a synovial membrane and a capsule that is richly supplied with nerves. Its surfaces are flat or gently curved.

Question 1858. Which orientation do the articular facets of the cervical vertebrae have, and what does it allow: about 45 degrees to the horizontal, allowing free flexion, extension and rotation; vertical, allowing only rotation; sagittal, allowing only flexion; or horizontal, allowing no movement? The answer is about 45 degrees to the horizontal, allowing free flexion, extension and rotation. The thoracic facets lie vertically and favor rotation instead.

Question 1859. A described axial section through a lumbar vertebra shows the two facet joints with their surfaces turned almost directly forward and backward, in the sagittal plane. Which movement does this arrangement limit? The answer is rotation. It allows free flexion and extension, but turning the trunk about a vertical axis is checked by the bone.

Question 1860. A described axial section through a cervical vertebra shows a small joint at the side of the body, between a raised lip on the upper surface and the sloping lower surface of the vertebra above. Which joint is shown? The answer is an uncovertebral joint, also called a joint of Luschka. It is found only in the cervical region.

Question 1861. Between which structures do the uncovertebral joints lie, and in which vertebrae are they found? The answer is between the uncinate process of one cervical vertebra and the lower surface of the body above, from the third to the seventh cervical vertebra. They are small joints at the side of the disc. They appear during childhood and are prominent in adult life.

Question 1862. In which region of the vertebral column are uncovertebral joints found: the cervical region, the thoracic region, the lumbar region, or the sacrum? The answer is the cervical region. They lie at the sides of the bodies from the third to the seventh cervical vertebra. With age their edges grow into bony spurs that can narrow the intervertebral foramen.

Question 1863. A constructed illustration: an older person has neck pain and tingling in one arm, and imaging shows bony spurs at the side of the cervical bodies narrowing an intervertebral foramen. Which joints have produced the spurs, and what do the spurs compress? The answer is the uncovertebral joints, whose spurs press on the spinal nerve. The management belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 1864. Of the three joints that make up the atlantoaxial complex, which one is the pivot about which the head turns? The answer is the median atlantoaxial joint, between the dens of the axis and the ring formed by the anterior arch of the atlas and the transverse ligament. The two lateral joints between the articular facets glide at the same time.

Question 1865. Name the three joints that together make up the atlantoaxial complex, spoken as a list. The answer is the median atlantoaxial joint and the two lateral atlantoaxial joints. The median joint lies between the dens and the ring of the atlas, and the lateral joints lie between the articular facets on each side. Together they allow the head to turn from side to side.

Question 1866. What is the name of the joint between the occipital condyles and the first cervical vertebra? The answer is the atlanto-occipital joint. There is no intervertebral disc between the skull and the atlas. The joint is synovial and permits the nodding of the head.

Question 1867. The atlanto-occipital joint is which kind of joint: a fibrous joint, a secondary cartilaginous joint, a synovial joint, or a primary cartilaginous joint? The answer is a synovial joint. Each condyle has its own cavity and capsule, and the pair acts as one joint. The movements are flexion and extension of the head, with a little lateral bending.

Question 1868. True or false: there is no intervertebral disc between the atlas and the axis. The answer is true. The two vertebrae are joined instead by the median atlantoaxial joint and the two lateral joints, with their ligaments. A disc appears only below the axis, between the second and the third cervical vertebrae.

Question 1869. Which ligament holds the dens of the axis against the anterior arch of the atlas? The answer is the transverse ligament of the atlas. It stretches across the ring of the atlas behind the dens. If it ruptures, the dens can move backward and press on the spinal cord.

Question 1870. Which ligaments check rotation of the head at the atlantoaxial joints: the alar ligaments, from the dens to the occipital condyles; the anterior longitudinal ligament; the ligamentum flavum; or the supraspinous ligament? The answer is the alar ligaments, from the dens to the occipital condyles. They are taut when the head is turned and stop the movement.

Question 1871. The tectorial membrane is the upward continuation of which ligament? The answer is the posterior longitudinal ligament. It leaves the vertebral canal at the second cervical vertebra and runs up over the dens into the skull. It forms part of the back wall of the space that carries the spinal cord.

Question 1872. A described sagittal magnetic resonance imaging scan of the upper neck shows a broad band lying behind the dens and in front of the spinal cord, continuing upward from the back of the vertebral bodies. Which structure is shown? The answer is the tectorial membrane. It is the continuation of the posterior longitudinal ligament and covers the alar and transverse ligaments.

Question 1873. A constructed illustration: a person whose transverse ligament of the atlas is lax bends the neck forward, and the dens moves backward against the spinal cord. Which joint is unstable, and which ligament has failed? The answer is the median atlantoaxial joint, with failure of the transverse ligament, so the dens is free to press on the cord. The management belongs to the treating clinician.

Question 1874. A constructed illustration: disease has fused the joints of the upper two cervical vertebrae on both sides. Which movement of the head is most reduced? The answer is rotation, the turning of the head from side to side. Most of that movement happens at the atlantoaxial joints, and it is lost when they fuse. Nodding at the atlanto-occipital joint is preserved.

Question 1875. Which ligament connects the tips of the spinous processes from the seventh cervical vertebra down to the sacrum? The answer is the supraspinous ligament. It is a strong cord felt in the midline of the back. In the neck its place is taken by the nuchal ligament.

Question 1876. What is the name of the strong elastic band in the midline of the back of the neck, running from the external occipital protuberance to the seventh cervical vertebra? The answer is the nuchal ligament. It is the continuation of the supraspinous ligament in the neck. It gives attachment to muscles and checks excessive flexion of the head.

Question 1877. Above which vertebra is the supraspinous ligament absent: the first cervical, the seventh cervical, the first thoracic, or the twelfth thoracic? The answer is the seventh cervical. Above it the ligament is replaced by the nuchal ligament, which is thicker and elastic. The supraspinous ligament runs from the seventh cervical vertebra to the sacrum.

Question 1878. A described lateral radiograph of the neck, taken with the head bent forward, shows a fan-shaped widening of the gaps between the spinous processes. Which ligaments have been stretched? The answer is the interspinous and the supraspinous ligaments. They lie between and behind the spinous processes. The ligamentum flavum behind the canal is stretched as well.

Question 1879. Which of these pairs belongs together: the intervertebral joint with a secondary cartilaginous joint, the zygapophyseal joint with a fibrous joint, the costovertebral joint with a primary cartilaginous joint, or the sacroiliac joint with a fibrous joint? The answer is the intervertebral joint with a secondary cartilaginous joint. The zygapophyseal, the costovertebral and the sacroiliac joints are all synovial joints with capsules and synovial fluid.

Question 1880. The head of a typical rib articulates with which structures? The answer is the bodies of two adjacent vertebrae and the disc between them. The head carries two facets, one for each body, and a ridge between them that meets the disc. The joint is synovial and lies at the back of the chest.

Question 1881. Which ribs articulate with the body of only one vertebra: the first, the eleventh and the twelfth; the second, the third and the fourth; the fifth, the sixth and the seventh; or the eighth, the ninth and the tenth? The answer is the first, the eleventh and the twelfth. Each of these has a single facet on its head, while all the others meet two vertebrae and the disc between them.

Question 1882. What is the name of the joint between the tubercle of a rib and the transverse process of a vertebra? The answer is the costotransverse joint. It is a small synovial joint with a capsule. It is absent on the eleventh and the twelfth ribs, whose tubercles carry no articular surface.

Question 1883. The first costal cartilage joins the manubrium by which kind of joint: a synovial joint, a cartilaginous joint with no cavity, a fibrous joint, or a saddle joint? The answer is a cartilaginous joint with no cavity, a synchondrosis. The second to the seventh costal cartilages form synovial joints with the sternum instead. The first rib is fixed and needs no cavity.

Question 1884. A described section through a vertebral motion segment shows a small joint behind the vertebral body, between the superior articular process of one vertebra and the inferior process of the next, with a capsule and a smooth lining. Which joint is shown? The answer is a zygapophyseal joint, or facet joint. It is a synovial plane joint.

Question 1885. The lumbosacral joint lies between which two bones, and which ligament steadies it from the side? The answer is the fifth lumbar vertebra and the first piece of the sacrum, steadied by the iliolumbar ligament. The ligament runs from the transverse process of the fifth lumbar vertebra to the iliac crest. It resists forward slipping of that vertebra.

Question 1886. The iliolumbar ligament attaches to which two bony points? The answer is the transverse process of the fifth lumbar vertebra and the iliac crest, with a band to the ilium behind it. It anchors the lowest lumbar vertebra to the pelvis. It resists forward slipping of the fifth lumbar vertebra on the sacrum.

Question 1887. Which statement about the sacroiliac joint is correct: it is a synovial joint with fibrocartilage-covered surfaces and very slight movement, it is a fibrous joint with no cavity, it is a ball and socket joint, or it is a cartilaginous joint that fuses after birth? The answer is that it is a synovial joint with fibrocartilage-covered surfaces and very slight movement. Its irregular surfaces and strong ligaments allow only a small rocking.

Question 1888. True or false: the sacroiliac joint allows a wide range of movement between the hip bone and the sacrum. The answer is false. The joint is held by very strong ligaments, and its surfaces are irregular and interlocking. Only a few degrees of movement are possible, and that movement is called nutation and counternutation.

Question 1889. Which of these pairs belongs together: the cervical region with facets at about 45 degrees, the thoracic region with sagittal facets, the lumbar region with coronal facets, or the sacrum with horizontal facets? The answer is the cervical region with facets at about 45 degrees. The thoracic facets lie in the coronal plane and favor rotation, and the lumbar facets lie in the sagittal plane.

Question 1890. Name two structures that pass through an intervertebral foramen, spoken as a list. The answer is the spinal nerve, with its sheath, and the small arteries and veins that accompany it. The foramen also carries a small recurrent branch that returns to the canal. It lies between the pedicles of two adjacent vertebrae.

Question 1891. A constructed illustration: a young athlete has low back pain, and imaging shows a defect in the part of bone between the superior and the inferior articular processes of the fifth lumbar vertebra. Which joint has been affected? The answer is the zygapophyseal joint on that side, whose two processes are separated by the break in the pars interarticularis.

Question 1892. In a forward slip of the fifth lumbar vertebra on the sacrum, which structures give way first: the intervertebral disc and the facet joints, the sacroiliac ligaments, the hip joint, or the iliolumbar ligament? The answer is the intervertebral disc and the facet joints. The disc loses height and the facets open, so the body of the fifth lumbar vertebra slides forward on the first sacral vertebra.

Question 1893. A described lateral radiograph of the lower back shows a step in the posterior border of the vertebral bodies at the junction of the lowest lumbar vertebra and the sacrum. What has happened? The answer is a forward slip of the fifth lumbar vertebra on the sacrum. The posterior borders of the two bones no longer form a smooth line.

Question 1894. A constructed illustration: a person bends to the side, and the facet joints on the side toward which the trunk bends are pressed together while those on the other side are stretched. Which joints are involved, and in which plane does the movement occur? The answer is the zygapophyseal joints, in the coronal plane. Lateral flexion is usually accompanied by a little rotation.

Question 1895. How much of the turning of the head from side to side takes place at the atlantoaxial joints: about one half, about one quarter, almost none, or all of it? The answer is about one half. The rest is shared by the remaining cervical joints, which also contribute to rotation. Fusion of the atlantoaxial joints therefore removes half of the movement.

Question 1896. A constructed illustration: a person in a stationary car is struck from behind, and the head is thrown first backward and then forward. Which ligament is most stretched in the first phase? The answer is the anterior longitudinal ligament, which runs in front of the vertebral bodies and resists extension. The ligaments behind the canal are stretched in the second phase, when the head is thrown forward.

Question 1897. The articular facets of the thoracic vertebrae lie in which plane, and which movement do they favor: the coronal plane, favoring rotation; the sagittal plane, favoring flexion; the transverse plane, favoring extension; or an oblique plane, favoring side bending only? The answer is the coronal plane, favoring rotation, because the facets face backward and forward. The region therefore turns easily but bends forward with difficulty.

Question 1898. True or false: the nucleus pulposus has no blood vessels of its own and is nourished by fluid passing through the vertebral bodies. The answer is true. Only the outer layers of the annulus fibrosus receive small vessels. The central part of the disc depends on diffusion through the end plates, so it heals poorly after injury.

Question 1899. A described magnetic resonance imaging scan shows a small dome-shaped hollow pressed into the upper surface of a vertebral body and filled with disc material. What is this appearance called? The answer is a Schmorl node, in which part of the nucleus pulposus has pushed upward through the end plate into the body. It is common and is often found by chance.

Question 1900. Which change in the intervertebral disc is the ordinary result of aging: the water content of the nucleus pulposus falls, it gains water, the annulus fibrosus becomes elastic, or the disc is replaced by bone in every adult? The answer is that the water content of the nucleus pulposus falls and the disc loses height. The annulus then tears and bony spurs follow.

To summarize, the skull is held together almost entirely by fibrous joints: the serrate sutures of the vault, the squamous suture above the ear, and the plane joints of the face, with the bregma, the lambda, the pterion and the asterion marking where they meet. The fontanelles of the newborn close at different times, and the cartilaginous joints of the skull base, above all the spheno-occipital synchondrosis, let the base grow until puberty. The temporomandibular joint is the only synovial joint of the head, its fibrocartilaginous disc divides it into an upper compartment for gliding and a lower one for rotation, and the head of the mandible dislocates in front of the articular tubercle. The vertebral column is joined by the discs between the bodies, by the facet joints behind, and by ligaments whose names record their work: the anterior longitudinal ligament checks extension, the posterior longitudinal ligament is weakest at the sides, and the ligamentum flavum is elastic. The atlanto-occipital joint nods the head and the atlantoaxial joints turn it, while the cervical facets lie at about 45 degrees, the thoracic in the coronal plane and the lumbar in the sagittal plane.
