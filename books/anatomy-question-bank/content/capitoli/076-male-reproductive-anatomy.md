# Chapter 76 — Male Reproductive Anatomy

This chapter examines the male reproductive organs, both those that lie outside the pelvis and those within it: the testis and its coverings, the epididymis, the vas deferens, and the seminal vesicle. It holds one hundred questions, and each one gives its answer immediately after the question and then explains why that answer is right, so nothing has to be held in mind until the end. The material runs from the descent of the gonad to the duct system that carries sperm and the gland that adds to the seminal fluid. It is educational material and supports rather than replaces an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 7501. Deep to the serous sac around the testis lies a tough white fibrous coat that sends septa into the gland. What is it called? The answer is the tunica albuginea. Its posterior thickening forms the mediastinum testis, and its septa divide the gland into lobules. The serous sac outside it is the tunica vaginalis.

Question 7502. At the posterior border of the testis the fibrous coat thickens into a partition that carries the rete testis. What is it called? The answer is the mediastinum testis. The septa of the gland radiate from it, and vessels and the duct system enter and leave at this border.

Question 7503. Which cells of the testis lie in the tissue between the seminiferous tubules and secrete testosterone? The answer is the interstitial cells, also called Leydig cells. They lie close to the capillaries between the tubules and respond to luteinizing hormone. The cells inside the tubules do not make testosterone.

Question 7504. Which cells of the seminiferous tubule form the blood-testis barrier and secrete inhibin? The answer is the sustentacular cells, also called Sertoli cells. They reach from the base of the tubule to its lumen and nurse the developing sperm. Their tight junctions shield the maturing germ cells from the immune system.

Question 7505. About twelve to twenty coiled tubes carry sperm from the rete testis into the head of the epididymis. What are they called? The answer is the efferent ductules. Their convolutions form the lobules of the head of the epididymis. Each is lined by ciliated and absorptive cells that move and concentrate the sperm.

Question 7506. A small remnant attached to the upper pole of the testis is derived from the duct that becomes the uterine tube in the female. What is it? The answer is the appendix testis. It is a remnant of the paramesonephric duct and lies at the upper pole under the tunica vaginalis. It can twist and cause acute scrotal pain in a child.

Question 7507. A small stalked appendage hangs from the head of the epididymis and comes from the duct that forms the epididymis itself. What is it? The answer is the appendix epididymis. It is a remnant of the mesonephric duct, from which the epididymis, the vas deferens and the seminal vesicle all develop.

Question 7508. Which network of veins wraps around the testicular artery in the spermatic cord and cools the blood before it reaches the testis? The answer is the pampiniform plexus. Its veins surround the artery and exchange heat with it in a countercurrent arrangement. Higher in the cord the plexus condenses into the testicular vein.

Question 7509. Which thin muscular covering of the spermatic cord comes from the internal oblique muscle and lifts the testis? The answer is the cremaster muscle. It forms loops over the cord and the testis and contracts in cold and when the skin of the upper thigh is stroked. The dartos muscle in the scrotal wall also wrinkles the skin.

Question 7510. Which vessel gives rise to the testicular artery: the internal iliac artery, the abdominal aorta, the external iliac artery, or the femoral artery? The answer is the abdominal aorta. The testicular artery arises from the aorta in the upper abdomen, below the renal artery, and descends to the deep inguinal ring. It can be injured high in the abdomen.

Question 7511. Lymph from the testis itself drains to which group of nodes: the superficial inguinal nodes, the para-aortic nodes, the internal iliac nodes, or the deep inguinal nodes? The answer is the para-aortic nodes. The testis develops high on the posterior abdominal wall and drags its lymphatics with it as it descends. The skin of the scrotum drains to the groin instead.

Question 7512. The right testicular vein ends in the inferior vena cava. Into which vessel does the left testicular vein usually drain? The answer is the left renal vein. The left vein is longer and joins the renal vein at a right angle, while the right joins the vena cava obliquely. That helps explain why a varicocele is far more common on the left.

Question 7513. Which covering of the testis is a remnant of the peritoneal outpouching that followed the gonad into the scrotum: the tunica albuginea, the tunica vaginalis, the internal spermatic fascia, or the dartos layer? The answer is the tunica vaginalis. It is the lower part of the processus vaginalis, which normally closes above the testis. Its visceral layer covers the gland.

Question 7514. Which part of the testis is not covered by the visceral layer of the tunica vaginalis: the anterior surface, the lateral surface, the posterior border, or the lower pole? The answer is the posterior border. There the serous layer is reflected onto the epididymis, leaving a bare strip for vessels, nerves and the duct system. The sinus of the epididymis is the recess beside it.

Question 7515. A young man has a soft swelling above the left testis that feels like a bag of worms and empties when he lies down. Which anatomical feature best explains why the left side is affected far more often? The answer is the drainage of the left testicular vein. It is longer than the right and joins the left renal vein at a right angle. That length and that angle make the left side far more vulnerable.

Question 7516. Pain from a testis is often felt first where: in the scrotum, in the periumbilical region, in the groin, or in the perineum? The answer is the periumbilical region. The testis carries its sympathetic supply from the tenth thoracic segment of the spinal cord, so visceral pain is referred to the matching dermatome around the navel. The skin of the scrotum feels pain where it lies.

Question 7517. Which of these structures is found within the spermatic cord: the femoral artery, the vas deferens, the inferior epigastric artery, or the ilioinguinal nerve? The answer is the vas deferens. The cord also holds the testicular artery, the artery to the vas deferens, the cremasteric artery, the pampiniform plexus, lymph vessels and sympathetic nerves. The inferior epigastric artery lies outside the cord.

Question 7518. The septa of the testis divide the gland into about how many lobules: about twenty to thirty, about one hundred, about two hundred to three hundred, or about one thousand? The answer is about two hundred to three hundred. Each lobule holds one to four highly coiled seminiferous tubules. Their combined uncoiled length is measured in hundreds of meters.

Question 7519. During descent of the testis, which structure guides the gland from the posterior abdominal wall to the scrotum: the processus vaginalis, the gubernaculum, the inguinal ligament, or the round ligament? The answer is the gubernaculum. It is a cord of mesenchyme that runs from the lower pole of the gonad to the future scrotum and holds the gland on its path. Androgens drive the descent.

Question 7520. When the skin of the upper medial thigh is stroked, the testis on that side rises. Which nerves carry this reflex: the femoral and obturator nerves, the ilioinguinal nerve and the genital branch of the genitofemoral nerve, the pudendal nerve alone, or the sciatic nerve? The answer is the ilioinguinal nerve as the afferent limb and the genital branch of the genitofemoral nerve as the efferent limb.

Question 7521. A boy has sudden severe pain in the scrotum, the testis lies high and horizontal, and the cremasteric reflex is absent on that side. Which anatomical arrangement is the usual cause? The answer is the bell-clapper deformity, in which the tunica vaginalis extends high onto the cord. The testis then hangs freely in the serous sac and can twist on its cord. The horizontal lie and the absent reflex separate this from inflammation.

Question 7522. A testis that has not descended into the scrotum carries an increased risk of which two problems: torsion and hernia, malignancy and impaired sperm production, hydrocele and varicocele, or infection and stones? The answer is malignancy and impaired sperm production. The undescended gland is exposed to a higher temperature, which damages the germinal epithelium. The scrotum keeps the testis cooler than the abdomen.

Question 7523. A described ultrasound of the scrotum shows two oval organs of even texture, each about four centimeters long, with a smaller structure hugging the back of each one. What is the smaller structure? The answer is the epididymis. It lies along the posterior and posterolateral border of the testis, head above and tail below. A thin dark line of fluid around the gland is the tunica vaginalis.

Question 7524. A described specimen has been cut across the testis, showing a fan of fine septa running from a thick white coat at the back of the gland to the surface. What does that show? The answer is the lobular structure of the testis. The septa pass from the mediastinum testis to the tunica albuginea and enclose the lobules. Each lobule holds one to four coiled seminiferous tubules.

Question 7525. A described magnetic resonance imaging scan of the groin shows a rounded cord of tissue passing through the inguinal canal, with a thick-walled tube at its posterior part. What is the tube? The answer is the vas deferens. It is the firmest and most posterior structure in the spermatic cord and feels like a whipcord through the scrotal skin. The vessels and nerves lie in front of it.

Question 7526. A described transverse section through the scrotum shows, from outside inward, thin pigmented skin, a layer of smooth muscle, a fibrous sac, and two oval glands separated by a midline partition. Which layer lies immediately outside the fibrous sac? The answer is the dartos muscle with its fascia. It replaces the fatty layer of the superficial fascia, so the scrotum holds no fat.

Question 7527. A described venogram of the spermatic cord shows a tangled mass of veins beside the left testis, with a single vein ascending from it to join a larger horizontal vein. Which vessel does that vein join? The answer is the left renal vein. The tangled mass is a varicocele of the pampiniform plexus. On the right the corresponding vein would end in the inferior vena cava.

Question 7528. A described section of a seminiferous tubule shows a wall several cells thick, with small cells near the base and tall columnar cells reaching from base to lumen. What are those tall cells? The answer is the sustentacular cells. They support and nourish the developing germ cells and form the blood-testis barrier with their tight junctions. The cells between them are germ cells.

Question 7529. Which structure lies immediately posterior and posterolateral to the testis, hugging it from the upper pole to the lower? The answer is the epididymis. Its head caps the upper pole, its body lies along the posterior border, and its tail turns upward at the lower pole. The tail then continues as the vas deferens.

Question 7530. The testis hangs at the lower end of the spermatic cord in the scrotum. Which structure lies immediately above and behind the testis at its junction with the cord? The answer is the tail of the epididymis. The tail is fixed to the lower pole and is the point at which the duct of the epididymis becomes the vas deferens.

Question 7531. Within the spermatic cord, which structure lies at the posterior part, behind the testicular artery and the pampiniform plexus? The answer is the vas deferens. It lies at the back of the cord and can be rolled between the fingers as a firm cord. The genital branch of the genitofemoral nerve runs with the vessels in front of it.

Question 7532. In the standing body, the testis lies in the scrotum below and in front of which bony landmark, close to the root of the penis? The answer is the pubic tubercle. The cord leaves the superficial inguinal ring just above and lateral to the tubercle and passes down to the testis. The scrotum hangs below the root of the penis.

Question 7533. The visceral layer of the tunica vaginalis covers the testis and then turns onto the wall of the scrotum. Where does it leave the gland uncovered, and what is the recess beside the epididymis called? The answer is the posterior border, and the recess is the sinus of the epididymis. Vessels and the duct enter the gland through that bare strip.

Question 7534. A constructed illustration: a painless swelling of one side of the scrotum in a newborn glows when a light is held against it. Between which two layers has the fluid collected? The answer is between the visceral and the parietal layers of the tunica vaginalis. That collection is a hydrocele. A swelling that does not glow is more likely to be a hernia.

Question 7535. A constructed illustration: a young boy has sudden scrotal pain, and a small tender blue nodule is seen at the upper pole of the testis, with the gland itself lying normally. Which structure has twisted? The answer is the appendix testis. It is a remnant of the paramesonephric duct and hangs from the upper pole, where it can twist on its pedicle.

Question 7536. A constructed illustration: a small ulcerated growth on the skin of the scrotum is found, and nodes in the groin are enlarged. Which nodes are involved, and why are they not the nodes that a testicular tumor would reach? The answer is the superficial inguinal nodes. The skin of the scrotum drains there as the skin of the perineum does.

Question 7537. A constructed illustration: a described computed tomography scan of the abdomen in a young man with a painless testicular lump shows enlarged nodes beside the aorta at the level of the second lumbar vertebra. Why are these nodes involved? The answer is that the testis develops on the posterior abdominal wall and carries its lymphatics with it as it descends. The para-aortic nodes are its first nodes.

Question 7538. True or false: in most men the right testis lies lower in the scrotum than the left. The answer is false. The left testis usually lies a little lower than the right, and the left cord is the longer of the two. That difference in height is a normal finding and not a sign of disease.

Question 7539. True or false: the scrotum has no layer of fat under its skin. The answer is true. The fatty layer of the superficial fascia is replaced in the scrotum by the dartos muscle and its fascia, which wrinkle the skin. The skin itself is thin and pigmented and carries sweat glands.

Question 7540. Which of these pairs belongs together: the interstitial cells with inhibin, the sustentacular cells with testosterone, the interstitial cells with testosterone, or the sustentacular cells with the pampiniform plexus? The answer is the interstitial cells with testosterone. These cells lie between the tubules and respond to luteinizing hormone. The sustentacular cells secrete inhibin and androgen-binding protein.

Question 7541. Which of these pairs belongs together: the rete testis with the mediastinum, the efferent ductules with the tail of the epididymis, the seminiferous tubules with the vas deferens, or the duct of the epididymis with the rete testis? The answer is the rete testis with the mediastinum. The straight tubules open into the rete, which lies in the fibrous mediastinum. The efferent ductules leave it to form the head of the epididymis.

Question 7542. Name, in order, the three parts of the duct system that carry sperm from the seminiferous tubules to the head of the epididymis, spoken as a list. The answer is the straight tubules, the rete testis and the efferent ductules. The straight tubules leave the lobules and open into the rete testis. From there the ductules carry the sperm into the head.

Question 7543. Which part of the epididymis is the largest, sits on the upper pole of the testis, and receives the efferent ductules? The answer is the head. The efferent ductules enter it and form its lobules, which are also called the coni vasculosi. The head is the only part that receives new sperm.

Question 7544. Which part of the epididymis lies at the lower pole of the testis and continues upward as the vas deferens? The answer is the tail. It is the lowest and smallest part of the epididymis and serves as the main store of mature sperm. From the tail the duct turns upward into the spermatic cord.

Question 7545. The duct of the epididymis is a single tube packed into the organ in tight coils. Roughly how long would it be if it were uncoiled: about six centimeters, about sixty centimeters, about six meters, or about sixty meters? The answer is about six meters. The coils are bound together by connective tissue, which gives the organ its firm texture. The vas deferens continues from it as a straighter tube.

Question 7546. What is the name of the narrow groove that separates the body of the epididymis from the side of the testis? The answer is the sinus of the epididymis. It is the recess between the gland and the serous covering that lies between them. Fluid can collect there and may look like a cyst on an ultrasound study.

Question 7547. What name is given to the lobules of the head of the epididymis, each formed by the coils of one efferent ductule? The answer is the coni vasculosi. Each efferent ductule coils on itself to form a cone-shaped lobule, and the lobules together make up the head. The ductules then join the single duct of the epididymis.

Question 7548. Which statement about the epididymis is correct: it lies along the anterior surface of the testis, it lies along the posterior border with its head above and its tail below, its head lies at the lower pole, or it lies free in the scrotum? The answer is that it lies along the posterior border with its head above and its tail below.

Question 7549. The epididymis is described as a comma-shaped organ. Which part of it lies at the upper pole of the testis: the head, the body, the tail, or the ampulla? The answer is the head. The head is the broad upper end and receives the efferent ductules. The body runs down the posterior border and the tail ends at the lower pole.

Question 7550. Which of these is the main function of the epididymis: production of sperm, storage and maturation of sperm, production of seminal fluid, or production of testosterone? The answer is storage and maturation of sperm. Sperm leave the testis immature and acquire progressive motility as they pass along the duct. The tail is the main reservoir until ejaculation.

Question 7551. Where do sperm acquire the ability to swim forward: in the seminiferous tubules, in the rete testis, in the duct of the epididymis, or in the seminal vesicle? The answer is in the duct of the epididymis. The cells lining the duct absorb fluid and add proteins to the surface of the sperm. Sperm that reach the tail are mature but held in an inactive state.

Question 7552. Which arteries supply the epididymis: the testicular artery and the artery to the vas deferens, the internal pudendal artery alone, the inferior epigastric artery alone, or the obturator artery? The answer is the testicular artery and the artery to the vas deferens. The two anastomose along the duct system, so the epididymis survives when one is tied. The cremasteric artery supplies the coverings.

Question 7553. Lymph from the epididymis drains to which nodes: the superficial inguinal nodes, the para-aortic nodes, the internal iliac nodes, or the deep inguinal nodes? The answer is the para-aortic nodes. The epididymis is a derivative of the mesonephric duct and keeps the drainage of the posterior abdominal wall. Only the skin of the scrotum drains to the groin.

Question 7554. A man has no sperm in his semen, but a biopsy of the testis shows normal spermatogenesis. Which part of the duct system is most likely blocked if the glands are normal: the seminiferous tubules, the efferent ductules, the ureter, or the renal pelvis? The answer is the efferent ductules, the only route out of the testis.

Question 7555. A described specimen shows a small comma-shaped organ lying in the groove along the back of a testis, firm to the touch, with a broad upper end and a pointed lower end. Which organ is shown? The answer is the epididymis. The broad upper end is the head and the pointed lower end is the tail. The coiled duct inside gives the organ its firmness.

Question 7556. A described ultrasound of the scrotum shows a small dark oval swelling at the upper pole of the epididymis, separate from the testis, with no flow inside it. Which structure is it, and why does it move with the gland? The answer is a cyst of the epididymis. It arises from the coils of the duct. The epididymis is fixed to the testis, so the two move together.

Question 7557. A described MRI of the scrotum shows a comma-shaped structure of intermediate signal hugging the back of each testis, broader above than below. Which part of it lies highest? The answer is the head of the epididymis. It caps the upper pole and is broader than the body and the tail. The tail lies at the lower pole and becomes the vas deferens.

Question 7558. A described section through the back of a testis shows a serous space between the gland and the wall of the scrotum, and a recess where the serous layer dips between the gland and a comma-shaped organ. What is that recess? The answer is the sinus of the epididymis. The serous layer is the tunica vaginalis, reflected off the posterior border.

Question 7559. Which part of the epididymis lies immediately above the upper pole of the testis, capping it? The answer is the head. It is the broad upper end and receives the efferent ductules from the rete testis. Below it the body runs down the posterior border of the gland.

Question 7560. The epididymis lies along one border of the testis. Is that border anterior, posterior, medial or lateral? The answer is posterior, though its body and tail also lie somewhat posterolateral. That position is why the epididymis cannot be felt from the front of the scrotum as easily as the gland. Vessels and the duct enter at that border.

Question 7561. Two small appendages may be seen in the scrotum. One hangs from the upper pole of the testis and the other from the head of the epididymis. Which is which? The answer is that the appendix testis lies on the upper pole of the gland and the appendix epididymis lies on the head of the epididymis. They come from different ducts.

Question 7562. A constructed illustration: a man has tenderness in the scrotum that is greatest behind and above the testis, and the gland itself feels normal. Which structure is inflamed? The answer is the epididymis. Inflammation usually begins in the tail and spreads along the body to the head. The testis may become tender later if the inflammation spreads.

Question 7563. A constructed illustration: an infection of the bladder and the prostate spreads down the duct system to the scrotum. Which structure carries it to the epididymis? The answer is the vas deferens. It joins the tail of the epididymis to the ejaculatory duct and the urethra. Infection travels along its lumen and the tissue around it.

Question 7564. A constructed illustration: a young man is found to have no vas deferens on either side, and his epididymis is small and poorly formed. Which developmental duct has failed? The answer is the mesonephric duct. The epididymis, the vas deferens, the seminal vesicle and the ejaculatory duct all arise from it. This combination is associated with cystic fibrosis.

Question 7565. True or false: the duct of the epididymis is formed from the same embryonic duct that forms the vas deferens. The answer is true. Both are derivatives of the mesonephric duct, which also gives rise to the seminal vesicle. The efferent ductules, by contrast, come from the mesonephric tubules.

Question 7566. Which of these pairs belongs together: the efferent ductules with the tail of the epididymis, the efferent ductules with the head of the epididymis, the duct of the epididymis with the rete testis, or the appendix testis with the mesonephric duct? The answer is the efferent ductules with the head. They enter the head and form its lobules.

Question 7567. Which thick-walled muscular tube begins at the tail of the epididymis, ascends in the spermatic cord and carries sperm toward the urethra? The answer is the vas deferens. Its wall is made of three coats of smooth muscle, which give it a cord-like feel. Its narrow lumen is lined by a columnar epithelium.

Question 7568. Just before it joins the duct of the seminal vesicle, the vas deferens widens into a dilated terminal part. What is that part called? The answer is the ampulla. It lies behind the base of the bladder, medial to the seminal vesicle. There the ampulla joins the duct of the seminal vesicle.

Question 7569. The duct of the seminal vesicle joins the ampulla of the vas deferens to form which structure, and where does it open? The answer is the ejaculatory duct, which opens into the prostatic urethra. It is a short tube that passes through the prostate. It ends on the seminal colliculus beside the opening of the prostatic utricle.

Question 7570. Near the base of the bladder the vas deferens crosses another tube that carries urine, and the ureter is said to pass beneath it like water under a bridge. Which tube lies deep to the vas at that point? The answer is the ureter. The vas deferens crosses it from lateral to medial on its way to the ampulla.

Question 7571. The vas deferens is a long tube that runs from the scrotum to the prostate. About how long is it: about five centimeters, about fifteen centimeters, about forty-five centimeters, or about ninety centimeters? The answer is about forty-five centimeters. Most of that length lies in the pelvis. Its thick muscular wall propels sperm during ejaculation.

Question 7572. Which of these structures lies within the spermatic cord: the appendix testis, the vas deferens, the head of the epididymis, or the tail of the epididymis? The answer is the vas deferens. It joins the cord at the lower end, where the tail of the epididymis turns upward. The appendix testis hangs from the upper pole and lies outside the cord.

Question 7573. Which covering of the spermatic cord is derived from the aponeurosis of the external oblique muscle: the internal spermatic fascia, the cremasteric fascia, the external spermatic fascia, or the tunica vaginalis? The answer is the external spermatic fascia. The internal spermatic fascia comes from the transversalis fascia and the cremasteric fascia from the internal oblique.

Question 7574. As the vas deferens enters the abdomen it hooks around a vessel on the deep surface of the anterior abdominal wall. Which vessel: the inferior epigastric artery, the external iliac artery, the obturator artery, or the superior vesical artery? The answer is the inferior epigastric artery. That hook marks the deep inguinal ring, where the vas turns downward.

Question 7575. Which artery supplies the vas deferens: the internal pudendal artery, the superior vesical artery, the obturator artery, or the middle rectal artery? The answer is the superior vesical artery, through a small artery to the vas deferens. That artery runs a long course in the cord and anastomoses with the testicular artery, so the vas can be divided safely.

Question 7576. After a vasectomy, in which the vas deferens is divided on each side, which statement is correct: the testes stop making sperm, the semen no longer contains sperm but still contains fluid from the glands, ejaculation stops, or the testes shrink? The answer is that the semen no longer contains sperm but still contains fluid from the glands. The testes continue to make sperm, which are absorbed above the block.

Question 7577. A described dissection of the scrotum shows a whitish cord that can be rolled between the fingers above the testis, firmer than the veins beside it. Which structure is it? The answer is the vas deferens. Its muscular wall makes it feel like a whipcord, which is how it is identified at operation. The veins of the pampiniform plexus are softer and lie in front of it.

Question 7578. A described MRI of the pelvis in cross section shows two thin-walled ducts running medially between the base of the bladder and the rectum, close to the midline, just above the prostate. What are they? The answer is the two ejaculatory ducts. Each is formed by the union of a vas deferens and a seminal vesicle duct. They pass forward through the prostate.

Question 7579. A described specimen shows a thick-walled tube that widens near its end and is joined from the side by the duct of a lobulated gland, the two forming a single narrower channel. Which two structures have joined? The answer is the ampulla of the vas deferens and the duct of the seminal vesicle. Their union forms the ejaculatory duct.

Question 7580. Within the spermatic cord, which structure lies directly posterior to the testicular artery and the pampiniform plexus? The answer is the vas deferens. It runs at the back of the cord, where it is protected by the other structures. The genital branch of the genitofemoral nerve runs with the vessels in front of it.

Question 7581. A constructed illustration: during the repair of an indirect inguinal hernia in a man, the surgeon identifies a firm white cord at the posterior part of the sac and takes care to preserve it. Which structure is it, and why is it preserved? The answer is the vas deferens. Dividing it would block the passage of sperm while leaving the testis to make them.

Question 7582. A constructed illustration: during an operation on the pelvis, a tube running downward on the lateral wall is traced toward the bladder, and the surgeon notes a second tube crossing it from above and lateral. Which structure crosses in front of the ureter? The answer is the vas deferens, which crosses the ureter just above the seminal vesicle.

Question 7583. True or false: the vas deferens and the appendix testis arise from the same embryonic duct. The answer is false. The vas deferens arises from the mesonephric duct, which also forms the epididymis, the seminal vesicle and the ejaculatory duct. The appendix testis is a remnant of the paramesonephric duct, which becomes the uterine tube.

Question 7584. Which of these pairs belongs together: the ejaculatory duct with the prostatic urethra, the ejaculatory duct with the membranous urethra, the ampulla of the vas deferens with the scrotum, or the vas deferens with the tunica vaginalis? The answer is the ejaculatory duct with the prostatic urethra. The duct passes through the prostate and opens on the seminal colliculus.

Question 7585. Name the two structures whose ducts join to form the ejaculatory duct, spoken as a sentence. The answer is the ampulla of the vas deferens and the duct of the seminal vesicle. Each side has one ejaculatory duct, and the two run through the prostate. They open into the prostatic urethra beside the prostatic utricle.

Question 7586. In a described transverse section of the male pelvis, the vas deferens lies lateral to the bladder and the seminal vesicle lies behind it. On which side of the seminal vesicle does the ampulla lie, and where does the ureter pass? The answer is that the ampulla lies medial to the seminal vesicle, and the ureter passes above and medial to it.

Question 7587. Which paired lobulated gland lies behind the base of the urinary bladder, above the prostate and in front of the rectum? The answer is the seminal vesicle. It is a coiled tube about five centimeters long, surrounded by a fibrous coat. Its lower end joins the ampulla of the vas deferens to form the ejaculatory duct.

Question 7588. Which sugar is found in high concentration in the fluid of the seminal vesicle and serves as a source of energy for sperm? The answer is fructose. Its presence in the semen marks seminal vesicle function, and its absence suggests blocked ejaculatory ducts or absent vesicles. The secretion is alkaline and makes up about sixty per cent of the seminal fluid.

Question 7589. Which statement about the position of the seminal vesicle is correct: it lies in front of the bladder, it lies in front of the rectum and behind the bladder, it lies below the prostate, or it lies above the bladder? The answer is that it lies in front of the rectum and behind the bladder. A thin fibrous septum separates it from the rectum. The prostate lies below it.

Question 7590. The seminal vesicle is a coiled tube that is about how long: about five millimeters, about five centimeters, about fifteen centimeters, or about forty-five centimeters? The answer is about five centimeters. It is much shorter than the vas deferens, which is about forty-five centimeters. Its coiled structure gives it a lobulated surface.

Question 7591. Lymph from the seminal vesicle drains mainly to which nodes: the superficial inguinal nodes, the para-aortic nodes, the internal iliac nodes, or the deep cervical nodes? The answer is the internal iliac nodes. The seminal vesicle is a pelvic organ and follows the pelvic pattern of drainage. The testis drains to the para-aortic nodes instead.

Question 7592. A man has a very small volume of semen, and the fluid contains no fructose. Which structure is most likely blocked on both sides: the vas deferens, the ejaculatory duct, the urethra, or the epididymis? The answer is the ejaculatory duct. Fructose comes from the seminal vesicle, whose duct joins the vas deferens to form that duct. A block there keeps the fluid of both glands out of the semen.

Question 7593. A described transrectal ultrasound shows two lobulated structures above the prostate, one on each side of the midline, each with a coiled and cystic texture. Which structures are shown? The answer is the seminal vesicles. They lie behind the base of the bladder and above the prostate. Their lobulated pattern reflects the coiled tube inside.

Question 7594. A described MRI of the pelvis shows a cyst replacing the seminal vesicle on one side, and the kidney on that same side is absent. Which developmental duct links the two findings? The answer is the mesonephric duct. The seminal vesicle arises from it, and the ureteric bud that forms the kidney also sprouts from it. A fault there can affect both.

Question 7595. The ampulla of the vas deferens and the seminal vesicle lie close together behind the bladder. Which lies medial to the other, and which structure passes just above the seminal vesicle? The answer is that the ampulla lies medial to the seminal vesicle, and the ureter passes above and medial to it.

Question 7596. A constructed illustration: during removal of the rectum, the surgeon must separate it from the bladder and the seminal vesicles, and a thin fibrous layer between them is divided. Which structures lie in front of that layer? The answer is the seminal vesicles, the ampullae of the vas deferens and the prostate. The layer is the rectovesical septum, which keeps the two surfaces apart.

Question 7597. True or false: the seminal vesicles store sperm until ejaculation. The answer is false. Sperm are stored mainly in the tail of the epididymis, where they are held in an inactive state. The seminal vesicles add a fructose-rich, alkaline fluid to the semen, but they are not a reservoir for sperm.

Question 7598. Which of these pairs belongs together: the seminal vesicle with the para-aortic nodes, the seminal vesicle with the internal iliac nodes, the testis with the internal iliac nodes, or the scrotal skin with the para-aortic nodes? The answer is the seminal vesicle with the internal iliac nodes. It is a pelvic organ. The testis drains to the para-aortic nodes.

Question 7599. Name two structures that lie immediately above the prostate and behind the base of the bladder, spoken as a list. The answer is the seminal vesicles and the ampullae of the vas deferens. Each ampulla joins the duct of the seminal vesicle on its own side to form the ejaculatory duct. Those ducts then pass downward through the prostate.

Question 7600. In a described sagittal section of the male pelvis, one structure lies just behind the base of the bladder and just in front of the rectum, with the prostate below it and the rectovesical pouch above it. Which structure is it, and where does it lie? The answer is the seminal vesicle, and it lies below the peritoneum, outside the peritoneal cavity.

To summarize, the testis is covered by the tunica vaginalis, a remnant of the processus vaginalis, and by the tunica albuginea, whose posterior thickening forms the mediastinum testis and holds the rete testis within it. The seminiferous tubules are lined by germ cells and sustentacular cells, the interstitial cells between them make testosterone, and sperm leave by the straight tubules, the rete testis and the efferent ductules into the head of the epididymis. The epididymis lies on the posterior border of the gland, matures and stores sperm, and continues at its tail as the vas deferens, which ascends in the spermatic cord, crosses the ureter above the seminal vesicle and joins the duct of that gland to form the ejaculatory duct. The testis and the epididymis drain to the para-aortic nodes, the scrotal skin to the superficial inguinal nodes, and the seminal vesicles to the internal iliac nodes.
