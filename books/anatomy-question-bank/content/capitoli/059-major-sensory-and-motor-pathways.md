# Chapter 59 — Major Sensory and Motor Pathways

This chapter follows the great sensory and motor pathways from the receptor to the cortex, and from the cortex back to the muscle. It covers the dorsal column and medial lemniscus pathway, the spinothalamic and trigeminal routes for pain and temperature, the pathways of taste, smell, sight and hearing, and then the descending tracts, the corticospinal and corticobulbar fibers, the brainstem motor tracts, the cerebellum and the basal ganglia. It holds one hundred questions, and each one gives its answer and its reason at once. Every pathway is described in words rather than shown in a diagram, so that it can be followed by ear. This is educational material, and it supports rather than replaces an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 5801. Which ascending pathway carries fine touch, vibration and the sense of position from the limbs to the cerebral cortex? The answer is the dorsal column and medial lemniscus pathway. Its fibers run up the back of the cord on the same side, cross in the medulla and end in the thalamus. Pain travels by a different route.

Question 5802. Which region sends its fibers into the fasciculus gracilis: the lower limb, the upper limb, the face, or the abdominal organs? The answer is the lower limb and the lower trunk. That bundle lies on the medial side of the dorsal column. Fibers from the upper limb run beside it in the fasciculus cuneatus.

Question 5803. In which part of the brain do the fibers of the fasciculus gracilis end? The answer is the nucleus gracilis, in the lower medulla. That nucleus lies beside the nucleus cuneatus, which receives the fibers from the upper limb. The cells there are the second neurons of the pathway.

Question 5804. At the back of the medulla, which of the two dorsal column bundles lies lateral to the other? The answer is the fasciculus cuneatus, which lies lateral to the fasciculus gracilis. It carries the fibers from the upper limb and the upper trunk, so it lies beside the cuneate nucleus. The gracile bundle lies nearest the midline.

Question 5805. A described transverse section of the lower medulla shows a stream of fibers arching across the midline just behind the pyramids. Which crossing is shown, and what does it form? The answer is the sensory decussation, formed by the internal arcuate fibers. Those fibers come from the two dorsal column nuclei and gather into the medial lemniscus.

Question 5806. True or false: the fibers of the dorsal column pathway cross the midline in the midbrain. The answer is false. They cross in the lower medulla as the internal arcuate fibers, which form the sensory decussation. By the time they reach the midbrain they have long been on the opposite side.

Question 5807. Which nucleus of the thalamus receives the medial lemniscus? The answer is the ventral posterolateral nucleus. It lies in the lateral part of the thalamus and receives fibers from the opposite side of the body. Its cells send the pathway on to the postcentral gyrus.

Question 5808. Where do the fibers of the dorsal column pathway end in the cortex: the precentral gyrus, the postcentral gyrus, the temporal lobe, or the occipital lobe? The answer is the postcentral gyrus. It is the primary sensory area for the body, and the fibers from the leg end on its medial surface. The precentral gyrus is the motor area instead.

Question 5809. In a coronal section of the hemisphere, where does the sensory area for the foot lie, and which artery supplies it? The answer is on the medial surface in the paracentral lobule, supplied by the anterior cerebral artery. The areas for the hand and the face lie out on the convexity, in the territory of the middle cerebral artery.

Question 5810. Which cell gives rise to the first neuron of the pain and temperature pathway? The answer is the cell in the dorsal root ganglion. Its fiber enters the cord and ends in the dorsal horn of the same segment or one nearby. The axon that then crosses the midline belongs to the second neuron.

Question 5811. Where do the fibers of the pain pathway cross to the opposite side: in the anterior white commissure, in the medulla, in the pons, or in the thalamus? The answer is the anterior white commissure. They cross close to the central canal within a segment or two of their entry, and then ascend in the lateral funiculus.

Question 5812. Within the medulla, which structure lies immediately dorsal to the pyramids? The answer is the medial lemniscus. The pyramids carry the corticospinal fibers downward on the front of the medulla, and the lemniscus lies just behind them. Near the midline the two lemnisci lie side by side.

Question 5813. A described transverse section of the cord shows a small cavity at the center of the gray matter, with the crossing fibers just in front of it destroyed while the dorsal columns and the corticospinal tracts look intact. Which fibers are destroyed, and what loss follows? The answer is the crossing spinothalamic fibers, with loss of pain and temperature on both sides.

Question 5814. A constructed illustration: a person loses the sense of pain and temperature over both shoulders and arms, while position sense is preserved, and a cavity is found in the center of the cervical cord. Which fibers have been destroyed? The answer is the crossing spinothalamic fibers near the central canal. Because they cross close to the center, one lesion cuts the fibers from both sides at those segments.

Question 5815. Within the lateral spinothalamic tract, which fibers lie most laterally: those from the sacral segments, the cervical segments, the thoracic segments, or the face? The answer is the fibers from the sacral segments. The tract is laminated, with the sacral fibers outside and the cervical fibers inside. The face is carried elsewhere.

Question 5816. What name is given to the group of tracts that carry pain, temperature and crude touch upward on the front and side of the spinal cord? The answer is the anterolateral system. It includes the spinothalamic, the spinoreticular and the spinomesencephalic tracts. All of them cross the midline close to the segment where they enter.

Question 5817. A described operation divides the anterolateral quadrant of the cord on one side to relieve pain. From which side of the body below the cut is the pain relieved: the same side, the opposite side, both sides, or neither side? The answer is the opposite side. Those fibers have already crossed, so cutting them on one side removes pain from the other. The dorsal column lies behind and is spared.

Question 5818. A constructed illustration: a wound divides the right half of the cord at the sixth thoracic segment. Which sensory loss appears on the right below the wound, and which on the left? The answer is loss of position sense on the right and loss of pain and temperature on the left. The dorsal column fibers cross only in the medulla, while the pain fibers have already crossed in the cord.

Question 5819. In which thalamic nucleus does the second neuron of the pain pathway end: the ventral posterolateral nucleus, the ventral posteromedial nucleus, the lateral geniculate body, or the medial geniculate body? The answer is the ventral posterolateral nucleus. That nucleus receives fibers from the trunk and the limbs. The ventral posteromedial nucleus receives fibers from the face instead.

Question 5820. Which of these pairs belongs together: the fasciculus gracilis with the upper limb, the fasciculus cuneatus with the lower limb, the substantia gelatinosa with the finest pain fibers, or the nucleus gracilis with the face? The answer is the substantia gelatinosa with the finest pain fibers. The gracile bundle carries the lower limb and the cuneate bundle the upper limb.

Question 5821. Which nucleus of the brainstem receives the fibers that carry pain and temperature from the face? The answer is the spinal nucleus of the trigeminal nerve. It runs down through the medulla and into the upper cervical cord. Its fibers cross to the opposite side and join the trigeminal lemniscus.

Question 5822. Which nucleus of the head is the only one whose primary cell bodies lie inside the central nervous system: the spinal nucleus of the trigeminal nerve, the principal sensory nucleus, the mesencephalic nucleus, or the solitary nucleus? The answer is the mesencephalic nucleus, which carries position sense from the jaw. Every other sensory system keeps its first cell body outside.

Question 5823. Which thalamic nucleus receives the fibers that carry touch from the face? The answer is the ventral posteromedial nucleus. The principal sensory nucleus of the trigeminal nerve sends its crossed fibers there. It lies next to the ventral posterolateral nucleus, which serves the rest of the body.

Question 5824. A described transverse section of the pons shows a large sensory root entering the side of the brainstem and ending in a nucleus beside the motor nucleus of the same nerve. Which nerve is shown, and which nucleus receives the fibers? The answer is the trigeminal nerve, with its principal sensory nucleus. Some of its fibers turn downward into the spinal nucleus.

Question 5825. A constructed illustration: the surface of the eye is touched lightly, and one eye fails to blink. Which nerve carries the sensation, and which carries the command to the muscle? The answer is the ophthalmic division of the trigeminal nerve and the facial nerve. The first carries the sensation inward, and the second supplies the muscle that closes the lid.

Question 5826. Where do the fibers that carry pain from the face cross the midline: in the midbrain, in the pons, in the medulla and upper cervical cord close to their nucleus, or in the thalamus? The answer is the medulla and upper cervical cord, close to the spinal nucleus. They cross as they leave it and then form the trigeminal lemniscus.

Question 5827. Which nucleus of the brainstem receives the fibers that carry taste from the tongue? The answer is the nucleus of the solitary tract. The fibers arrive with the seventh, the ninth and the tenth cranial nerves. From there the pathway runs to the thalamus and then to the cortex beside the tongue area.

Question 5828. Taste from the front two thirds of the tongue travels by which route: the chorda tympani joining the seventh cranial nerve, the glossopharyngeal nerve alone, the vagus nerve, or the hypoglossal nerve? The answer is the chorda tympani joining the seventh cranial nerve. The back third of the tongue is served by the ninth. The hypoglossal nerve carries movement only.

Question 5829. A described section of the medulla shows a lesion at its lateral edge that destroys the spinal nucleus of the trigeminal nerve and the spinothalamic tract on that side. Which losses follow? The answer is loss of pain and temperature on the opposite side of the body and on the same side of the face, because the body fibers have crossed while the facial fibers have not.

Question 5830. Which sensory pathway reaches the cerebral cortex without a relay in the thalamus? The answer is the olfactory pathway. Its fibers run from the olfactory bulb directly to the primary olfactory cortex. Every other sensory pathway is relayed through a thalamic nucleus first.

Question 5831. Which structure is the relay of the visual pathway between the optic tract and the visual cortex: the medial geniculate body, the lateral geniculate body, the pulvinar, or the superior colliculus? The answer is the lateral geniculate body. It lies at the back of the thalamus and sends its fibers along the optic radiation. The medial geniculate body serves hearing.

Question 5832. A described map of the visual pathway marks a lesion at the optic chiasm that divides only the crossing fibers. Which part of the visual field is lost? The answer is the temporal half of each visual field. The crossing fibers come from the nasal half of each retina, which receives light from the temporal field.

Question 5833. Which part of the midbrain is a relay station for hearing on the way to the thalamus? The answer is the inferior colliculus. It lies on the back of the midbrain below the superior colliculus. From it the fibers run to the medial geniculate body and then to the temporal lobe.

Question 5834. Which part of the visual field is lost when the fibers of the optic radiation that sweep forward into the temporal lobe are destroyed: the upper part of the opposite field, the lower part of that field, the same side of the field, or the whole field? The answer is the upper part of the opposite field. That sweep is Meyer's loop, and the parietal fibers carry the lower part instead.

Question 5835. Which tract runs through the brainstem just beneath the floor of the fourth ventricle, close to the midline, and links the nuclei that move the two eyes together? The answer is the medial longitudinal fasciculus. It joins the abducens nucleus on one side to the oculomotor nucleus on the other, and it also carries fibers for the neck reflexes.

Question 5836. A constructed illustration: a person looks to the left, and the right eye fails to turn inward while the left eye turns out normally. Which structure is damaged? The answer is the medial longitudinal fasciculus on the left side, which carries the command from the left abducens nucleus to the right oculomotor nucleus. Convergence is preserved because that pathway lies outside.

Question 5837. Which of these pairs belongs together: the medial geniculate body with vision, the lateral geniculate body with hearing, the ventral posteromedial nucleus with the face, or the ventral posterolateral nucleus with the face? The answer is the ventral posteromedial nucleus with the face. The lateral geniculate body serves vision and the medial geniculate body serves hearing.

Question 5838. Name the two nuclei of the lower medulla that receive the fibers of the dorsal columns, spoken as a list. The answer is the nucleus gracilis and the nucleus cuneatus. The first receives the fibers from the lower limb and the lower trunk. The second receives the fibers from the upper limb and the upper trunk.

Question 5839. Which cells of the cerebral cortex give rise to the large fibers of the corticospinal tract? The answer is the large pyramidal cells of the fifth layer of the motor cortex, named for Betz. They lie chiefly in the precentral gyrus. Their axons form the longest descending pathway in the body.

Question 5840. From which gyrus of the cerebral cortex does the corticospinal tract arise: the postcentral gyrus, the precentral gyrus, the superior temporal gyrus, or the cingulate gyrus? The answer is the precentral gyrus. Cells in its fifth layer send their axons downward, and the area for the leg lies on the medial surface. The postcentral gyrus is the sensory area.

Question 5841. Through which part of the internal capsule do the corticospinal fibers pass on their way to the brainstem? The answer is the posterior limb. They run there in company with the sensory fibers that are traveling upward to the cortex. The fibers for the head lie nearest the bend, in the genu.

Question 5842. A described transverse section of the midbrain shows two large bundles of fibers on the front, one on each side, with a dark band of pigment behind them. Which structure are the bundles, and what lies in the dark band? The answer is the crus cerebri, with the substantia nigra behind it. The fibers for the head lie at the medial edge of each crus.

Question 5843. In the crus cerebri of the midbrain, which fibers lie nearest the midline: those for the head, those for the leg, those for the arm, or those for the trunk? The answer is those for the head. They lie at the medial edge, and the fibers for the leg lie farthest out.

Question 5844. As the corticospinal fibers leave the cortex and gather toward the internal capsule, how do they run: as a fan called the corona radiata, as a compact bundle in the centrum semiovale, as a single tract in the anterior limb, or as a crossing bundle in the corpus callosum? The answer is as a fan called the corona radiata. The fibers converge from a wide area of cortex toward the narrow capsule.

Question 5845. At which level do most of the corticospinal fibers cross the midline, and what is that crossing called? The answer is the lower medulla, at the decussation of the pyramids. About nine tenths of the fibers cross there and descend in the lateral funiculus. The rest continue on the same side in the anterior funiculus.

Question 5846. What proportion of the corticospinal fibers crosses at the decussation of the pyramids: about one tenth, about one half, about nine tenths, or all of them? The answer is about nine tenths. The remainder stays on the same side and forms the anterior corticospinal tract. Those fibers cross later, close to the segments they supply.

Question 5847. A described transverse section of the mid-thoracic cord shows a compact oval bundle in the lateral funiculus, lying among the fibers of the lateral column. Which tract is it, and where do its fibers end? The answer is the lateral corticospinal tract, whose fibers end on the motor neurons of the same side. The fibers for the leg lie farthest out within it.

Question 5848. Within the lateral corticospinal tract, which fibers lie farthest from the gray matter: those for the sacral segments, the cervical segments, the thoracic segments, or the face? The answer is the fibers for the sacral segments. They lie at the outer edge, so a lesion pressing on the surface weakens the legs before the arms.

Question 5849. Which tract descends in the anterior funiculus, crosses in the segment it supplies, and ends on the motor neurons of the trunk muscles: the lateral corticospinal, the anterior corticospinal, the rubrospinal, or the vestibulospinal tract? The answer is the anterior corticospinal tract. It carries the minority of the corticospinal fibers that did not cross in the medulla.

Question 5850. A constructed illustration: a small lesion destroys the posterior limb of the internal capsule on the left side. What pattern of weakness and sensory loss appears? The answer is weakness of the right side of the body with loss of sensation on the same side. Motor and sensory fibers are packed together there, so a small lesion produces a dense deficit.

Question 5851. Which sign of an upper motor neuron lesion is looked for by stroking the sole of the foot? The answer is the Babinski sign. The great toe extends slowly and the other toes spread instead of curling down. The same response occurs in a healthy infant before the tract matures.

Question 5852. Which set of findings belongs to a lower motor neuron lesion: spasticity with exaggerated reflexes, flaccid weakness with wasting and loss of reflexes, a positive Babinski sign with clonus, or weakness without any change in tone? The answer is flaccid weakness with wasting and loss of reflexes. Spasticity with a positive Babinski sign points to a lesion above the decussation.

Question 5853. True or false: most corticospinal fibers end directly on the motor neurons of the spinal cord. The answer is false. Most of them end on small interneurons that then pass the command to the motor neurons. A minority, mainly those serving the hands and the feet, end directly on the motor neurons.

Question 5854. A constructed illustration: one person has a weak, wasted limb with absent reflexes after a nerve injury, and another has a stiff, weak limb with brisk reflexes after a lesion higher up. Which of the two has the upper motor neuron lesion? The answer is the second person, with the stiff limb and the brisk reflexes. Wasting and lost reflexes point to the lower motor neuron.

Question 5855. Name two signs that follow a lesion of the lower motor neuron, spoken as a list. The answer is wasting of the muscle and loss of its reflexes. The muscle also becomes flaccid and may show fine twitching. Sensation is unaffected, because the sensory fibers run by a separate route.

Question 5856. Through which part of the internal capsule do the cortical fibers for the muscles of the head and neck pass? The answer is the genu, the bend between the anterior and the posterior limbs. They lie there in front of the fibers for the limbs. Below, they end on the motor nuclei of the cranial nerves in the brainstem.

Question 5857. A lesion of the left motor cortex weakens the lower half of the face on the right, yet the forehead moves. Why: the upper half is supplied from both sides, the lower half is supplied from both sides, the facial nerve is spared, or the trigeminal nerve takes over? The answer is that the upper half is supplied from both sides. The lower half comes only from the opposite hemisphere.

Question 5858. Which cranial nerve nucleus lies close to the midline of the medulla, beneath the floor of the fourth ventricle, and is reached by cortical fibers from both sides? The answer is the nucleus of the twelfth cranial nerve, which moves the tongue. A lesion of one cortical area therefore does not paralyze the tongue.

Question 5859. A described illustration shows two faces. In the first the whole of one side of the face is weak, forehead included, and in the second only the lower half is weak. Which lesion lies behind the second picture? The answer is a lesion of the motor cortex or of its fibers above the nucleus. The forehead is spared because it receives fibers from both hemispheres.

Question 5860. Which of these pairs belongs together: the twelfth cranial nerve nucleus with the muscles of the tongue, the seventh with the muscles of chewing, the fifth with the muscles of the face, or the tenth with the muscles of the eye? The answer is the twelfth cranial nerve nucleus with the muscles of the tongue. The fifth serves chewing and the seventh the face.

Question 5861. A constructed illustration: a deep cut in the neck on one side leaves the tongue pushed toward the weak side when it is put out, with no loss of sensation. Which nerve has been injured? The answer is the twelfth cranial nerve on that side. The tongue is pushed across by the healthy muscle on the other side.

Question 5862. By which course do the cortical fibers reach the nucleus that moves the tongue: through the genu of the internal capsule, through the posterior limb on the same side only, through the dorsal column, or from the cerebellum? The answer is through the genu of the internal capsule. They cross on the way down and reach the nucleus mainly from the opposite hemisphere.

Question 5863. Which descending tract begins in the red nucleus, crosses in the midbrain and ends in the cervical cord, where it helps to control the muscles of the upper limb? The answer is the rubrospinal tract. It crosses just below the red nucleus, in the ventral tegmental decussation. Its fibers lie in front of the lateral corticospinal tract in the cord.

Question 5864. Which descending tract arises in the vestibular nuclei, runs uncrossed in the anterior funiculus, and increases the tone of the extensor muscles that keep a person upright: the rubrospinal, the vestibulospinal, the tectospinal, or the reticulospinal tract? The answer is the vestibulospinal tract. It carries signals from the inner ear and the cerebellum to the muscles that resist gravity.

Question 5865. Which descending tract begins in the superior colliculus of the midbrain and turns the head toward a sudden sight or sound? The answer is the tectospinal tract. It crosses in the midbrain before descending. Its fibers run in the anterior funiculus of the cervical cord, close to the midline.

Question 5866. A described diagram of the brainstem marks a scattered network of cells running through the pons and the medulla, with fibers passing down on both sides of the cord. Which system is shown, and what does it do? The answer is the reticular formation and its descending tracts, which set the level of activity of the cord and adjust posture and muscle tone.

Question 5867. True or false: the rubrospinal tract crosses the midline in the midbrain and ends mainly on the motor neurons of the upper limb. The answer is true. It crosses in the ventral tegmental decussation below the red nucleus. Its fibers reach the cervical enlargement, where they help to control the flexor muscles of the arm.

Question 5868. Which of these descending tracts arises in the cerebral cortex: the rubrospinal, the vestibulospinal, the reticulospinal, or the corticospinal tract? The answer is the corticospinal tract. It begins in the precentral gyrus and ends on the motor neurons of the cord. The other three begin in the brainstem and belong to the older system for posture.

Question 5869. Which group of cells in the dorsal horn gives rise to the tract that carries unconscious position sense from the lower limb to the cerebellum? The answer is the nucleus thoracicus, also called the dorsal nucleus or the column of Clarke. It lies from the first thoracic to the second lumbar segment, and its fibers ascend on the same side.

Question 5870. Which statement about the ventral spinocerebellar tract is correct: it crosses twice, it never crosses, it crosses only in the medulla, or it ends in the thalamus? The answer is that it crosses twice. Its fibers cross in the cord, run up on the opposite side and cross again in the superior cerebellar peduncle.

Question 5871. A described dissection of the cerebellum shows three pairs of thick stalks joining it to the brainstem, one above, one in the middle and one below. Which stalks are they, and which carries most of the entering fibers from the pons? The answer is the superior, the middle and the inferior cerebellar peduncles, and the middle one carries the pontine fibers.

Question 5872. Which of these fiber groups enters the cerebellum through the inferior cerebellar peduncle: the pontocerebellar fibers, the dorsal spinocerebellar tract, the fibers from the red nucleus, or the fibers from the globus pallidus? The answer is the dorsal spinocerebellar tract. The inferior peduncle also carries the olivocerebellar and the vestibulocerebellar fibers. The pontocerebellar fibers enter by the middle peduncle.

Question 5873. Over how many segments does the nucleus thoracicus extend: from the first cervical to the first thoracic, from the first thoracic to the second lumbar, from the second lumbar to the fifth sacral, or through the whole cord? The answer is from the first thoracic to the second lumbar segment. It therefore serves the lower limb alone.

Question 5874. A constructed illustration: a lesion destroys one cerebellar hemisphere. Would the disturbance affect the arm and the leg on the same side or on the opposite side? The answer is the same side. The cerebellar hemisphere serves the limbs of its own side, and the person overshoots the target when reaching.

Question 5875. A lesion of the midline part of the cerebellum, the vermis, produces which disturbance: an unsteady trunk with a wide-based gait, weakness of one hand, loss of pain and temperature, or loss of position sense in the toes? The answer is an unsteady trunk with a wide-based gait. The vermis serves posture and the trunk, while the hemispheres control the limbs.

Question 5876. Name the three pairs of cerebellar peduncles, spoken as a list from above downward. The answer is the superior, the middle and the inferior cerebellar peduncles. The superior pair carries most of the fibers leaving the cerebellum. The middle pair carries the pontine fibers, and the inferior pair carries fibers from the medulla and the cord.

Question 5877. Which cells of the cerebellar cortex provide its only output? The answer is the Purkinje cells. They lie in a single row in the middle layer of the cortex. Their axons run to the deep cerebellar nuclei and carry an inhibitory signal.

Question 5878. A described scan of the head shows wide folds on the surface of the cerebellum with wide spaces between them, while the rest of the brain looks normal. Which tissue has been lost, and what does it mean? The answer is cerebellar tissue, lost from the folia of the cortex. A person with such a scan is likely to be unsteady and to overshoot the target.

Question 5879. Which two masses of gray matter together form the striatum? The answer is the caudate nucleus and the putamen. They lie side by side in the wall of the lateral ventricle and in the base of the hemisphere. They share the same kind of cells and the same connections.

Question 5880. In the direct pathway through the basal ganglia, which structure does the striatum inhibit: the internal segment of the globus pallidus, the subthalamic nucleus, the external segment of the globus pallidus, or the thalamus? The answer is the internal segment of the globus pallidus. Inhibiting it releases the thalamus from inhibition, so the cortex is excited and movement is helped.

Question 5881. Which nucleus forms the relay of the indirect pathway between the external segment of the globus pallidus and the internal segment: the caudate nucleus, the subthalamic nucleus, the substantia nigra, or the red nucleus? The answer is the subthalamic nucleus. It excites the internal segment, so the thalamus is held back and movement is restrained. Damage to the nucleus removes that brake.

Question 5882. The output of the basal ganglia leaves the internal segment of the globus pallidus and passes to which part of the thalamus? The answer is the ventral anterior and the ventral lateral nuclei. From there the fibers pass to the motor and premotor areas of the frontal lobe.

Question 5883. A described circuit diagram shows a large mass of gray matter receiving fibers from the whole cortex, sending them by two routes to a pair of deep nuclei, and receiving a return from the thalamus, with no direct connection to the spinal cord. Which system is shown? The answer is the basal ganglia and their loop through the thalamus, which adjust the movement planned by the cortex.

Question 5884. A constructed illustration: a small stroke destroys the subthalamic nucleus on one side, and the opposite arm and leg fling themselves about. Which pathway has lost its brake? The answer is the indirect pathway of the basal ganglia, whose hold on the thalamus is lost. The disturbance shows on the opposite side because the cortical fibers cross.

Question 5885. In the common disorder of slow movement with tremor and rigidity, which cells are lost: the dopamine cells of the substantia nigra, the cells of the caudate nucleus, the cells of the subthalamic nucleus, or the Purkinje cells? The answer is the dopamine cells of the substantia nigra. Losing that signal to the striatum holds the thalamus back, so there is too little movement.

Question 5886. Which of these pairs belongs together: the caudate nucleus with the thalamus, the putamen with the subthalamic nucleus, the internal segment of the globus pallidus with the output of the basal ganglia, or the substantia nigra with the cerebellum? The answer is the internal segment of the globus pallidus with the output. The caudate and the putamen form the striatum, which takes the input.

Question 5887. Which deep nucleus of the cerebellum is the largest and gives rise to most of the fibers that leave it? The answer is the dentate nucleus. It lies in the white matter of each hemisphere, close to the midline of the cerebellum. Its fibers leave by the superior cerebellar peduncle and cross in the midbrain.

Question 5888. A person has weakness of the right arm and leg with a brisk knee reflex, while the face and tongue are spared. Which site fits best: the left internal capsule behind the genu, the left medulla, the right cervical cord, or the left cerebellum? The answer is the left internal capsule behind the genu, which carries the limb fibers and spares the head. A medullary lesion would weaken the tongue too.

Question 5889. A constructed illustration: a person has wasting and twitching of the hand muscles with brisk reflexes and a positive Babinski sign in the same limb. Why do signs of both a lower and an upper motor neuron lesion appear together? The answer is that the disease attacks the motor neurons of the cord and the corticospinal tract at once. The wasted hand reflects the first loss and the brisk reflex the second.

Question 5890. A described magnetic resonance image shows a small bright lesion deep in the left hemisphere, tucked between the lentiform nucleus and the thalamus, and the right side of the body is weak. Which structure is affected, and why is the deficit so complete? The answer is the posterior limb of the internal capsule. Motor and sensory fibers from a wide area of cortex are crowded into that small space.

Question 5891. Which vessels supply the internal capsule: the lenticulostriate branches of the middle cerebral artery, the anterior cerebral artery, the posterior cerebral artery, or the vertebral artery? The answer is the lenticulostriate branches of the middle cerebral artery. They run from the base of the brain into the depths of the hemisphere. Their small size makes them a common site of bleeding.

Question 5892. The sensory and motor areas for the leg lie on the medial surface of the hemisphere. Which artery supplies that surface: the anterior cerebral artery, the middle cerebral artery, the posterior cerebral artery, or the basilar artery? The answer is the anterior cerebral artery. The middle cerebral artery supplies the lateral surface, where the face and the hand are represented.

Question 5893. Which of these pairs belongs together: the corticospinal tract with the posterior limb of the internal capsule, the corticobulbar fibers with the posterior limb, the optic radiation with the anterior limb, or the medial lemniscus with the anterior limb? The answer is the corticospinal tract with the posterior limb. The corticobulbar fibers lie at the genu.

Question 5894. True or false: the tracts that carry unconscious position sense to the cerebellum are relayed through the thalamus on the way. The answer is false. The spinocerebellar tracts reach the cerebellum directly, without a relay in the thalamus and without reaching conscious awareness. The dorsal tract stays on the same side of the cord throughout.

Question 5895. Which statement contrasts the two great sensory pathways from the body: the dorsal column crosses in the medulla and the pain pathway in the cord, the dorsal column crosses in the cord and the pain pathway in the medulla, both cross in the midbrain, or neither crosses? The answer is the first. That difference explains the mixed pattern of loss in a half-section of the cord.

Question 5896. A described specimen of the pons shows on each side of the midline a flattened band of fibers lying just behind the basilar part. Which tract is shown, and from where has it come? The answer is the medial lemniscus, which has come up from the medulla and carries the fibers of the dorsal column pathway.

Question 5897. True or false: the corticospinal tract is the only route by which the cerebral cortex influences movement. The answer is false. The cortex also sends fibers to the cranial nerve nuclei, to the basal ganglia, to the cerebellum by way of the pons, and to the centers of the brainstem.

Question 5898. A person cannot feel a pinprick on the right leg but feels the vibration of a tuning fork on that leg normally. Where is the lesion: the left spinothalamic tract, the right dorsal column, the right corticospinal tract, or the left medial lemniscus? The answer is the left spinothalamic tract, since the pain fibers have crossed while the position fibers for that leg still ascend on the right.

Question 5899. Which structure carries the third neuron of the pain pathway from the thalamus to the cerebral cortex? The answer is the posterior limb of the internal capsule. The fibers leave the ventral posterolateral nucleus and run through it in company with the motor fibers. They end in the postcentral gyrus.

Question 5900. A described section of the upper cervical cord shows a band of fibers in the lateral funiculus just behind the dorsal horn, and a second band in the anterior funiculus close to the midline. Which tracts are shown? The answer is the lateral and the anterior corticospinal tracts. The lateral one has already crossed and carries most of the fibers, while the anterior one crosses in the segment it supplies.

To summarize, the dorsal column and medial lemniscus pathway carries fine touch, vibration and position sense, ascends on the same side of the cord, crosses in the lower medulla, relays in the ventral posterolateral nucleus and ends in the postcentral gyrus. Pain and temperature from the body travel by the anterolateral system, whose fibers cross in the anterior white commissure within a segment or two of entry, while pain from the face crosses near the spinal nucleus of the trigeminal nerve and relays in the ventral posteromedial nucleus. The motor pathway begins in the precentral gyrus, passes through the genu and the posterior limb of the internal capsule, the crus cerebri and the pyramids, and crosses in the medulla to form the lateral corticospinal tract. The cortex also reaches the cranial nerve nuclei and the cerebellum by way of the pons, and the loops of the basal ganglia through the thalamus set the amount of movement rather than execute it. The cerebellum corrects movement as it happens, with the vermis serving posture and the hemispheres the limbs, and the spinocerebellar tracts bring it the unconscious sense of position without a relay in the thalamus.
