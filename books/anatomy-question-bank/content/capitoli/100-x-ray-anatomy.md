# Chapter 100 — X-ray Anatomy

Plain radiography is the oldest and still the most widely used view of the skeleton and the chest, and this chapter reads the anatomy that its shadows reveal. It begins with the densities that produce a radiograph, then follows the chest, the abdomen, the spine, the pelvis and the limbs, and it closes with the anatomy displayed by computed tomography. The chapter holds one hundred questions, and each one states its answer straight after the question and then explains why that answer is right. Nothing here depends on a figure, because every image is described in words. This is educational material, and it supports rather than replaces an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 9901. A described radiograph of the chest shows the lungs as dark areas, the ribs and the spine as bright white bands, and the heart as a gray shadow between them. Which of these substances absorbs the fewest of the rays that made the image? The answer is air. Air stops almost none of the beam, so the film beneath it is heavily exposed and appears dark. Fat, muscle and bone stop progressively more of it.

Question 9902. In a described radiograph a large white area has replaced part of a lung, and dark branching tubes run through the white area. What are those dark branching tubes? The answer is the bronchi. The air spaces around them are filled with fluid or cells and so look white. The bronchi still hold air, so they stay dark and stand out against the opaque lung.

Question 9903. A described radiograph of a long bone shows a bright white shell around a darker center. What are the two zones that the description names? The answer is the cortex outside and the medullary cavity within. The compact bone of the cortex is dense and stops many rays, so it looks white. The marrow cavity holds fat and loose tissue, stops few rays, and looks dark.

Question 9904. Which of these four substances casts the densest shadow on a radiograph: fat, air, muscle, or bone? The answer is bone. Density on a radiograph follows the amount of material that stops the beam. Bone carries calcium salts and stops more rays than muscle, fat or air. Metal stops still more of them and so looks brighter than bone.

Question 9905. A described radiograph shows fat as a darker gray than the muscle beside it. Which statement explains that difference: fat holds more calcium, fat stops fewer rays, fat lies deeper, or fat is thicker? The answer is that fat stops fewer rays. Fat is built of light elements with a low atomic number, so it absorbs less of the beam than muscle.

Question 9906. A constructed illustration: a child falls on an outstretched hand, and a described radiograph shows a band of cartilage across the lower end of the radius with a small fragment of bone beside it. Which structure has been injured? The answer is the growth plate, or physis. In a child that plate is the weakest part of the bone and gives way before the shaft breaks. Management belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 9907. A described radiograph shows a small rounded ossicle lying within a tendon beside the joint at the base of the first toe. What is that ossicle? The answer is a sesamoid bone. It develops inside a tendon where the tendon crosses a joint, and it is smooth and fully outlined by cortex. A fracture fragment would show an irregular edge instead.

Question 9908. A described radiograph of a child's wrist shows a dark band running across the end of each long bone. Which interpretation is right: a fracture, a growth plate, a nutrient canal, or a tumor? The answer is a growth plate. It lies between the shaft and the epiphysis and stays open until growth ends.

Question 9909. A described radiograph of the ankle shows a small separate bone behind the talus, with smooth white margins and a clear dark gap around it. What is it? The answer is an accessory ossicle. Accessory bones are common variants and are found beside many joints, and this one is a normal variant of the ankle. A fracture would show a ragged edge instead.

Question 9910. True or false: on a normal radiograph the periosteum of a long bone is visible as a thin white line along the shaft. The answer is false. The periosteum is too thin, and too close in density to the cortex, to be seen on a plain film. It becomes visible only when it is lifted and new bone forms beneath it, which is a sign of disease rather than a normal structure.

Question 9911. Which projection of the chest places the heart closest to the film and therefore shows its size most truly: posteroanterior, anteroposterior, lateral, or oblique? The answer is posteroanterior. In that view the beam passes from the back to the front, so the heart, which lies in front, is close to the film and only slightly enlarged by the projection. An anteroposterior film magnifies it.

Question 9912. Which of these pairs belongs together: the aortic knuckle with the stomach, the gastric bubble with the arch of the aorta, the psoas shadow with the muscle of the back wall, or the horizontal fissure with the left lung? The answer is the psoas shadow with the muscle of the back wall. The aortic knuckle belongs to the arch of the aorta, and the gastric bubble to the stomach.

Question 9913. On a described chest radiograph, which structure forms the upper part of the right border of the heart, above the right atrium? The answer is the superior vena cava. It runs down the right side of the mediastinum and its outer edge becomes the border. In an older person the ascending aorta may take its place, which is a common normal variation.

Question 9914. Which chamber of the heart lies immediately behind the lower part of the sternum on a described lateral radiograph? The answer is the right ventricle. It is the most anterior chamber and forms the front of the heart shadow. Behind it lie the left ventricle and the left atrium, and the esophagus runs down behind the left atrium.

Question 9915. A described chest radiograph taken at the bedside with the beam passing from front to back shows a heart shadow wider than half the chest. Which explanation fits: the heart is enlarged, the heart is magnified because it lies far from the film, the lungs are small, or the diaphragm is high? The answer is that the heart is magnified because it lies far from the film.

Question 9916. On a described posteroanterior chest radiograph, which set of ribs runs almost horizontally across the lung fields: the posterior ribs, the anterior ribs, the costal cartilages, or the clavicles? The answer is the posterior ribs. They leave the vertebrae, curve outward, and run across the film with only a slight downward slope. The anterior ribs slope steeply down toward the sternum.

Question 9917. A described chest radiograph shows the windpipe ending in a sharp angle where it divides into two dark tubes. What is that angle called? The answer is the carina. It lies at the level of the fourth thoracic vertebra and marks the last cartilage ring of the trachea.

Question 9918. A described chest radiograph shows a rounded white shadow beside the upper left border of the heart, with a concave notch below it. Which structure does that shadow represent, and what is the notch? The answer is the pulmonary trunk, with the pulmonary bay below it. The bay is the hollow where the left lung meets the mediastinum between the aortic arch and the heart.

Question 9919. Name the two structures that form the right border of the heart on a posteroanterior chest radiograph, spoken as a list. The answer is the superior vena cava above and the right atrium below. The vein runs down the right side of the mediastinum, and the atrium lies at the lower part of that border.

Question 9920. Which lobe of the lung lies immediately behind the lower part of the right border of the heart on a described chest radiograph? The answer is the right middle lobe. It sits between the horizontal fissure above and the oblique fissure below. Because it touches the heart, disease within it erases that part of the heart border.

Question 9921. A constructed illustration: a described chest radiograph after a fall shows a hazy white area that merges into the right heart border, so that the border can no longer be followed. Which lobe is affected? The answer is the right middle lobe. Its tissue lies against the heart, and when it fills with fluid or cells the two shadows join. This is the silhouette sign.

Question 9922. A described chest radiograph shows a thin white line running horizontally from the right hilum to the lateral chest wall. What is that line? The answer is the horizontal fissure of the right lung. It separates the right upper lobe above from the right middle lobe below.

Question 9923. Which of these appears on a described chest radiograph as a rounded dark area just below the left hemidiaphragm: the liver, the gastric bubble, the spleen, or the left kidney? The answer is the gastric bubble. Gas in the fundus of the stomach rises to the highest point under the left dome of the diaphragm when the person stands.

Question 9924. A described chest radiograph shows the sharp angle where the outer edge of the diaphragm meets the inner edge of the chest wall, and on one side that angle is blunted. Which space holds the fluid that rounds it off? The answer is the pleural space at the costophrenic angle. Fluid sinks to the lowest part of the pleural cavity when the person stands.

Question 9925. A constructed illustration: after a described injury to the lower chest, the angle between the diaphragm and the chest wall is blunted on the left and the base of the lung looks hazy. Which space holds the abnormality? The answer is the pleural space. Blood, fluid or air in that space separates the lung from the chest wall. Management belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 9926. On a normal described chest radiograph, which hemidiaphragm lies higher, the right or the left, or do the two lie at the same level? The answer is the right. The liver lies beneath the right dome and pushes it upward. The left dome sits lower because the stomach and the spleen lie beneath it.

Question 9927. Which of these pairs belongs together: the horizontal fissure with the left lung, the oblique fissure with the right middle lobe, the azygoesophageal recess with the right lung, or the gastric bubble with the pleura? The answer is the azygoesophageal recess with the right lung. The recess is the part of the right lung that reaches in beside the esophagus behind the heart.

Question 9928. On a described chest radiograph a soft tissue band runs down beside the spine behind the heart and forms a smooth curve with the mediastinum. Which vein lies there? The answer is the azygos vein. It arches forward over the right main bronchus and joins the superior vena cava, and its body fills the space beside the vertebral column behind the esophagus.

Question 9929. In the described upper chest, which structure lies immediately in front of the trachea as it runs down from the neck? The answer is the brachiocephalic artery, which crosses in front of the windpipe to reach the right side. The esophagus and the thoracic duct lie behind the trachea.

Question 9930. Which of these structures lies immediately behind the trachea in a described cross section of the upper chest: the esophagus, the aortic arch, the sternum, or the thymus? The answer is the esophagus. It begins in the neck directly behind the windpipe and keeps that relation down to the level of the carina.

Question 9931. A described chest radiograph is made with the beam passing from the back to the front of a standing person. Which plane of the body does the resulting shadow represent? The answer is the coronal plane. The image is a projection of the whole thickness of the chest onto one plane, so structures overlap.

Question 9932. A described chest radiograph shows a thin white stripe running down beside the trachea on the right, with air on both sides of it. What is that stripe? The answer is the right paratracheal stripe. It is formed by the wall of the trachea and the mediastinal pleura, seen edge-on between the air of the windpipe and the air of the right lung.

Question 9933. A described lateral chest radiograph shows a dark area behind the upper sternum and in front of the heart and the ascending aorta. What is the name of that space? The answer is the retrosternal clear space. The lung reaches forward into it on both sides, so it looks dark.

Question 9934. A described chest radiograph shows a widened soft tissue shadow beside the trachea on the right. Which structures lie in that space: the left lung, the lymph nodes beside the trachea, the pulmonary trunk, or the left atrium? The answer is the lymph nodes beside the trachea.

Question 9935. Which large vein lies immediately to the right of the trachea in the described upper chest? The answer is the superior vena cava. It is formed behind the right side of the sternum by the joining of the two brachiocephalic veins. It runs down beside the trachea and enters the right atrium.

Question 9936. A constructed illustration: a described chest radiograph shows the trachea and the heart pulled toward a dense white side of the chest, with the ribs crowded on that side. What process has occurred? The answer is collapse of a lung or a lobe. When lung volume is lost the mediastinum moves toward the loss, unlike an effusion, which pushes it away. Management belongs to the treating clinician.

Question 9937. A described chest radiograph shows white lines radiating from the hilum into the dark lung fields and dividing as they go. What are those lines? The answer is the pulmonary blood vessels. The arteries and the veins run beside the bronchi and branch with them, so their shadows taper as they run outward toward the edge of the lung.

Question 9938. Which of these structures forms the left border of the mediastinum in the described chest radiograph just above the heart, where the border turns inward: the left ventricle, the pulmonary trunk, the left atrium, or the aorta? The answer is the pulmonary trunk. The concavity it fills, between the aortic arch above and the left ventricle below, is the pulmonary bay.

Question 9939. A described lateral chest radiograph shows one hemidiaphragm outlined against the lung above it and a rounded dark area beneath it. Which structure lies there? The answer is the stomach, filled with gas. In a lateral film the two hemidiaphragms overlap, and the left dome lies lower and shows the gastric gas beneath it.

Question 9940. True or false: on a normal chest radiograph the left hilum usually sits slightly higher than the right. The answer is true. The left pulmonary artery arches over the left main bronchus, and that arch lifts the left hilar shadow above the right one.

Question 9941. On a described abdominal radiograph, which appearance identifies gas in the small bowel: lines that cross the whole width of the loop, lines that cross part of it, a single large bubble, or a smooth round shadow? The answer is lines that cross the whole width of the loop. Those lines are the valvulae conniventes.

Question 9942. A described abdominal radiograph shows several loops of gas-filled bowel in the middle of the abdomen, with thin lines that cross each loop from wall to wall. Which part of the bowel is shown? The answer is the jejunum. Its folds are close together and run right across the lumen.

Question 9943. On a described abdominal radiograph, which part of the bowel is recognized by sacculations that cross only part of its width, together with a wider caliber? The answer is the large intestine. The haustra are those sacculations, and they stop short of crossing the lumen because the taeniae coli bunch the wall between them.

Question 9944. On a described colon, between which three longitudinal bands do the haustra lie? The answer is the taeniae coli. The three flat bands run along the colon from the base of the appendix to the rectum. They are shorter than the colon itself, so the wall between them bulges outward as haustra.

Question 9945. Which parts of the colon lie behind the peritoneum and are therefore fixed to the posterior wall of the abdomen: the ascending and descending parts, the transverse and sigmoid parts, the sigmoid and the rectum, or the transverse colon and the cecum? The answer is the ascending and descending parts. The transverse colon and the sigmoid colon hang on a mesentery and move freely.

Question 9946. Which of these normally casts a dark shadow in the left upper abdomen on a described radiograph: the liver, the spleen, the stomach, or the left kidney? The answer is the stomach. Gas rises to its fundus, which lies under the left dome of the diaphragm.

Question 9947. A described abdominal radiograph shows a soft tissue band running downward and outward on each side of the lumbar spine and fading as it descends. What is it? The answer is the psoas muscle. It lies against the side of the vertebral bodies and is outlined by the fat around it.

Question 9948. A described erect abdominal radiograph shows a dark crescent of gas under the right dome of the diaphragm, with the liver below it. Where is that gas? The answer is in the peritoneal cavity, outside the bowel. Free gas rises to the highest point in the abdomen when the person stands.

Question 9949. On a described erect radiograph of the abdomen, gas that lies outside the bowel collects in which position: under the diaphragm, in the pelvis, beside the spine, or in the stomach? The answer is under the diaphragm. The peritoneal cavity is closed, so free gas rises to its highest point.

Question 9950. A constructed illustration: a described person has sudden severe pain in the upper abdomen, and an erect radiograph shows a dark crescent of gas under both domes of the diaphragm. Which structure is likely to have given way? The answer is the wall of a hollow organ, most often the stomach or the first part of the duodenum. Management belongs to the treating clinician.

Question 9951. A constructed illustration: a described abdominal radiograph of an older person shows a cluster of rounded dense shadows in the right upper quadrant below the ribs. Which organ lies there? The answer is the gallbladder. It rests on the under surface of the liver beside the duodenum, and calcified stones within it cast the shadows. Management belongs to the treating clinician.

Question 9952. A described abdominal radiograph shows a small dense shadow lying beside the transverse process of a lumbar vertebra, along the line a tube follows from the kidney to the bladder. Which structure does the shadow lie in? The answer is the ureter. It runs down the posterior wall of the abdomen in front of the transverse processes and crosses the pelvic brim to reach the bladder.

Question 9953. Name two structures whose outlines are often visible on a plain abdominal radiograph, spoken as a list. The answer is the kidneys and the psoas muscles. Fat around each kidney and beside each psoas muscle gives the outline, because fat stops fewer rays than the tissue it surrounds.

Question 9954. A described radiograph of the lumbar spine shows a row of square blocks of bone with dark bands between them. What are those dark bands? The answer is the intervertebral disks. They lie between the bodies of the vertebrae and are not themselves visible. The dark band is the space they occupy, and the bone above and below it is white.

Question 9955. True or false: on a plain radiograph the intervertebral disk can be seen as a gray band between two vertebrae. The answer is false. The disk is made of cartilage and fibrous tissue, which stop rays much as muscle does, so it casts no separate shadow of its own. Only the space between the vertebral bodies is seen, and narrowing of that space is the sign of a worn disk.

Question 9956. Which projection shows the dens of the second cervical vertebra clear of the other bones: the open mouth view, the lateral view, the anteroposterior view, or the oblique view? The answer is the open mouth view. The beam passes through the open mouth, and the dens is seen between the two halves of the arch of the first vertebra.

Question 9957. Which of these pairs belongs together: the dens with the occipitomental view, the ankle joint with the skyline view, the facial bones with the open mouth view, or the seventh cervical vertebra with the swimmer's view? The answer is the seventh cervical vertebra with the swimmer's view. That projection lowers the shoulder so the last cervical vertebra and the first thoracic vertebra can be seen.

Question 9958. A described lateral radiograph of the neck shows the line along the front of the vertebral bodies broken, with one body sitting in front of the one below it. Which structure has lost its alignment? The answer is a vertebral body, and the finding points to a dislocation of the facet joints.

Question 9959. In a described lateral radiograph of the neck, which structure lies immediately in front of the bodies of the cervical vertebrae? The answer is the prevertebral muscles, with the pharynx above and the esophagus below in front of them. Their soft tissue shadow lies against the vertebral column and can be measured.

Question 9960. On a described lateral radiograph of the neck, which vertebra is recognized by an anterior arch and a posterior arch with no body between them: the first, the second, the fourth, or the seventh? The answer is the first cervical vertebra. Its anterior arch carries a facet for the dens of the second vertebra.

Question 9961. On a described chest radiograph, which bone lies across the upper part of the chest and partly hides the apex of the lung: the clavicle, the first rib, the scapula, or the humerus? The answer is the clavicle. It runs from the sternum to the shoulder across the top of the chest.

Question 9962. A described radiograph of the hip shows a smooth curved line that runs along the lower border of the superior pubic ramus and continues along the medial border of the neck of the femur. What line is it? The answer is Shenton's line.

Question 9963. A described radiograph of the pelvis shows a smooth white line running forward along the brim of the true pelvis and ending at the pubic tubercle. Which line is shown? The answer is the iliopectineal line. It marks the brim of the pelvis and continues forward as the iliopubic line.

Question 9964. On a described radiograph of a child's pelvis, a Y-shaped band of cartilage lies in the depths of the acetabulum. Which parts of the hip bone meet at that cartilage: the ilium with the ischium and the pubis, the sacrum with the ilium, the femur with the pubis, or the ilium with the sacrum? The answer is the ilium with the ischium and the pubis. They meet in the triradiate cartilage, which closes after growth.

Question 9965. Which part of the hip bone lies immediately above the acetabulum on a described radiograph? The answer is the ilium. Its broad fan-shaped body forms the upper part of the acetabulum and the greater part of the pelvic wall. The ischium lies below and behind the acetabulum, and the pubis lies in front of it and below.

Question 9966. A constructed illustration: a described older person falls, the injured leg is shortened and turned outward, and the hip radiograph shows a break through the neck of the femur with the shaft riding upward. Which structure is injured? The answer is the neck of the femur. Blood reaches the head of the femur mainly along vessels in that neck, so the head is at risk. Management belongs to the treating clinician.

Question 9967. A described radiograph of the knee shows a deep dark hollow between the two rounded ends of the lower end of the femur. What is that hollow called? The answer is the intercondylar notch. The cruciate ligaments lie within it and are separated from the joint cavity only by their own covering.

Question 9968. A described radiograph of the knee shows a small rounded bone lying in a tendon at the back of the joint, behind the lateral condyle. Which bone is it: the patella, the fabella, the medial malleolus, or the head of the fibula? The answer is the fabella. It is a sesamoid bone in the tendon of the lateral head of the gastrocnemius and is a normal variant in many people.

Question 9969. A described radiograph of the knee shows a dark gap between the lower end of the femur and the upper end of the tibia. What lies in that gap? The answer is the articular cartilage covering the ends of the two bones, with the menisci between them. Cartilage casts almost no shadow, so the gap looks dark.

Question 9970. True or false: in a correctly positioned lateral radiograph of the knee the patella is superimposed on the lower end of the femur. The answer is false. A true lateral film shows the patella in profile in front of the femur and free of overlap. If it overlaps the femur, the knee is rotated and the film must be repeated.

Question 9971. A described radiograph of the knee shows two bony points rising from the upper surface of the tibia between the condyles. What are they: the tibial tuberosity, the intercondylar eminence, the head of the fibula, or the adductor tubercle? The answer is the intercondylar eminence. Its two spines give attachment to the cruciate ligaments and to the menisci.

Question 9972. Which of these pairs belongs together: the scaphoid with the open mouth view, the dens with a posteroanterior view of the wrist, the patella with a skyline view of the knee, or the ankle with a lateral view of the chest? The answer is the patella with a skyline view of the knee. In that view the beam runs along the back of the patella, which is shown in profile against the femur.

Question 9973. A described radiograph of the wrist shows three smooth curved lines that follow the rows of carpal bones. What are they? The answer is the carpal arcs. One arc follows the proximal row, one follows the distal row, and the third follows the bases of the metacarpal bones.

Question 9974. On a described radiograph of the hand, which carpal bone lies in the proximal row on the thumb side, next to the radius? The answer is the scaphoid. It lies in the floor of the hollow at the base of the thumb. The lunate lies beside it nearer the midline, and the triquetral lies on the little finger side.

Question 9975. A described radiograph shows a break running across the waist of a carpal bone that lies in the hollow at the base of the thumb. Which bone is it: the lunate, the scaphoid, the triquetral, or the pisiform? The answer is the scaphoid. It is the carpal bone most often broken, and its blood supply enters near the waist and runs backward, so the proximal part may lose its blood.

Question 9976. On computed tomography the density of a tissue is expressed relative to a reference substance. Which substance is used as that reference: air, water, fat, or bone? The answer is water. Water is given the value of zero on the scale, and other tissues are placed above or below it.

Question 9977. What is the name of the scale on which the density of a tissue is expressed on computed tomography? The answer is the Hounsfield scale. Its values run from air, far below water, to dense bone and metal, far above it, and water itself is the zero point.

Question 9978. In a described transverse image of the upper abdomen, which structure lies immediately to the right of the aorta at the level of the kidneys? The answer is the inferior vena cava. It lies in front of and to the right of the vertebral column, while the aorta lies in front of the column and slightly to the left.

Question 9979. On a described computed tomography scan of the abdomen displayed in the soft tissue window, which structures are shown at their best: the cortex of the bones, the muscles and the fat, the air in the lungs, or the trabeculae of bone? The answer is the muscles and the fat. The window spreads the gray tones across the range of soft tissue densities.

Question 9980. True or false: a single window setting of a computed tomography scan can show both the fine trabeculae of bone and the small differences between the abdominal organs. The answer is false. A window narrow enough to separate the soft tissues drives bone to one white value, and a window wide enough for bone hides those soft tissue differences.

Question 9981. Which window setting is used to look at the air spaces and the vessels of the lung on a described computed tomography scan of the chest: the brain window, the bone window, the lung window, or the soft tissue window? The answer is the lung window. It is wide and centered on air, so the thin walls of the airways and the fine vessels stand out against the dark lung.

Question 9982. What is the name of the plane in which the standard computed tomography images of the body are displayed, cutting the body across at right angles to its long axis? The answer is the transverse plane, also called the axial plane. Each image shows the structures of one slice as they lie from side to side.

Question 9983. Which of these pairs belongs together: the bone window with the gray and white matter, the brain window with the cortex of the skull, the lung window with the airways, or the soft tissue window with the air in the colon? The answer is the lung window with the airways. The brain window belongs with the gray matter and the white matter of the brain.

Question 9984. Which reconstruction plane of a described computed tomography scan gives a view from the front of the body, showing both kidneys and the aorta side by side: the axial plane, the coronal plane, the sagittal plane, or the oblique plane? The answer is the coronal plane. It is rebuilt from the axial data so that a frontal view of the abdomen can be followed.

Question 9985. Name two structures that appear denser than muscle on a computed tomography scan of the abdomen made without contrast, spoken as a list. The answer is bone and calcification, to which acute blood may be added. Bone carries calcium and reaches a far higher value than muscle.

Question 9986. A described image shows the body of a vertebra behind, the aorta in front of it and slightly to the left, and a kidney on each side. In which plane was this image made? The answer is the transverse plane. The image cuts the body across, so structures that lie one behind another on a frontal view are seen here side by side.

Question 9987. On a described transverse image of the abdomen, which vessel lies in the midline in front of the vertebral body and slightly to the left? The answer is the aorta. It descends in front of the spine and gives off the renal arteries and the mesenteric arteries. The inferior vena cava lies beside it on the right.

Question 9988. A described computed tomography scan of the head shows an outer band of gray tissue, an inner white matter, and dark fluid-filled spaces in the center. What is that outer band? The answer is the gray matter of the cerebral cortex. It lies on the surface of the brain and dips into the sulci.

Question 9989. In a described transverse image of the chest taken at the level of the carina, which structure lies immediately to the left of the esophagus and in front of the vertebral body? The answer is the descending thoracic aorta. It runs down beside the esophagus on the left.

Question 9990. A constructed illustration: a described person is brought in after a fall from a height, and a computed tomography scan of the thoracic spine shows a vertebral body broken with a fragment pushed backward into the canal. Which structure lies at risk? The answer is the spinal cord. It lies in the canal behind the body of the vertebra. Management belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 9991. In a described study in which contrast is given into a vein, which phase shows the arteries at their densest before the contrast has reached the organs: the phase before contrast, the arterial phase, the portal venous phase, or the delayed phase? The answer is the arterial phase. The portal venous phase follows it and shows the liver and the portal vein at their best.

Question 9992. What is the name of the large vessel that carries blood from the bowel and the spleen into the liver and appears as a bright branching structure in the center of the liver on a described contrast study? The answer is the portal vein. It is formed behind the neck of the pancreas by the joining of the splenic vein and the superior mesenteric vein.

Question 9993. A described transverse image of the upper abdomen shows a long lobulated organ lying across the midline behind the stomach and in front of the aorta and the left kidney. Which organ is it? The answer is the pancreas. Its head lies in the curve of the duodenum and its tail reaches the spleen.

Question 9994. Which of these organs lies behind the peritoneum and against the posterior wall of the abdomen: the stomach, the spleen, the left kidney, or the gallbladder? The answer is the left kidney. The kidneys, the pancreas, the aorta and the inferior vena cava all lie behind the peritoneum.

Question 9995. A constructed illustration: a described person is struck in the upper abdomen, and a computed tomography scan shows a collection of fluid behind the stomach and in front of the aorta. Which organ lies in that space and is most often injured? The answer is the pancreas. It lies across the posterior wall of the abdomen in front of the great vessels. Management belongs to the treating clinician.

Question 9996. Which substance appears black on both a plain radiograph and a computed tomography scan of the abdomen: fat, air, water, or muscle? The answer is air. It has the lowest density of the four, and both methods display it as darkness.

Question 9997. On a described computed tomography scan of the head, a bright ring of bone surrounds the brain, and a dense petrous part lies on each side at its base. Which structure forms the bright ring? The answer is the skull vault, formed by the frontal, the parietal and the occipital bones.

Question 9998. A described computed tomography image of the head shows a pair of dark butterfly-shaped spaces in the middle of the brain, filled with fluid and outlined by a thin white line. What are those spaces? The answer is the lateral ventricles. They lie one on each side of the midline within the cerebral hemispheres and contain cerebrospinal fluid.

Question 9999. A described computed tomography scan shows bright streaks radiating between two dense metal objects. What is that appearance called: a motion artifact, a beam hardening artifact, a partial volume artifact, or an artifact of the window? The answer is a beam hardening artifact. The dense objects filter the beam, and the reconstruction draws bright and dark bands around them.

Question 10000. A constructed illustration: a described person is brought in after a blow to the head, and the scan shows a lens-shaped collection of high density against the inner surface of the skull. Which space holds it? The answer is the space outside the dura mater, between the skull and the dura. A torn middle meningeal artery bleeds there. Management belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

To summarize, a radiograph is a shadow of the body in which air is the most radiolucent tissue and bone the densest, and the cortex, the medullary cavity, the growth plate, the sesamoid bone and the accessory ossicle each have their own appearance on it. The right border of the heart is formed by the superior vena cava above and the right atrium below, the left border by the left ventricle, the left atrial appendage and the pulmonary trunk, the right hemidiaphragm normally sits higher than the left, and loss of a border where the right middle lobe meets the heart is the silhouette sign. The plain abdominal film shows the valvulae conniventes of the small bowel, the haustra and the taeniae coli of the colon, the psoas shadows and the kidneys, while the spine, the pelvis and the limbs are read with Shenton's line, the carpal arcs and the intercondylar eminence in mind. On computed tomography the density of a tissue is given on the Hounsfield scale with water at the zero point, and the same data are displayed in bone, soft tissue, lung and brain windows and in the transverse, coronal and sagittal planes, with the aorta in front of and to the left of the vertebral body and the inferior vena cava to its right.
