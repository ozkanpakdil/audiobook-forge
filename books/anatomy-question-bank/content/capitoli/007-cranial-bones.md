# Chapter 7 — Cranial Bones

Bone has a small vocabulary of shapes, and this chapter tests that vocabulary before it turns to the bones of the skull. The first half covers general osteology: the processes that project from a bone, the tuberosities that give a tendon its grip, the condyles that carry a joint surface, the fossae that hold a neighbor, and the foramina that let vessels and nerves pass. The second half works through five cranial bones, the frontal, the parietal, the temporal, the occipital and the sphenoid. It holds one hundred questions, and every one gives its answer immediately after the question and then explains why. This is educational material, and it supports rather than replaces an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 601. Which term describes a projection that stands out from the surface of a bone, such as the spinous process of a vertebra? The answer is a process. A process grows out from the body or the shaft of a bone. Most processes give attachment to a muscle, a tendon or a ligament, and some also take part in a joint.

Question 602. Which of these is a process of the temporal bone: the mastoid process, the coronoid process, the olecranon process, or the xiphoid process? The answer is the mastoid process, the rounded projection felt behind the ear. The coronoid process belongs to the ulna and to the mandible, the olecranon process to the ulna, and the xiphoid process to the lower end of the sternum.

Question 603. In a vertebra, which process projects backward in the midline, and which two project to the sides? The answer is the spinous process behind and the transverse processes at the sides. The spinous process can be felt down the middle of the back. Each transverse process carries a facet for a rib in the thoracic region.

Question 604. The zygomatic arch is built from two processes. Which one forms its front part: the temporal process of the zygomatic bone, the zygomatic process of the temporal bone, the frontal process of the maxilla, or the mastoid process? The answer is the temporal process of the zygomatic bone, which meets the zygomatic process of the temporal bone behind it.

Question 605. Which slender, pointed process hangs down from the under surface of the temporal bone, just in front of the mastoid process? The answer is the styloid process. Several small muscles of the tongue and the throat take origin from it, and the stylohyoid ligament runs from its tip to the hyoid bone. It is long in some people and short in others.

Question 606. The styloid process lies in which direction from the mastoid process, and which large vessel lies just medial to its tip? The answer is that it lies anterior and medial to the mastoid process, with the internal carotid artery just medial to its tip. The internal jugular vein lies lateral to that artery in the same sheath.

Question 607. A constructed illustration: an elongated styloid process is felt through the wall of the tonsillar fossa during an examination of the throat, and its tip lies beside the internal carotid artery. Which structure is being felt, and which vessel lies next to it? The answer is the styloid process, beside the internal carotid artery. This elongation is a variation and is usually without symptoms.

Question 608. A described radiograph of the elbow taken from the side shows a beak-shaped projection at the front of the upper end of the ulna, fitting into a hollow of the humerus when the arm is bent. Which structure is shown? The answer is the coronoid process of the ulna. It lies below the trochlear notch and is received by the coronoid fossa.

Question 609. Which process of the scapula forms the outer point of the shoulder and can be felt under the skin: the acromion, the coracoid process, the glenoid process, or the styloid process? The answer is the acromion, the flattened process that continues the spine of the scapula. The coracoid process lies in front of it and is covered by muscle.

Question 610. Name two processes of the temporal bone, spoken as a list. The answer is the mastoid process and the styloid process. The mastoid process is the rounded projection behind the ear canal, and the styloid process is the slender spike in front of it. The zygomatic process of the same bone forms the back of the cheek arch.

Question 611. A described dissection of the upper arm shows a rough elevation on the lateral surface of the middle of the humerus, where the deltoid muscle pulls on the bone. Which structure is shown? The answer is the deltoid tuberosity. It is a roughened area rather than a spike, which is what gives a muscle a firm grip on the shaft.

Question 612. On the upper end of the radius, which rounded roughened area receives the tendon of the biceps brachii? The answer is the radial tuberosity. It lies just below the neck of the radius, on the side that faces the ulna. The tendon of the biceps brachii turns around the bone to reach it.

Question 613. Which of these pairs belongs together: the radial tuberosity with the radius, the deltoid tuberosity with the ulna, the ischial tuberosity with the femur, or the tibial tuberosity with the fibula? The answer is the radial tuberosity with the radius. The deltoid tuberosity lies on the humerus, the ischial tuberosity on the hip bone, and the tibial tuberosity on the tibia.

Question 614. Which bony prominence of the hip bone bears the weight of the body in sitting and gives origin to the hamstring muscles behind the thigh? The answer is the ischial tuberosity, at the lower and back part of the hip bone. A bursa separates it from the skin, which is why it can be sat upon for long periods.

Question 615. A constructed illustration: a person reports soreness at the lowest point of the buttock after a long ride on a hard seat. Which bony structure is being pressed, and what lies between that bone and the skin? The answer is the ischial tuberosity, with a bursa between the bone and the skin. The bursa is a normal cushion.

Question 616. Which prominence stands out on the front of the upper end of the tibia, just below the knee, and receives the patellar ligament? The answer is the tibial tuberosity, the bony point felt below the kneecap. The tendon of the quadriceps muscle continues over the patella and attaches here.

Question 617. Which of these is a tubercle rather than a tuberosity: the greater tubercle of the humerus, the deltoid tuberosity, the radial tuberosity, or the ischial tuberosity? The answer is the greater tubercle of the humerus. A tubercle is a smaller rounded prominence, and a tuberosity is a larger one. Both tubercles of the humerus lie at its upper end.

Question 618. A described radiograph of the knee from the side shows a raised bony point on the front of the upper end of the tibia, separated from the shaft by a line that has not yet closed in a growing child. Which structure is shown? The answer is the tibial tuberosity, whose own center of bone joins the shaft in the late teens.

Question 619. The ischial tuberosity lies in which direction from the acetabulum, and which strong ligament is attached to its upper border? The answer is that it lies below and behind the acetabulum, and the sacrotuberous ligament is attached to its upper border. That ligament runs up to the sacrum and helps to close the lesser sciatic notch.

Question 620. Why does the deltoid tuberosity lie on the lateral surface of the humerus while the radial tuberosity faces medially? The answer is that each lies in the line of pull of its own muscle. The deltoid pulls on the shaft from the side, and the tendon of the biceps brachii crosses to the medial side of the radius.

Question 621. What is the name for the smooth, rounded articular surface at the end of a bone, such as the two at the lower end of the femur? The answer is a condyle. A condyle is covered with hyaline cartilage and takes part in a joint. It differs from a process, which is a projection for the attachment of soft tissue.

Question 622. Which two structures at the lower end of the femur are smooth, rounded and covered with cartilage for the knee joint? The answer is the medial condyle and the lateral condyle. They are separated behind by the intercondylar fossa and in front by a shallow groove for the patella.

Question 623. Which of these is a condyle: the occipital condyle, the mastoid process, the deltoid tuberosity, or the styloid process? The answer is the occipital condyle. The two occipital condyles lie on either side of the foramen magnum and articulate with the first cervical vertebra. The other three are processes or a tuberosity.

Question 624. Between the two condyles of the femur, which depression lies behind, and what crosses it? The answer is the intercondylar fossa, crossed by the cruciate ligaments. The anterior and posterior cruciate ligaments pass through it in the middle of the knee. In front the two condyles are joined by a smooth surface for the patella.

Question 625. Which rounded projection forms the part of the mandible that fits into the joint with the temporal bone: the coronoid process, the condylar process, the angle of the mandible, or the mental protuberance? The answer is the condylar process, whose rounded head is the condyle of the mandible. The coronoid process lies in front of it.

Question 626. A described radiograph of the knee from the front shows two large rounded shadows at the lower end of the femur, separated in the middle by a notch. Which structures are shown? The answer is the medial and lateral condyles of the femur, with the intercondylar fossa between them. The shadow of the patella overlaps them in this view.

Question 627. A constructed illustration: a fall onto the point of the chin drives the lower jaw backward, and the force is carried along the bone. Which part of the mandible is most likely to break, and why? The answer is the neck of the mandible, below the condyle. It is the thinnest part of the bone on that path.

Question 628. Which statement about a condyle is correct: it is covered with hyaline cartilage, it is a rough area for tendon attachment, it is a hole for a vessel, or it is a depression that holds a nerve? The answer is that it is covered with hyaline cartilage, which lets the two surfaces of a joint glide on one another. A rough area for a tendon is a tuberosity.

Question 629. True or false: the occipital condyles lie on either side of the foramen magnum and articulate with the first cervical vertebra. The answer is true. Each condyle is a rounded surface that meets the corresponding facet of the atlas. This joint allows the head to nod, while turning the head happens lower down.

Question 630. In the knee seen from the front, which condyle of the femur lies nearer the midline, and which structure separates the two condyles at the back? The answer is the medial condyle, with the intercondylar fossa between them behind. The medial condyle reaches farther down than the lateral one, which is why the femur stands slightly oblique.

Question 631. Which hollow on the back of the lower end of the humerus receives the tip of the olecranon when the elbow is straightened? The answer is the olecranon fossa. It is a deep depression just above the trochlea, and the bone forming its floor is thin. The olecranon slides into it as the arm straightens.

Question 632. A described radiograph of the lower end of the humerus from the front shows two shallow hollows above the trochlea, one in the middle and one to the outer side. Which structures are shown? The answer is the coronoid fossa in the middle and the radial fossa to the outer side. The coronoid process and the head of the radius enter them when the elbow bends.

Question 633. Which of these is a depression on the scapula: the supraspinous fossa, the olecranon fossa, the iliac fossa, or the acetabular fossa? The answer is the supraspinous fossa, which lies above the spine of the scapula and holds the supraspinatus muscle. The olecranon fossa lies on the humerus, the iliac fossa on the hip bone.

Question 634. Which of these pairs belongs together: the supraspinous fossa with the scapula, the olecranon fossa with the hip bone, the iliac fossa with the knee, or the acetabular fossa with the shoulder? The answer is the supraspinous fossa with the scapula. The infraspinous fossa lies below the spine of the same bone, and the subscapular fossa on its front.

Question 635. Which of these is a broad, smooth hollow on the inner surface of the hip bone that gives origin to a muscle of the same name: the iliac fossa, the olecranon fossa, the coronoid fossa, or the radial fossa? The answer is the iliac fossa, which gives origin to the iliacus muscle and forms part of the wall of the false pelvis.

Question 636. In a described radiograph of the straight elbow taken from behind, the tip of the ulna lies in a hollow in the lower end of the humerus. Which hollow is it, and what happens when the elbow bends? The answer is the olecranon fossa. When the elbow bends, the olecranon leaves it and the coronoid process enters the hollow in front.

Question 637. A constructed illustration: after a fall on the elbow the arm cannot be straightened fully, and a described radiograph shows a small piece of bone resting in the hollow behind the lower humerus. Which hollow is involved? The answer is the olecranon fossa, because the loose fragment blocks the tip of the olecranon from entering it.

Question 638. Which of these fossae lies inside the socket of the hip joint and does not bear weight: the acetabular fossa, the iliac fossa, the olecranon fossa, or the supraspinous fossa? The answer is the acetabular fossa, a depression at the bottom of the acetabulum. It holds fat and the ligament of the head of the femur, while the horseshoe-shaped surface around it takes the load.

Question 639. Which of these pairs belongs together: the olecranon fossa with the elbow, the iliac fossa with the ankle, the acetabular fossa with the shoulder, or the popliteal fossa with the wrist? The answer is the olecranon fossa with the elbow. The iliac fossa belongs to the hip bone, the acetabular fossa to the hip joint, and the popliteal fossa to the back of the knee.

Question 640. Name the three cranial fossae, from front to back, spoken as a list. The answer is the anterior cranial fossa, the middle cranial fossa and the posterior cranial fossa. The front of the cranial base lies highest and the back lies lowest, so the floor steps down as it runs backward.

Question 641. What is the name for an opening or a passage through a bone, through which vessels and nerves travel? The answer is a foramen, and its plural is foramina. A foramen may be a short hole or a long canal. The skeleton is full of them, including one between each pair of vertebrae.

Question 642. Which opening in the occipital bone is the largest foramen in the body, and what passes through it? The answer is the foramen magnum. The medulla oblongata and its membranes pass through it, and so do the two vertebral arteries and the eleventh cranial nerve. The spinal cord becomes the medulla just above this opening.

Question 643. The vertebral arteries enter the skull through which opening: the foramen magnum, the jugular foramen, the foramen ovale, or the optic canal? The answer is the foramen magnum. The two arteries climb through the openings in the transverse processes of the cervical vertebrae and then turn inward to reach it. The jugular foramen carries the internal jugular vein.

Question 644. The intervertebral foramina lie between which parts of neighboring vertebrae, and what passes through each one? The answer is that each lies between the lower part of one vertebral body and the upper part of the next, behind the disc, and a spinal nerve passes through it. The opening is bounded behind by the facet joint.

Question 645. A constructed illustration: a disc between two lumbar vertebrae bulges backward and to one side, narrowing the opening through which a spinal nerve leaves the canal. Which opening is narrowed, and which structure is compressed? The answer is the intervertebral foramen, and the spinal nerve within it is compressed.

Question 646. Which of these statements about the obturator foramen is correct: it lies below and in front of the acetabulum, it transmits the sciatic nerve, it lies above the hip socket, or it is a joint surface? The answer is that it lies below and in front of the acetabulum. It is bounded by the pubis and the ischium and carries the obturator nerve and vessels.

Question 647. A described radiograph of the pelvis shows a large oval opening on each side, below and in front of the rounded socket of the hip joint. Which structure is shown, and which nerve passes through it? The answer is the obturator foramen, with the obturator nerve passing through its upper part. The opening is closed by a fibrous membrane that the nerve pierces.

Question 648. The jugular foramen lies between which two cranial bones, and which large vein runs through it? The answer is that it lies between the occipital bone and the temporal bone, and the internal jugular vein runs through it. The ninth, tenth and eleventh cranial nerves also pass through it, just behind the carotid canal.

Question 649. True or false: a foramen and a fossa both describe an opening in bone. The answer is false. A foramen is an opening through which something passes, while a fossa is a shallow depression that holds a neighboring structure. The olecranon fossa holds the tip of the ulna, and the foramen magnum lets the spinal cord pass.

Question 650. Which of these pairs belongs together: the nutrient foramen with the artery that feeds the marrow, the nutrient foramen with a nerve of the skin, the optic canal with the vertebral artery, or the obturator foramen with the optic nerve? The answer is the nutrient foramen with the artery that feeds the marrow. It is a small channel in the shaft, and it runs away from the growing end.

Question 651. Which bone forms the forehead, the roof of each orbit and the front part of the floor of the cranial cavity? The answer is the frontal bone. It is a single flat bone in the adult, though it begins as two halves that join at the frontal suture. The frontal sinus lies inside it above the root of the nose.

Question 652. Which opening lies at the upper margin of the orbit and carries the supraorbital nerve and vessels: the supraorbital foramen, the infraorbital foramen, the optic canal, or the mental foramen? The answer is the supraorbital foramen, which is a notch in some people rather than a closed hole. The infraorbital foramen lies below the orbit.

Question 653. A described radiograph of the skull from the front shows two air-filled spaces above the orbits, one on each side of a thin midline partition. Which structures are shown, and in which bone do they lie? The answer is the frontal sinuses, lying in the frontal bone. They drain into the nasal cavity through a duct in the front of the middle meatus.

Question 654. The frontal bone articulates with the two parietal bones at which suture: the coronal suture, the sagittal suture, the lambdoid suture, or the squamous suture? The answer is the coronal suture, which runs across the vault from side to side. The sagittal suture joins the two parietal bones to each other, and the lambdoid suture joins the occipital bone to them.

Question 655. The frontal sinus lies in which part of the frontal bone, and into which part of the nasal cavity does it drain? The answer is that it lies between the two tables of the bone above the orbit, and it drains into the middle meatus of the nasal cavity. It is small or absent in some people, and its size varies widely.

Question 656. A constructed illustration: a blow lands on the brow just above the eye, and blood later collects in the orbit around the eyeball. Which thin part of the frontal bone is most likely broken? The answer is the orbital plate, which forms the roof of the orbit and the floor of the front of the cranial cavity. It is thin enough to crack under a blow to the brow.

Question 657. On the inner surface of the frontal bone, the frontal crest gives attachment to which structure: the falx cerebri, the tentorium cerebelli, the falx cerebelli, or the diaphragm? The answer is the falx cerebri. Below the crest a groove called the sagittal sulcus runs down and lodges the superior sagittal sinus. The tentorium cerebelli is attached to other bones.

Question 658. True or false: the frontal bone forms part of the floor of the anterior cranial fossa and part of the roof of each orbit. The answer is true. The same thin plate of bone serves as the floor of the cranial cavity above and the roof of the orbit below. That is why a blow to the brow can damage both regions at once.

Question 659. Which of these pairs belongs together: the frontal bone with the supraorbital margin, the maxilla with the supraorbital margin, the parietal bone with the eyebrow, or the occipital bone with the orbit? The answer is the frontal bone with the supraorbital margin, the arched upper edge of the orbit. The infraorbital margin below the orbit is formed by the maxilla and the zygomatic bone.

Question 660. In a sagittal section through the head, the frontal sinus lies behind which ridge of the frontal bone, and above which cavity? The answer is that it lies behind the superciliary arch and above the orbit. It sits between the front and back tables of the bone, close to the midline, and only a thin plate separates it from the cranial cavity behind.

Question 661. Which bone forms the greater part of the roof and the side wall of the skull and is named from the Latin word for wall? The answer is the parietal bone. There are two of them, one on each side, and they meet in the midline above. Each is roughly quadrilateral and curved like a shell.

Question 662. Which suture joins the two parietal bones to each other in the midline of the vault: the sagittal suture, the coronal suture, the lambdoid suture, or the squamous suture? The answer is the sagittal suture, running from front to back between the two bones. The coronal suture lies in front of them and the lambdoid suture behind.

Question 663. Which bone carries the superior and inferior temporal lines and gives attachment to the temporalis muscle and its fascia below the lower line? The answer is the parietal bone. The lines curve across its outer surface. The temporalis muscle arises from the whole of the temporal fossa, which the parietal bone helps to form.

Question 664. How many other cranial bones does each parietal bone articulate with, and which are they? The answer is five: the frontal, the occipital, the temporal, the sphenoid and the parietal bone on the other side. Four of those joints are sutures, and the sphenoid meets the parietal bone only at its front lower corner.

Question 665. A described radiograph of the skull taken from above shows a midline suture running from front to back between two large curved bones, with a small opening near the back of the suture on one side. Which suture and which opening are shown? The answer is the sagittal suture and a parietal foramen, present in some skulls.

Question 666. When a parietal foramen is present, what does it transmit: a small vein to the superior sagittal sinus, the middle meningeal artery, the optic nerve, or the facial nerve? The answer is a small vein to the superior sagittal sinus. The opening lies near the upper border of the parietal bone and is a variation present in only some skulls.

Question 667. A constructed illustration: an infant has a soft, pulsating spot on the top of the head, where four bones meet and the sutures have not yet closed. Which region is this, and which four bones border it? The answer is the anterior fontanelle, bordered by the two frontal bones and the two parietal bones. It closes in the second year of life.

Question 668. True or false: the inner surface of the parietal bone is marked by grooves for the middle meningeal vessels and for the superior sagittal sinus. The answer is true. The grooves are deeper than the smooth outer surface suggests, and the bone between them can be thin. The outer surface is convex and smooth.

Question 669. A described view of the vault from above shows four corners on each parietal bone: a front upper angle, a front lower angle, a back upper angle and a back lower angle. Which angle lies at the bregma? The answer is the front upper angle. It meets the frontal bone at the bregma, where the coronal and sagittal sutures cross.

Question 670. A fracture of the parietal bone tears a vessel running in a groove on its inner surface, and blood collects outside the dura. Which vessel is most likely torn: the anterior branch of the middle meningeal artery, a diploic vein, the superior sagittal sinus, or a cerebral vein? The answer is the anterior branch of the middle meningeal artery.

Question 671. Which bone of the skull contains the organs of hearing and balance and carries the mastoid process behind the ear? The answer is the temporal bone. It also forms part of the side wall and the base of the skull. The lower jaw articulates with it, and several cranial nerves pass through it.

Question 672. Which part of the temporal bone is the hard, dense pyramid that houses the internal ear: the petrous part, the squamous part, the mastoid part, or the tympanic part? The answer is the petrous part. It is wedged between the sphenoid bone in front and the occipital bone behind. The squamous part is the thin fan of bone above the ear.

Question 673. The temporal bone is described as having four parts. Which part lies above the ear canal, and which part forms the bony canal itself? The answer is the squamous part above and the tympanic part for the canal. The mastoid part lies behind the canal, and the petrous part forms the dense wedge of the base.

Question 674. Which opening lies at the base of the skull just behind the mastoid process and lets the seventh cranial nerve leave the skull? The answer is the stylomastoid foramen. It lies between the styloid process in front and the mastoid process behind. The facial nerve leaves through it and then turns forward into the parotid gland.

Question 675. A described radiograph of the base of the skull shows a dense triangular shadow of bone on each side, pointing forward and inward, with the ear structures inside it. Which part of which bone is shown? The answer is the petrous part of the temporal bone. Its apex points toward the body of the sphenoid.

Question 676. A constructed illustration: a blow to the side of the head is followed by bleeding from the ear and bruising of the skin behind the ear. Which part of which bone is most likely fractured, and which nerve may be injured as it runs through it? The answer is the petrous part of the temporal bone, with injury to the facial nerve.

Question 677. Which structures pass through the internal acoustic meatus: the facial nerve and the vestibulocochlear nerve, the internal carotid artery, the internal jugular vein, or the glossopharyngeal nerve? The answer is the facial nerve and the vestibulocochlear nerve, the seventh and eighth cranial nerves. The labyrinthine artery runs with them to the internal ear.

Question 678. The mandibular fossa, which receives the condyle of the mandible, lies on which part of the temporal bone: the squamous part, the petrous part, the mastoid part, or the tympanic part? The answer is the squamous part, below the root of the zygomatic process. A small ridge, the postglenoid tubercle, lies just behind it.

Question 679. Which part of the temporal bone forms the rounded boss that can be felt behind the ear, and which muscle of the neck attaches to it? The answer is the mastoid part, and the sternocleidomastoid muscle attaches to it. The mastoid air cells inside it connect with the middle ear. It is small in some people and large in others.

Question 680. True or false: the jugular foramen lies between the temporal bone and the occipital bone, and the internal jugular vein begins there. The answer is true. The sigmoid sinus, a venous channel inside the skull, turns downward and becomes the internal jugular vein as it leaves through that opening. The ninth, tenth and eleventh cranial nerves leave with it.

Question 681. Which bone forms the back and the greater part of the base of the skull and carries the large opening for the spinal cord? The answer is the occipital bone. It is pierced by the foramen magnum and bears the two occipital condyles. Part of it is laid down in cartilage and part in membrane.

Question 682. The occipital condyles articulate with which bone: the first cervical vertebra, the second cervical vertebra, the temporal bone, or the sphenoid bone? The answer is the first cervical vertebra, the atlas. That joint allows the head to nod forward and backward. Turning the head from side to side happens between the first and second cervical vertebrae.

Question 683. The occipital bone is described in four parts around the foramen magnum. Which part lies in front of the opening, and which parts lie at its sides? The answer is the basilar part in front and the two condylar parts at the sides. The squamous part lies behind the opening and forms the back of the skull.

Question 684. A described view of the base of the skull shows a large opening in the middle, with two smooth rounded surfaces on either side of it and a rough ridge running backward in the midline. Which structures are shown? The answer is the foramen magnum, the two occipital condyles and the external occipital crest.

Question 685. The hypoglossal canal lies in which position and carries which cranial nerve: above and in front of the occipital condyle, carrying the twelfth cranial nerve; behind the condyle, carrying the eleventh cranial nerve; below the condyle, carrying the ninth cranial nerve; or inside the foramen magnum, carrying the tenth cranial nerve? The answer is above and in front of the occipital condyle, carrying the twelfth cranial nerve.

Question 686. A constructed illustration: after a fall from a height, a described radiograph suggests a fracture running through the base of the skull beside the foramen magnum. Which bone is involved, and which cranial nerve may be injured as it leaves through its own small canal? The answer is the occipital bone, with injury to the twelfth cranial nerve in the hypoglossal canal.

Question 687. Why is the occipital bone unusual among the bones of the skull: it develops partly in cartilage and partly in membrane, it is the only bone with no joints, it contains no marrow, or it never fuses into one bone? The answer is that it develops partly in cartilage and partly in membrane. The basilar and condylar parts form in cartilage, while the upper part of the squamous part forms in membrane.

Question 688. A described view of the inside of the base of the skull shows a raised cross-shaped ridge at the back, with a groove running forward from it and another running to each side. Which structure is shown, and what do the grooves carry? The answer is the internal occipital protuberance, with grooves for the superior sagittal sinus in front and the transverse sinuses at the sides.

Question 689. Name the three structures that pass through the foramen magnum, spoken as a list. The answer is the medulla oblongata, the two vertebral arteries and the eleventh cranial nerve. The meninges pass with the medulla, and a small artery of the dura also runs through. This is the largest foramen in the skeleton.

Question 690. Which statement about the external occipital protuberance is correct: it gives attachment to the ligamentum nuchae and neck muscles, it articulates with the atlas, it contains the occipital sinus, or it belongs to the temporal bone? The answer is that it gives attachment to the ligamentum nuchae and the muscles of the back of the neck. It lies in the midline at the back of the head.

Question 691. Which bone lies in the middle of the base of the skull and is called the keystone of the cranial floor because it joins with almost every other cranial bone? The answer is the sphenoid bone. It has a body, two greater wings, two lesser wings and two pterygoid processes. The pituitary gland sits in a hollow on its upper surface.

Question 692. In which bone does the sella turcica with its hypophyseal fossa lie, and what does the hollow hold: the sphenoid bone, holding the pituitary gland; the occipital bone, holding the medulla; the temporal bone, holding the internal ear; or the frontal bone, holding the olfactory bulbs? The answer is the sphenoid bone, holding the pituitary gland. The hollow is roofed by a fold of dura.

Question 693. A described radiograph of the base of the skull shows a butterfly-shaped bone in the middle, with a hollow like a Turkish saddle on its upper surface and two wings sweeping out to each side. Which bone is shown? The answer is the sphenoid bone, with the sella turcica on its body and the greater wings to each side.

Question 694. The optic canal lies between which two parts of the sphenoid bone, and which two structures pass through it? The answer is that it lies between the two roots of the lesser wing, and the optic nerve and the ophthalmic artery pass through it. It opens into the orbit, and the optic chiasma lies just behind it.

Question 695. A constructed illustration: a growth at the base of the skull compresses the structures passing through the gap between the greater and the lesser wings of the sphenoid. Which gap is described, and which nerves lie in it? The answer is the superior orbital fissure, carrying the third, fourth and sixth cranial nerves and the ophthalmic division of the fifth.

Question 696. Which opening in the greater wing of the sphenoid transmits the maxillary division of the fifth cranial nerve: the foramen rotundum, the foramen ovale, the foramen spinosum, or the optic canal? The answer is the foramen rotundum. The foramen ovale carries the mandibular division of the same nerve, and the foramen spinosum carries the middle meningeal artery.

Question 697. A described section through the middle of the skull shows a hollow behind the orbits whose floor is formed by a bone with two wings, and the hollow contains a rounded gland on a stalk. Which hollow is shown, and which bone forms its floor? The answer is the hypophyseal fossa, formed by the body of the sphenoid. The gland is the pituitary.

Question 698. Which of these statements about the pterygoid processes of the sphenoid is correct: they hang below the base of the skull and give origin to jaw muscles, they form the back of the orbit, they articulate with the atlas, or they carry the internal carotid artery? The answer is that they hang below the base of the skull and give origin to jaw muscles. Each has a medial and a lateral plate.

Question 699. Which two openings lie in the greater wing of the sphenoid bone, one carrying the maxillary division and the other the mandibular division of the fifth cranial nerve? The answer is the foramen rotundum and the foramen ovale. The foramen rotundum lies in front of the foramen ovale. Behind the foramen ovale is a third, smaller opening, the foramen spinosum.

Question 700. Why is the sphenoid bone called the keystone of the cranial base: it joins almost every other cranial bone and holds the pituitary, it is the largest bone of the skull, it is the only bone that moves, or it contains the inner ear? The answer is that it joins almost every other cranial bone and holds the pituitary. Its parts meet the frontal, temporal, occipital, ethmoid and palatine bones.

To summarize, the skeleton names its shapes with a small set of words: a process projects, a tuberosity gives a tendon its grip, a condyle carries a cartilage-covered joint surface, a fossa is a shallow hollow that holds a neighbor, and a foramen is an opening for a vessel or a nerve. Each of those shapes lies where the soft tissue that uses it must pull or pass, so the deltoid tuberosity sits on the lateral humerus and the nutrient foramen runs away from the growing end of the bone. The frontal bone builds the forehead, the orbital roof and the front of the cranial floor, and the two parietal bones meet in the sagittal suture. The temporal bone carries the ear and the mastoid process, the occipital bone carries the foramen magnum and the occipital condyles, and the sphenoid bone joins almost every other cranial bone around the pituitary.
