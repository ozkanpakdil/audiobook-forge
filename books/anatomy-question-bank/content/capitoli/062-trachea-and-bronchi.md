# Chapter 62 — Trachea and Bronchi

This chapter follows the airway from the voice box down to the branches inside the lung. It begins with the larynx, where the laryngeal nerves control the voice and guard the entrance to the airway, and then turns to the trachea and the bronchi, their shape, their relations in the neck and the chest, and the way they divide. It holds one hundred questions, and every question gives its answer immediately afterwards, so nothing has to be held in mind until the end. The explanation comes in the same paragraph, and it often says why the tempting wrong answer is wrong as well. This is educational material, and it supports rather than replaces an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 6101. Which nerve of the neck carries the motor supply to the cricothyroid muscle, the muscle that tenses the vocal cord? The answer is the external laryngeal nerve. It is the smaller of the two branches of the superior laryngeal nerve, which itself arises from the vagus, the tenth cranial nerve. The recurrent laryngeal nerve supplies the other intrinsic muscles of the larynx.

Question 6102. Which of these structures pierces the thyrohyoid membrane to reach the lining of the larynx above the vocal folds: the internal laryngeal nerve, the external laryngeal nerve, the recurrent laryngeal nerve, or the hypoglossal nerve? The answer is the internal laryngeal nerve. It carries sensation from the upper part of the laryngeal lining. The external laryngeal nerve stays outside the larynx and supplies the cricothyroid muscle.

Question 6103. A described dissection of the neck follows a small nerve running down beneath the sternothyroid muscle to reach the cricothyroid muscle. Which nerve is described? The answer is the external laryngeal nerve. It is the smaller branch of the superior laryngeal nerve and it carries no sensation from the larynx. The internal laryngeal nerve, by contrast, takes the sensory route through the thyrohyoid membrane.

Question 6104. A described sagittal section of the larynx shows two folds on each side, one above the other, with a pouch between them. Which is the lower fold? The answer is the vocal fold, which contains the vocal ligament. The upper fold is the vestibular fold, and the pouch is the laryngeal ventricle. The vocal folds guard the opening called the rima glottidis.

Question 6105. A constructed illustration: after an operation on the thyroid gland, a person speaks with a hoarse and breathy voice. Which nerve is most likely to have been injured? The answer is the recurrent laryngeal nerve. It climbs in the groove between the trachea and the esophagus, close to the inferior thyroid artery, and it supplies nearly all the laryngeal muscles. Injury leaves the vocal fold unable to close.

Question 6106. Which cartilage of the larynx is the only one that forms a complete ring, so that the airway cannot be closed by pressing on it? The answer is the cricoid cartilage. It is shaped like a signet ring, narrow in front and tall at the back. The thyroid cartilage and the epiglottis do not form complete rings.

Question 6107. The recurrent laryngeal nerve climbs upward in a groove between two structures of the neck and the upper chest. Which two? The answer is the trachea and the esophagus. That groove is called the tracheoesophageal groove. The nerve runs upward in it to reach the larynx, which is why disease of the esophagus or the trachea can damage the voice.

Question 6108. True or false: the recurrent laryngeal nerve supplies every intrinsic muscle of the larynx except one, and the exception is the cricothyroid. The answer is true. The cricothyroid is supplied by the external laryngeal nerve instead. All of the muscles that move the arytenoid cartilages receive their fibers from the recurrent laryngeal nerve.

Question 6109. Which muscle of the larynx is the only one that pulls the vocal folds apart and so opens the airway? The answer is the posterior cricoarytenoid muscle. It arises from the back of the cricoid cartilage and pulls the muscular process of the arytenoid backward. Its action is the opposite of the lateral cricoarytenoid muscle, which closes the folds.

Question 6110. Which statement about the cricothyroid muscle is correct: it tenses the vocal cords and raises the pitch, it relaxes them and lowers the pitch, it pulls the cords apart, or it closes the laryngeal inlet? The answer is that it tenses the vocal cords and raises the pitch. It tilts the thyroid cartilage forward on the cricoid. The pitch of the voice depends on that tension.

Question 6111. A described coronal section through the larynx shows a vocal fold on one side turned toward the middle of the airway, while the fold on the other side is held out to the side. What does this suggest? The answer is a paralyzed fold on the side turned inward. The arytenoid cartilage has swung toward the midline, carrying the vocal ligament with it.

Question 6112. Which of these pairs belongs together: the cricothyroid muscle with the internal laryngeal nerve, the posterior cricoarytenoid with the recurrent laryngeal nerve, the thyroarytenoid with the external laryngeal nerve, or the vocal fold with the hypoglossal nerve? The answer is the posterior cricoarytenoid with the recurrent laryngeal nerve. Every intrinsic muscle of the larynx is supplied by that nerve except the cricothyroid.

Question 6113. Name the two branches into which the superior laryngeal nerve divides, spoken as a sentence. The answer is the internal laryngeal nerve and the external laryngeal nerve. The internal branch is sensory and pierces the thyrohyoid membrane to supply the lining above the vocal folds. The external branch is motor and supplies the cricothyroid muscle.

Question 6114. A constructed illustration: a person with a large swelling of the aortic arch speaks with a hoarse voice, and no disease can be found in the larynx itself. Which nerve is compressed, and why that side? The answer is the left recurrent laryngeal nerve. It hooks around the arch of the aorta and lies against it, so a swelling there presses on it.

Question 6115. Which paired cartilage of the larynx carries the vocal process at its front and the muscular process at its side, and so steers the vocal fold? The answer is the arytenoid cartilage. The vocal ligament attaches to the vocal process. Muscles pull on the muscular process to open and to close the airway.

Question 6116. Which nerve carries sensation from the lining of the larynx above the vocal folds: the internal laryngeal nerve, the external laryngeal nerve, the recurrent laryngeal nerve, or the glossopharyngeal nerve? The answer is the internal laryngeal nerve. It pierces the thyrohyoid membrane beside the superior laryngeal artery. The recurrent laryngeal nerve supplies the lining below the folds instead.

Question 6117. Two structures pass through the thyrohyoid membrane side by side to reach the inside of the larynx. One of them is the internal laryngeal nerve. Which is the other? The answer is the superior laryngeal artery, a branch of the superior thyroid artery. The nerve lies just behind the artery as both pass through the membrane.

Question 6118. A described specimen shows a ring of cartilage shaped like a signet ring, narrow at the front and tall at the back, sitting below the thyroid cartilage. Which cartilage is shown? The answer is the cricoid cartilage. Its tall back part carries the arytenoid cartilages. It is the only complete ring in the airway.

Question 6119. A section cut across the larynx shows the two vocal folds facing each other with a narrow gap between them. In which plane was the section cut, and what is the gap called? The answer is the transverse plane, and the gap is the rima glottidis. A section along the length of the airway would show the folds from the side instead.

Question 6120. Which intrinsic muscle of the larynx runs from the thyroid cartilage to the arytenoid cartilage and shortens the vocal fold, so relaxing it and lowering the pitch? The answer is the thyroarytenoid muscle. Its inner fibers form the vocalis muscle, which lies beside the vocal ligament. Shortening the fold makes it thicker and lowers the note.

Question 6121. A constructed illustration: an emergency airway must be opened through the front of the neck, and the surgeon must avoid cutting the cricoid cartilage. Which space is used, and why is the cricoid left alone? The answer is the cricothyroid space between the thyroid cartilage and the cricoid. Damage to the cricoid can narrow the airway later.

Question 6122. The nerves of the larynx all arise from one cranial nerve. Which is it: the ninth, the tenth, the eleventh, or the twelfth? The answer is the tenth cranial nerve, the vagus. Its superior laryngeal branch divides into an internal and an external laryngeal nerve. Its recurrent branch climbs back up to the larynx.

Question 6123. The vocal ligament is a thickened band of elastic tissue. Between which two points does it stretch: the vocal process and the thyroid cartilage, the muscular process and the cricoid cartilage, the epiglottis and the arytenoid, or the corniculate and the cuneiform cartilages? The answer is the vocal process and the thyroid cartilage. Its free edge forms the vocal fold.

Question 6124. Which leaf-shaped cartilage of the larynx stands above the opening of the airway and folds down during swallowing? The answer is the epiglottis. Its stem is attached to the thyroid cartilage, and its broad upper part projects into the throat. It helps keep food out of the larynx.

Question 6125. Which of these pairs belongs together: the cricoid cartilage with a complete ring, the thyroid cartilage with the vocal ligament, the arytenoid cartilage with the hyoid bone, or the epiglottis with the cricothyroid joint? The answer is the cricoid cartilage with a complete ring. The arytenoid cartilages sit on the back of the cricoid, and the cricothyroid joint lies between the cricoid and the thyroid.

Question 6126. At which point does the trachea begin in the neck? The answer is at the lower border of the cricoid cartilage. That lies at the level of the sixth cervical vertebra. Above it the airway continues as the larynx, and below it the trachea runs down into the chest.

Question 6127. How many C-shaped rings of hyaline cartilage support the human trachea: 6 to 8, 16 to 20, 30 to 34, or more than 40? The answer is 16 to 20 of them. They are incomplete at the back, where the gap is filled by fibrous tissue and the trachealis muscle. The rings keep the airway open while still allowing the neck to bend.

Question 6128. What is the name of the ridge of cartilage that separates the two main bronchi at the lower end of the trachea? The answer is the carina. It is the last tracheal cartilage, drawn up into a keel between the two openings. Its lining is very sensitive, and touching it during a bronchoscopy makes a person cough.

Question 6129. Which structure lies immediately behind the trachea from the neck down to the bifurcation? The answer is the esophagus. The two tubes share a wall of connective tissue, and the trachea lies in front of the esophagus throughout its course. The esophagus is the softer tube and it is flattened from front to back.

Question 6130. At what level does the trachea divide into the two main bronchi: the sternal angle, the jugular notch, the xiphoid process, or the first rib? The answer is the sternal angle, which corresponds to the fourth thoracic vertebra. The ridge at the division is the carina. The bifurcation lies behind the joint where the manubrium meets the body of the sternum.

Question 6131. A described chest radiograph shows a dark vertical band in the middle of the neck and upper chest, with the heart shadow below and slightly to the left. What is that band? The answer is the air-filled trachea. Air absorbs very few radiograph beams, so it appears dark against the soft tissues. The band ends at the bifurcation behind the sternal angle.

Question 6132. Which main bronchus is wider, shorter and more vertical: the right, the left, both equally, or neither, because the two are the same? The answer is the right main bronchus. It is about 2.5 centimeters long and runs more steeply downward than the left. Because of that shape, an inhaled object is more likely to enter it.

Question 6133. Which muscle forms the back wall of the trachea and fills the gap between the ends of the cartilage rings? The answer is the trachealis muscle. Its fibers run across the gap, and when it contracts it narrows the airway. The lining lies directly against it, without cartilage between them.

Question 6134. A constructed illustration: a small child inhales a peanut and begins to cough and wheeze on the right side. Into which airway has the object most probably passed? The answer is the right main bronchus. It is wider and more vertical than the left, so objects follow the straighter path. The object may then lodge in the right lower lobe.

Question 6135. Which large vein crosses in front of the trachea just above the sternal angle: the left brachiocephalic vein, the right brachiocephalic vein, the superior vena cava, or the azygos vein? The answer is the left brachiocephalic vein, formed by the union of the left internal jugular and subclavian veins. It crosses the midline in front of the trachea on its way down.

Question 6136. Which structure arches over the left main bronchus just after the bronchus leaves the trachea: the arch of the aorta, the azygos vein, the pulmonary trunk, or the left subclavian artery? The answer is the arch of the aorta. It hooks over the left bronchus and then descends behind it as the descending aorta. On the right the corresponding structure is the azygos vein.

Question 6137. True or false: the trachea is a complete cartilaginous tube, so that its wall cannot be compressed from behind. The answer is false. The cartilage rings are C-shaped and the back of the tube is completed by muscle and fibrous tissue. That soft back wall allows the esophagus behind it to expand when food is swallowed.

Question 6138. A described computed tomography section of the chest shows a rounded air-filled tube in the middle, with a smaller flattened tube behind it, and two air-filled branches just below. What does the section show? The answer is the trachea dividing at the carina, with the esophagus behind it. The two branches are the right and left main bronchi.

Question 6139. Name three structures that lie in front of the trachea in the lower part of the neck, spoken as a sentence. The answer is the isthmus of the thyroid gland, the inferior thyroid veins, and the sternothyroid muscle. The isthmus lies across the second, third and fourth rings of the trachea. The veins drain downward into the left brachiocephalic vein.

Question 6140. Which kind of cartilage forms the rings of the trachea: hyaline cartilage, elastic cartilage, fibrocartilage, or calcified bone? The answer is hyaline cartilage. The rings are firm but they can calcify with age, which makes them appear pale on a radiograph. The cartilage of the epiglottis is elastic instead.

Question 6141. What is the name of the pyramidal unit of lung tissue that has its own bronchus and its own artery, and can be removed on its own? The answer is the bronchopulmonary segment. Its apex points toward the hilum, and its base lies at the surface of the lung. The segments are separated by connective tissue septa.

Question 6142. Two tubes descend through the upper chest, one in front of the other, and the softer one is flattened from front to back. Which lies in front, and which lies behind? The answer is the trachea in front and the esophagus behind. The esophagus is the softer of the two and its walls fall together when it is empty. Both are crossed by the arch of the aorta.

Question 6143. A constructed illustration: a tube placed in the trachea through a low opening in the neck has pressed for a long time against the artery that crosses the front of the trachea. Which artery is at risk? The answer is the brachiocephalic trunk. It runs obliquely across the front of the trachea behind the upper part of the sternum. The management of such a problem belongs to the treating clinician.

Question 6144. Which structure lies immediately behind the left main bronchus: the esophagus, the azygos vein, the right pulmonary artery, or the thymus? The answer is the esophagus. The left main bronchus runs forward and to the left, passing in front of the esophagus and the descending aorta. On the right the corresponding bronchus passes below the azygos vein.

Question 6145. Which bronchus arises from the right main bronchus above the right pulmonary artery, and so is called eparterial? The answer is the right superior lobar bronchus. It supplies the upper lobe of the right lung. On the left the corresponding artery lies above the bronchus instead.

Question 6146. Which of these pairs belongs together: the right upper lobe with the eparterial bronchus, the right middle lobe with three segments, the left upper lobe with the superior segment, or the right lower lobe with the lingula? The answer is the right upper lobe with the eparterial bronchus. It arises above the right pulmonary artery. The lingula belongs to the left upper lobe.

Question 6147. A described radiograph of a child who choked while eating shows a small dense object lying in the right main bronchus. Why does it lie on that side? The answer is that the right main bronchus is wider, shorter and more vertical than the left. It therefore continues the line of the trachea more closely than the left does. The object follows that straighter course.

Question 6148. Within a bronchopulmonary segment, one vessel runs at its center beside the bronchus, and another runs at its edge. Which is which: the artery at the center and the vein at the edge, the vein at the center and the artery at the edge, both at the center, or both at the edge? The answer is the artery at the center with the bronchus, and the vein at the edge.

Question 6149. How many lobes does the right lung have, and what are they called? The answer is three, named the superior lobe, the middle lobe and the inferior lobe. The left lung has only two lobes, because the space that the heart occupies on that side leaves no room for a third. The oblique and horizontal fissures separate the lobes on the right.

Question 6150. Which structure lies in front of the second, third and fourth rings of the trachea: the isthmus of the thyroid gland, the thymus, the left brachiocephalic vein, or the cricoid cartilage? The answer is the isthmus of the thyroid gland. It is the bridge of tissue joining the two lobes of the gland across the front of the airway. Below it the trachea is covered only by veins and strap muscles.

Question 6151. The right main bronchus is called eparterial and the left hyparterial. Which is correct: the right above the right pulmonary artery and the left below the left, both above, both below, or the right below and the left above? The answer is that the right lies above the right pulmonary artery and the left below the left pulmonary artery. The arrangement arises in the embryo.

Question 6152. True or false: the right main bronchus is wider, shorter and more vertical than the left main bronchus. The answer is true. The left main bronchus is longer, narrower and more horizontal, because it must pass under the arch of the aorta. That difference explains why inhaled objects usually go to the right.

Question 6153. Which part of the airway keeps a fixed opening because cartilage holds it open, so that a narrowing there does not relax with the smooth muscle: the trachea, a bronchiole, an alveolar duct, or a respiratory bronchiole? The answer is the trachea. Its C-shaped rings of cartilage keep the tube open, and the muscle at the back can only narrow it a little. The small airways have no cartilage at all.

Question 6154. A described chest radiograph shows the dark band of the trachea shifted to the right of the midline, with a large white area filling the left side of the chest. What has pushed the trachea? The answer is a collection in the left pleural space, such as fluid, which pushes the middle of the chest toward the opposite side. Lung collapse instead pulls the trachea toward the affected side.

Question 6155. Which structure lies immediately behind the carina, where the trachea divides? The answer is the esophagus. The left main bronchus then runs forward across the front of the esophagus, and the descending aorta lies behind it. The carina is separated from the esophagus only by a thin layer of tissue.

Question 6156. What is the name of the tongue-shaped part of the left lung that hangs below the left upper lobe bronchus and corresponds to the middle lobe on the right? The answer is the lingula. It lies in the left upper lobe and has two segments of its own. It is not a separate lobe, because no fissure separates it.

Question 6157. The bronchi have their own blood supply, separate from the vessels that exchange gas. Where do the bronchial arteries usually arise? The answer is from the descending thoracic aorta. There are usually two on the left and one on the right, and the right one often arises from a posterior intercostal artery.

Question 6158. Which kind of epithelium lines the trachea and the larger bronchi: pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium with goblet cells, simple squamous epithelium, transitional epithelium, or stratified squamous epithelium? The answer is pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium with goblet cells. The cilia sweep a film of mucus upward toward the throat. The goblet cells secrete that mucus, which traps dust and organisms.

Question 6159. A constructed illustration: a tumor at the hilum of the left lung presses on a nerve and the voice becomes hoarse, while the vocal fold on that side stops moving. Which nerve is involved, and why is it on that side? The answer is the left recurrent laryngeal nerve, which hooks around the aortic arch and then climbs beside the left bronchus.

Question 6160. A section in one plane shows the trachea cut along its length, with the carina rising as a small ridge between the two main bronchi. Which plane is that? The answer is the coronal plane, which runs from side to side across the chest. A section across the trachea would show its lumen as a circle.

Question 6161. On a chest radiograph, the two main bronchi leave the trachea at a wide angle. What is that angle called and roughly how large is it: the carinal angle at about 60 to 70 degrees, the costal angle at about 90 degrees, the sternal angle at about 30 degrees, or the bronchial angle at about 150 degrees? The answer is the carinal angle, at about 60 to 70 degrees.

Question 6162. In the main bronchi, the C-shaped cartilage rings are replaced by what kind of support? The answer is irregular plates of cartilage, arranged around the tube rather than as rings. Further down, in the bronchioles, the cartilage disappears altogether. The change allows the smaller airways to alter their diameter.

Question 6163. At the hilum of the left lung, which structure lies above the main bronchus: the pulmonary artery, the pulmonary vein, the eparterial bronchus, or the esophagus? The answer is the pulmonary artery. On the right the eparterial bronchus lies above the right pulmonary artery, so the two sides differ. The pulmonary veins lie in front of the bronchus on both sides.

Question 6164. A described bronchogram shows a branching tree within one lobe, filling with contrast material, and the branches end in rounded tips that do not join their neighbors. What is shown? The answer is the bronchial tree within one lobe, with the branches of the individual bronchopulmonary segments. The segments do not communicate with one another, so a segment can be removed alone.

Question 6165. The thoracic duct climbs through the chest behind the trachea. Which structure does it lie behind in the upper part of its course, and where does it cross to the left? The answer is the esophagus, and it crosses at about the level of the fifth thoracic vertebra. Below that level it lies between the aorta and the azygos vein.

Question 6166. How many bronchopulmonary segments are usually present in the right lung: six, eight, ten, or fourteen? The answer is ten. The left lung usually has eight or ten, because some of its segments are fused in the upper lobe. Each segment has its own bronchus and its own artery.

Question 6167. A lung has only two lobes, separated by a single long fissure that runs obliquely. Which lung is it, and what is the fissure called: the left lung with the oblique fissure, the right lung with the oblique fissure, the left lung with the horizontal fissure, or the right lung with the horizontal fissure? The answer is the left lung with the oblique fissure.

Question 6168. A constructed illustration: a person has a chronic infection that has destroyed the support of one bronchus, so that it stays wide and dilated. Which tissue has been lost from the wall? The answer is the cartilage and the elastic tissue of the bronchus. Without that support the airway cannot keep its shape. The dilated airway is described as bronchiectasis when it is seen.

Question 6169. At which level does the trachea end by dividing into the two main bronchi? The answer is at the level of the sternal angle. That point lies at the fourth thoracic vertebra, where the manubrium joins the body of the sternum. Below it the airway continues as two separate bronchi.

Question 6170. Which main bronchus is the longer of the two: the right, the left, neither because they are equal, or the right in some people and the left in others? The answer is the left main bronchus. It is about twice as long as the right and it runs more horizontally to reach the left lung. It passes beneath the arch of the aorta on its way.

Question 6171. A described specimen of the trachea is viewed from behind after the esophagus has been removed. A soft, flat band of tissue spans a gap between the ends of the cartilage rings. What is that band? The answer is the trachealis muscle with its fibrous tissue. The rings are incomplete at the back, and this band completes the tube.

Question 6172. In a young child, which organ lies in front of the lower part of the trachea and the great vessels, and largely disappears with age? The answer is the thymus. It occupies the front of the upper mediastinum in infancy and childhood. In the adult it is replaced by fat and fibrous tissue.

Question 6173. Which statement about the lining of the trachea is correct: the mucus is swept upward toward the throat, the mucus is swept downward into the lungs, the cilia beat in both directions at once, or the lining has no cilia at all? The answer is that the mucus is swept upward toward the throat. The cilia beat in one direction only, carrying trapped dust out of the airway.

Question 6174. Name the two sets of vessels that bring blood to the lungs, spoken as a sentence. The answer is the pulmonary vessels and the bronchial vessels. The pulmonary vessels carry blood for the exchange of gases and they form the greater part of the flow. The bronchial vessels are much smaller and they nourish the walls of the airways themselves.

Question 6175. A constructed illustration: an opening is made into the trachea in the lower part of the neck to secure the airway. Which rings are usually chosen, and which structure in front of them lies across the airway? The answer is the second to fourth rings, with the isthmus of the thyroid gland. The isthmus lies across those rings and covers them.

Question 6176. Which of these pairs belongs together: the right upper lobe with the eparterial bronchus, the left upper lobe with three lobar bronchi, the right middle lobe with the lingula, or the left lower lobe with the horizontal fissure? The answer is the right upper lobe with the eparterial bronchus. The lingula belongs to the left upper lobe, and the horizontal fissure belongs to the right lung.

Question 6177. A small object is inhaled while a person lies on the back. Where is it most likely to come to rest: the lower lobe of the right lung, the left lower lobe, the right upper lobe, or the left upper lobe? The answer is the lower lobe of the right lung. The right main bronchus is more vertical, and gravity carries the object to the lowest part of that lung.

Question 6178. A described lateral radiograph of the chest shows a triangular shadow with its apex at the hilum and its base against the front of the chest, below the horizontal fissure. Which lobe is collapsed? The answer is the right middle lobe. The middle lobe is bounded above by the horizontal fissure and behind by the oblique fissure, so it collapses into that wedge shape.

Question 6179. What is the name given to the bronchi that supply the lobes of the lung, one for each lobe? The answer is the lobar bronchi, also called secondary bronchi. They arise from the main bronchi, which are the primary bronchi. The bronchi that supply the bronchopulmonary segments are the segmental or tertiary bronchi.

Question 6180. Which structure lies above the left main bronchus as the bronchus runs toward the left lung? The answer is the arch of the aorta. The bronchus passes beneath the arch, and the aorta then continues downward behind it. On the right side the corresponding structure overhead is the azygos vein.

Question 6181. At which level of the airway does cartilage finally disappear from the wall: the trachea, the main bronchi, the bronchioles, or the alveolar ducts? The answer is at the bronchioles. The bronchi still have plates of cartilage in their walls, but the smallest conducting tubes have none. Smooth muscle alone controls their diameter.

Question 6182. True or false: the pulmonary veins run in the septa between the bronchopulmonary segments, so that each vein drains two neighboring segments. The answer is true. The veins lie at the edges of the segments rather than at their centers. The segmental artery and the bronchus lie together at the center.

Question 6183. A constructed illustration: a child who choked on a small toy now has a lung that is too full of air, with the middle of the chest pushed toward the other side. What has the object done, and why does the right side suffer more often? The answer is that it has acted as a valve, letting air in but not out, and the right main bronchus is wider and more vertical.

Question 6184. A described cross section through the hilum of the right lung shows a wide air tube with a large vessel lying below it. Which structure is above, and why is that arrangement named? The answer is the right main bronchus above the right pulmonary artery. Because the bronchus lies above the artery, the airway and that vessel are described as eparterial.

Question 6185. Which part of the airway is the most sensitive to touch, so that a bronchoscope touching it sets off a violent cough: the carina, the vocal folds, the trachealis muscle, or the epiglottis? The answer is the carina. Its lining is packed with nerve endings that guard the entrance to the lungs. The same reflex clears the airway when dust reaches that point.

Question 6186. What happens to the lumen of the trachea when the trachealis muscle contracts during a cough? The answer is that the lumen narrows, and the air leaves faster through the smaller opening. The muscle can also narrow the airway when the lining becomes irritated. Its fibers run across the soft back wall of the tube.

Question 6187. Which large vessel lies behind the left main bronchus and to the left of it as it descends through the chest? The answer is the descending thoracic aorta. It begins at the arch, which hooks over the left main bronchus, and then runs down behind and to the left of the bronchus. The esophagus lies between the two on that side.

Question 6188. Which kind of cell in the lining of the airway produces the mucus that traps dust? The answer is the goblet cell. Goblet cells lie among the ciliated cells of the respiratory lining. Their secretion forms a thin film that the cilia carry upward to the throat.

Question 6189. A described barium swallow shows a soft tube descending in front of the vertebral column and behind the trachea, and the swallow stops for a moment as the tube is pressed from the left above. Which two structures are shown in relation? The answer is the esophagus behind and the trachea in front, with the aortic arch pressing from the left above.

Question 6190. A small object is inhaled into a main bronchus. In which part of the lung does it most often come to rest: the lower lobe of the right lung, the lower lobe of the left lung, the middle lobe of the right lung, or the upper lobe of the right lung? The answer is the lower lobe of the right lung, in its posterior basal segment.

Question 6191. Which statement about a bronchopulmonary segment is correct: it has its own bronchus and artery and is separated by a septum, it shares a bronchus with its neighbor, its vein runs at its center, or it cannot be removed without removing the whole lobe? The answer is that it has its own bronchus and artery and is separated by a septum. That is why a single segment can be removed.

Question 6192. Which vein arches over the right main bronchus before it joins the superior vena cava? The answer is the azygos vein. It comes up on the right side of the vertebral column and hooks forward over the bronchus. On the left side the arch above the corresponding bronchus is made by the aorta instead.

Question 6193. Which part of the trachea forms the carina at its lower end? The answer is the last tracheal cartilage. It is drawn up into a ridge that separates the openings of the two main bronchi. Its mucous membrane is very sensitive to touch.

Question 6194. A described computed tomography section across the upper chest shows a rounded air-filled tube in the midline, a flattened soft tube directly behind it, and the vertebral column behind that. What are the two tubes? The answer is the trachea in front and the esophagus behind. The esophagus is flattened because its walls are relaxed and touch each other.

Question 6195. True or false: the left main bronchus runs more horizontally than the right main bronchus. The answer is true. It must pass beneath the arch of the aorta before it reaches the left lung. The right main bronchus descends more steeply, closer to the line of the trachea.

Question 6196. A described chest radiograph of a young child shows one lung larger and more translucent than the other, a flattened diaphragm on that side, and the heart pushed toward the opposite side. What does this pattern show? The answer is air trapped in that lung, which cannot escape. An inhaled object has let air in but not out, and the lung has overinflated.

Question 6197. A constructed illustration: a person who needed a tube in the trachea for a long time later breathes with a whistling sound, and the narrowing lies where the tube pressed against the wall. Why does pressure at that point damage the airway? The answer is that the lining is supplied by small vessels, and pressure stops the flow, so the wall heals with scar tissue.

Question 6198. Where can the trachea be felt through the skin: in the midline of the neck above the sternal notch, at the side of the neck behind the sternocleidomastoid muscle, just below the middle of the clavicle, or in the floor of the mouth? The answer is the midline of the neck above the sternal notch. The rings can be felt there through the skin and the thin strap muscles.

Question 6199. Which of these pairs belongs together: the carina with the fourth thoracic vertebra, the cricoid cartilage with the fourth cervical vertebra, the hilum of the lung with the eighth cervical vertebra, or the thyroid isthmus with the first thoracic vertebra? The answer is the carina with the fourth thoracic vertebra. The cricoid cartilage lies at the sixth cervical vertebra, where the trachea begins.

Question 6200. A described radiograph of the chest shows the dark air column of the trachea ending in a keel, with one branch running steeply downward and the other more horizontally. Which branch is the steeper one, and what follows from that? The answer is the right main bronchus. Its steeper course is why an inhaled object enters the right lung more often than the left.

To summarize, the larynx is supplied by branches of the vagus, the tenth cranial nerve: the internal laryngeal nerve carries sensation from above the vocal folds, the external laryngeal nerve supplies the cricothyroid muscle, and the recurrent laryngeal nerve supplies every other intrinsic muscle and the lining below the folds. The trachea begins below the cricoid cartilage, is held open by 16 to 20 C-shaped rings of hyaline cartilage, and divides at the carina at the level of the sternal angle and the fourth thoracic vertebra. The right main bronchus is wider, shorter and more vertical, which is why inhaled objects usually enter it, while the left main bronchus is longer and passes beneath the arch of the aorta. Each lung is divided into bronchopulmonary segments, each with its own bronchus and artery and with its veins in the septa between segments. The mucosa of the carina is the most sensitive part of the airway, and the cough it provokes is the reflex that protects the entrance to the lungs.
