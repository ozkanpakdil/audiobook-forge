# Chapter 22 — Hip

This chapter takes up the joints that carry the body and the joint that places the hand, moving from the wrist and the hand to the hip in detail and closing with the cruciate ligaments of the knee. It holds one hundred questions, and each one gives its answer in the same paragraph as the question and then says why that answer is right, so no answer has to be carried in mind to the end. The hip is taken apart into its socket, the head of the femur, the capsule and the ligaments that steady them. This is educational material, and it supports rather than replaces an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 2101. At the wrist, the broad lower end of the radius joins which two bones of the carpus? The answer is the scaphoid and the lunate. The radius carries the hand, and its lower end meets those two bones of the proximal row. The head of the ulna is kept away from the carpus by a disc of fibrocartilage.

Question 2102. In the hollow at the base of the thumb, which carpal bone forms the floor of the anatomical snuffbox? The answer is the scaphoid. Tenderness there after a fall points to that bone. The tendons of the abductor pollicis longus and the extensor pollicis brevis form one border of the hollow.

Question 2103. Which bone of the wrist carries the saddle-shaped surface on which the first metacarpal sits? The answer is the trapezium. It lies at the radial side of the distal row of carpal bones. The joint it makes is the saddle joint, and it lets the thumb be carried across the palm.

Question 2104. Which carpal bone lies in the middle of the distal row, between the scaphoid and the hamate, and is the largest bone of the wrist? The answer is the capitate. It is the keystone of the carpus, and force passes through it from the middle of the hand to the radius. Its rounded head sits in a socket formed largely by the scaphoid and the lunate.

Question 2105. Which structure passes through the carpal tunnel with the flexor tendons: the median nerve, the ulnar nerve, the radial artery, or the ulnar artery? The answer is the median nerve. It lies just beneath the flexor retinaculum, the band that roofs the tunnel. The ulnar nerve and the ulnar artery reach the hand outside it.

Question 2106. Which structure crosses the front of the wrist superficial to the flexor retinaculum: the tendon of the flexor pollicis longus, the median nerve, the ulnar nerve, or the tendon of the flexor digitorum profundus? The answer is the ulnar nerve. It passes with the ulnar artery and then divides into a superficial and a deep branch. The other three lie inside the tunnel.

Question 2107. Which movement is the special gift of the saddle joint at the base of the thumb: simple flexion, rotation about the long axis of the arm, opposition, or pure gliding? The answer is opposition. That movement swings the thumb across the palm so that its pad meets the pads of the fingers. The joint also flexes, extends, abducts and adducts.

Question 2108. A constructed illustration: a fall drives the thumb away from the hand, and the ligament joining the first metacarpal to the proximal phalanx on the side toward the index finger gives way. Which ligament is injured? The answer is the ulnar collateral ligament of the thumb. It resists exactly that force, and the injury is described as a skier's thumb.

Question 2109. Most of the flexion of the wrist is contributed by which joint: the radiocarpal joint, the midcarpal joint, the first carpometacarpal joint, or the distal radioulnar joint? The answer is the midcarpal joint. There the two rows of carpal bones fold on each other. The radiocarpal joint supplies most of the extension, and the other two joints serve the thumb and the forearm.

Question 2110. A described radiograph of the wrist shows a crack across the middle of the bone in the floor of the anatomical snuffbox, and the fragment nearer the forearm looks denser. Which bone is broken, and why does that fragment often fail to unite? The answer is the scaphoid, whose blood enters at the far end and runs back toward the forearm. Such a break can starve the fragment near the forearm.

Question 2111. A described section through the wrist shows a triangular plate of fibrocartilage stretched between the lower end of the ulna and the carpus, keeping the ulna away from the carpal bones. What is that plate? The answer is the triangular fibrocartilage complex. It cushions the ulnar side of the wrist, so the ulna takes no direct part in the radiocarpal joint.

Question 2112. At the wrist, does the ulnar nerve lie medial or lateral to the ulnar artery? The answer is medial. Both structures cross the front of the wrist on the little finger side, with the nerve a little nearer the midline of the hand. The artery can be felt there, beside the tendon of the flexor carpi ulnaris.

Question 2113. The deep branch of the ulnar nerve leaves the palm by passing between which two bony landmarks, and how does it lie relative to the artery beside it? The answer is between the pisiform and the hook of the hamate, with the nerve medial to the artery. It then curves around the hook to supply the muscles of the little finger and the interossei.

Question 2114. A constructed illustration: a person falls onto the outstretched hand, and a described radiograph shows the lower end of the radius broken, with the fragment nearer the hand displaced toward the back. Which bone is broken, and which nerve is most at risk? The answer is the radius, with the median nerve lying closest to it. Care of the fracture belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 2115. A constructed illustration: an older person has a firm nodule in the palm with a cord that draws the fourth and fifth fingers into the palm, and the skin over the cord is tethered. Which structure is contracted? The answer is the palmar aponeurosis. The fibrous sheet thickens and shortens, pulling on the sheaths of those fingers. What is offered as treatment belongs to the treating clinician.

Question 2116. True or false: the ulna articulates directly with the carpal bones at the wrist. The answer is false. A disc of fibrocartilage lies between the head of the ulna and the carpus. The radius alone takes the hand at the radiocarpal joint, and the ulna stays clear of it.

Question 2117. Which three bones of the pelvis meet to form the cup of the hip joint? The answer is the ilium, the ischium and the pubis. Their three parts join in the socket at a Y-shaped cartilage that closes in the second decade of life. The cup they make together is the acetabulum.

Question 2118. What is the name of the smooth horseshoe-shaped surface within the acetabulum that carries the weight of the body? The answer is the lunate surface. It is covered with hyaline cartilage and receives the head of the femur. Its two horns meet below at the notch in the rim.

Question 2119. What is the name of the rough, non-articular pit in the floor of the acetabulum? The answer is the acetabular fossa. It holds a pad of fat and gives attachment to the ligament of the head of the femur. Below, it is continuous with the notch in the rim.

Question 2120. Which ring of fibrocartilage is fixed to the margin of the acetabulum, deepens the socket and helps to seal the head of the femur within it? The answer is the acetabular labrum. It bridges the notch below and turns the socket into a deeper cup. A tear of it lets fluid cut into the rim and makes the hip catch and hurt.

Question 2121. True or false: the transverse acetabular ligament bridges the gap in the lower part of the rim of the socket. The answer is true. It spans the acetabular notch and so completes the ring of the socket. Beneath it a small gap remains, through which vessels and the ligament of the head pass.

Question 2122. Which part of the acetabulum bears the weight of the body in standing: the acetabular fossa, the lunate surface, the acetabular notch, or the transverse acetabular ligament? The answer is the lunate surface. Its broad upper part forms the roof of the socket and receives the load. The fossa and the notch bear no articular cartilage.

Question 2123. By about which year of life do the three parts of the acetabulum finish joining at the Y-shaped cartilage: the second year, the sixth year, the sixteenth year, or the thirtieth year? The answer is the sixteenth year. Until then the three bones are separated by the triradiate cartilage, which is seen clearly in the radiograph of a child.

Question 2124. Beneath the transverse acetabular ligament, the notch in the rim of the socket allows which structures to reach the inside of the joint: the sciatic nerve, the ligament of the head with its small vessels, the obturator artery alone, or the tendon of the iliopsoas? The answer is the ligament of the head with its small vessels. The sciatic nerve and the iliopsoas lie well outside the joint.

Question 2125. In the standing body, the opening of the acetabulum faces in which direction: straight laterally, directly forward, laterally with a tilt forward and downward, or straight upward? The answer is laterally, with a tilt forward and downward. The socket therefore covers the front and the top of the head. That tilt is one reason the hip dislocates backward more often than forward.

Question 2126. A described magnetic resonance image of the hip shows a thin cleft of fluid cutting into the front edge of the rim of the socket, with bruising of the bone beside it. Which structure is torn: the acetabular labrum, the ligament of the head of the femur, the transverse acetabular ligament, or the zona orbicularis? The answer is the acetabular labrum. The cleft enters the ring of fibrocartilage that deepens the socket.

Question 2127. Which statement about the acetabular labrum is correct: it is made of hyaline cartilage, it is fixed to the rim and increases the hold on the head, it lies within the acetabular fossa, or it is a fold of synovium? The answer is that it is fixed to the rim and increases the hold on the head. It is a ring of fibrocartilage rather than hyaline cartilage.

Question 2128. A described radiograph of a child's pelvis shows a Y-shaped line of cartilage running through each socket and separating three bony parts. What is that line? The answer is the triradiate cartilage. It is the growth plate where the ilium, the ischium and the pubis meet. When the child stops growing, the three parts become one bone.

Question 2129. A described radiograph of an adult pelvis shows a socket whose roof slopes steeply, so that the head of the femur rides high and is only partly covered. What does this describe? The answer is a shallow socket, the kind seen when the hip has been undercovered since birth. The labrum is overloaded and the joint wears early. Care belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 2130. A described section through a hip shows a fat-filled pit in the floor of the socket, continuous below with the gap in the rim, and a band running from the pit to a depression on the head of the femur. What are those two structures? The answer is the acetabular fossa and the ligament of the head. The ligament crosses the joint and carries a small vessel in a child.

Question 2131. The obturator nerve leaves the pelvis through a canal that lies in which position relative to the acetabulum, and which joint does that nerve also supply? The answer is below and in front of the socket, and the nerve supplies the hip joint. It gives off a branch to the joint as it passes through the canal on its way to the thigh.

Question 2132. Which muscle and its bursa lie immediately in front of the capsule of the hip, separating the joint from the femoral vessels? The answer is the iliopsoas. Its tendon glides over the front of the joint, and its bursa often joins the cavity of the joint. The femoral nerve lies on the surface of the muscle, with the artery just medial to it.

Question 2133. A constructed illustration: a newborn is examined and the head of the femur can be slipped out of the socket and back in again, and the fold of skin at the back of the thigh looks uneven. Which part of the joint is at fault? The answer is a shallow acetabulum with a steep roof. What is done about it belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 2134. A constructed illustration: a young adult has pain at the front of the hip at the end of a deep squat, and a described radiograph shows an extra lip of bone over the front of the socket and a bump at the junction of the head and the neck of the femur. What is the mechanism? The answer is that the rim and the bump meet early. Management belongs to the treating clinician.

Question 2135. Which ligament runs from the pit on the head of the femur to the notch in the rim of the socket? The answer is the ligament of the head of the femur. It lies inside the capsule and is wrapped in synovium. It is slack in most positions and becomes taut when the hip is adducted.

Question 2136. What is the name of the small depression on the head of the femur where that ligament is attached? The answer is the fovea capitis. It lies a little below and behind the center of the head. It is the one part of the head not covered by articular cartilage.

Question 2137. Which artery provides most of the blood to the adult head of the femur? The answer is the medial circumflex femoral artery. Its branches run up along the neck beneath the capsule and enter the head near its edge. These are the vessels torn in a break across the neck.

Question 2138. The head of the femur forms what part of a sphere: one half, two thirds, the whole sphere, or one quarter? The answer is two thirds. Its surface is covered with hyaline cartilage except at the fovea, where the ligament of the head is attached. The shape lets it turn in the socket through a wide range.

Question 2139. In the adult, the angle between the neck and the shaft of the femur is about how many degrees: 90, 110, 125, or 160? The answer is about 125 degrees. In a young child the angle is larger, and it falls as the child begins to walk and to bear weight. A smaller angle is described as the neck bending inward.

Question 2140. Which vessel is the chief source of blood for the head of the femur in an adult: the obturator artery, the medial circumflex femoral artery, the lateral circumflex femoral artery, or the artery of the ligament of the head? The answer is the medial circumflex femoral artery. Its branches climb the neck under the capsule. The artery of the ligament is small in adults.

Question 2141. A described radiograph shows a break across the neck of the femur, with the line of the break lying inside the capsule. Which vessel is most likely to be damaged, and what is the danger? The answer is the medial circumflex femoral artery and its branches along the neck. If they are torn, the head loses its supply and part of it may die.

Question 2142. In a child, which artery adds a share of the blood supply to the head of the femur, entering through the pit where the ligament is attached: the inferior gluteal artery, the obturator artery through that ligament, the lateral circumflex femoral artery alone, or the first perforating artery? The answer is the obturator artery through the ligament of the head. Its share is larger in childhood.

Question 2143. A described radiograph of the hip of a child shows the head of the femur flattened and in part fragmented, with a widened joint space and thickened cartilage. What has happened? The answer is that the blood supply of the head has been interrupted, and the bone is being replaced. The head may later flatten and the neck may shorten.

Question 2144. A described magnetic resonance image of the hip shows a curved band of low signal running through the head of the femur, with a rim of swelling around it. What does this represent? The answer is bone that has lost its blood supply and is dying. The band separates living bone from dead bone. The same picture may follow a break across the neck.

Question 2145. Which tendon is attached to the lesser trochanter, the small prominence on the posteromedial side of the upper end of the femur? The answer is the tendon of the iliopsoas. The lesser trochanter lies behind and below the neck. Because it lies at the back, a break of the neck often leaves the lower fragment pulled upward.

Question 2146. Which muscle leaves the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen just above the upper border of the greater trochanter, and which vessels leave just above that muscle? The answer is the piriformis, with the superior gluteal vessels above it. Below it pass the sciatic nerve and the inferior gluteal vessels. The muscle is the key to the gluteal region.

Question 2147. A constructed illustration: a person walks with a lurching gait and, when standing on one leg, the hip on the other side drops. Which muscle is weak, and which nerve supplies it? The answer is the gluteus medius, supplied by the superior gluteal nerve. That muscle holds the pelvis level when the opposite foot is off it.

Question 2148. A constructed illustration: a child limps and complains of pain at the knee, but the knee itself looks and moves normally. Why is the hip the likely source? The answer is that the hip and the knee share nerve supply from the same segments of the spinal cord. Fibers from the obturator and femoral nerves carry the pain to the knee.

Question 2149. True or false: the fovea capitis lies above the center of the head of the femur. The answer is false. It lies a little below and behind the center. That is why the ligament of the head is slack when the hip is extended and taut when the hip is adducted.

Question 2150. Which of these pairs belongs together: the fovea capitis with the attachment of the ligament of the head, the greater trochanter with the insertion of the iliopsoas, the lesser trochanter with the insertion of the gluteus medius, or the neck of the femur with the fovea? The answer is the fovea capitis with the attachment of the ligament of the head. The iliopsoas inserts on the lesser trochanter.

Question 2151. Name two muscles that are attached to the greater trochanter of the femur, spoken as a list. The answer is the gluteus medius and the gluteus minimus. The medius is attached to the lateral surface and the minimus to the front surface. The piriformis and the obturator internus with the gemelli reach its upper and medial borders.

Question 2152. Two angles describe the upper end of the femur: the angle between the neck and the shaft, and the angle by which the neck is twisted forward on the shaft. In which plane is each measured? The answer is that the first is measured in the frontal plane and the twist of the neck in the transverse plane. The twist points the knee and the foot forward.

Question 2153. What is the name of the strong fibrous sleeve that encloses the hip joint and is attached above to the rim of the socket? The answer is the capsule of the hip joint. It is attached below to the front of the femur along the line between the two trochanters. Behind, it reaches only part way down the neck.

Question 2154. Along which line on the front of the femur is the capsule of the hip attached below? The answer is the intertrochanteric line. The line runs from the greater trochanter down and across to the lesser trochanter. In front, the whole of the neck therefore lies outside the joint.

Question 2155. What is the name of the ring of circular fibers deep within the capsule of the hip that forms a collar around the neck of the femur? The answer is the zona orbicularis. Its fibers run around the neck rather than along it, and they resist a pull that would separate the surfaces of the joint. The three named ligaments lie outside them.

Question 2156. Where is the capsule of the hip thickest: at the front, at the back, above the socket, or below it? The answer is at the front. It is reinforced there by the strongest ligament of the body. Behind, the capsule is thinner and reaches less far down the neck.

Question 2157. Which part of the neck of the femur lies outside the capsule of the hip: the whole of it, the front of it, the back of it, or none of it? The answer is the front. The capsule is attached in front along the intertrochanteric line. Behind, it reaches only part way down, so part of the back of the neck lies within the joint.

Question 2158. In which position is the capsule of the hip tightest: full flexion, full extension, abduction with flexion, or sitting? The answer is full extension. When a person stands, the capsule and its ligaments wind around the neck and steady the joint. That is one reason a standing person can rest without muscular effort.

Question 2159. In which position is the capsule of the hip slackest, so that the joint holds the greatest volume: full extension, flexion with abduction and lateral rotation, standing on one leg, or adduction with medial rotation? The answer is flexion with abduction and lateral rotation. That is the position a hip takes when it is filled with fluid and painful.

Question 2160. What does the synovial membrane of the hip cover: only the articular cartilage, the inner surface of the capsule together with the bone inside it and the ligament of the head, the outer surface of the capsule, or the labrum alone? The answer is the inner surface of the capsule with the bone inside it and the ligament of the head. Articular cartilage is not covered.

Question 2161. A described image of the hip after contrast has been placed in the joint shows the material running down the neck of the femur in front but stopping short behind, with a narrow collar of fibers around the middle of the neck. What structures are shown? The answer is the capsule of the hip and the zona orbicularis. The capsule reaches further down in front than behind.

Question 2162. A described magnetic resonance image of a painful hip shows fluid distending the joint and a second collection of fluid in front of the joint, beneath the muscle that flexes the hip. What is that second collection? The answer is the iliopsoas bursa, which communicates with the cavity of the hip in many people. When the joint is inflamed, the bursa fills with the same fluid.

Question 2163. Which bursa lies in front of the hip joint beneath the iliopsoas muscle and often communicates with the cavity of the joint? The answer is the iliopsoas bursa. It lies between the muscle and the front of the capsule. Because the two cavities may join, disease within the hip can spread into the bursa.

Question 2164. Which nerve lies immediately in front of the capsule of the hip, resting on the surface of the iliopsoas muscle? The answer is the femoral nerve. It lies in the groove between the iliopsoas and the psoas, within the fascia that covers them. The femoral artery lies just medial to the nerve.

Question 2165. A constructed illustration: a child with an inflamed hip lies with the hip flexed, abducted and turned outward, and cries when the hip is moved. Why is the limb held in that position? The answer is that the position gives the capsule the greatest volume. Fluid inside the joint stretches the capsule, and the hip is held where the stretch is least.

Question 2166. A constructed illustration: after a break across the neck of the femur, blood collects inside the capsule and the joint becomes tight. Which movement is lost first, and why? The answer is extension, because the capsule is tightest in extension and cannot stretch further when blood fills it. The joint is then held flexed. Care belongs to the treating clinician.

Question 2167. Which of these pairs belongs together: the zona orbicularis with fibers that run around the neck, the iliofemoral ligament with the thinnest part of the capsule, the synovial membrane with the outer surface of the capsule, or the labrum with the acetabular fossa? The answer is the zona orbicularis with fibers that run around the neck. The synovial membrane lines the inside of the capsule.

Question 2168. A described transverse section through the hip shows that behind, the capsule stops well above the ridge on the back of the femur, while in front it reaches the line between the trochanters. Which part of the neck therefore lies within the joint? The answer is the back of the neck. A break there may lie inside the joint and endanger the vessels running along the neck.

Question 2169. Which ligament, shaped like an inverted Y across the front of the hip, is the strongest ligament in the body? The answer is the iliofemoral ligament. It runs from the front of the hip bone down to the line between the two trochanters. It checks extension of the hip when a person stands upright.

Question 2170. Which ligament runs from the ischium, below and behind the socket, upward and outward to the base of the greater trochanter? The answer is the ischiofemoral ligament. It is the weakest of the three ligaments that reinforce the hip. Its fibers spiral upward as they pass to the femur.

Question 2171. Which ligament arises from the pubis and blends with the front of the capsule of the hip, helping to limit abduction? The answer is the pubofemoral ligament. It runs downward and outward from the pubic part of the rim of the socket. It also helps to limit extension of the hip.

Question 2172. Which movement of the hip is chiefly checked by the iliofemoral ligament: flexion, extension, abduction, or medial rotation? The answer is extension. The ligament is taut when the hip is extended and slack when the hip is flexed. It is the chief reason a person can stand with little muscular effort.

Question 2173. Which ligament of the hip chiefly limits abduction: the iliofemoral, the pubofemoral, the ischiofemoral, or the ligament of the head? The answer is the pubofemoral ligament. It lies below the front of the joint, where a limb carried out to the side would stretch it. The iliofemoral ligament chiefly limits extension.

Question 2174. Which ligament chiefly resists medial rotation of the hip: the pubofemoral, the ligament of the head, the ischiofemoral, or the iliofemoral? The answer is the ischiofemoral ligament. It spirals from the ischium to the base of the greater trochanter and tightens as the thigh turns inward. The pubofemoral ligament resists abduction instead.

Question 2175. In which position is the ligament of the head of the femur taut: extension, abduction, adduction, or lateral rotation? The answer is adduction. The ligament runs from the pit on the head to the notch in the rim of the socket, and carrying the thigh across the midline stretches it. It carries a small artery to the head in a child.

Question 2176. Which ligament bridges the gap in the lower rim of the acetabulum and turns the notch into a foramen: the iliofemoral, the transverse acetabular, the pubofemoral, or the zona orbicularis? The answer is the transverse acetabular ligament. Vessels and the ligament of the head pass beneath it into the joint. It also completes the ring to which the labrum is fixed.

Question 2177. Which of these pairs belongs together: the iliofemoral ligament with the checking of extension, the pubofemoral ligament with the checking of medial rotation, the ischiofemoral ligament with the checking of abduction, or the ligament of the head with the carrying of a vessel into the socket? The answer is the iliofemoral ligament with the checking of extension. The pubofemoral ligament checks abduction, and the ischiofemoral checks medial rotation.

Question 2178. A described dissection of the front of the hip shows a thick band of fibers shaped like an inverted Y, running from the front of the hip bone to the ridge between the trochanters, with a thinner band below it passing toward the pubis. Which two ligaments are shown? The answer is the iliofemoral ligament and the pubofemoral ligament. The first is the strongest ligament in the body.

Question 2179. A described dissection of the back of the hip shows a band of fibers spiraling from the ischium upward and outward to the base of the greater trochanter, lying over the back of the capsule. Which ligament is shown, and how does it rank among the three? The answer is the ischiofemoral ligament, the weakest of the three. It resists medial rotation.

Question 2180. A described section through the socket shows a band of fibers crossing the gap in the lower rim and closing it into a hole, and a rounded cord passing through it to reach a pit in the floor of the socket. What are the band and the cord? The answer is the transverse acetabular ligament and the ligament of the head. They guide a small vessel into the joint.

Question 2181. The iliofemoral ligament is attached above to the front of the hip bone. Which bony point gives it part of that attachment and also gives origin to the straight head of the rectus femoris? The answer is the anterior inferior iliac spine. The ligament and the muscle therefore share the same landmark. Just above it lies the anterior superior iliac spine.

Question 2182. A constructed illustration: after a blow against the front of a bent knee, the hip is held flexed, adducted and turned inward, the limb looks short, and sensation is lost over the back of the thigh. Which injury does this describe? The answer is a backward dislocation of the hip with injury to the sciatic nerve. Care belongs to the treating clinician.

Question 2183. True or false: the pubofemoral ligament helps to limit abduction of the hip. The answer is true. It lies below the front of the joint, where a thigh carried away from the midline stretches it. The iliofemoral ligament above it chiefly limits extension.

Question 2184. Which of these pairs belongs together: the iliofemoral ligament with the front of the joint, the pubofemoral ligament with the back of the joint, the ischiofemoral ligament with the front of the joint, or the ligament of the head with the outer surface of the capsule? The answer is the iliofemoral ligament with the front of the joint. The pubofemoral lies below and in front, and the ischiofemoral behind.

Question 2185. Name the three ligaments that reinforce the capsule of the hip, spoken as a list. The answer is the iliofemoral, the pubofemoral and the ischiofemoral ligaments. The iliofemoral lies in front, the pubofemoral below and in front, and the ischiofemoral behind. The strongest of the three is the iliofemoral.

Question 2186. Which ligament runs from the front of the tibia upward and backward to the inner side of the outer condyle of the femur? The answer is the anterior cruciate ligament. It crosses the joint within the capsule. It stops the tibia from sliding forward on the femur.

Question 2187. Which ligament of the knee runs from the back of the tibia upward and forward to the outer side of the inner condyle of the femur, and is the stronger of the two that cross? The answer is the posterior cruciate ligament. It is the strongest ligament of the knee. It stops the tibia from sliding backward on the femur.

Question 2188. Within the knee, the two ligaments that cross each other lie inside the capsule, yet a fold of synovium wraps around them. What are they called? The answer is the cruciate ligaments, the anterior and the posterior. Because they are wrapped in synovium, fluid from the joint does not bathe them directly. Their position helps to explain why they heal poorly.

Question 2189. Which movement of the knee does the anterior cruciate ligament chiefly resist: the tibia sliding forward on the femur, the tibia sliding backward, the knee bending, or the patella moving sideways? The answer is the tibia sliding forward on the femur. The ligament is taut when the knee is straight and when the tibia is turned inward.

Question 2190. Which movement of the knee does the posterior cruciate ligament chiefly resist: the tibia sliding forward, the tibia sliding backward, the knee straightening, or the patella being pulled upward? The answer is the tibia sliding backward on the femur. The ligament is taut when the knee is bent. It is the strongest ligament of the joint.

Question 2191. In which position is the anterior cruciate ligament most taut: the knee straight with the tibia turned inward, the knee bent with the tibia turned outward, the knee bent at a right angle, or the knee straight with the tibia turned outward? The answer is the knee straight with the tibia turned inward. In that position it checks both forward glide and hyperextension.

Question 2192. Which artery supplies the greater part of the blood reaching the cruciate ligaments: the femoral artery, the middle geniculate artery, the anterior tibial artery, or the obturator artery? The answer is the middle geniculate artery, a branch of the popliteal artery. Its branches enter through the fat pad and the synovial covering. That supply is meager, so a torn ligament heals poorly.

Question 2193. A described sagittal magnetic resonance image of the knee shows a dark band running from the front of the tibia upward and backward to the inner side of the outer condyle of the femur, crossing in front of another dark band. Which ligament is in front? The answer is the anterior cruciate ligament. The two bands cross like the limbs of the letter X.

Question 2194. A described dissection of the knee opened from behind shows a strong band rising from the back of the tibia and running upward and forward to the outer side of the inner condyle of the femur, with a small slip joining it from the outer meniscus. Which ligament is this, and what is the slip? The answer is the posterior cruciate ligament, with a meniscofemoral ligament beside it.

Question 2195. A described radiograph of the knee of a young person shows a flake of bone pulled from between the two spines on the top of the tibia, with the joint still in line. Which ligament has torn away its bony attachment? The answer is the anterior cruciate ligament, which is attached to the front of that space. Care belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 2196. Within the notch between the condyles of the femur, which of the two cruciate ligaments lies in front of the other, and which condyle does each reach above? The answer is that the anterior cruciate ligament lies in front and reaches the outer condyle, while the posterior cruciate reaches the inner condyle. The two cross as they rise.

Question 2197. A constructed illustration: a person plants a foot and turns the body while the knee is slightly bent, feels a pop, and afterward the knee gives way on turning. Which ligament is torn, and which two structures are often injured with it? The answer is the anterior cruciate ligament, often with the inner meniscus and the ligament on the inner side of the knee.

Question 2198. True or false: the posterior cruciate ligament is the stronger of the two cruciate ligaments. The answer is true. It is the strongest ligament of the knee joint. It is taut when the knee is bent and stops the tibia from sliding backward on the femur.

Question 2199. Which of these pairs belongs together: the anterior cruciate ligament with the checking of forward glide of the tibia, the posterior cruciate ligament with the checking of forward glide, the medial meniscus with the lateral side of the knee, or the patella with the tibial spines? The answer is the anterior cruciate ligament with the checking of forward glide. The posterior cruciate ligament checks backward glide.

Question 2200. Name the two ligaments that cross within the knee joint, spoken as a list. The answer is the anterior cruciate ligament and the posterior cruciate ligament. The anterior one is attached in front on the tibia and the posterior one behind. Both lie inside the capsule and are wrapped in synovium.

To summarize, the wrist is formed by the radius with the scaphoid and the lunate, the ulna being kept away by a disc of fibrocartilage, and the carpal tunnel carries the median nerve with the long flexor tendons while the ulnar nerve passes outside it. The socket of the hip is built from the ilium, the ischium and the pubis, deepened by the labrum and bridged below by the transverse acetabular ligament, and the head of the femur carries the fovea and depends for its blood on the medial circumflex femoral artery. The capsule of the hip is thick in front and thin behind, tightest in extension and lined by synovium, and the iliofemoral, pubofemoral and ischiofemoral ligaments steady it, the iliofemoral being the strongest ligament in the body. At the knee the anterior cruciate ligament checks forward sliding of the tibia and the posterior cruciate ligament, the stronger of the two, checks backward sliding, and both lie inside the capsule but outside the synovial lining.
