# Chapter 44 — Lymphatic Vessels

This chapter follows the lymph from the tissue spaces back to the blood, and it holds one hundred questions about the vessels that carry it and the nodes that filter it. It begins with the lymphatic capillaries, then follows the collecting vessels and their valves, the lymphatic trunks, the thoracic duct and the right lymphatic duct, and the way lymph returns to the great veins. The first seventy-five questions deal with the vessels and the last twenty-five with the lymph nodes. Each question is answered immediately, and the reason for the answer follows at once. This is educational material, and it supports rather than replaces an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 4301. Which feature sets a lymphatic capillary apart from a blood capillary of the same region? The answer is that it begins as a blind-ended tube in the tissue spaces. A blood capillary carries blood from an arterial end to a venous end. A lymphatic capillary has no such upstream end, so the fluid it takes up can travel only away from the tissues.

Question 4302. In the wall of a lymphatic capillary, why does tissue fluid enter easily while the lymph inside does not escape? The answer is that the endothelial cells overlap like roof tiles. The flaps swing open when the pressure of the tissue fluid outside them rises. When the pressure inside the vessel is greater, the flaps press shut and hold the lymph in.

Question 4303. True or false: the anchoring filaments pull the endothelial flaps of a lymphatic capillary apart when the tissue around the vessel swells. The answer is true. The filaments run from the endothelial cells to the collagen fibers of the surrounding tissue. As fluid collects and the tissue swells, they pull the flaps open and let more fluid enter.

Question 4304. Which of these is missing from the wall of a lymphatic capillary: an endothelial lining, a complete basement membrane, anchoring filaments, or overlapping cell edges? The answer is a complete basement membrane. The lymphatic capillary rests on a thin and incomplete basal lamina. That loose support helps explain why the vessel is so permeable to fluid and to protein.

Question 4305. A described specimen shows a fine mesh of vessels in the wall of the small intestine, with one vessel running up the middle of each villus. What is that central vessel called? The answer is the central lacteal. It lies in the core of the villus among the blood capillaries. The lacteal takes up the fatty products of digestion and carries them away as chyle.

Question 4306. Which of these is carried back to the blood mainly by the lymphatic vessels: oxygen, digested fat, glucose, or carbon dioxide? The answer is digested fat. Fat enters the lacteals of the small intestine and reaches the bloodstream through the thoracic duct. Oxygen, glucose and carbon dioxide travel in the blood itself.

Question 4307. What is the name of the creamy white fluid that fills the intestinal lymphatics after a fatty meal? The answer is chyle. The finely divided fat gives the fluid its milky appearance. Once it leaves the intestine and joins the rest of the lymph, the same fluid is simply called lymph.

Question 4308. Which coat of a collecting lymphatic vessel is lined by endothelium: the tunica intima, the tunica media, the tunica adventitia, or the tunica serosa? The answer is the tunica intima. Around it lies the tunica media, which contains smooth muscle. The outer coat is the tunica adventitia, built largely of collagen, and the three coats are named as they are in a vein.

Question 4309. Which coat of a large collecting lymphatic holds the smooth muscle that drives lymph onward? The answer is the tunica media. Its muscle cells contract in a rhythm and squeeze the column of lymph forward. A vessel with a thicker media can generate a stronger contraction, and the other two coats add little to that pumping.

Question 4310. What is the name of the short segment of a lymphatic vessel that lies between two valves: a lymphangion, a lacteal, a cisterna, or a trabecula? The answer is a lymphangion. Each segment has its own muscular wall and acts as a small pump. The valves at its two ends keep the lymph moving in one direction only.

Question 4311. Why are valves more numerous in lymphatic vessels than in veins of the same size? The answer is that lymph has no central pump behind it. The valves divide the vessel into short pumping segments and stop the lymph from falling back. Blood, by contrast, is driven onward by the heart.

Question 4312. Which of these movements helps push lymph along the thoracic duct: contraction of the diaphragm in breathing, closure of the aortic valve, movement of the eyeball, or chewing? The answer is contraction of the diaphragm in breathing. Its descent lowers the pressure in the chest and draws lymph upward, while the same movement raises the pressure in the abdomen and pushes lymph ahead of it.

Question 4313. Name two pumps, other than the heart, that keep lymph moving in a person who is standing still. The answer is the contraction of skeletal muscle and the movement of the chest in breathing. Muscles squeeze the vessels that run beside them, and the valves make the squeezed lymph move only forward. Both pumps fail when a person is completely still.

Question 4314. A constructed illustration: a person sits without moving for many hours on a long flight, and the ankles swell. Which mechanism of lymph return has failed in that illustration? The answer is the skeletal muscle pump. Without the calf muscles squeezing the deep vessels, both lymph and venous blood pool in the leg. Movement, rather than the heart alone, is what empties the leg.

Question 4315. Which of these gives the correct order of flow through the lymphatic system: lymphatic capillary, collecting vessel, lymph node, trunk; lymph node, lymphatic capillary, collecting vessel, trunk; trunk, lymph node, collecting vessel, lymphatic capillary; or collecting vessel, lymphatic capillary, lymph node, trunk? The answer is lymphatic capillary, collecting vessel, lymph node, trunk. Lymph enters the capillaries first and leaves the body through a trunk.

Question 4316. The superficial collecting lymphatic vessels of the lower limb run in the fatty tissue beside which vein, and where do they end? The answer is the great saphenous vein, and they end in the superficial inguinal nodes. The deep vessels of the limb run instead beside the femoral vessels to the deep inguinal nodes.

Question 4317. A described dissection shows a chain of thin vessels running beside a large artery in the thigh, buried between the muscles. Which set of lymphatic vessels is shown? The answer is a set of deep lymphatic vessels. Deep vessels run with the deep arteries and veins of a limb. Superficial vessels lie instead in the fatty tissue just under the skin.

Question 4318. Which statement about the deep lymphatic vessels of a limb is correct: they run in the subcutaneous fat, they accompany the deep arteries and veins, they drain only the skin, or they open directly into the great veins? The answer is that they accompany the deep arteries and veins. They drain the muscles, the bones and the joints, while the skin drains into superficial vessels.

Question 4319. Which group of lymph nodes receives most of the lymph from the upper limb? The answer is the axillary nodes. The superficial vessels of the limb run beside the basilic vein up the medial side of the arm. The deep vessels follow the brachial vessels to the same group.

Question 4320. A constructed illustration: a small cut on the little finger becomes infected, and a tender lump appears in the armpit. Which group of nodes is reacting in that illustration? The answer is the axillary lymph nodes. Lymph from the little finger travels with the superficial vessels along the medial side of the limb. The nodes enlarge as they filter what the infection has carried to them.

Question 4321. The deep cervical lymph nodes lie along which vessel, and which trunk do they form? The answer is the internal jugular vein, and they form the jugular trunk. They lie beneath the sternocleidomastoid muscle. It ends in the thoracic duct on the left and in the right lymphatic duct on the right.

Question 4322. The lymph nodes of the axilla lie around which vessels, and which trunk do they form? The answer is the axillary vessels, and they form the subclavian trunk. They lie among the fat of the armpit, around the axillary vein and artery. Like the jugular trunk it ends at the venous angle of its own side, where the subclavian vein meets the internal jugular vein.

Question 4323. Which lymphatic trunk drains the chest wall and the thoracic viscera on one side? The answer is the bronchomediastinal trunk. It collects lymph from the nodes of the mediastinum and from the nodes along the trachea and the bronchi. It ends at the venous angle of its own side.

Question 4324. Which unpaired lymphatic trunk lies in the abdomen and drains the small intestine, the colon and part of the liver? The answer is the intestinal trunk. It is formed by the lymph nodes of the mesentery and the nodes in front of the aorta. It joins the cisterna chyli, and its lymph is rich in fat after a meal.

Question 4325. The right and the left lumbar trunks drain the lower limbs and the pelvis. Which two vessels do they lie beside, and what do they join? The answer is the aorta and the inferior vena cava, and they join the cisterna chyli. They are formed by the nodes beside those two vessels. Together with the intestinal trunk they empty into the cisterna chyli.

Question 4326. At which vertebral level does the thoracic duct usually begin in the abdomen? The answer is at the level of the first and second lumbar vertebrae. The sac that forms its lower end is the cisterna chyli. The duct then passes upward through the aortic opening of the diaphragm into the chest.

Question 4327. What is the name of the sac that forms the lower end of the thoracic duct? The answer is the cisterna chyli. It lies on the bodies of the first and second lumbar vertebrae. The intestinal trunk and the two lumbar trunks empty into it. Its shape varies, and in some people it is no more than a small plexus of vessels.

Question 4328. Which part of the skeleton lies immediately behind the thoracic duct as the duct crosses the chest? The answer is the vertebral column, with the thoracic vertebrae forming its wall. The duct lies in the posterior mediastinum, applied to the bodies of those vertebrae. The esophagus and the pericardium lie in front of it.

Question 4329. In the posterior mediastinum, which structure lies on the left side of the thoracic duct and which one lies on its right? The answer is the aorta on its left and the azygos vein on its right. That pair of neighbors can be used to trace the duct. The vertebral column lies behind it and the esophagus lies in front.

Question 4330. Which structure lies immediately anterior to the thoracic duct in the posterior mediastinum? The answer is the esophagus. The duct lies between the aorta and the azygos vein, against the bodies of the vertebrae. The esophagus lies in front of both the duct and the aorta at that level.

Question 4331. A described lymphangiogram of the chest shows a single thin duct rising through the middle of the back and crossing to one side near the top. Which duct is shown, and to which side does it cross? The answer is the thoracic duct, which crosses to the left. It ends at the left venous angle in the root of the neck.

Question 4332. Where does the thoracic duct end, and into which vein does it open? The answer is at the left venous angle, where the left internal jugular and the left subclavian veins join. In the root of the neck the duct arches laterally and crosses in front of the left subclavian artery. Its terminal part may open as one mouth or as several small ones.

Question 4333. Which regions of the body send their lymph into the thoracic duct? The answer is both lower limbs, the pelvis, the abdomen, the left half of the chest, the left upper limb and the left side of the head and neck. Only the right upper quarter of the body drains elsewhere. The duct therefore carries far more lymph than any other single vessel.

Question 4334. The right lymphatic duct ends at which venous angle, and which two veins form that angle? The answer is the right venous angle, formed by the right internal jugular and the right subclavian veins. The duct drains the right upper limb, the right side of the chest and the right side of the head and neck. In many people it is short or absent.

Question 4335. True or false: the thoracic duct carries lymph from the left side of the head and neck as well as from both lower limbs. The answer is true. The left jugular trunk opens into the duct, while the right jugular trunk opens into the right lymphatic duct. The two sides of the head therefore drain by different routes.

Question 4336. Which of these structures receives lymph from the left half of the thorax: the right lymphatic duct, the thoracic duct, the cisterna chyli only, or the intestinal trunk? The answer is the thoracic duct. The right lymphatic duct takes the right half of the chest. The cisterna chyli lies below the diaphragm and the intestinal trunk drains the gut.

Question 4337. A described dissection of the root of the neck shows a thin-walled duct arching over an artery and turning down into a vein. Which vein is it entering? The answer is the left brachiocephalic vein at the left venous angle. The duct crosses in front of the left subclavian artery to reach it. On the right side the same task is done by the right lymphatic duct.

Question 4338. Which statement about the termination of the lymphatic ducts is correct: they open into the inferior vena cava, they open into the superior vena cava, they open at the venous angles, or they open into the pulmonary veins? The answer is that they open at the venous angles. There the subclavian and internal jugular veins join, so lymph returns to the blood just before it reaches the heart.

Question 4339. What happens to the lymph after it enters a venous angle? The answer is that it mixes with the venous blood and is carried to the right side of the heart. From there it passes through the lungs and returns to the arterial circulation. Every drop of tissue fluid that leaves the blood capillaries can therefore find its way back to them.

Question 4340. Which property of lymphatic vessels keeps lymph moving in one direction even when the body is still? The answer is the presence of valves along the vessel. Each valve has two delicate cusps that meet in the middle of the lumen. Lymph pushed past a valve cannot fall back through it, so flow continues in one direction only.

Question 4341. A described section of a collecting lymphatic shows two delicate cusps meeting in the middle of the lumen. What is that structure, and which way does it direct the lymph? The answer is a valve, which directs the lymph in the direction of flow. Fluid coming from the tissue side pushes the cusps apart. Any lymph that tries to return presses them together and closes the vessel.

Question 4342. In the standard anatomical position, in which direction does lymph travel along a superficial lymphatic vessel of the arm: from the armpit toward the fingers, from the fingers toward the armpit, from the skin toward the bone, or in both directions at once? The answer is from the fingers toward the armpit. The vessel begins in the tissue spaces of the hand, and its valves make that the only possible direction.

Question 4343. Which of these tissues has no lymphatic capillaries at all: the dermis, the periosteum, the cornea, or the subcutaneous fat? The answer is the cornea. It is an avascular and transparent tissue that needs no lymph drainage. The dermis, the periosteum and the fatty tissue all contain lymphatic capillaries.

Question 4344. Which of these organs contains no afferent lymphatic vessels: the liver, the spleen, the kidney, or the lung? The answer is the spleen. It has no vessels carrying lymph into it, so lymph leaves it by efferent vessels only. The other three organs have rich lymphatic networks, and lymph from them is filtered by nodes on the way.

Question 4345. A classically taught rule states that one part of the body has no lymphatic vessels in its substance. Which part is it? The answer is the central nervous system, the brain and the spinal cord. Tissue fluid there returns along the spaces around the blood vessels. Lymphatic vessels have since been described in the dura mater beside the venous sinuses.

Question 4346. Which of these tissues is said to lack lymphatic vessels: the skin of the eyelid, the cartilage of the trachea, the lining of the small intestine, or the capsule of the kidney? The answer is the cartilage of the trachea. Cartilage is avascular throughout, so it needs no lymphatics. The other three are vascular tissues with lymphatic capillaries.

Question 4347. In which layer of the skin do lymphatic capillaries begin: the keratin layer, the basal layer of the epidermis, the papillary layer of the dermis, or the subcutaneous fat? The answer is the papillary layer of the dermis. The epidermis itself is avascular and has no lymphatics. The dermal papillae, which carry the capillary loops, are where the lymphatic mesh begins.

Question 4348. Which of these is the main force that drives tissue fluid into a lymphatic capillary: the pumping of the heart, the pressure of the tissue fluid itself, the suction of the veins, or gravity? The answer is the pressure of the tissue fluid itself. Fluid filtered from the blood capillaries raises that pressure and pushes the flaps open.

Question 4349. Which statement about the protein in lymph is correct: it is higher than in blood plasma, equal to blood plasma, lower than blood plasma but higher than freshly filtered tissue fluid, or entirely absent? The answer is that it is lower than plasma but higher than freshly filtered fluid. Protein that leaks into the tissues is collected by the lymphatics and returned to the blood.

Question 4350. What happens to the small amount of protein that constantly leaks from the blood capillaries into the tissues? The answer is that it is collected by the lymphatic capillaries and returned to the blood. A blood capillary cannot take protein back in. Without the lymphatics that protein would accumulate in the tissues and hold water there.

Question 4351. Which of these would follow if the lymphatic vessels of a limb were blocked: tissue fluid would drain faster, protein would accumulate in the tissues and cause swelling, the blood would clot in the capillaries, or the arteries would dilate? The answer is that protein would accumulate and cause swelling. That protein holds water in the tissues, and the swelling is called lymphedema.

Question 4352. A constructed illustration: after an operation in the armpit, a person's arm swells and the skin of the arm feels firm. Which vessels have been interrupted in that illustration? The answer is the lymphatic vessels of the upper limb. Removing the axillary nodes also removes the vessels that ran through them. The swelling is lymphedema, and its management belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 4353. Which of these agents may block the lymphatic vessels and produce the massive swelling called elephantiasis: a virus, a worm, a fungus, or a crystal? The answer is a worm. The parasite Wuchereria bancrofti lives in the lymphatic vessels and blocks them. The limb then swells with lymph that cannot pass, and the skin thickens over time.

Question 4354. What is the name of the swelling of a limb caused by obstruction of its lymphatic vessels? The answer is lymphedema. The fluid that collects is rich in protein, which is why the swelling is firm rather than soft. It does not pit as readily as the swelling of heart failure or of a low protein state.

Question 4355. A constructed illustration: a newborn has a soft swelling in the side of the neck that lets light through. Which developmental abnormality of lymphatic vessels is illustrated? The answer is a lymphatic malformation, once called a cystic hygroma. Part of the lymphatic network failed to join the veins and dilated into cysts. Such swellings often lie in the posterior triangle of the neck.

Question 4356. Which statement about the origin of the lymphatic system is correct: it grows out from the veins, it grows out from the arteries, it forms from the lymph nodes first, or it forms from the spleen? The answer is that it grows out from the veins. Lymphatic sacs bud from the embryonic veins and spread through the body, and the two largest become the ducts.

Question 4357. Into which veins do the lymphatic vessels of the embryo first open? The answer is the large veins of the neck, near the future venous angles. The lymphatic sacs keep that connection as the body grows. That is why the adult ducts end at the venous angles, where the subclavian and internal jugular veins meet.

Question 4358. Name the largest lymphatic vessel in the body, spoken as a sentence. The answer is the thoracic duct. It begins in the abdomen at the cisterna chyli and ends in the root of the left side of the neck. In an adult it is roughly 38 to 45 centimeters long and about 3 millimeters wide.

Question 4359. Name the three coats of a collecting lymphatic vessel, from the lumen outward, spoken as a sentence. The answer is the tunica intima, the tunica media and the tunica adventitia. The intima is lined by endothelium, the media carries smooth muscle, and the adventitia is the outer coat of collagen. The same three coats are found in veins.

Question 4360. A described lymphangiogram shows a beaded chain of thin vessels climbing the medial side of the thigh toward the groin. What makes the chain look beaded, and where does it end? The answer is the valves along the vessel, and it ends in the inguinal nodes. Each valve marks a short segment, so the column of contrast looks like a string of beads.

Question 4361. What is the effect of breathing on the flow of lymph in the thoracic duct? The answer is that breathing increases the flow. Descent of the diaphragm lowers the pressure in the chest and raises it in the abdomen. Lymph is thus drawn upward from the abdomen and onward toward the neck.

Question 4362. Which statement about the flow of lymph in the body is correct: it depends on a central pump, it depends on the heart alone, it depends on muscle contraction, breathing and the pulsation of arteries, or it stops completely during sleep? The answer is that it depends on muscle contraction, breathing and the pulsation of nearby arteries. There is no central lymphatic pump.

Question 4363. A described transverse section of the abdomen at the level of the second lumbar vertebra shows a small sac lying against the vertebral bodies, with the aorta to its left and the inferior vena cava to its right. What is the sac, and where does it lie? The answer is the cisterna chyli, which lies behind the peritoneum in the posterior part of the abdomen.

Question 4364. Which of these structures does lymph pass through on its way from the small intestine to the thoracic duct: the cisterna chyli, the right lymphatic duct, the axillary nodes, or the deep cervical nodes? The answer is the cisterna chyli. The intestinal trunk opens into it in the abdomen. From there the lymph enters the thoracic duct.

Question 4365. A constructed illustration: a lump in the left side of the neck just above the collarbone presses on the end of the thoracic duct. Which lymph does that lump obstruct? The answer is the lymph from almost the whole body, since the thoracic duct ends there. Such a lump can block the return from the lower limbs, the abdomen and the left upper quarter.

Question 4366. Lymph from the stomach reaches the left supraclavicular lymph nodes by which route? The answer is through the intestinal trunk, the cisterna chyli and the thoracic duct. The duct ends at the left venous angle just below those nodes. Lymph that leaves the duct there can be carried into the left supraclavicular nodes.

Question 4367. Which of these pairs belongs together: the thoracic duct with the right venous angle, the right lymphatic duct with the left venous angle, the thoracic duct with the left venous angle, or the cisterna chyli with the right venous angle? The answer is the thoracic duct with the left venous angle. The right lymphatic duct ends at the right venous angle, and the cisterna chyli lies below the diaphragm.

Question 4368. Which of these pairs belongs together: the intestinal trunk with the lower limbs, the lumbar trunks with the small intestine, the jugular trunk with the head and neck, or the bronchomediastinal trunk with the upper limb? The answer is the jugular trunk with the head and neck. The lumbar trunks drain the lower limbs and the pelvis, and the bronchomediastinal trunk drains the chest.

Question 4369. Which of these pairs belongs together: the subclavian trunk with the upper limb, the subclavian trunk with the head, the jugular trunk with the arm, or the intestinal trunk with the neck? The answer is the subclavian trunk with the upper limb. It is formed by the axillary nodes, and each trunk is named for the region it drains.

Question 4370. Which of these pairs belongs together: chyle with the intestine, chyle with the skin, lymph with the blood, or lymph with the stomach only? The answer is chyle with the intestine. Chyle is the milky lymph formed in the small intestine after a fatty meal. All lymph is tissue fluid that has entered the lymphatic vessels.

Question 4371. Which of these pairs belongs together: the anchoring filaments with the lymphatic capillary, the anchoring filaments with the valve, the valve with the tissue spaces, or the lacteal with the skin? The answer is the anchoring filaments with the lymphatic capillary. They tie the endothelial cells of that capillary to the surrounding tissue. A lacteal is a lymphatic capillary of the small intestine.

Question 4372. A described section of the intestinal wall shows a blunt-ended vessel in the center of a finger-like projection, with a network of finer vessels around it. Which vessel is the blunt-ended one? The answer is the lacteal, the central lymphatic of the villus. The fine network around it is the blood capillary bed. Its blind end lets fat enter the villus but not leave it.

Question 4373. A described specimen shows the wall of a large lymphatic vessel cut across, with an inner endothelial lining, a middle layer of smooth muscle and an outer fibrous coat. Which coat is the middle one? The answer is the tunica media. It lies between the intima and the adventitia and carries the muscle that pumps. The outer coat is the tunica adventitia.

Question 4374. Which of these statements about the lymphatic system is correct: it begins and ends in the tissues, it begins in the tissues and ends in the blood, it begins in the blood and ends in the tissues, or it begins and ends in the blood? The answer is that it begins in the tissue spaces and ends in the blood. The capillaries are blind at their tissue end.

Question 4375. What is the name of the fluid in the tissue spaces before it enters a lymphatic capillary? The answer is tissue fluid, also called interstitial fluid. Once it passes between the endothelial flaps it is called lymph. The change of name marks the change of compartment, not a change in the fluid itself.

Question 4376. A described section of a lymph node shows a thin outer coat of connective tissue, with narrow bands passing from it into the substance of the node. What are the outer coat and the bands called? The answer is the capsule and the trabeculae. The trabeculae divide the outer part of the node into compartments. Small blood vessels travel inside them.

Question 4377. A described section of a lymph node shows a narrow channel lying just beneath the capsule and receiving several vessels that pierce the capsule from the surface. What is that channel? The answer is the subcapsular sinus, also called the marginal sinus. The afferent vessels open into it. From it the lymph seeps inward through the cortex toward the medulla.

Question 4378. In which part of a lymph node do the rounded lymphoid follicles lie: the cortex, the medulla, the hilum, or the capsule? The answer is the cortex, the outer part of the node. Each follicle is a mass of lymphocytes, mainly B lymphocytes. The deeper part of the cortex, called the paracortex, holds mainly T lymphocytes.

Question 4379. True or false: a lymph node has more efferent vessels than afferent vessels. The answer is false. Several afferent vessels bring lymph into the node through its convex surface, while only one or two efferent vessels leave it at the hilum. Lymph therefore slows as it passes through the node.

Question 4380. On which part of a lymph node does the efferent vessel leave, and what else passes through that same part? The answer is the hilum, the concave notch on one side of the node, and the artery and vein pass through it too. The hilum is the only place where lymph leaves the node. The convex surface carries the afferent vessels instead.

Question 4381. A described section of a lymph node shows a rounded mass of cells with a pale center and a darker ring around it. What is the pale center called, and what happens there? The answer is the germinal center, where lymphocytes multiply. The cells there have met an antigen carried in by the lymph. The darker ring around it is made of small resting lymphocytes.

Question 4382. Which cells occupy the paracortex of a lymph node in the greatest number: T lymphocytes, B lymphocytes, plasma cells, or red blood cells? The answer is T lymphocytes. The paracortex lies deep to the follicles of the cortex. It is also the zone through which lymphocytes enter the node from the blood.

Question 4383. The high endothelial venules of a lymph node lie in which zone relative to the lymphoid follicles, and what enters the node through them? The answer is the paracortex, which lies deep to the follicles, and lymphocytes enter through them. Their tall endothelial cells let those cells leave the blood. The lymphocytes then pass into the substance of the node.

Question 4384. A described section of the central part of a lymph node shows branching cords of cells separated by a network of channels. What are the cords and the channels called? The answer is the medullary cords and the medullary sinuses. Lymph reaches them after seeping through the cortex. The sinuses converge on the hilum, where the efferent vessel begins.

Question 4385. Which cells line the sinuses of a lymph node and remove particles from the lymph: macrophages, lymphocytes, plasma cells, or fibroblasts? The answer is macrophages. They engulf bacteria, dead cells and other particles carried in the lymph. Some of them also present what they have taken up to the lymphocytes of the node.

Question 4386. In which part of a lymph node are plasma cells most numerous: the medullary cords, the germinal centers, the subcapsular sinus, or the capsule? The answer is the medullary cords of the medulla. They are the descendants of activated B lymphocytes. They secrete antibody into the lymph that is passing through the node.

Question 4387. A described cross section of a lymph node shows a dark outer rim, a paler middle zone and a central network of channels. Which zone is the paler middle one? The answer is the paracortex. It lies between the follicles of the outer cortex and the medulla. Lymphocytes enter it from the blood through high endothelial venules.

Question 4388. A constructed illustration: a person has a sore throat and feels tender lumps in the neck below the jaw. Which nodes are involved, and why are they tender? The answer is the deep cervical nodes, and they are tender because the swollen tissue stretches their capsule. They filter the lymph coming from the throat and the tonsils. This illustration is not a particular case.

Question 4389. A constructed illustration: a small lump is found in the upper outer part of the breast, and the first node shown to receive its cells lies in the armpit. What name is given to that first node? The answer is the sentinel node. It is the node that receives lymph from that part of the breast first, as the anatomy predicts.

Question 4390. A constructed illustration: after a long walk a person has a tender swelling in the hollow behind the knee. Which group of nodes is inflamed there? The answer is the popliteal nodes. They lie in the fat of the popliteal fossa. They receive lymph from the deep structures of the leg and from the territory of the small saphenous vein.

Question 4391. Which group of nodes lies in the groin just below the inguinal ligament and drains the skin of the lower limb and the perineum? The answer is the superficial inguinal nodes. They lie in the fatty tissue below the ligament. Their efferent vessels pass to the deep inguinal and the external iliac nodes.

Question 4392. A described lymphangiogram of the groin shows a fan of vessels ending in a group of small rounded nodes just below the inguinal ligament. Which group is shown, and where does its lymph go next? The answer is the superficial inguinal nodes, and their lymph passes to the deep inguinal and external iliac nodes. The iliac nodes lie inside the pelvis along the external iliac vessels.

Question 4393. Which statement about the size of a normal lymph node is correct: it is about 1 to 2 centimeters long, it is about 10 centimeters long, it is always smaller than 1 millimeter, or it is the size of a walnut? The answer is that a normal node is about 1 to 2 centimeters long. Most are smaller and cannot be felt through the skin.

Question 4394. A constructed illustration: a young man has a swelling of one testis, and the nodes first involved are found in the abdomen rather than in the groin. Which nodes are they, and why? The answer is the para-aortic nodes. The testis carried its vessels and its lymphatics with it as it descended from the abdomen. The skin of the scrotum, by contrast, drains to the inguinal nodes.

Question 4395. A constructed illustration: a person has a firm lump just above the left clavicle, in the angle between the two heads of the sternocleidomastoid muscle. Which group of nodes lies there? The answer is the deep cervical nodes, at their lowest point beside the internal jugular vein. The thoracic duct ends just below that point. A lump there can block the return of lymph from much of the body.

Question 4396. A described dissection shows a chain of small rounded nodes running down the neck along the internal jugular vein, deep to the sternocleidomastoid muscle. Which chain is shown, and which trunk does it form? The answer is the deep cervical chain, which forms the jugular trunk. Its efferent vessels end at the venous angle of their own side.

Question 4397. Which group of nodes lies in the mesentery and drains the small intestine and the colon, and what becomes of their efferent vessels? The answer is the mesenteric nodes, and their efferent vessels form the intestinal trunk. That trunk joins the cisterna chyli in the abdomen. The lymph they carry is rich in fat after a meal.

Question 4398. True or false: the lymph in the medullary sinuses of a lymph node has already passed through the cortex. The answer is true. Lymph enters at the subcapsular sinus, seeps through the cortex and the paracortex, and reaches the medulla. It then leaves by the efferent vessel at the hilum, so the order of flow is from the convex surface inward.

Question 4399. True or false: the capsule and the trabeculae form the framework of a lymph node. The answer is true. The capsule is the outer coat, and the trabeculae are its inward extensions. They divide the cortex into compartments and carry small blood vessels into the node.

Question 4400. Which of these is the direction of lymph flow through a lymph node: from the hilum to the capsule, from the capsule to the hilum, from the medulla to the cortex, or from the efferent vessel to the afferent vessel? The answer is from the capsule to the hilum. Lymph enters the subcapsular sinus and leaves by the efferent vessel at the hilum.

To summarize, the lymphatic system begins in the tissue spaces as blind-ended capillaries whose overlapping endothelial cells and anchoring filaments let fluid in but not out, and it ends where the thoracic duct and the right lymphatic duct open at the venous angles. Lymph is driven by muscle contraction, breathing, the pulsation of nearby arteries and the valves that divide each vessel into pumping segments, because there is no central lymphatic pump. The intestinal trunk and the two lumbar trunks form the cisterna chyli, and the thoracic duct drains both lower limbs, the abdomen and the whole left upper quarter. A lymph node receives lymph by afferent vessels on its convex surface, filters it through the subcapsular sinus, the cortex and the medulla, and returns it by an efferent vessel at the hilum. The deep cervical, axillary, inguinal, popliteal and para-aortic nodes are the groups a listener meets most often, and each is named for the region whose lymph it filters.
