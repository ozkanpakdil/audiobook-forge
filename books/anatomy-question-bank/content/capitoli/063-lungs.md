# Chapter 63 — Lungs

This chapter examines the lungs as organs: their lobes, the fissures between them, the bronchopulmonary segments within them, the hilum, and the root of the lung. It holds one hundred questions, and each one gives its answer immediately and then explains why it is right, so nothing has to be held in mind. The lungs are the paired organs of gas exchange, and their internal geography matters to the radiologist, the physician and the surgeon alike. Every item is written to be answered out loud, so descriptions of films and sections are spoken rather than shown. This is educational material, and it supports rather than replaces an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 6201. Which of the two lungs is divided into three lobes by its fissures? The answer is the right lung. It carries a superior lobe, a middle lobe and an inferior lobe, separated by an oblique fissure and a horizontal fissure. The left lung is divided into two lobes only, because the heart occupies much of the space on that side.

Question 6202. How many lobes are found in the left lung: two, three, four, or five? The answer is two. The left lung has a superior lobe and an inferior lobe, separated by a single oblique fissure. It has no middle lobe, because the heart lies against that side of the chest and takes the space a middle lobe would need.

Question 6203. What is the name of the tongue-shaped projection of lung tissue that hangs below the cardiac notch of the left lung? The answer is the lingula. It is part of the superior lobe, and it lies just above the oblique fissure. The word means a little tongue, which is what its shape suggests.

Question 6204. The lingula belongs to which lobe of the left lung: the superior lobe, the inferior lobe, the middle lobe, or a lobe of its own? The answer is the superior lobe. The lingula is the lower, forward part of that lobe and lies below the cardiac notch. It is the counterpart of the right middle lobe, but not a separate lobe.

Question 6205. A described chest radiograph shows a deep notch cut into the front of the left lung, with a small tongue of tissue below it. Which lobe carries the notch, and what is the tongue called? The answer is the superior lobe and the lingula. The notch itself is the cardiac notch, produced by the heart pressing against that border.

Question 6206. Does the lingula lie above or below the oblique fissure of the left lung, and in which lobe does that place it? The answer is above the fissure, in the superior lobe. The oblique fissure runs downward and forward across the lung and separates the superior lobe from the inferior lobe. Everything above it on the left, including the lingula, belongs to the superior lobe.

Question 6207. Which surface of a lung lies against the ribs: the mediastinal surface, the diaphragmatic surface, the costal surface, or the interlobar surface? The answer is the costal surface. It is the broad, convex outer surface that follows the curve of the chest wall. The mediastinal surface faces the middle of the chest, and the diaphragmatic surface rests on the diaphragm.

Question 6208. What is the name of the surface of the lung that rests on the dome of the diaphragm? The answer is the diaphragmatic surface. It is concave, because it is molded to the dome of the muscle beneath it. The right diaphragmatic surface is higher than the left, since the liver pushes the right dome upward.

Question 6209. A constructed illustration: a growth at the left hilum narrows the left main bronchus, and airflow to the whole left lung falls. Which lobes of that lung are affected? The answer is both the superior lobe and the inferior lobe. The left main bronchus supplies the entire left lung before it divides.

Question 6210. True or false: the left lung has a middle lobe that is simply smaller than the middle lobe on the right. The answer is false. The left lung has only a superior lobe and an inferior lobe. The lingula occupies the position of that lobe, but it is part of the superior lobe and not a lobe of its own.

Question 6211. Which of these pairs belongs together: the lingula with the left superior lobe, the middle lobe with the left lung, the cardiac notch with the right, or the horizontal fissure with the left? The answer is the lingula with the left superior lobe. It lies below the cardiac notch and above the oblique fissure.

Question 6212. Which lung is shorter and wider than the other, and what accounts for that difference? The answer is the right lung, because the right dome of the diaphragm lies higher than the left. The liver fills the space beneath the right dome and pushes it upward, so the right lung has more room across.

Question 6213. Which statement about the lobes is correct: the right lung has three lobes and the left has two, both lungs have three, the left has three and the right has two, or neither lung has a fissure? The answer is that the right lung has three lobes and the left has two. The middle lobe exists only on the right, where the heart does not intrude.

Question 6214. A described left lung shows a broad groove curving over the mediastinal surface above the hilum, and a notch on the anterior border below it. Name the two features. The answer is an impression of the aortic arch and the cardiac notch. The aorta lies against the mediastinal surface above the root, and the heart presses on the border below.

Question 6215. Which lobe of the right lung is bounded above by the horizontal fissure and below by the oblique fissure: the superior lobe, the middle lobe, the inferior lobe, or the lingula? The answer is the middle lobe, a wedge on the front and outer side of the lung. The lingula lies in the same position on the left, but it is part of the superior lobe.

Question 6216. Which part of the lung rises above the first rib into the neck, and what covers it there? The answer is the apex of the lung, covered by the suprapleural membrane. The apex reaches about 2 to 3 centimeters above the medial third of the clavicle. The tough membrane keeps the apex from expanding upward when the pressure inside the chest falls.

Question 6217. What is the name of the fibrous sheet that stretches over the apex of the lung? The answer is the suprapleural membrane. It is attached to the inner border of the first rib and to the transverse process of the seventh cervical vertebra. Because it is tense, the apex of the lung stays below it even during a deep breath.

Question 6218. A constructed illustration: a sharp instrument enters the base of the neck above the left clavicle. Which part of the lung lies there and is at risk? The answer is the apex of the left lung, which rises into the root of the neck. Such a wound can open the pleural cavity and let air enter.

Question 6219. A described lateral radiograph of the chest shows two fissure lines crossing the right lung, one almost horizontal and one running downward and forward, so that three lobes are outlined. Which lobes are shown, and which fissure separates the middle lobe from the inferior lobe? The answer is the superior, the middle and the inferior lobes, separated by the oblique fissure.

Question 6220. Which statement about the cardiac notch is correct: it lies on the anterior border of the left lung, on the posterior border of the right lung, in the groove for the aorta, or in the groove for the azygos vein? The answer is the anterior border of the left lung. The heart presses there and leaves a concave notch, and the lingula lies just below it.

Question 6221. Which fissure separates the superior lobe of the right lung from the middle lobe? The answer is the horizontal fissure. It runs forward from the oblique fissure to the anterior border of the lung. It is found only in the right lung, because only the right lung has a middle lobe.

Question 6222. Which fissure is present in both the right lung and the left lung? The answer is the oblique fissure. It runs downward and forward across each lung and separates the upper part of the lung from the lower part. On the right it separates the superior and middle lobes above from the inferior lobe below.

Question 6223. Which fissure lies between the middle lobe and the inferior lobe of the right lung: the horizontal fissure, the oblique fissure, the azygos fissure, or the inferior accessory fissure? The answer is the oblique fissure. The middle lobe sits above it and the inferior lobe below. The horizontal fissure lies above the middle lobe and separates it from the superior lobe.

Question 6224. On the front of the chest, the horizontal fissure of the right lung lies at the level of which costal cartilage: the second, the fourth, the sixth, or the eighth? The answer is the fourth costal cartilage. From there the fissure runs backward to meet the oblique fissure near the middle of the side of the chest.

Question 6225. A described lateral radiograph of the chest shows a single line running downward and forward from the back of the chest to end near the front at the level of the sixth costal cartilage. Which fissure is shown, and which two parts of the lung does it separate? The answer is the oblique fissure, and it separates the upper lobes above from the lower lobe below.

Question 6226. On the chest wall, the oblique fissure follows the course of which rib? The answer is the sixth rib. The fissure begins behind at about the level of the third thoracic spine and runs downward and forward. Its surface marking is a line that curves along the sixth rib to reach the sixth costal cartilage in front.

Question 6227. A described section of the chest is cut in such a way that the whole length of the oblique fissure lies in the plane of the cut. How does that plane run? The answer is obliquely, downward and forward, at an angle to the sagittal, coronal and transverse planes. The fissure therefore cannot be shown in full in any one of the three standard planes.

Question 6228. Which of these pairs belongs together: the horizontal fissure with the right lung, the oblique fissure with the right only, the azygos fissure with the left lung, or the lingula with the inferior lobe? The answer is the horizontal fissure with the right lung. The oblique fissure belongs to both lungs, the azygos fissure is a right-sided variant, and the lingula lies in the superior lobe.

Question 6229. True or false: the horizontal fissure is found in both lungs. The answer is false. Only the right lung has a horizontal fissure, and it separates the superior lobe from the middle lobe. The left lung has a single oblique fissure, because it has no middle lobe.

Question 6230. A constructed illustration: a wound enters the right side of the chest in the midaxillary line at the level of the sixth rib and cuts the lung along the tissue plane that lies there. Which fissure has been opened, and which lobes does it separate? The answer is the oblique fissure, which separates the superior and middle lobes above from the inferior lobe below.

Question 6231. A curved line like a comma crosses the apex of the right lung. Which fissure is described, and what is the wedge of lung behind it called? The answer is the azygos fissure, and the wedge is the azygos lobe. The fold is produced by the arch of the azygos vein and is a normal variant, not a true lobe.

Question 6232. When a small extra fissure separates the medial basal segment from the rest of the right lower lobe, what is it called: the horizontal fissure, the superior accessory fissure, the inferior accessory fissure, or the azygos fissure? The answer is the inferior accessory fissure. It is a normal variant and is absent in most people. The superior accessory fissure separates the superior segment of the lower lobe.

Question 6233. A described chest radiograph shows a fine line crossing the right lung from the hilum toward the front, with a small wedge of lung lying below it and above the oblique fissure. Which fissure is shown, and which lobe does the wedge represent? The answer is the horizontal fissure, and the wedge is the middle lobe.

Question 6234. A described computed tomography section through the chest shows a thin line running forward from the hilum across the right lung, dividing it into an upper part that reaches the front of the chest and a smaller part below. Which fissure is this, and which two lobes does it separate? The answer is the horizontal fissure, separating the superior lobe above from the middle lobe below.

Question 6235. In most people the fissures are not complete, and air can pass from one lobe to another where the pleura is missing. What follows from this: a lobe whose own bronchus is blocked may still be ventilated, the lobes cannot move, the pleural cavities join, or the fissure disappears? The answer is that a lobe with a blocked bronchus may still be ventilated.

Question 6236. What is the name of the wedge-shaped unit of a lobe that is supplied by its own segmental bronchus and its own segmental artery? The answer is the bronchopulmonary segment. It is the largest subdivision of a lobe, and it has its own connective tissue septum. Because each one is separate, a surgeon can remove a single segment.

Question 6237. How many bronchopulmonary segments are usually described in the right lung: eight, nine, ten, or twelve? The answer is ten. The right upper lobe holds three segments, the middle lobe holds two, and the right lower lobe holds five. The segments are named for their position, such as apical, posterior and anterior.

Question 6238. How many bronchopulmonary segments are usually described in the left lung? The answer is eight. The left upper lobe holds four segments and the left lower lobe holds four. The count is smaller than on the right because two pairs of segments are joined.

Question 6239. Which airway supplies one bronchopulmonary segment: the trachea, a main bronchus, a lobar bronchus, or a segmental bronchus? The answer is a segmental bronchus. The trachea divides into the two main bronchi, each main bronchus supplies a whole lung, and each lobar bronchus supplies a lobe. The segmental bronchus is also called the tertiary bronchus.

Question 6240. Which two bronchopulmonary segments make up the middle lobe of the right lung? The answer is the lateral segment and the medial segment. They lie one behind the other in the wedge of the middle lobe. The medial segment rests against the heart, and the lateral segment reaches the side of the chest.

Question 6241. Which segment of a lower lobe lies highest and furthest back, behind all of the other segments of that lobe? The answer is the superior segment. It occupies the top of the lower lobe, immediately behind the oblique fissure. It has its own bronchus, which arises behind the other bronchi of the lower lobe.

Question 6242. Which segment of the right upper lobe occupies the very apex of the lung: the apical segment, the posterior segment, the anterior segment, or the medial segment? The answer is the apical segment. It rises into the root of the neck and lies above the rest of the upper lobe. The posterior segment lies behind it and the anterior segment in front of it.

Question 6243. Within a bronchopulmonary segment the bronchus and the artery run together at the center, while the veins run in the septum at the edge. Which vessels therefore separate one segment from the next? The answer is the pulmonary veins. Because the veins lie between segments rather than inside them, a surgeon removing one segment can follow them as a guide.

Question 6244. A described computed tomography section shows a wedge of lung whose base lies against the pleura and whose apex points toward the hilum, separated from its neighbor by a thin septum in which a vein runs. Which unit is shown, and why does the vein lie in the septum rather than inside the wedge? The answer is a bronchopulmonary segment, and the veins run between segments.

Question 6245. What lies at the center of a bronchopulmonary segment: the segmental bronchus with its artery, the pulmonary vein, the pleural reflection, or the bronchial vein? The answer is the segmental bronchus with its artery. They enter the segment together at its apex, which points toward the hilum. The pulmonary veins lie at the edges of the segment, in the septa between segments.

Question 6246. Which single segment of the left lower lobe takes the place of the anterior basal and medial basal segments of the right lung? The answer is the anteromedial basal segment. On the left these two segments are fused into one. This fusion is one reason the left lung has eight segments rather than ten.

Question 6247. Name the four bronchopulmonary segments of the left upper lobe, spoken as a list. The answer is the apicoposterior segment, the anterior segment, the superior lingular segment and the inferior lingular segment. The apicoposterior segment is formed by the fusion of the apical and posterior segments. The two lingular segments together form the lingula.

Question 6248. True or false: every bronchopulmonary segment has its own segmental bronchus and its own segmental artery. The answer is true. The bronchus and the artery enter the segment together at its apex and branch within it. The pulmonary veins, by contrast, run between segments in the connective tissue septa.

Question 6249. Which of these pairs belongs together: the lingular segments with the left upper lobe, the medial basal segment with the left lower lobe, the apical segment with the middle lobe, or the lateral segment with the right lower lobe? The answer is the lingular segments with the left upper lobe. The left lower lobe has no separate medial basal segment.

Question 6250. A constructed illustration: a child sitting upright inhales a small object, and it lodges in an airway instead of passing further. Which main bronchus is more likely to receive it, and why? The answer is the right main bronchus. It is wider, shorter and more vertical than the left, and the aortic arch displaces the left main bronchus sideways.

Question 6251. A constructed illustration: a person lying flat on the back inhales vomited material, and it settles in the lowest parts of the lung. Which segments are those? The answer is the posterior segment of the upper lobe and the superior segment of the lower lobe. Flat on the back, those two segments lie furthest back and lowest, so material runs into them.

Question 6252. A described chest radiograph of a person who has been standing shows an opacity in the lowest and most posterior part of the right lower lobe, close to the spine. Which segment is affected? The answer is the posterior basal segment. When a person stands, that segment is the most dependent part of the lung, and material inhaled in that position collects there.

Question 6253. Which segments are removed when a surgeon operates on the lingula: the superior and inferior lingular segments, the lateral and medial segments of the middle lobe, the apical and posterior segments, or the medial basal and lateral basal segments? The answer is the superior and inferior lingular segments of the left superior lobe.

Question 6254. A described specimen shows a wedge of lung with its own bronchus and artery entering at the apex and a thin sheet of connective tissue separating it from the neighboring wedge. What is the wedge, and what runs in the sheet? The answer is a bronchopulmonary segment, with pulmonary veins running in the sheet. The sheet is the intersegmental septum.

Question 6255. On the right side, the bronchus of the superior segment of the lower lobe arises at almost the same level as which other bronchus? The answer is the middle lobe bronchus. Both take origin near the point where the right main bronchus continues into the bronchus of the lower lobe. Surgeons use that level as a landmark when they dissect the hilum.

Question 6256. Which statement about the eparterial bronchus is correct: it is the right upper lobe bronchus and lies above the pulmonary artery, it is the left upper lobe bronchus and lies below the pulmonary artery, it supplies the middle lobe, or it is the first branch of the trachea? The answer is that it is the right upper lobe bronchus, lying above the pulmonary artery.

Question 6257. Which segment of the right lung has no separate counterpart on the left, because it is fused with a neighboring segment there: the medial basal segment, the lateral basal segment, the posterior basal segment, or the superior segment? The answer is the medial basal segment, which is fused with the anterior basal segment to form the anteromedial basal segment of the left lung.

Question 6258. Which segment of the right lower lobe lies against the mediastinal surface of the lung, next to the right atrium? The answer is the medial basal segment. It occupies the inner and lower part of the lobe, close to the heart. It is the segment that has no separate counterpart on the left.

Question 6259. A described axial computed tomography section shows a wedge of dense lung tissue in the posterior part of the right upper lobe, reaching the pleural surface behind the rest of the lobe. Which segment is affected? The answer is the posterior segment of the right upper lobe. It lies behind the apical and anterior segments and reaches the posterior chest wall.

Question 6260. What structure separates one bronchopulmonary segment from the next, and what does it carry? The answer is the intersegmental septum, which carries the pulmonary veins. The septum is a thin sheet of connective tissue continuous with the visceral pleura. Because the veins lie within it, they can be preserved when a single segment is removed.

Question 6261. The left lung is usually described as having eight bronchopulmonary segments rather than ten. Why: two pairs of segments are fused on the left, the left lung has no lower lobe, the lingula replaces four segments, or there are no segmental bronchi? The answer is that two pairs are fused, forming the apicoposterior and anteromedial basal segments.

Question 6262. Into which two segments is the lingula divided? The answer is the superior lingular segment and the inferior lingular segment. They lie one above the other in the tongue of lung below the cardiac notch. Both of them belong to the left superior lobe.

Question 6263. A described specimen shows the trachea dividing into two main bronchi, one wider, shorter and more vertical than the other. Which is the right main bronchus, and why is the left more horizontal? The answer is that the wider, shorter and more vertical tube is the right main bronchus. The left runs more horizontally because the arch of the aorta crosses over it.

Question 6264. Which two segments are supplied by the middle lobe bronchus: the lateral and medial segments, the apical and posterior segments, the anterior and medial basal segments, or the superior and lateral basal segments? The answer is the lateral and medial segments. The middle lobe bronchus arises from the right main bronchus a little below the upper lobe bronchus.

Question 6265. A constructed illustration: a surgeon plans to remove one segment of the right lower lobe that lies against the mediastinum, and must leave the neighboring segments with their venous drainage intact. Which segment is to be removed, and which vessels must be preserved? The answer is the medial basal segment, with the pulmonary veins in the intersegmental septa preserved.

Question 6266. What is the name of the area on the mediastinal surface of the lung where the structures of the root enter and leave? The answer is the hilum of the lung. It lies on the mediastinal surface, roughly in the middle of that surface. The pleura reflects from the mediastinum onto the root at the hilum.

Question 6267. Which structure lies highest in the hilum of the left lung? The answer is the left pulmonary artery. It arches over the left main bronchus before entering the lung. On the right, by contrast, the upper lobe bronchus lies above the pulmonary artery, so the artery is not the highest structure there.

Question 6268. Which structure is the most superior one in the hilum of the right lung? The answer is the eparterial bronchus, which is the bronchus of the upper lobe. It arises from the right main bronchus before that tube enters the lung, and it lies above the pulmonary artery. The lower lobe bronchus lies below the artery.

Question 6269. Which structure lies most anterior in the hilum of each lung: the superior pulmonary vein, the pulmonary artery, the principal bronchus, or the bronchial artery? The answer is the superior pulmonary vein. From front to back the order is the vein, then the pulmonary artery, then the bronchus. The pulmonary veins are the most anterior structures of the root.

Question 6270. How many pulmonary veins leave each hilum: one, two, three, or four? The answer is two, a superior pulmonary vein and an inferior pulmonary vein. Each of them drains part of the lung and carries oxygenated blood. They open into the left atrium, one above the other.

Question 6271. On a normal frontal chest radiograph, how do the two hila usually relate: the left lies higher than the right, the right lies higher than the left, the two lie level, or the hila are formed by the bronchi? The answer is that the left hilum lies higher than the right, usually by about 1 to 2 centimeters. A right hilum that lies higher suggests disease.

Question 6272. A described frontal chest radiograph shows a branching shadow at each hilum, and the left shadow lies slightly higher than the right. Which vessels form most of each hilar shadow, and why do the bronchi contribute little? The answer is the pulmonary arteries. The bronchi are filled with air and cast almost no shadow.

Question 6273. A described axial computed tomography section at the level of the hila shows, on one side, an air-filled tube with the branching vessel above it and in front of it. Which hilum is shown, and what is the relation between the artery and the bronchus there? The answer is the left hilum, where the pulmonary artery lies above the bronchus.

Question 6274. Which nerve lies immediately anterior to the root of the lung on each side, running down in front of the hilum? The answer is the phrenic nerve. It descends between the mediastinal pleura and the pericardium, in front of the root. Because of that position it is at risk during operations on the hilum.

Question 6275. Which nerve lies immediately posterior to the root of the lung, where it gives branches to the pulmonary plexus? The answer is the vagus nerve. It passes behind the root and breaks up into the posterior pulmonary plexus. The phrenic nerve, by contrast, lies in front of the root.

Question 6276. A constructed illustration: a growth at the hilum of the left lung presses on the nerve that lies immediately behind the root there. Which nerve is compressed, and what does it supply in the chest? The answer is the vagus nerve, which supplies parasympathetic fibers to the bronchi and the lungs through the pulmonary plexus.

Question 6277. A constructed illustration: enlarged lymph nodes at the right hilum press on a nerve that runs in front of the root, and the diaphragm on that side stops moving. Which nerve is affected, and where does it lie in relation to the root? The answer is the right phrenic nerve, which lies anterior to the root of the lung.

Question 6278. True or false: the hilum of each lung is surrounded by a reflection of pleura that continues below the root as the pulmonary ligament. The answer is true. The pleura leaves the mediastinum and wraps around the root as a sleeve. Below the hilum the two layers hang down together and form the pulmonary ligament.

Question 6279. Which of these pairs belongs together: the pulmonary ligament with the inferior pulmonary vein, the pulmonary ligament with the main bronchus, the phrenic nerve with the back of the root, or the vagus nerve with the front of the root? The answer is the pulmonary ligament with the inferior pulmonary vein. The ligament hangs below the root and allows the vein to distend.

Question 6280. Name three kinds of structure that pass through the hilum of a lung, spoken as a list. The answer is the principal bronchus, the pulmonary artery and the pulmonary veins, together with the bronchial vessels, the lymphatics and the nerves. The bronchi lie behind and the veins in front. The whole group is wrapped in a sleeve of pleura.

Question 6281. In a described transverse section across the hilum, three structures lie in a row, with a thin-walled vessel in front, a thick-walled vessel in the middle and a cartilage-bearing tube at the back. Name them in order from front to back. The answer is the superior pulmonary vein, the pulmonary artery and the principal bronchus.

Question 6282. Which structure curves over the hilum of the right lung, above the root: the arch of the azygos vein, the arch of the aorta, the left ventricle, or the descending aorta? The answer is the arch of the azygos vein. It crosses above the root and joins the superior vena cava. The arch of the aorta passes over the left root instead.

Question 6283. Which structure arches over the hilum of the left lung, and which two structures pass beneath it to reach that hilum? The answer is the arch of the aorta, and the left pulmonary artery and the left main bronchus pass beneath it. The left main bronchus lies below the arch, which is why it runs more horizontally than the right one.

Question 6284. What is the name of the bundle of structures that suspends the lung from the mediastinum and connects it to the rest of the body? The answer is the root of the lung. It lies at the hilum, where the pleura wraps around it. The root carries the airway, the vessels, the nerves and the lymphatics of the lung.

Question 6285. Which of these structures lies outside the root of the lung: the trachea, the principal bronchus, the pulmonary artery, or the superior pulmonary vein? The answer is the trachea. It ends above the hila by dividing into the two main bronchi. The root of each lung begins with the main bronchus and includes the vessels and the nerves.

Question 6286. Which arteries supply the tissue of the lung itself, as distinct from the pulmonary arteries that carry blood for gas exchange? The answer is the bronchial arteries. They arise from the descending thoracic aorta and from the posterior intercostal arteries. They follow the bronchi and nourish the walls of the airways and the supporting tissue of the lung.

Question 6287. From which vessel do the left bronchial arteries usually arise, and how many of them are there? The answer is the descending thoracic aorta, which gives two left bronchial arteries. The right bronchial artery is usually single and arises from a posterior intercostal artery or from a common trunk with a left one.

Question 6288. What is the main function of the bronchial vessels in the lung: to nourish the tissues of the lung, to exchange oxygen and carbon dioxide, to drain the pleural cavity, or to supply the pericardium? The answer is to nourish the tissues of the lung. Gas exchange belongs to the pulmonary arteries and veins. The bronchial arteries are much smaller than the pulmonary arteries.

Question 6289. Into which vessel do the bronchial veins of the right lung usually drain: the azygos vein, the superior vena cava, the left atrium, or the internal thoracic vein? The answer is the azygos vein. On the left the bronchial veins drain into the accessory hemiazygos vein. A small amount of bronchial venous blood also returns through the pulmonary veins.

Question 6290. A described dissection of the root of a lung shows the structures wrapped in a tube of pleura, with a double fold hanging down below the hilum like a curtain. What is the fold, and what does it contain? The answer is the pulmonary ligament, which contains the inferior pulmonary vein and some lymphatics.

Question 6291. A described cross section through the root of a lung shows, from front to back, a thin-walled vessel, a thick-walled vessel and a tube with plates of cartilage in its wall. Identify them in order, and say which lies lowest in the left hilum. The answer is the superior pulmonary vein, the pulmonary artery and the principal bronchus, the bronchus lying lowest on the left.

Question 6292. Which large vein arches over the root of the right lung before it enters the superior vena cava? The answer is the azygos vein. Its arch crosses above the hilum and is a useful landmark in the chest. On the left the corresponding structure above the root is the arch of the aorta.

Question 6293. What structure forms a sleeve around the root of the lung and continues below it as the pulmonary ligament? The answer is the pleura. It leaves the mediastinum, covers the root, and follows the lung into the chest. That sleeve allows the root to move as the lung expands without tearing the pleural lining.

Question 6294. A constructed illustration: during an operation a surgeon divides the fold of pleura that hangs below the hilum in order to free the lung. Which structure lying in that fold must be secured before the fold is cut? The answer is the inferior pulmonary vein. It runs in the pulmonary ligament below the root.

Question 6295. A constructed illustration: during removal of the right lung, the surgeon must avoid the structure that arches over the root, because tearing it would cause serious bleeding. Which structure is at risk, and where does it lie relative to the root? The answer is the azygos vein, which arches above and behind the root.

Question 6296. True or false: the pulmonary arteries carry oxygenated blood away from the heart to the lungs. The answer is false. The pulmonary arteries carry deoxygenated blood from the right ventricle to the lungs for gas exchange. The pulmonary veins carry oxygenated blood back to the left atrium.

Question 6297. Which of these pairs belongs together: the bronchial arteries with the descending thoracic aorta, the pulmonary arteries with the aorta, the bronchial veins with the left atrium, or the pulmonary veins with the azygos vein? The answer is the bronchial arteries with the descending thoracic aorta. The pulmonary arteries arise from the right ventricle, and the pulmonary veins open into the left atrium.

Question 6298. Name the two sets of arteries that carry blood into the lungs, spoken as a list. The answer is the pulmonary arteries and the bronchial arteries. The pulmonary arteries carry deoxygenated blood for gas exchange and take the greater part of the flow. The bronchial arteries are small and nourish the tissues of the lung.

Question 6299. Which statement about the pulmonary plexus is correct: it surrounds the root and carries sympathetic and parasympathetic fibers, it lies only inside the hilum, it carries only motor fibers, or it is a branch of the phrenic nerve? The answer is that it surrounds the root and carries both kinds of fiber. The sympathetic fibers widen the bronchi, while the vagal fibers narrow them and increase secretion.

Question 6300. Which structure lies directly in front of the root of the right lung: the superior vena cava, the descending aorta, the esophagus, or the thoracic duct? The answer is the superior vena cava. The esophagus, the descending aorta and the thoracic duct lie behind the root. In front of the left root, by contrast, lies the left ventricle.

To summarize, the right lung has three lobes and the left lung has two, and the lingula below the cardiac notch is the left counterpart of the right middle lobe, though it remains part of the left superior lobe. The oblique fissure is present in both lungs, runs downward and forward to end near the sixth costal cartilage, and the horizontal fissure belongs to the right lung alone at the level of the fourth costal cartilage. Each bronchopulmonary segment has its own segmental bronchus and artery at its center, with the pulmonary veins in the septa between segments, and the right lung holds ten segments against eight on the left. The hilum is the point at which the root enters the mediastinal surface, with the bronchus behind and the superior pulmonary vein in front, the eparterial bronchus highest on the right and the pulmonary artery highest on the left, and the left hilum lying about 1 to 2 centimeters above the right. The root carries the main bronchus, the pulmonary artery, the pulmonary veins, the bronchial vessels, the lymphatics and the nerves, wrapped in pleura that hangs below as the pulmonary ligament.
