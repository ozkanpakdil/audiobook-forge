# Chapter 2 — Body Cavities and Membranes

This chapter walks through the spaces inside the body and the membranes that line them. It takes the cavities in order, from the cranial cavity inside the skull through the vertebral, thoracic, abdominal and pelvic cavities, and then treats the smaller serous spaces of the pleura, the pericardium and the peritoneum together with the two layers that form each one. The last part turns to epithelial tissue, the sheet of cells that lines every one of those spaces and covers the surfaces of the body. It holds one hundred questions, and each one gives its answer immediately after the question and then explains why that answer is right, so nothing has to be held in mind until the end. This is educational material, and it supports rather than replaces an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 101. Which cavity is enclosed by the bones of the skull, and what does it hold? The answer is the cranial cavity, which holds the brain with its membranes and the cerebrospinal fluid around it. The vault and the base of the skull form a rigid box that protects its contents.

Question 102. The floor of the cranial cavity is divided into three fossae. Which of them lies most anteriorly: the anterior cranial fossa, the middle cranial fossa, the posterior cranial fossa, or the infratemporal fossa? The answer is the anterior cranial fossa. It lies above the orbits and the nasal cavity and holds the frontal lobes of the brain.

Question 103. A described section through the base of the skull shows a shallow shelf above the orbits, a deeper hollow behind it, and a broad basin at the back. Which fossa does the shelf represent? The answer is the anterior cranial fossa. It is the highest of the three fossae and holds the frontal lobes.

Question 104. Which fold of dura mater forms a tent over the cerebellum and separates it from the occipital lobes of the cerebrum? The answer is the tentorium cerebelli. Its free inner edge bounds the tentorial notch, through which the midbrain passes. The falx cerebri, in contrast, stands upright between the two cerebral hemispheres.

Question 105. Which sickle-shaped fold of dura mater lies in the midline between the two cerebral hemispheres? The answer is the falx cerebri. It runs in the longitudinal fissure from the roof of the skull down to the corpus callosum. Its upper edge holds the superior sagittal sinus and its lower edge the inferior sagittal sinus.

Question 106. Which of these venous channels runs in the free lower edge of the falx cerebri: the superior sagittal sinus, the inferior sagittal sinus, the straight sinus, or the transverse sinus? The answer is the inferior sagittal sinus. It joins the great cerebral vein to form the straight sinus.

Question 107. A constructed illustration: a blow to the temple is followed by a quickly enlarging collection of arterial blood between the skull and the dura mater. Which vessel has most likely been torn? The answer is the middle meningeal artery. It runs in a groove on the inner surface of the temporal and parietal bones. Its branches lie close to the bone, so a fracture there can tear them.

Question 108. True or false: the cranial cavity communicates with the vertebral cavity through the foramen magnum. The answer is true. The foramen magnum lies in the occipital bone at the base of the skull. The medulla oblongata, its membranes and the vertebral arteries pass through it.

Question 109. Which cavity is formed by the vertebral arches behind the bodies of the vertebrae, and what runs inside it? The answer is the vertebral cavity, which holds the spinal cord, its membranes and the cerebrospinal fluid around it. It begins at the foramen magnum and runs down to the lower part of the sacrum.

Question 110. Which of these lies outside the dura mater in the vertebral canal: the spinal cord, the cerebrospinal fluid, the epidural fat and venous plexus, or the arachnoid mater? The answer is the epidural fat and the internal vertebral venous plexus. That space lies between the dura mater and the periosteum lining the canal.

Question 111. The spinal cord tapers to a cone at its lower end. What is that cone called, and at what level does it lie? The answer is the conus medullaris, which ends at about the level of the first or second lumbar vertebra. Below it the canal holds a leash of nerve roots instead of cord.

Question 112. Which band of fibrous tissue lies immediately behind the bodies of the vertebrae, separating them from the dura mater of the spinal cord? The answer is the posterior longitudinal ligament. It runs inside the vertebral canal along the back of the vertebral bodies and the discs. In front of the bodies lies the anterior longitudinal ligament.

Question 113. In the adult the spinal cord ends well above the lower end of the canal. Which statement explains that: the cord outgrows the column, the column outgrows the cord, the filum terminale pulls the cord up, or the sacrum fuses early? The answer is that the column outgrows the cord. In the embryo they begin at the same level. The lower nerve roots must then travel down to reach their openings.

Question 114. A described magnetic resonance image of the lower back shows the vertebral canal below the end of the spinal cord filled with fine strands that fan downward. What is that bundle called? The answer is the cauda equina. It consists of the lumbar and sacral nerve roots running down inside the canal to their openings.

Question 115. A constructed illustration: a needle is placed in the midline of the lower back to sample cerebrospinal fluid below the second lumbar vertebra. Which space does the needle enter, and why is that level chosen? The answer is the subarachnoid space, which holds the fluid. The level is chosen because the cord has already ended above it, so only the moving roots of the cauda equina lie in the way.

Question 116. Which sheet of muscle forms the floor of the thoracic cavity and separates it from the abdominal cavity? The answer is the diaphragm. It is a dome-shaped muscle attached to the lower ribs, the sternum and the lumbar vertebrae. The esophagus, the aorta and the inferior vena cava pass through openings in it.

Question 117. Which of these divides the thoracic cavity into two lateral spaces and a central compartment: the diaphragm, the mediastinum, the pericardium, or the sternum? The answer is the mediastinum. It is the central partition between the two pleural cavities and holds the heart, the great vessels and the esophagus.

Question 118. Which of these pairs belongs together: the mediastinum with the space between the two lungs, the pleural cavity with the space around the heart, the pericardial cavity with the space around a lung, or the thoracic cavity with the space below the diaphragm? The answer is the mediastinum with the space between the two lungs. The pleural cavity surrounds a lung, and the pericardial cavity surrounds the heart.

Question 119. The superior thoracic aperture is bounded by the first thoracic vertebra, the first ribs, and which part of the sternum: the manubrium, the body, the xiphoid process, or the clavicle? The answer is the manubrium. That opening admits the trachea, the esophagus and the great vessels into the chest.

Question 120. A described coronal section through the chest shows two cone-shaped spaces, one on each side of a central mass, each lined by a smooth shiny membrane. What are those two spaces? The answer is the right and left pleural cavities. Each is lined by pleura and contains a lung. The central mass between them is the mediastinum.

Question 121. Which of these structures passes through the diaphragm at the level of the tenth thoracic vertebra, together with the vagus nerves: the aorta, the inferior vena cava, the esophagus, or the thoracic duct? The answer is the esophagus. The aorta passes behind the diaphragm at the twelfth thoracic vertebra and the inferior vena cava at the eighth.

Question 122. The heart lies in the middle mediastinum. Which structures lie immediately in front of it and immediately behind it? The answer is the body of the sternum and the costal cartilages in front, and the esophagus and the descending aorta behind. The vertebral column forms the posterior wall behind those.

Question 123. A constructed illustration: a wound enters the chest just below the left fifth rib near the sternum. Which cavity is entered, and which structure lies immediately deep to that point? The answer is the pleural cavity, and the pericardium with the heart lies deep to it there. The left lung is notched over the heart, so the pericardium comes close to the chest wall at that spot.

Question 124. Which cavity lies below the diaphragm and above the pelvic inlet, and holds the stomach, the intestines, the liver and the kidneys? The answer is the abdominal cavity. It is bounded behind by the lumbar vertebrae and the muscles of the posterior abdominal wall. Above it the diaphragm separates it from the chest.

Question 125. Which of these forms the greater part of the muscular posterior wall of the abdominal cavity: the rectus abdominis, the psoas major and quadratus lumborum, the external oblique, or the transversus abdominis? The answer is the psoas major and the quadratus lumborum. They lie behind the peritoneum beside the lumbar vertebrae.

Question 126. Name in a sentence the muscle that forms the roof of the abdominal cavity and the bony ring that marks its lower limit. The answer is the diaphragm above and the pelvic inlet below. The diaphragm arches over the liver, the stomach and the spleen. The pelvic inlet is the brim of the pelvis, formed by the sacral promontory and the upper border of the pubic symphysis.

Question 127. Which of these organs lies behind the peritoneum against the posterior abdominal wall: the stomach, the spleen, the kidney, or the transverse colon? The answer is the kidney. It lies retroperitoneally with its fat, its fascia and the adrenal gland above it. The stomach, the spleen and the transverse colon are all suspended within the peritoneal cavity.

Question 128. A described transverse section of the upper abdomen shows a large solid organ on the right, a muscular bag to the left of the midline, and a bony column behind them with two great vessels in front of it. Which structures are shown? The answer is the liver, the stomach and the vertebral column with the aorta and the inferior vena cava.

Question 129. Which vein crosses in front of the abdominal aorta just below the origin of the superior mesenteric artery, running from left to right? The answer is the left renal vein. It drains the left kidney into the inferior vena cava and is longer than the right renal vein.

Question 130. Which double layer of peritoneum suspends the coils of the small intestine from the posterior abdominal wall and carries their blood vessels and nerves? The answer is the mesentery. It is fan-shaped, with its root running down from the duodenojejunal flexure to the right iliac fossa. The vessels run between its two layers to reach the gut.

Question 131. The lesser sac lies behind the stomach. Which statement about it is correct: it communicates with the greater sac through the epiploic foramen, it is completely closed in the adult, it opens into the pleural cavity, or it surrounds the spleen on every side? The answer is that it communicates with the greater sac through the epiploic foramen. That opening lies behind the free edge of the lesser omentum.

Question 132. Which cavity lies below the pelvic inlet, is bounded by the bones of the pelvis and the pelvic floor, and holds the bladder and the rectum? The answer is the pelvic cavity. It is the lowest part of the abdominopelvic cavity and is continuous with the abdominal cavity above.

Question 133. Which of these forms the floor of the pelvic cavity: the pelvic inlet, the pelvic diaphragm, the perineal membrane, or the inguinal ligament? The answer is the pelvic diaphragm, formed by the levator ani and the coccygeus muscles. It slopes downward and forward from the pelvic wall to the midline.

Question 134. A described median section of the female pelvis shows the rectum behind and the uterus with the vagina in front, with a narrow pouch dipping down between them. Which pouch is shown? The answer is the rectouterine pouch, which lies between the rectum and the uterus. It is the lowest part of the peritoneal cavity when a person lies on the back, so fluid settling in the abdomen gathers there.

Question 135. Which structure lies immediately in front of the rectum in the male pelvis and which in the female pelvis? The answer is the urinary bladder with the seminal vesicles in the male, and the vagina with the uterus in the female. The rectum lies behind both. The prostate lies below the bladder in the male.

Question 136. Which of these openings lies in the pelvic floor and transmits the urethra and, in the female, the vagina: the obturator foramen, the urogenital hiatus, the greater sciatic foramen, or the pelvic inlet? The answer is the urogenital hiatus. It lies in the front part of the pelvic diaphragm between the two levator ani muscles.

Question 137. The pelvis is divided into a greater part above and a lesser part below. In which plane does the boundary lie, and which two bony points mark it behind and in front? The answer is the oblique plane of the pelvic inlet, marked by the sacral promontory behind and the upper border of the pubic symphysis in front. The plane tilts downward and forward.

Question 138. A constructed illustration: a fracture of the pubic bones on both sides is followed by blood in the pelvis and difficulty in passing urine. Which structure lying behind the pubic symphysis is most likely injured? The answer is the urinary bladder, with the urethra below it. The bladder lies in the front of the pelvic cavity directly behind the pubic bones. A broken pubic ramus can therefore tear it.

Question 139. What is the narrow space between the two layers of pleura on each side of the chest called, and what does it hold? The answer is the pleural cavity, which holds only a thin film of fluid. It is a potential space rather than an air-filled one, because the two layers lie in contact.

Question 140. Which layer of pleura covers the surface of the lung itself and dips into the fissures between its lobes? The answer is the visceral pleura. It is firmly stuck to the lung and cannot be peeled from it. The parietal pleura, by contrast, lines the chest wall, the diaphragm and the mediastinum.

Question 141. Which part of the parietal pleura lines the inner surface of the ribs: the costal pleura, the diaphragmatic pleura, the mediastinal pleura, or the cervical pleura? The answer is the costal pleura. It covers the inner surface of the ribs and the intercostal muscles and the sides of the vertebral bodies.

Question 142. Air enters the pleural cavity and separates the lung from the chest wall. What is that condition called: a pneumothorax, a hemothorax, a pleural effusion, or an empyema? The answer is a pneumothorax, which means air in the pleural cavity. A hemothorax is blood in the cavity and an empyema is pus.

Question 143. A described radiograph of the chest taken at the end of a deep breath shows the lower edge of each lung sitting well above the level of the diaphragm at the side of the chest. What is the narrow space between the lung and the diaphragm at that point called? The answer is the costodiaphragmatic recess. It is the lowest part of the pleural cavity and the place where fluid first collects.

Question 144. Which of these structures lies immediately above the first rib, under a dome of pleura: the apex of the lung, the arch of the aorta, the trachea, or the first rib? The answer is the apex of the lung. It rises above the first rib into the root of the neck, covered by the cervical pleura. The subclavian artery and vein arch over that dome in front of it.

Question 145. Two nerves descend through the chest close to the root of each lung, one passing in front of the root and one behind it. Which lies in front, and which lies behind? The answer is the phrenic nerve in front and the vagus nerve behind. The phrenic nerve runs down on the mediastinal pleura to reach the diaphragm.

Question 146. A constructed illustration: a blunt injury to the left side of the chest breaks a rib, and both air and blood gather in the left pleural cavity. Which layer of pleura has been torn, and where does the fluid collect? The answer is the parietal pleura, which lines the chest wall, and the fluid settles in the costodiaphragmatic recess.

Question 147. Which sac encloses the heart, and what is the narrow space between its two inner layers called? The answer is the pericardium, and the space is the pericardial cavity. The sac has a tough fibrous outer layer and a serous inner layer folded into two sheets. The cavity itself holds only a film of fluid.

Question 148. Which of these is a function of the tough fibrous pericardium: it secretes the pericardial fluid, it prevents the heart from overfilling suddenly, it lines the inside of the lungs, or it carries the coronary arteries? The answer is that it prevents sudden overfilling. It is a strong, unyielding bag fused to the central tendon of the diaphragm below.

Question 149. Which of these pairs belongs together: the fibrous pericardium with the tough outer sac, the visceral pericardium with the lining of the chest wall, the parietal pericardium with the surface of the heart, or the pericardial cavity with the space around a lung? The answer is the fibrous pericardium with the tough outer sac. The visceral layer, also called the epicardium, is the one on the surface of the heart.

Question 150. Which of these describes the transverse sinus of the pericardium: a passage behind the ascending aorta and the pulmonary trunk, a passage behind the left atrium, the space between the two pleural cavities, or a fold of the visceral pleura? The answer is the passage behind the ascending aorta and the pulmonary trunk. A finger can be passed through it from one side to the other.

Question 151. A described section through the wall of the heart shows a smooth shiny layer lying directly on the muscle and continuous with the lining of the great vessels at the top. Which layer of the serous pericardium is shown? The answer is the visceral layer, which is also called the epicardium.

Question 152. Which structure lies immediately behind the left atrium and is related to it through the pericardium? The answer is the esophagus, with the descending aorta behind it. The esophagus descends in the posterior mediastinum against the vertebral column. Because of that relation, the left atrium lies directly in front of the esophagus.

Question 153. Which statement about the serous pericardium is correct: its parietal layer lines the fibrous sac, its visceral layer lines the chest wall, its two layers are continuous at the roots of the great vessels, or the cavity normally holds a large volume of fluid? The answer is that the two layers are continuous at the roots of the great vessels. The visceral layer covers the heart and the parietal layer lines the fibrous sac.

Question 154. A constructed illustration: fluid gathers quickly inside the pericardial sac, the heart cannot fill, and the pulse weakens. Which space is involved, and why does the tough outer layer matter? The answer is the pericardial cavity, and the fibrous pericardium cannot stretch quickly. Because it is unyielding, even a small volume of fluid raises the pressure inside and presses on the heart.

Question 155. Which membrane lines the wall of the abdominal cavity and covers the organs within it, and what is the space between its layers called? The answer is the peritoneum, and the space is the peritoneal cavity. It is a closed sac in the male, holding only a thin film of fluid. The parietal layer lines the wall and the visceral layer covers the organs.

Question 156. What is a double layer of peritoneum that attaches an organ to the abdominal wall or to another organ called? The answer is a fold of peritoneum, such as a mesentery, an omentum or a ligament. A mesentery attaches the gut to the posterior wall and carries its vessels and nerves.

Question 157. Which of these organs is completely surrounded by peritoneum and suspended within the abdominal cavity: the pancreas, the kidney, the spleen, or the abdominal aorta? The answer is the spleen. It lies in the left upper part of the abdomen and is covered by peritoneum except where the vessels enter at its hilum.

Question 158. Which of these parts of the gut lies behind the peritoneum and is fixed against the posterior abdominal wall: the stomach, the descending part of the duodenum, the transverse colon, or the spleen? The answer is the descending part of the duodenum, which is retroperitoneal. Most of the duodenum and the ascending and descending parts of the colon share that position.

Question 159. A described sagittal section of the abdomen shows a double fold of peritoneum hanging down from the greater curvature of the stomach like an apron over the coils of intestine. What is that fold? The answer is the greater omentum. It hangs down from the stomach and then turns back up to the transverse colon.

Question 160. Which structure lies immediately behind the epiploic foramen, so that it forms the posterior wall of that opening? The answer is the inferior vena cava. The foramen is bounded in front by the free edge of the lesser omentum and behind by the vena cava covered with peritoneum. The portal vein lies in that free edge in front.

Question 161. Which statement about the peritoneal cavity is correct: in the female it opens through the uterine tubes, it communicates with the pleural cavities, it is divided into two halves by the diaphragm, or it holds the kidneys? The answer is that in the female it opens through the uterine tubes, the uterus and the vagina. In the male it is a completely closed sac.

Question 162. A constructed illustration: after a blunt abdominal injury, a described radiograph shows a crescent of air under the right dome of the diaphragm. Which cavity holds the air, and which recess is its lowest part when the person lies on the back? The answer is the peritoneal cavity, and the hepatorenal recess is the lowest part in that position.

Question 163. What name is given to a smooth, moist, two-layered membrane that lines a closed body cavity and covers the organs inside it? The answer is a serous membrane. The pleura, the pericardium and the peritoneum are its three examples in the trunk. Each consists of a parietal layer on the wall and a visceral layer on the organ.

Question 164. Name the three serous membranes found in the trunk of the body. The answer is the pleura, the pericardium and the peritoneum. The pleura surrounds each lung and the pericardium encloses the heart. The peritoneum lines the abdominal cavity and covers the organs within it.

Question 165. Serous membranes have two layers. Which of these describes the parietal layer: it covers the surface of the organ, it lines the wall of the cavity, it forms the outer skin of the body, or it lines the inside of the gut? The answer is that it lines the wall of the cavity. The visceral layer is the one that covers the organ.

Question 166. Which of these best describes the fluid in a serous cavity: it nourishes the organ, it lets the two layers slide over each other with almost no friction, it carries oxygen, or it seals the cavity shut? The answer is that it lets the layers slide with almost no friction. The film of fluid is thin, and the two smooth surfaces slide over one another.

Question 167. A described section shows a smooth membrane whose two layers become continuous where a tube enters the organ it covers. What does that arrangement allow the organ to do? The answer is that it allows the organ to move inside the cavity. The visceral layer follows the organ while the parietal layer stays with the wall.

Question 168. True or false: the two layers of a serous membrane are normally separated by a cavity filled with a large pool of fluid. The answer is false. The two layers lie against each other, separated only by a thin film of fluid that lubricates them.

Question 169. Which of these pairs belongs together: the parietal layer with pain that is sharp and localized, the visceral layer with pain that is sharp and localized, the parietal layer with pain felt only in the midline, or the visceral layer with pain felt at the ribs? The answer is the parietal layer with sharp, localized pain. It carries the same somatic nerves as the body wall over it.

Question 170. Which layer of a serous membrane lies against the wall of the cavity, and which lies against the organ? The answer is the parietal layer against the wall and the visceral layer against the organ. The two are continuous where the vessels and ducts of the organ enter it.

Question 171. Sharp pain felt over the ribs when the pleura is irritated comes from which layer: the visceral pleura, the parietal pleura, the lung tissue, or the pleural cavity? The answer is the parietal pleura. It is supplied by the intercostal and phrenic nerves and is sensitive to pain, touch and temperature.

Question 172. A described dissection shows a membrane that lifts easily from the inner surface of the chest wall but cannot be peeled from the surface of the lung. Which two layers are being shown? The answer is the parietal pleura on the wall and the visceral pleura on the lung. The visceral layer is fused to the lung and follows it into the fissures.

Question 173. In the abdomen, which layer of peritoneum is supplied by the same somatic nerves as the body wall that lies over it? The answer is the parietal peritoneum. Pain from it is sharp and well localized over the affected area. The visceral peritoneum is supplied by the autonomic nerves of the gut and its pain is dull and poorly placed.

Question 174. Name in a sentence the two layers of the serous pericardium and say which of them is firmly attached to the heart muscle. The answer is the parietal layer, which lines the fibrous sac, and the visceral layer, which lies on the heart. The visceral layer is the one firmly attached to the muscle, where it is called the epicardium.

Question 175. True or false: pain from the visceral peritoneum is usually dull and felt in the midline, while pain from the parietal peritoneum is sharp and well localized. The answer is true. The visceral peritoneum follows the gut and receives autonomic nerves, so its pain is felt centrally and vaguely. The parietal peritoneum receives somatic nerves and places pain precisely over the inflamed area.

Question 176. What name is given to the tissue that covers the surfaces of the body, lines its cavities and tubes, and forms the glands? The answer is epithelial tissue. Its cells lie close together with very little material between them. It rests on a basement membrane and has no blood vessels of its own.

Question 177. Which of these is a characteristic of epithelial tissue: a large amount of material between the cells, a rich network of blood vessels within it, cells packed closely together, or a loose arrangement of cells? The answer is cells packed closely together. Epithelium is described as avascular, because its nourishment comes by diffusion from the tissue beneath it.

Question 178. A described section shows a single row of cells that are taller than they are wide, standing side by side on a thin basement membrane. Which type of epithelium is shown? The answer is simple columnar epithelium. It lines the stomach, the intestines and parts of the respiratory and reproductive tracts. Its height suits cells that secrete or absorb.

Question 179. On which thin layer do all epithelial cells rest, and what separates it from the tissue below? The answer is the basement membrane. It is a thin sheet of material between the epithelium and the connective tissue beneath. It gives the sheet a surface to stand on and a boundary to hold it.

Question 180. Which type of epithelium lines the air sacs of the lung, where gases must pass quickly between air and blood: simple squamous, simple cuboidal, stratified squamous, or transitional? The answer is simple squamous epithelium. It is only one cell thick, so the distance for diffusion is short. A thicker lining would slow that exchange.

Question 181. Which epithelium lines the urinary bladder and the ureter and can stretch as the bladder fills? The answer is transitional epithelium. Its surface cells are large and rounded, and they flatten as the organ stretches. It is also called urothelium. It lines the renal pelvis, the ureters, the bladder and the first part of the urethra.

Question 182. Which epithelium lines the trachea and sweeps mucus upward toward the throat: simple squamous, pseudostratified ciliated columnar, stratified squamous, or simple cuboidal? The answer is pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium. It looks layered because the nuclei lie at different heights, but every cell reaches the basement membrane. The cilia move the mucus and the particles trapped in it.

Question 183. A described section through thick skin shows many layers of cells, flattened and filled with a tough protein at the surface, over a deeper layer of dividing cells. Which epithelium is shown? The answer is stratified squamous keratinized epithelium. The surface cells are dead and packed with keratin, which makes the layer resistant to wear. The dividing cells lie deepest.

Question 184. True or false: epithelial tissue has no blood vessels of its own and is nourished by diffusion from the connective tissue beneath it. The answer is true. Because epithelium is avascular, oxygen and nutrients must travel from the vessels in the tissue below. An epithelium cannot survive once it is lifted from its bed.

Question 185. Which of these is not one of the shapes used to classify epithelial cells: squamous, cuboidal, columnar, or fibroblastic? The answer is fibroblastic. Squamous cells are flat and cuboidal cells are about as tall as they are wide. Fibroblasts are cells of connective tissue, not of epithelium.

Question 186. A described section of the small intestine shows a lining thrown into folds, and the free surface of every cell bears a dense fringe of fine projections. What are those projections called? The answer is microvilli, sometimes called the brush border. They are tiny folds of the cell membrane that increase the surface for absorption. They cannot move, unlike the cilia of the airway.

Question 187. What is the name of the motile, hair-like structure on the free surface of the cells lining the respiratory tract, built from microtubules? The answer is a cilium, and each cell carries many of them. They beat in a coordinated wave that moves mucus and trapped particles along the surface. Microvilli look similar but are not motile.

Question 188. A described section shows a tube lined by epithelium that leads to the surface, and deeper down a cluster of secretory cells around a small central lumen. What kind of gland is shown? The answer is an exocrine gland, whose secretion leaves through a duct. The cluster of cells is an acinus.

Question 189. Which of these is a single mucus-secreting cell scattered among the cells of the airway and the intestine: the goblet cell, the acinar cell, the basal cell, or the squamous cell? The answer is the goblet cell. It is a unicellular exocrine gland shaped like a wine glass. The other named cells take no part in that secretion.

Question 190. Which of these pairs belongs together: a tight junction with sealing the space between neighboring cells, a desmosome with the free passage of ions between cells, a gap junction with anchoring filaments, or a hemidesmosome with joining two cell tops? The answer is a tight junction with sealing the space between cells. A desmosome anchors neighboring cells to one another and a gap junction lets small molecules pass between them.

Question 191. Which epithelium lines the esophagus: simple squamous, stratified squamous non-keratinized, simple columnar, or transitional? The answer is stratified squamous non-keratinized epithelium. Several layers protect the lining from the passage of food and the surface stays moist. A single layer would be worn through by the same traffic.

Question 192. A described section of a lining shows nuclei lying at several different levels, so that the epithelium looks like several layers, yet every cell reaches the basement membrane. Which epithelium is shown? The answer is pseudostratified columnar epithelium. All the cells are attached to the basement membrane but only some reach the free surface.

Question 193. Which structure lies immediately deep to an epithelium and carries the blood vessels that nourish it? The answer is the connective tissue beneath the basement membrane, often called the lamina propria in a mucous membrane. The epithelium itself has no vessels. Nutrition passes from those vessels across the basement membrane.

Question 194. A constructed illustration: a burn destroys the whole thickness of the epidermis over part of an arm. Which epithelium has been lost, and what allows the surface to be covered again? The answer is stratified squamous keratinized epithelium, and the dividing cells deep in the surviving follicles allow new cells to replace it.

Question 195. A constructed illustration: long-standing irritation of the lower esophagus changes its lining from the epithelium that resists abrasion to one that secretes mucus. Which two epithelia are involved? The answer is stratified squamous epithelium, which normally lines the esophagus, replaced by simple columnar epithelium. The two epithelia meet at the junction with the stomach.

Question 196. A constructed illustration: the ciliated lining of the airways is damaged and loses its cilia. Which function of that epithelium is lost? The answer is the movement of mucus with the particles trapped in it toward the throat. Without the cilia that layer must be coughed up or it collects. The mucus is still produced by the goblet cells.

Question 197. True or false: epithelial cells are joined to one another by junctions such as tight junctions and desmosomes, and the sheet rests on a basement membrane. The answer is true. The junctions hold the cells together and seal the space between them. The basement membrane anchors the whole sheet to the connective tissue beneath.

Question 198. Which of these pairs belongs together: simple squamous epithelium with rapid diffusion, stratified squamous epithelium with secretion of mucus, transitional epithelium with gas exchange, or simple cuboidal epithelium with protection against abrasion? The answer is simple squamous epithelium with rapid diffusion. Its single flat layer is the thinnest possible barrier. Stratified squamous epithelium is built for protection and transitional epithelium for stretching.

Question 199. Name in a sentence the two opposite surfaces of an epithelial cell and say what each one faces. The answer is the apical surface, which faces the lumen or the outside of the body, and the basal surface, which rests on the basement membrane. The two differ in structure because they do different work.

Question 200. In a section through a gland, the secretory cells stand around a central lumen. Which way do the cells face the lumen, and which way do they rest on the basement membrane? The answer is that the apical surfaces face the lumen at the center and the bases rest on the basement membrane outside. Secretion leaves by the apical end.

To summarize, the body is built around cavities that are named for the walls that enclose them. The cranial cavity holds the brain and the vertebral cavity holds the spinal cord, while the thoracic cavity is divided by the mediastinum into two pleural cavities and the pericardial cavity of the heart. Below the diaphragm the abdominal and pelvic cavities are lined by the peritoneum, a closed sac in the male that opens through the uterine tubes in the female. Every one of these spaces is lined by a serous membrane with a parietal layer on the wall and a visceral layer on the organ, and the thin film of fluid between them lets the two surfaces slide without friction. Epithelial tissue covers the surfaces of the body, lines every cavity and tube and forms the glands, resting on a basement membrane, holding its cells close together and taking its nourishment by diffusion.
