# Chapter 18 — General Classification

Joints hold the skeleton together and decide how much of it can move, and this chapter sorts them by the tissue that binds the bones. It works through the fibrous joints with their sutures, syndesmoses and gomphoses, the cartilaginous joints with their synchondroses and symphyses, and the synovial joints with their cavity, cartilage, capsule and fluid. It closes with the joints of the skull, where sutures and one freely movable joint share the same bony vault. The chapter holds one hundred questions, and every question gives its answer at once and then explains why that answer is right, so nothing has to be carried in mind from one item to the next. This is educational material, and it supports rather than replaces an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 1701. Which class of joint unites two bones with dense fibrous tissue and allows almost no movement at all? The answer is a fibrous joint, also called a synarthrosis. Collagen fibers run from the substance of one bone into the substance of the other. The sutures of the skull are the clearest example, and the two bones there behave almost as one.

Question 1702. A joint between two bones has no cavity and holds its surfaces together with fibrous tissue. Which class does it belong to: a synovial joint, a cartilaginous joint, a fibrous joint, or a secondary cartilaginous joint? The answer is a fibrous joint. The absent cavity and the fibrous sheet between the bones place it in that class, while a synovial joint always has a cavity.

Question 1703. What kind of tissue forms the union in every fibrous joint, and how does the length of its fibers differ from one such joint to another? The answer is dense fibrous connective tissue, whose fibers vary in length. In a suture the fibers are very short, while in a syndesmosis a longer sheet separates the bones, and the longer the fibers, the more movement the joint allows.

Question 1704. The interosseous membrane of the forearm attaches to which border of the radius and which border of the ulna, and in which direction do its fibers run? The answer is the interosseous border of each bone, with the fibers running obliquely downward from the radius to the ulna. The membrane is a syndesmosis and not a suture.

Question 1705. Two flat bones of the skull vault meet edge to edge, united by a very thin layer of fibrous tissue. Which name fits that union: a gomphosis, a syndesmosis, a suture, or a symphysis? The answer is a suture. The bones meet edge to edge, with only an extremely thin fibrous layer between them. A symphysis is cartilaginous and a gomphosis holds a tooth.

Question 1706. A constructed illustration: a heavy fall drives the ankle bone upward between the lower ends of the tibia and the fibula, and the short fibrous bond joining those two bones tears. Which joint has been injured? The answer is the inferior tibiofibular syndesmosis. When its fibers tear, the two bones separate and the ankle mortise widens, and management belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 1707. Are the fibrous joints of the skull permanent through life, and what becomes of many sutures in later years? The answer is that they are not permanent, because many sutures close and turn into solid bone. The process is called synostosis. The times differ from one suture to another and from one person to another.

Question 1708. True or false: a fibrous joint always allows free movement between the bones it joins. The answer is false. Fibrous tissue binds the bones closely together, so movement is very slight or entirely absent. Free movement belongs to the synovial joints, which have a cavity and synovial fluid between their surfaces.

Question 1709. One statement about a fibrous union is correct: the fibers of one bone continue into the other, the surfaces are lined by hyaline cartilage, a cavity separates the bones, or the union is replaced by scar tissue. The answer is that the fibers of one bone continue into the other. That continuity gives the union its great strength.

Question 1710. A described specimen shows two long bones lying side by side with a broad fibrous sheet between their facing borders, its fibers running obliquely from one bone to the other. What kind of union is shown? The answer is a syndesmosis of the fibrous class. The sheet is longer than the tissue in a suture, so it allows a small amount of movement.

Question 1711. What name is given to the thin layer of fibrous tissue that fills the gap between two skull bones at a suture? The answer is the sutural ligament. It is continuous with the periosteum outside the skull and with the dura mater inside. In the newborn it is wide enough to permit some movement of the vault during birth.

Question 1712. Which of these pairs belongs together: a suture with a tooth, a syndesmosis with a sheet of fibrous tissue, a gomphosis with the skull vault, or a synchondrosis with a joint cavity? The answer is a syndesmosis with a sheet of fibrous tissue. A suture belongs with the skull vault, and a gomphosis with a tooth in its socket.

Question 1713. Cartilage, either hyaline or fibrocartilage, unites the bones in which class of joint, and what is the other name for that class? The answer is the cartilaginous joint, also called an amphiarthrosis. No cavity lies between the bones, and the cartilage forms the whole union. The joints between the vertebral bodies and the pubic symphysis belong to this class.

Question 1714. A joint between two bones is filled by a pad of cartilage and has no cavity. Which two named kinds of joint belong to that class: suture and syndesmosis, synchondrosis and symphysis, hinge and pivot, or gomphosis and plane joint? The answer is the synchondrosis and the symphysis. The first is united by hyaline cartilage and the second by fibrocartilage.

Question 1715. One statement about the two kinds of cartilaginous joint is correct: a synchondrosis is usually temporary and a symphysis usually permanent, both are temporary, a symphysis is united by hyaline cartilage, or a synchondrosis has a cavity. The answer is that a synchondrosis is usually temporary and a symphysis usually permanent. The epiphyseal plate is replaced by bone, and the pubic symphysis persists for life.

Question 1716. What is the name of the cartilaginous joint in which hyaline cartilage unites the bones, such as the plate of cartilage near the end of a growing long bone? The answer is a synchondrosis. It is also called a primary cartilaginous joint. Because the cartilage is hyaline, the joint is usually a temporary one that disappears when the cartilage turns to bone.

Question 1717. Which cartilage unites the bones at a symphysis: hyaline cartilage alone, fibrocartilage, elastic cartilage, or a mixture of bone and fibrous tissue? The answer is fibrocartilage. A symphysis is also called a secondary cartilaginous joint. On each bony surface a thin layer of hyaline cartilage lies against the bone, with fibrocartilage between the two layers.

Question 1718. A described section through the growing end of a long bone shows a plate of smooth bluish cartilage between the shaft and the bony cap, with columns of cartilage cells in orderly rows and no cavity. What joint is shown? The answer is a synchondrosis, the epiphyseal plate. The columns of cells lengthen the bone, and when growth stops the plate is replaced by bone.

Question 1719. The first rib joins the sternum through which kind of cartilage, and what kind of joint is formed there? The answer is hyaline cartilage, forming a synchondrosis. The first costal cartilage fuses directly with the manubrium, so no cavity intervenes. The joints of the second to the seventh ribs with the sternum are synovial instead.

Question 1720. Which statement distinguishes a synchondrosis from a symphysis correctly: a synchondrosis uses hyaline cartilage and a symphysis uses fibrocartilage, both use hyaline cartilage, a synchondrosis uses fibrocartilage and a symphysis uses hyaline cartilage, or neither contains cartilage? The answer is that a synchondrosis uses hyaline cartilage and a symphysis uses fibrocartilage. That is why a synchondrosis is often temporary, since hyaline cartilage is readily replaced by bone.

Question 1721. A constructed illustration: a child has pain over the growing plate at the lower end of the femur after a twisting injury, and the imaging shows the plate widened. Which structure is injured? The answer is the epiphyseal plate, a synchondrosis of hyaline cartilage. An injury at the plate may disturb growth, and management belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 1722. Name the joint at the growing plate of a long bone and the joint between the bodies of two adjacent vertebrae, in that order. The answer is a synchondrosis first and a symphysis second. The first is a primary cartilaginous joint united by hyaline cartilage. The second is a secondary cartilaginous joint united by fibrocartilage.

Question 1723. Name the class of joint that has a cavity between the bones, a lining membrane that makes fluid, and free movement. The answer is a synovial joint. The other classes are the fibrous joints, which have no cavity, and the cartilaginous joints, which are filled by cartilage. Only the synovial class is freely movable.

Question 1724. Which class of joint is found at the shoulder, the elbow and the knee, and which single feature separates it from every other class? The answer is the synovial class, separated by its joint cavity. The cavity is lined by synovial membrane and holds a film of synovial fluid. Fibrous and cartilaginous joints have no such space.

Question 1725. A synovial joint moves in one plane only, like a door on its hinges. Which named type is it: a plane joint, a hinge joint, a saddle joint, or a ball and socket joint? The answer is a hinge joint. Its surfaces are shaped so that movement is confined to flexion and extension. The elbow is the standard example.

Question 1726. True or false: a synovial joint always has a cavity filled with fluid. The answer is true. The synovial membrane lines the capsule and secretes the fluid into the cavity. The fluid lubricates the surfaces and nourishes the articular cartilage, which has no blood vessels of its own.

Question 1727. One statement about classifying joints by structure and by function is correct: every fibrous joint is freely movable, every cartilaginous joint has a cavity, a synovial joint is always a diarthrosis, or a synarthrosis is always synovial. The answer is that a synovial joint is always a diarthrosis, because a cavity and fluid make free movement inevitable. Fibrous joints are called synarthroses and cartilaginous joints amphiarthroses.

Question 1728. At what line does the synovial membrane of a joint end, and what structure covers the joint surface inside that line? The answer is the margin of the articular cartilage, which covers the surface inside the line. The membrane does not cross the cartilage. That is why the cartilage itself has no synovial covering.

Question 1729. The rounded surface of one bone lies inside a ring formed by bone and ligament, and the joint turns about a single axis. Which type of synovial joint is that? The answer is a pivot joint. The joint between the first and the second cervical vertebrae is the standard example. The head turns around the upright peg inside the ring.

Question 1730. Which of these synovial joints is a saddle joint: the elbow, the joint at the base of the thumb, the shoulder, or the ankle? The answer is the joint at the base of the thumb. Its two surfaces are concave in one direction and convex in the other, which allows movement in two planes and lets the thumb cross the palm.

Question 1731. A described specimen shows two almost flat bony surfaces, one sliding over the other, with a capsule around them and no ridge on either surface. Which type of synovial joint is shown? The answer is a plane joint, also called a gliding joint. The surfaces are flat, so one glides on the other, as between the small bones of the foot.

Question 1732. Which statement about the shape of a synovial joint is correct: every joint moves about three axes, a hinge moves in two planes, the shape of the surfaces decides the number of axes, or a saddle moves in one plane? The answer is that the shape of the surfaces decides the number of axes. A hinge has one, a saddle has two, and a ball and socket joint three.

Question 1733. A constructed illustration: an older runner has pain and grating in the knee, and the smooth covering of the joint surfaces has worn away over the years. Which structure has failed? The answer is the articular cartilage of the synovial joint. It cannot renew itself well because it has no blood supply, and management belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 1734. A described specimen shows a rounded head sitting in a shallow socket whose rim deepens the cup only a little, and the joint moves freely in every direction. Which type of synovial joint is shown? The answer is a ball and socket joint at the shoulder. The shallow socket gives the widest range of movement but the least stability of the two examples of this type.

Question 1735. Which suture runs across the back of the skull, between the two parietal bones and the occipital bone? The answer is the lambdoid suture. It is named for its resemblance to the Greek letter lambda. Behind it lies the occipital bone, and in front of it lie the two parietal bones.

Question 1736. The joint between the two parietal bones in the midline of the vault is which kind of suture: a squamous suture, a serrate suture, a plane suture, or a schindylesis? The answer is a serrate suture. The two edges interlock like the teeth of a saw. The sagittal suture in the middle of the vault is the standard example.

Question 1737. Where the lower part of the temporal bone meets the parietal bone, the edges are bevelled and one bone overlaps the other. Which named suture is that: the coronal suture, the sagittal suture, the squamosal suture, or the lambdoid suture? The answer is the squamosal suture. Its bevelled edges overlap, which makes it a squamous suture.

Question 1738. Which suture separates the frontal bone from the two parietal bones? The answer is the coronal suture. It runs across the vault from side to side, roughly above the ears. It lies in a coronal plane, so it divides the front of the skull from the back.

Question 1739. A described skull viewed from above shows a joint between two bones whose edges interlock like the teeth of a saw, with no gap and no overlap. Which named kind of suture is shown? The answer is a serrate suture, at the sagittal suture in the midline of the vault. The interlocking edges resist separating forces.

Question 1740. The pterion is the meeting point of which four bones, and which two sutures cross there? The answer is the frontal, parietal, temporal and sphenoid bones, with the coronal suture and the squamous suture meeting there. The thin bone at that point overlies the middle meningeal artery. A blow there can tear the artery.

Question 1741. Which statement about the fate of the skull sutures is correct: all of them remain fibrous for life, they close by synostosis at different ages in different people, they all turn into synovial joints, or they never close at all? The answer is that they close by synostosis at different ages in different people. A suture that closes early may change the shape of the skull.

Question 1742. Which of these pairs belongs together: the coronal suture with the two parietal bones, the sagittal suture with the frontal and occipital bones, the lambdoid suture with the occipital and parietal bones, or the squamosal suture with the two parietal bones? The answer is the lambdoid suture with the occipital and parietal bones. The sagittal suture lies between the two parietal bones.

Question 1743. The coronal suture runs across the vault from one side to the other. In which plane does that suture lie, and does it divide the skull into right and left parts or into front and back parts? The answer is a coronal plane, which divides the skull into front and back parts. A section along that suture would separate the frontal bone from the parietals.

Question 1744. A constructed illustration: a newborn has a soft gap at the meeting point of the sagittal and coronal sutures, and the gap bulges when the baby cries. What is that gap called? The answer is the anterior fontanelle. The sutures there are still membranous, so pressure inside the skull pushes the soft tissue outward, and the gap normally closes in the second year.

Question 1745. Which fibrous joint unites two bones by a sheet of fibrous tissue that is longer than the tissue in a suture, so that a little movement is possible? The answer is a syndesmosis. The greater length of the fibers allows the slight give. The interosseous membrane between the shafts of the forearm bones is an example.

Question 1746. The union between the lower ends of the tibia and the fibula is which kind of joint: a suture, a syndesmosis, a symphysis, or a synovial joint? The answer is a syndesmosis. Short fibrous bands join the two bones, and no cavity lies between them. The union holds the two bones together while the ankle moves.

Question 1747. A described dissection of the ankle shows a short thick band of fibrous tissue running from the lower end of the tibia to the lower end of the fibula, with no cavity between the bones. Which joint is shown? The answer is the inferior tibiofibular syndesmosis, of the fibrous class. It allows only a trace of movement, enough to let the ankle mortise widen slightly.

Question 1748. What is the name of the fibrous joint between the lower ends of the tibia and the fibula, whose fibers are much shorter than those of the interosseous membrane above it? The answer is the inferior tibiofibular syndesmosis. It lies just above the ankle joint. The ankle joint itself is a synovial joint and lies below it.

Question 1749. One statement about movement at a syndesmosis is correct: no movement is possible, a small amount occurs and is greatest where the fibrous sheet is longest, the joint has a cavity, or movement is as free as at a synovial joint. The answer is that a small amount occurs and is greatest where the sheet is longest. A syndesmosis has no cavity.

Question 1750. Which two groups of muscles take origin from the front and the back of the interosseous membrane of the leg, and which vessel passes between them? The answer is the extensor muscles in front and the flexor muscles behind, with the anterior tibial artery passing forward through the upper part of the membrane. The membrane therefore divides the leg into two compartments.

Question 1751. One statement about the interosseous membrane of the forearm is correct: it is a synovial joint, its fibers run between the radius and the ulna and carry force, it lies between the humerus and the ulna, or it prevents all movement. The answer is that its fibers run between the radius and the ulna and carry force between them. It still allows pronation and supination.

Question 1752. True or false: the inferior tibiofibular joint is a syndesmosis and not a synovial joint. The answer is true. The two bones are joined by fibrous bands and no cavity lies between them. Its movement is only a slight widening, which the ankle needs when the foot is lifted.

Question 1753. What is the name of the fibrous joint in which a peg of hard tissue fits into a socket and is held there by fibrous tissue, as a tooth is held in its bony socket? The answer is a gomphosis. The peg is the root of the tooth. The fibrous tissue is the periodontal ligament.

Question 1754. Which fibrous tissue holds a tooth in its socket: the sutural ligament, the periodontal ligament, the interosseous membrane, or the joint capsule? The answer is the periodontal ligament. It runs from the bone of the socket to the surface of the root. It also carries the small vessels and nerves that supply the tooth.

Question 1755. One statement about the gomphosis is correct: it joins two bones of the skeleton, it joins a tooth to bone and is not a union of two bones, it is a synovial joint, or it is a cartilaginous joint. The answer is that it joins a tooth to bone, not two bones. Some anatomists therefore exclude it from the joints of the skeleton.

Question 1756. In which two bones of the skull are gomphoses found? The answer is the maxilla of the upper jaw and the mandible of the lower jaw. Each tooth root sits in its own socket in one of those bones. The number of these joints equals the number of teeth present.

Question 1757. A described specimen of a jaw shows a row of pegs set into deep sockets, each peg held by a thin band of fibrous tissue running from the socket to the surface of the peg. Which joint is shown? The answer is a gomphosis, which allows only a slight springing movement. That small give cushions the tooth against the force of biting.

Question 1758. Name the fibrous joint that holds a tooth in its socket and the fibrous joint that unites the shafts of the tibia and the fibula, in that order. The answer is the gomphosis first and the syndesmosis second. The gomphosis binds a tooth to bone, while the syndesmosis binds two bones by a longer sheet of fibers.

Question 1759. Which cartilage unites the bones at a synchondrosis: hyaline cartilage, fibrocartilage, elastic cartilage, or fibrous tissue? The answer is hyaline cartilage. That is why a synchondrosis is called a primary cartilaginous joint. The epiphyseal plate and the joint between the first rib and the sternum are examples.

Question 1760. Which joint unites the first rib with the sternum, and to which functional class does it belong? The answer is a synchondrosis, which belongs to the amphiarthroses. Hyaline cartilage joins the first costal cartilage directly to the manubrium. The joint is therefore almost immovable, which helps to keep the first rib fixed when the arm moves.

Question 1761. Which of these is a synchondrosis: the pubic symphysis, the joint between the first rib and the sternum, the joint between two vertebral bodies, or the knee joint? The answer is the joint between the first rib and the sternum. It is united by hyaline cartilage. The pubic symphysis and the intervertebral joints are symphyses, and the knee is synovial.

Question 1762. A described section through a growing bone shows a plate of smooth bluish cartilage between the shaft and the bony end, with no cavity anywhere in it. What is that plate, and what kind of joint is it? The answer is the epiphyseal plate, and the joint is a synchondrosis. Growth in length takes place at that plate.

Question 1763. The spheno-occipital synchondrosis lies between which two bones of the skull base, and how is it directed? The answer is between the body of the sphenoid and the basilar part of the occipital bone, running across the base from side to side. It closes in early adult life, and until then it allows the base of the skull to grow.

Question 1764. One statement about the fate of a synchondrosis is correct: it remains cartilaginous for life, it is usually temporary and is replaced by bone, it turns into a synovial joint, or it becomes fibrocartilage. The answer is that it is usually temporary and is replaced by bone. The change is called synostosis, and the epiphyseal plate shows it best, since the plate disappears when growth stops.

Question 1765. Which cartilaginous joint unites the bony part of a rib with its costal cartilage, and which cartilage forms it? The answer is a synchondrosis, formed by hyaline cartilage. The joint lies between the end of the rib and the cartilage that continues forward to the sternum. Because the cartilage is hyaline, the joint allows only a trace of movement.

Question 1766. What name is given to the cartilaginous joint that unites two bones by a pad of fibrocartilage and remains permanent, such as the joint between the two pubic bones? The answer is a symphysis. It is also called a secondary cartilaginous joint. A thin layer of hyaline cartilage covers each bony surface, and fibrocartilage lies between them.

Question 1767. The joint between the bodies of two adjacent vertebrae is which kind of joint: a synchondrosis, a symphysis, a synovial joint, or a suture? The answer is a symphysis. A pad of fibrocartilage, the intervertebral disc, joins the two bodies. Small movements at each of these joints add up to the movement of the whole spine.

Question 1768. The pubic symphysis lies between which two bones, and which cartilage lies between their surfaces? The answer is the two pubic bones, with a disc of fibrocartilage between them. A thin layer of hyaline cartilage covers each bony surface. The disc is the pad that joins the bones.

Question 1769. True or false: the pubic symphysis is a cartilaginous joint. The answer is true. It is a secondary cartilaginous joint, united by fibrocartilage. No cavity lies between the two pubic bones. Its slight movement allows the pelvis a little give when weight is transferred from one leg to the other.

Question 1770. A described section through the middle of the pelvis shows a wedge of fibrocartilage between two bones, with a thin layer of hyaline cartilage on each bony surface and no cavity. Which joint is shown? The answer is the pubic symphysis, a secondary cartilaginous joint. The fibrocartilaginous disc is the feature that marks it as a symphysis rather than a synchondrosis.

Question 1771. One statement about the joint between the manubrium and the sternum is correct: it is synovial, it is a secondary cartilaginous joint in most people, it is a suture, or it never moves. The answer is that it is a secondary cartilaginous joint in most people. A disc of fibrocartilage lies between the two parts of the sternum, and in some people the joint later becomes bony.

Question 1772. A constructed illustration: late in pregnancy the pelvic joints soften, and the gap between the two pubic bones widens a little. Which structure widens, and what is the reason? The answer is the fibrocartilaginous disc of the pubic symphysis. Softening of the pelvic ligaments is a normal change in late pregnancy, and management belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 1773. Which of these is a symphysis: the epiphyseal plate, the pubic symphysis, the temporomandibular joint, or the sagittal suture? The answer is the pubic symphysis. It is united by fibrocartilage and is a secondary cartilaginous joint. The epiphyseal plate is a synchondrosis, the jaw joint is synovial, and the sagittal suture is fibrous.

Question 1774. Which of these pairs belongs together: a synchondrosis with fibrocartilage, a symphysis with hyaline cartilage, a synchondrosis with a temporary joint later replaced by bone, or a symphysis with a joint cavity? The answer is a synchondrosis with a temporary joint later replaced by bone. The epiphyseal plate is the clearest example. A symphysis is united by fibrocartilage and has no cavity.

Question 1775. Which smooth glassy cartilage covers the ends of the bones inside a synovial joint, and does it contain blood vessels? The answer is hyaline cartilage, and it contains no blood vessels and no nerves. It is nourished by the synovial fluid that bathes it. Because it has no blood supply, it heals poorly after injury.

Question 1776. Which structure secretes the fluid that fills the cavity of a synovial joint: the articular cartilage, the synovial membrane, the fibrous capsule, or the periosteum? The answer is the synovial membrane. It lines the inner surface of the capsule and stops at the margin of the articular cartilage. The fibrous capsule is the tough outer sleeve and secretes nothing.

Question 1777. A described section through a joint shows a tough sleeve of fibrous tissue attached to both bones, a smooth pink lining on its inner surface, and a thin film of fluid in the space between the bones. Which two layers are shown? The answer is the fibrous capsule outside and the synovial membrane inside. The membrane makes the fluid, and the capsule holds the joint together.

Question 1778. A constructed illustration: after a fall a knee swells with fluid, and the fluid lies inside the joint itself rather than in the skin. Which cavity holds it? The answer is the synovial cavity of the knee joint. The synovial membrane lines that cavity and secretes the fluid. The fluid is what fills the joint and makes it look swollen.

Question 1779. Which of these structures divides the cavity of a synovial joint into two separate compartments: the meniscus of the knee, the disc of the temporomandibular joint, the labrum of the hip, or a bursa beside the joint? The answer is the disc of the temporomandibular joint. It divides the cavity into an upper and a lower part, while a meniscus is only a partial crescent.

Question 1780. Which rule states that the nerve supplying a joint also supplies the muscles that move the joint and the skin over their attachments? The answer is Hilton's law. The same nerve therefore carries sensation from the joint and from the structures around it. That is why disease in a joint can be felt as pain over the muscles and skin the same nerve supplies.

Question 1781. A described specimen shows a socket with a raised rim of fibrocartilage around its edge, gripping the head of the bone inside it. What is that rim called? The answer is the labrum, here the acetabular labrum of the hip, with the glenoid labrum of the shoulder as the other example. It deepens the socket and helps to hold the head in place.

Question 1782. What is the name of the tough sleeve of fibrous tissue that surrounds a synovial joint and is attached to the bones on either side? The answer is the fibrous capsule. It is the outer layer of the joint wall. Thickenings in it form the capsular ligaments, and it resists the forces that would pull the joint apart.

Question 1783. A constructed illustration: a joint is kept still in a cast for several weeks, and afterward the joint is stiff and the smooth covering of its surfaces has thinned. Why does that covering suffer? The answer is that the articular cartilage has no blood vessels and depends on synovial fluid. Movement spreads the fluid over the cartilage, so without movement it is poorly fed.

Question 1784. One statement about the articular cartilage of a synovial joint is correct: it is rich in blood vessels, it has no blood vessels or nerves and repairs poorly, it is fibrocartilage like the meniscus, or the synovial membrane covers its free surface. The answer is that it has no blood vessels or nerves and repairs poorly, because it is nourished only by synovial fluid. Injury to it therefore heals slowly.

Question 1785. A described radiograph of a normal knee shows a clear dark gap between the lower end of the femur and the upper end of the tibia. What fills that gap? The answer is the articular cartilage covering the two bone ends. Cartilage contains no calcium, so it casts no shadow, and the dark gap is the joint space.

Question 1786. A described dissection of a joint shows a band of white fibrous tissue running from one bone to the other across the joint, separate from the capsule. What is that band called? The answer is a ligament. It resists movement of the joint in one particular direction. Many ligaments are only thickenings of the capsule itself.

Question 1787. Which ligament of the knee lies outside the capsule and which lies inside it? The answer is that the fibular collateral ligament lies outside the capsule, while the anterior cruciate ligament lies inside it. Structures inside the capsule are bathed in synovial fluid, so injury there lets blood collect within the joint.

Question 1788. A constructed illustration: a twisting injury fills a knee joint with blood, and the joint cannot be straightened fully. Which structure inside the joint is most likely torn? The answer is the anterior cruciate ligament. It lies inside the capsule, so its torn ends bleed into the cavity, and the joint then holds itself in the position that holds the most fluid.

Question 1789. A joint surface is described as convex in one direction and concave at right angles to that direction. What is that shape called? The answer is a saddle shape, which allows movement about two axes. The joint at the base of the thumb has this shape, and its two axes lie at right angles to each other.

Question 1790. A described section through a knee shows a crescent-shaped pad of fibrocartilage attached at both ends to the capsule but free along its inner edge. What is that pad, and what does it do? The answer is a meniscus. It deepens the surface, spreads the load, and helps the joint to glide, and its loose attachment leaves it open to tearing.

Question 1791. True or false: the synovial membrane covers the articular cartilage as well as the inner surface of the capsule. The answer is false. The membrane lines the capsule and stops at the margin of the cartilage. The cartilage is left bare so that it can meet the cartilage of the other bone.

Question 1792. A constructed illustration: an older person has a hip that has lost its smooth articular covering, the joint space has narrowed, and bone now rubs on bone. Which structure has failed? The answer is the articular cartilage of a synovial joint. It has no blood vessels and very few cells, so it does not regenerate.

Question 1793. Which of these pairs belongs together: articular cartilage with an abundant blood supply, the synovial membrane with the secretion of synovial fluid, the fibrous capsule with the lining of the cavity, or the meniscus with the joint at the base of the thumb? The answer is the synovial membrane with the secretion of synovial fluid. The membrane is the lining, and the capsule is the outer sleeve.

Question 1794. Which is the only freely movable joint of the skull, and which two bones does it join? The answer is the temporomandibular joint, which joins the mandible with the temporal bone. It lies just in front of the ear. All the other joints of the skull are fibrous or cartilaginous.

Question 1795. The articular surfaces of the temporomandibular joint are covered by which tissue: hyaline cartilage, fibrocartilage, bone, or synovial membrane? The answer is fibrocartilage. That covering is unusual for a synovial joint, where hyaline cartilage is the rule. Fibrocartilage resists the heavy wear of chewing.

Question 1796. A constructed illustration: a person yawns widely and feels a click and then pain in front of the ear, and the jaw shifts to one side. Which joint is involved, and which muscle pulls the disc forward? The answer is the temporomandibular joint, with the lateral pterygoid muscle. That muscle attaches to the disc and carries it forward with the bone.

Question 1797. A described specimen shows a joint between the mandible and the temporal bone, with a disc between the two surfaces and a capsule around them, and the surfaces are not covered by the usual glassy cartilage. Which joint is shown? The answer is the temporomandibular joint. It is the only synovial joint of the skull. Its covering is fibrocartilage rather than hyaline cartilage.

Question 1798. In the temporomandibular joint, which compartment allows gliding and which allows hinge movement? The answer is that the upper compartment, between the disc and the temporal bone, allows gliding, and the lower compartment, between the disc and the mandible, allows hinge movement. Opening the mouth uses both. The disc moves forward with the condyle in the upper compartment.

Question 1799. Which of these pairs belongs together: the coronal suture with the occipital bone, the temporomandibular joint with a fibrous union, the spheno-occipital synchondrosis with the base of the skull, or the gomphosis with the vault of the skull? The answer is the spheno-occipital synchondrosis with the base of the skull. The coronal suture lies between the frontal bone and the parietals.

Question 1800. Name the kinds of joint that can be found in the skull, spoken as a list. The answer is the suture, the gomphosis at the teeth, the synchondrosis of the skull base, and the synovial temporomandibular joint. The sutures and the gomphoses are fibrous, the skull base joint is cartilaginous, and the jaw joint is synovial.

To summarize, the joints of the body are classified by the tissue that unites the bones. Fibrous joints, or synarthroses, include the sutures of the skull, the syndesmoses such as the interosseous membranes, and the gomphoses that hold the teeth in their sockets. Cartilaginous joints, or amphiarthroses, are either synchondroses, united by hyaline cartilage and usually temporary, as at the epiphyseal plate, or symphyses, united by fibrocartilage and permanent, as at the pubic symphysis and between the vertebral bodies. Synovial joints, or diarthroses, have a cavity, articular hyaline cartilage, a fibrous capsule, a synovial membrane and synovial fluid, and the shape of their surfaces decides how many axes they move about. Ligaments, discs, menisci and labra are the accessory structures that steady them, and the skull holds a suture, a gomphosis, a synchondrosis and one synovial joint at the jaw.
