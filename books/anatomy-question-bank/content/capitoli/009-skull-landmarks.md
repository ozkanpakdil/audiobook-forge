# Chapter 9 — Skull Landmarks

The skull is a landscape of named hollows, openings, seams and projections, and this chapter crosses it landmark by landmark. It begins with the orbits and their walls, moves back through the three cranial fossae and the foramina that pierce the base, and then follows the sutures that join the vault. It goes on to the temporal bone and its two downward projections, the mastoid process and the styloid process, and ends at the occipital condyles and the sella turcica. It holds one hundred questions, and every question gives its answer at once and then explains why that answer is right. This is educational material, and it supports rather than replaces an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 801. Which bone forms the greater part of the roof of the orbit, and which bone completes that roof behind? The answer is the frontal bone in front and the lesser wing of the sphenoid behind. The orbital plate of the frontal bone is smooth and separates the orbit from the frontal lobe above it. A blow to the brow therefore threatens the frontal sinus as well as the eye.

Question 802. Which bone forms most of the floor of the orbit, the thin surface that separates the eye from the maxillary sinus? The answer is the maxilla. Its orbital surface slopes downward and laterally, and it is the part most often broken by a blow to the eye. The zygomatic bone and the orbital process of the palatine bone complete the floor behind and laterally.

Question 803. Which of these bones takes part in the medial wall of the orbit: the zygomatic bone, the lacrimal bone, the palatine bone, or the greater wing of the sphenoid? The answer is the lacrimal bone. It lies at the front of the medial wall and holds the lacrimal sac. The zygomatic bone belongs to the lateral wall, and the greater wing of the sphenoid forms the lateral wall behind.

Question 804. A described radiograph of the orbital region shows a small canal in the medial wall, at the junction of the roof with that wall, carrying a nerve forward into the nasal cavity. Which canal is described, and which nerve runs in it? The answer is the anterior ethmoidal canal, with the anterior ethmoidal nerve, a branch of the nasociliary nerve. A smaller posterior ethmoidal canal lies behind it.

Question 805. Within the orbit, the optic canal lies in which wall, and which opening lies immediately lateral to it? The answer is the posterior wall, in the lesser wing of the sphenoid, with the superior orbital fissure just lateral to it. The optic nerve and the ophthalmic artery pass through the canal.

Question 806. A constructed illustration: a blow from a fist strikes the eye, and afterward the skin of the cheek is numb. Which wall of the orbit has most likely been broken, and which nerve is injured? The answer is the floor of the orbit, with the infraorbital nerve. That nerve runs forward in the infraorbital groove and canal of the maxilla. Management belongs to the treating clinician.

Question 807. The lateral wall of the orbit is formed by which two bones, one in front and one behind? The answer is the zygomatic bone in front and the greater wing of the sphenoid behind. The lateral wall slants backward and medially toward the apex. The medial wall lies almost parallel to the median plane, so the two walls meet at a wide angle.

Question 808. Which of these pairs belongs together: the supraorbital foramen with the maxillary nerve, the infraorbital foramen with the maxillary nerve, the optic canal with the facial nerve, or the superior orbital fissure with the optic nerve? The answer is the infraorbital foramen with the maxillary nerve. That foramen lies in the floor of the orbit, in the maxilla. The supraorbital foramen carries the frontal nerve instead.

Question 809. True or false: a single thin plate of bone separates the two orbits in the midline. The answer is false. The two medial walls lie parallel to each other on either side of the nasal cavity. The labyrinth of the ethmoid lies between them, so the separation is an air-filled box of bone rather than one plate.

Question 810. Which of these bones forms the fossa that holds the lacrimal sac: the lacrimal bone, the maxilla alone, the palatine bone, or the zygomatic bone? The answer is the lacrimal bone, with the frontal process of the maxilla. Together they form the lacrimal fossa at the front of the medial wall. The sac lies in it and leads down into the nasolacrimal duct.

Question 811. Which of these structures passes through the superior orbital fissure: the optic nerve, the ophthalmic artery, the abducens nerve, or the maxillary nerve? The answer is the abducens nerve, which runs through the fissure to reach the lateral rectus muscle. The optic nerve and the ophthalmic artery pass through the optic canal. The maxillary nerve leaves by the foramen rotundum.

Question 812. A described dissection shows a fibrous ring at the apex of the orbit, with several nerves passing through it and others skirting its outer edge. Which named ring is described, and which nerve runs outside it to reach the superior oblique muscle? The answer is the common tendinous ring, with the trochlear nerve outside it. The ring gives origin to the four rectus muscles and the optic nerve passes within it.

Question 813. Which cranial fossa lies highest of the three and holds the frontal lobes of the brain? The answer is the anterior cranial fossa. Its floor is formed by the orbital plates of the frontal bone, the cribriform plate of the ethmoid, and the lesser wings of the sphenoid. It sits above the orbits and the nasal cavity.

Question 814. Which bony ridge separates the middle cranial fossa from the posterior cranial fossa on each side? The answer is the superior border of the petrous part of the temporal bone. It runs backward and medially from the region of the sphenoid. The tentorium cerebelli lies along this petrous ridge.

Question 815. The middle cranial fossa contains which of these: the cerebellum, the temporal lobes, the medulla oblongata, or the olfactory bulbs? The answer is the temporal lobes. The fossa is shallow in the midline over the body of the sphenoid and deep on each side. The cerebellum and the brainstem lie in the posterior fossa, and the olfactory bulbs rest in the anterior fossa.

Question 816. A described section through the base of the skull shows a small perforated plate with many fine holes, lying between two ridges that rise above it. Which plate is described, and what passes through its holes? The answer is the cribriform plate of the ethmoid, with the fibers of the olfactory nerve. The midline ridge above it is the crista galli.

Question 817. Which fossa lies immediately behind the dorsum sellae, and which parts of the brain occupy it? The answer is the posterior cranial fossa, which holds the cerebellum, the pons and the medulla oblongata. It is the deepest and largest of the three fossae, and its floor is formed mainly by the occipital bone. The foramen magnum opens through it.

Question 818. A constructed illustration: after a blow to the temple, blood collects above the dura on the side of the head. In which fossa does the blood lie, and which vessel is the usual source? The answer is the middle cranial fossa, with the middle meningeal artery. That artery grooves the inner surface of the bone there. Management belongs to the treating clinician.

Question 819. Which of these openings lies in the floor of the middle cranial fossa: the foramen magnum, the internal acoustic meatus, the foramen ovale, or the hypoglossal canal? The answer is the foramen ovale. It lies in the greater wing of the sphenoid and carries the mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve. The other three lie in the posterior cranial fossa.

Question 820. True or false: the pituitary gland lies in the anterior cranial fossa. The answer is false. The gland lies in the hypophysial fossa, a depression in the body of the sphenoid in the middle cranial fossa. The anterior fossa lies higher, above the nose and the orbits, and holds the frontal lobes.

Question 821. Which of these pairs belongs together: the anterior cranial fossa with the cerebellum, the middle cranial fossa with the temporal lobe, the posterior cranial fossa with the frontal lobe, or the middle cranial fossa with the olfactory bulb? The answer is the middle cranial fossa with the temporal lobe. The frontal lobe rests in the anterior fossa and the cerebellum in the posterior fossa.

Question 822. A described section shows a sloping plate of bone rising forward from the front of the foramen magnum toward the back of the body of the sphenoid. What is this surface called, and which fossa lies above it? The answer is the clivus, beneath the posterior cranial fossa. The pons rests against it.

Question 823. Which of these structures lies in the posterior cranial fossa: the optic chiasm, the trigeminal ganglion, the internal acoustic meatus, or the foramen rotundum? The answer is the internal acoustic meatus. It opens on the posterior surface of the petrous part of the temporal bone and carries the facial and vestibulocochlear nerves. The other three lie in the middle cranial fossa.

Question 824. Which of these openings lies in the posterior cranial fossa: the foramen ovale, the foramen magnum, the foramen rotundum, or the superior orbital fissure? The answer is the foramen magnum. It lies in the occipital bone and carries the medulla oblongata and the vertebral arteries. The other three lie in the middle cranial fossa.

Question 825. Which foramen in the greater wing of the sphenoid transmits the mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve? The answer is the foramen ovale. It lies behind and lateral to the foramen rotundum. Through it pass the mandibular nerve and the accessory meningeal artery, one of a row of openings that pierce the greater wing.

Question 826. Which opening carries the maxillary division of the trigeminal nerve from the middle cranial fossa to the pterygopalatine fossa? The answer is the foramen rotundum. It lies in the greater wing of the sphenoid, in front of the foramen ovale. The nerve it carries reaches the cheek, the upper teeth and the nose.

Question 827. Which small foramen in the greater wing of the sphenoid admits the middle meningeal artery? The answer is the foramen spinosum. It lies behind and lateral to the foramen ovale. The artery then runs forward in a groove on the inner surface of the bone, accompanied by a small meningeal branch of the mandibular nerve.

Question 828. Which of these structures passes through the jugular foramen: the hypoglossal nerve, the glossopharyngeal nerve, the facial nerve, or the optic nerve? The answer is the glossopharyngeal nerve. The vagus and accessory nerves and the internal jugular vein pass through the same opening. The hypoglossal nerve leaves by the hypoglossal canal, and the facial nerve by the stylomastoid foramen.

Question 829. Which nerve leaves the skull through the hypoglossal canal, and where does that canal lie? The answer is the hypoglossal nerve, which supplies the muscles of the tongue. The canal lies above and in front of the occipital condyle, medial to the jugular foramen. A small emissary vein often runs beside the nerve.

Question 830. A described view of the base of the skull shows a bent tunnel of bone that opens on the underside of the temporal bone and turns forward and medially to end near the apex of the petrous part. Which canal is described, and what runs through it? The answer is the carotid canal, with the internal carotid artery.

Question 831. A described dry skull shows a probe passed through an opening at the back of the hard palate, just medial to the last upper molar, into a canal that runs forward along the palate. Which foramen is described, and which nerve does it carry? The answer is the greater palatine foramen, with the greater palatine nerve and vessels. It opens from the pterygopalatine fossa onto the palate.

Question 832. Which of the three openings of the greater wing of the sphenoid lies most anterior, and which opening lies immediately medial and above it? The answer is the foramen rotundum, with the superior orbital fissure just medial and above it. The foramen ovale lies behind the rotundum, and the foramen spinosum behind that. The maxillary nerve runs forward through the rotundum.

Question 833. A constructed illustration: after a fracture that crosses the base of the skull, sensation is lost over the chin and the muscles of chewing are weak. Which opening has been involved, and which nerve is injured? The answer is the foramen ovale, with the mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve. Management belongs to the treating clinician.

Question 834. True or false: the internal carotid artery passes through the foramen lacerum on its way into the skull. The answer is false. The foramen lacerum is filled in life by cartilage, and no structure crosses it from below. The artery enters by the carotid canal and then passes across the upper part of the foramen lacerum.

Question 835. Which of these pairs belongs together: the foramen ovale with the maxillary nerve, the foramen rotundum with the mandibular nerve, the stylomastoid foramen with the facial nerve, or the hypoglossal canal with the vagus nerve? The answer is the stylomastoid foramen with the facial nerve. The foramen ovale carries the mandibular nerve and the foramen rotundum the maxillary nerve.

Question 836. Which of these passes through the foramen magnum: the spinal part of the accessory nerve, the glossopharyngeal nerve, the internal jugular vein, or the facial nerve? The answer is the spinal part of the accessory nerve, which ascends through the opening to join the cranial part inside the skull. The vertebral arteries and the medulla oblongata pass through it as well.

Question 837. Name two openings that pierce the greater wing of the sphenoid, spoken as a list. The answer is the foramen rotundum and the foramen ovale, with the foramen spinosum as a third. Each carries a branch of the trigeminal nerve or the middle meningeal artery. They lie in a row from front to back.

Question 838. Which of these openings carries the fibers of the olfactory nerve: the optic canal, the cribriform plate, the foramen rotundum, or the internal acoustic meatus? The answer is the cribriform plate. Its many small holes lie in the roof of the nasal cavity, in the anterior cranial fossa. The internal acoustic meatus carries the facial nerve instead.

Question 839. Which suture runs across the top of the skull from side to side, joining the frontal bone to the two parietal bones? The answer is the coronal suture. It crosses the vault above the forehead and meets the sagittal suture at the bregma. It remains open through childhood and fuses in adult life.

Question 840. Which suture runs from front to back in the midline of the vault between the two parietal bones? The answer is the sagittal suture. It meets the coronal suture in front at the bregma and the lambdoid suture behind at the lambda. Its early fusion lengthens the skull from front to back.

Question 841. Which suture joins the occipital bone to the parietal bones: the coronal suture, the squamosal suture, the lambdoid suture, or the sagittal suture? The answer is the lambdoid suture. It arches across the back of the skull and meets the sagittal suture at the lambda. The squamosal suture joins the temporal bone to the parietal bone.

Question 842. At which named point of the skull do the frontal, parietal, temporal and sphenoid bones meet: the bregma, the lambda, the pterion, or the asterion? The answer is the pterion. It lies on the side of the vault, a little above and behind the temple. The bone there is thin, and the middle meningeal artery runs just deep to it.

Question 843. A described radiograph of a child's skull shows a diamond-shaped gap of soft tissue at the top of the forehead, where four bones meet. What is that gap called, and which bones border it? The answer is the anterior fontanelle, bordered by the two frontal bones and the two parietal bones. It lies at the bregma and closes in the second year of life.

Question 844. A described specimen shows a star-shaped meeting point low on the back of the skull, where the parietal, occipital and temporal bones come together behind the ear. What is this point called, and which venous channel lies deep to it? The answer is the asterion, with the junction of the transverse and sigmoid sinuses beneath.

Question 845. Which suture lies just above the external acoustic meatus, and which bone lies immediately above that suture? The answer is the squamosal suture, with the parietal bone above it. The suture runs forward and joins the sphenoid region at the pterion. The temporal bone lies below it, and the suture stays visible throughout life.

Question 846. A constructed illustration: a baby is brought in with a sunken soft spot at the top of the forehead, and it is used to judge the state of hydration. Which structure is being examined, and which four bones border it? The answer is the anterior fontanelle, bordered by the two frontal bones and the two parietal bones. It lies at the bregma.

Question 847. True or false: the frontal bone begins life as two halves separated by a suture that runs down the middle of the forehead. The answer is true. That suture is the frontal suture, also called the metopic suture, and it usually fuses in the first years of life. A small part of it persists in some adults.

Question 848. Which of these pairs belongs together: the bregma with the meeting of the coronal and sagittal sutures, the lambda with the meeting of the two frontal bones, the pterion with the meeting of the two parietal bones, or the asterion with the meeting of the temporal and zygomatic bones? The answer is the bregma with the meeting of the coronal and sagittal sutures.

Question 849. Which of these joints of the skull is a cartilaginous joint rather than a fibrous suture: the coronal suture, the sagittal suture, the spheno-occipital synchondrosis, or the squamosal suture? The answer is the spheno-occipital synchondrosis, between the body of the sphenoid and the basilar part of the occipital bone. It is filled with cartilage and fuses in early adult life.

Question 850. Small irregular plates of bone are sometimes found within the lambdoid suture. What are they called, and in which part of the skull do they lie? The answer is sutural bones, also called wormian bones. They lie in the occipital region at the back of the vault. They are a normal variation and are more common in some developmental disorders.

Question 851. Which bone of the skull houses the organs of hearing and balance, and which of its parts is the densest? The answer is the temporal bone, and its petrous part is the densest. The petrous part is a pyramid of bone that projects medially and forward between the sphenoid and the occipital bone. It shelters the internal ear.

Question 852. Which part of the temporal bone forms the flat plate above and in front of the ear, and which process does it send forward to meet the cheek bone? The answer is the squamous part, which sends forward the zygomatic process. That process joins the temporal process of the zygomatic bone to complete the zygomatic arch. The temporalis muscle passes beneath the arch.

Question 853. Which shallow depression on the temporal bone receives the head of the lower jaw, and which ridge lies just in front of it? The answer is the mandibular fossa, with the articular tubercle in front. Together they form the upper part of the temporomandibular joint. A disc of fibrocartilage divides that joint into two compartments.

Question 854. Which of these openings lies on the posterior surface of the petrous part of the temporal bone: the foramen ovale, the internal acoustic meatus, the stylomastoid foramen, or the carotid canal? The answer is the internal acoustic meatus. It carries the facial nerve and the vestibulocochlear nerve into the internal ear. The stylomastoid foramen lies below, where the facial nerve leaves the skull.

Question 855. A described radiograph of the temporal bone in side view shows a curved ridge running backward and medially, with a small canal opening on the surface below and behind it. Which ridge and which opening are described? The answer is the petrous ridge and the internal acoustic meatus. The ridge separates the middle cranial fossa from the posterior cranial fossa.

Question 856. A described dissection of the inner surface of the temporal bone shows a deep S-shaped groove behind the petrous part, ending in a rounded pit below. Which groove and which pit are described, and what lies in each? The answer is the groove for the sigmoid sinus and the jugular fossa. The internal jugular vein begins in the fossa.

Question 857. On the inferior surface of the temporal bone, which opening lies in front of the jugular fossa, and what passes through it? The answer is the opening of the carotid canal, which carries the internal carotid artery. The canal runs upward and then turns forward and medially. The styloid process projects downward lateral to that opening.

Question 858. A constructed illustration: after a blow to the side of the head, the face on that side droops and hearing is reduced. Which bone is fractured, and along which part of it does the fracture run? The answer is the temporal bone, through its petrous part, crossing the facial canal and the internal ear. Management belongs to the treating clinician.

Question 859. A constructed illustration: after a wide yawn the jaw locks open, and the head of the mandible can be felt in front of the ear. Which depression of the temporal bone has the jaw left, and which ridge lies in front of it? The answer is the mandibular fossa, with the articular tubercle in front. Management belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 860. True or false: the middle ear and the internal ear both lie within the petrous part of the temporal bone. The answer is true. The petrous part is a dense pyramid that projects medially and forward. The middle ear, with its three small bones, and the internal ear both lie inside it.

Question 861. Which small opening on the temporal bone transmits the chorda tympani, the nerve that carries taste from the front of the tongue: the stylomastoid foramen, the petrotympanic fissure, the mastoid foramen, or the carotid canal? The answer is the petrotympanic fissure. It lies behind the mandibular fossa, and the nerve joins the lingual nerve after leaving it.

Question 862. Which two bones form the zygomatic arch: the temporal bone and the maxilla, the temporal bone and the zygomatic bone, the frontal bone and the zygomatic bone, or the sphenoid and the zygomatic bone? The answer is the temporal bone and the zygomatic bone. The zygomatic process of the temporal bone meets the temporal process of the zygomatic bone. The masseter muscle arises from the arch.

Question 863. Which bony projection hangs down behind the external acoustic meatus and can be felt behind the ear? The answer is the mastoid process. It belongs to the mastoid part of the temporal bone. Its rough outer surface gives attachment to muscles of the neck, and it is filled with air cells that communicate with the middle ear.

Question 864. Which large muscle of the neck attaches to the outer surface of the mastoid process and to the sternum and clavicle below? The answer is the sternocleidomastoid muscle. Its mastoid attachment lies on the outer surface of the process. It turns the head to the opposite side and flexes the neck. Deeper muscles of the neck attach nearby as well.

Question 865. Into which part of the ear do the mastoid air cells drain: the external acoustic meatus, the middle ear through the mastoid antrum, the internal ear, or the nasopharynx? The answer is the middle ear, through the mastoid antrum. The antrum lies behind the middle ear and above the air cells. An infection of the middle ear can therefore spread into the mastoid.

Question 866. A described radiograph of the temporal bone shows a honeycomb of small dark spaces behind and below the ear canal. Which part of the bone is shown, and with which cavity does it communicate? The answer is the mastoid part, filled with mastoid air cells. They communicate with the middle ear through the mastoid antrum. Their number and size vary greatly from person to person.

Question 867. Deep to the mastoid process, which large venous channel runs downward behind the petrous part of the temporal bone? The answer is the sigmoid sinus. It lies in a groove on the inner surface of the mastoid and occipital bones and turns into the internal jugular vein below. The facial nerve lies anteromedial to the mastoid process, in its own canal.

Question 868. A constructed illustration: a child has a red, tender swelling over the bone behind the ear, and the ear canal is discharging. Which part of the temporal bone is inflamed, and through which space did the infection reach it? The answer is the mastoid part, reached from the middle ear through the mastoid antrum. Management belongs to the treating clinician.

Question 869. Which opening lies on the inner surface of the mastoid process near its posterior border and carries an emissary vein: the stylomastoid foramen, the mastoid foramen, the jugular foramen, or the hypoglossal canal? The answer is the mastoid foramen, and the vein it carries joins the sigmoid sinus. It is a small and inconstant opening.

Question 870. Name two muscles that take attachment from the mastoid process, spoken as a list. The answer is the sternocleidomastoid and the posterior belly of the digastric, with the splenius capitis and the longissimus capitis also attached. All of them lie in the neck and act on the head or the hyoid bone. The digastric arises from the mastoid notch on the inner side.

Question 871. Which slender projection of bone points downward and forward from the underside of the temporal bone, just in front of the mastoid process? The answer is the styloid process. It belongs to the temporal bone and gives attachment to three muscles and one ligament. It is about 2 to 3 centimeters long in the adult.

Question 872. To which bone is the styloid process connected below by a long ligament? The answer is the hyoid bone. The stylohyoid ligament runs from the tip of the styloid process to the lesser horn of the hyoid. It is a remnant of the cartilage of the second pharyngeal arch, and the lesser horn shares that same origin.

Question 873. Which three muscles arise from the styloid process: the stylohyoid, the styloglossus and the stylopharyngeus, the temporalis, the masseter and the pterygoids, the digastric, the mylohyoid and the geniohyoid, or the trapezius, the levator scapulae and the rhomboids? The answer is the stylohyoid, the styloglossus and the stylopharyngeus. They move the hyoid bone, the tongue and the pharynx.

Question 874. Which nerve supplies the stylopharyngeus, the only muscle that nerve supplies: the hypoglossal nerve, the glossopharyngeal nerve, the vagus nerve, or the mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve? The answer is the glossopharyngeal nerve. That nerve also carries sensation from the back of the tongue and the pharynx. The stylohyoid, by contrast, is supplied by the facial nerve.

Question 875. A described dry skull shows a slender spike of bone on the underside of the temporal bone that has grown unusually long. Which process is described, and from which pharyngeal arch cartilage did it develop? The answer is the styloid process, from the cartilage of the second pharyngeal arch. The same cartilage forms the stylohyoid ligament and the lesser horn of the hyoid.

Question 876. Which large artery ascends through the neck deep to the styloid process and its muscles, lying medial to them? The answer is the internal carotid artery. It passes up to the carotid canal, which lies medial to the styloid process. The external carotid artery lies superficial and lateral to it.

Question 877. On the underside of the temporal bone, the styloid process and the mastoid process project downward on either side of a small opening. What is that opening called, and what leaves the skull through it? The answer is the stylomastoid foramen, which the facial nerve leaves. It lies between the two processes, behind the styloid and in front of the mastoid.

Question 878. A constructed illustration: a sharp blow below the ear tears a nerve as it leaves a small opening between two downward projections of the temporal bone, and the face on that side cannot move. Which opening and which nerve are involved? The answer is the stylomastoid foramen and the facial nerve. Management belongs to the treating clinician.

Question 879. Which two oval projections on the base of the skull rest on the first cervical vertebra and carry the weight of the head? The answer is the occipital condyles. They lie on either side of the foramen magnum and are convex downward and outward. They form the joints by which the head nods on the neck.

Question 880. Which canal opens above and in front of each occipital condyle, and which nerve passes through it? The answer is the hypoglossal canal, carrying the hypoglossal nerve. That nerve supplies the muscles of the tongue. The canal lies above the condyle and medial to the jugular foramen, and a small emissary vein often runs beside the nerve.

Question 881. The occipital condyles articulate with the first cervical vertebra at a joint that allows which movement: rotation of the head, nodding of the head, side to side gliding without movement, or no movement at all? The answer is nodding of the head. The joint is the atlanto-occipital joint, and it allows flexion and extension. Rotation takes place lower down, between the first and second cervical vertebrae.

Question 882. Which structure lies in the condylar canal, just behind each occipital condyle: the vertebral artery, an emissary vein, the hypoglossal nerve, or the spinal part of the accessory nerve? The answer is an emissary vein, which joins the sigmoid sinus. The vertebral artery passes through the foramen magnum instead. The condylar canal is a small and inconstant opening.

Question 883. A described view of the base of the skull shows a large central opening with a smooth oval facet on each side of it, and a smaller canal above and in front of each facet. Which three features are described? The answer is the foramen magnum, the two occipital condyles, and the two hypoglossal canals. The condyles face downward and articulate with the first cervical vertebra.

Question 884. Relative to the occipital condyle, where does the jugular foramen lie, and what does it transmit? The answer is behind and lateral to the condyle. It transmits the glossopharyngeal, vagus and accessory nerves and the internal jugular vein. The hypoglossal canal lies in front of it, above the condyle, and a small ridge of bone separates the two.

Question 885. About which axis does the head nod at the atlanto-occipital joint, and which plane does that movement occupy? The answer is a transverse axis, with the movement in the sagittal plane. The condyles slide on the facets of the first cervical vertebra. The axis passes from side to side through the two joints.

Question 886. A constructed illustration: a blow to the top of the head leads to bleeding behind the ear and around the eye. Which part of the skull base is most likely fractured, and which opening lies within it? The answer is the basilar part of the occipital bone, with the foramen magnum. The fracture may run into the occipital condyles beside it. Assessment belongs to the treating clinician.

Question 887. On which bone does the sella turcica lie, and in which cranial fossa is it found? The answer is the body of the sphenoid bone, in the middle cranial fossa. It is a saddle-shaped depression on the upper surface of the body, in the midline. It lies behind the chiasmatic groove and above the sphenoid sinus.

Question 888. Which part of the sella turcica holds the pituitary gland? The answer is the hypophysial fossa, the deepest part of the sella. It lies in the middle of the saddle, between the tuberculum sellae in front and the dorsum sellae behind. The gland sits in it, covered by a fold of dura.

Question 889. Which of these forms the posterior wall of the sella turcica: the tuberculum sellae, the dorsum sellae, the chiasmatic groove, or the anterior clinoid process? The answer is the dorsum sellae. It rises behind the hypophysial fossa and ends on each side as a posterior clinoid process. The tuberculum sellae forms the front wall, and the tentorium cerebelli attaches to the dorsum.

Question 890. Which structure forms the roof of the hypophysial fossa, and what passes through the small opening in its middle? The answer is the diaphragma sellae, a fold of dura, with the stalk of the pituitary gland passing through it. That stalk connects the gland to the base of the brain. The optic chiasm lies above and in front of the diaphragm.

Question 891. A described radiograph of the skull in side view shows a saddle-shaped hollow with a rounded front wall, a floor, and a square back wall that rises above the floor. Which region is shown, and which gland lies within it? The answer is the sella turcica, with the pituitary gland. The back wall is the dorsum sellae.

Question 892. A described CT section through the head in the coronal plane shows a small bony cup with a soft tissue mass resting inside it and air-filled cavities in front of and below it. Which bone forms the cup, and what is the mass? The answer is the body of the sphenoid bone, and the mass is the pituitary gland. The air cavity below is the sphenoid sinus.

Question 893. Which venous channel lies immediately on each side of the body of the sphenoid, beside the sella turcica? The answer is the cavernous sinus. Through it run the internal carotid artery and several nerves that move the eye. The abducens nerve runs within the sinus itself, beside the artery, while the other nerves lie in its lateral wall.

Question 894. Which structure lies immediately above the diaphragma sellae and in front of the stalk of the pituitary gland? The answer is the optic chiasm. It lies in the chiasmatic groove, above and in front of the sella. Because it lies so close, an expanding pituitary gland presses on it.

Question 895. From which bone do the anterior clinoid processes arise, and what do they border? The answer is the lesser wing of the sphenoid, and they border the front of the sella region. They project backward over the sides of the hypophysial fossa. The free edge of the tentorium cerebelli runs forward to them, and the internal carotid artery lies close by.

Question 896. Which air-filled cavity lies immediately below the floor of the sella turcica: the frontal sinus, the sphenoid sinus, the maxillary sinus, or the mastoid air cells? The answer is the sphenoid sinus. It lies within the body of the sphenoid bone, under the hypophysial fossa. Because the floor there is thin, the sinus lies close to the pituitary gland.

Question 897. Which of these pairs belongs together: the tuberculum sellae with the back of the sella, the dorsum sellae with the back of the sella, the hypophysial fossa with the roof of the sella, or the diaphragma sellae with the floor of the sella? The answer is the dorsum sellae with the back of the sella. The tuberculum sellae forms the front wall.

Question 898. Name two parts of the sella turcica, spoken as a list. The answer is the tuberculum sellae and the hypophysial fossa, with the dorsum sellae as a third. The tuberculum sellae is the rounded ridge at the front, and the fossa is the hollow that holds the pituitary gland. The dorsum sellae rises behind the fossa.

Question 899. A constructed illustration: a growth in the pituitary gland expands the hypophysial fossa upward, and the person loses the outer half of vision in both eyes. Where does the growth lie, and which structure above it is compressed? The answer is the pituitary gland in the hypophysial fossa, with the optic chiasm compressed above and in front of it. Management belongs to the treating clinician.

Question 900. Which of these statements about the sella turcica is correct: it lies in the posterior cranial fossa, it lies above the sphenoid sinus, it lies behind the foramen magnum, or it lies within the frontal bone? The answer is that it lies above the sphenoid sinus. The sella is a hollow in the body of the sphenoid, in the middle cranial fossa, and the foramen magnum lies behind it.

To summarize, the orbit is built from seven bones, with the frontal bone and the lesser wing of the sphenoid forming the roof, the maxilla most of the floor, and the lacrimal, ethmoid and sphenoid bones the medial wall. The anterior cranial fossa holds the frontal lobes above the cribriform plate, the middle cranial fossa holds the temporal lobes and the sella turcica, and the posterior cranial fossa holds the cerebellum and the brainstem. Each great opening of the base carries a named nerve, from the optic canal and the superior orbital fissure to the foramen rotundum, the foramen ovale, the jugular foramen and the hypoglossal canal. The sutures meet at the bregma, the lambda, the pterion and the asterion, and the thin bone of the pterion overlies the middle meningeal artery. The temporal bone carries the ear and its two downward processes, the occipital condyles nod the head on the first cervical vertebra, and the sella turcica cups the pituitary gland above the sphenoid sinus.
