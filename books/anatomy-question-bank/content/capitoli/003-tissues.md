# Chapter 3 — Tissues

This chapter examines the four basic tissues of the body and the way each one is built, beginning with connective tissue and its cells, fibers and ground substance, then cartilage, bone, muscle tissue and nervous tissue, and closing with the features that let one tissue be told from another. It holds one hundred questions, and every question gives its answer at once and then explains why that answer is right, so nothing has to be carried forward in the mind. The tissues described here are the materials from which every organ is assembled. This is educational material, and it supports rather than replaces an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 201. Which of the four basic tissue types consists of cells scattered through an abundant extracellular matrix that the cells themselves secrete? The answer is connective tissue. Its cells are widely separated by the material between them, and that material carries the fibers and the ground substance. Epithelium, muscle tissue and nervous tissue are far more crowded with cells and hold much less matrix.

Question 202. Which cell is the most numerous in ordinary loose connective tissue and is responsible for producing its collagen and its ground substance? The answer is the fibroblast. It lies between the collagen bundles and keeps them renewed throughout life. Macrophages, mast cells and fat cells are present in the same tissue but in much smaller numbers.

Question 203. Which of these gives loose connective tissue its resistance to pulling: collagen fibers, elastic fibers, reticular fibers, or ground substance? The answer is collagen fibers. Collagen is the commonest protein in the body, and its bundles resist tension. Elastic fibers stretch and recoil, and reticular fibers form fine supporting nets.

Question 204. Which fiber type forms the fine branching framework that supports the cells of a lymph node, the spleen and the bone marrow? The answer is the reticular fiber. It is a delicate collagen fiber that forms a net rather than a thick bundle. That net is the stroma on which the cells of those organs rest.

Question 205. Which statement about adipose tissue is correct: it stores energy in lipid droplets, it conducts electrical impulses, it lines the inner surface of blood vessels, or it forms the framework of the liver? The answer is that it stores energy in lipid droplets. The droplet fills the cell and pushes the nucleus to one edge. The tissue also cushions the body and insulates it.

Question 206. A described microscope field shows loose wavy bundles running in several directions, scattered flattened nuclei lying against the bundles, and clear spaces between them. Which tissue is shown? The answer is loose connective tissue, also called areolar tissue. The wavy bundles are collagen, and the clear spaces held ground substance and tissue fluid before the preparation was made.

Question 207. Which layer of connective tissue lies immediately deep to the skin and carries the fat that pads the body? The answer is the superficial fascia, also called the subcutaneous layer. It lies between the skin and the deep fascia that wraps the muscles. It is loose enough for fluid to spread along it.

Question 208. A constructed illustration: a clean laceration is repaired, and the wound is described as filling with a new tissue rich in small vessels, fibroblasts and loose matrix. Which tissue is filling the wound? The answer is granulation tissue. It is a temporary connective tissue built during repair. As it matures the vessels withdraw, collagen is laid down, and a scar forms.

Question 209. Which cell of connective tissue carries granules of histamine and heparin and lies close to small blood vessels? The answer is the mast cell. Its granules are released in allergic reactions and in local inflammation. Macrophages engulf material and plasma cells make antibody, so neither of those cells fits this description.

Question 210. True or false: in connective tissue the cells occupy more of the volume than the extracellular matrix does. The answer is false. Connective tissue is defined by an abundance of matrix, and its cells are widely scattered through it. Epithelium is the tissue in which cells are packed tightly together with almost nothing between them.

Question 211. Which of these pairs belongs together: fibroblasts with collagen, mast cells with myelin, plasma cells with lipid storage, or macrophages with ground substance? The answer is fibroblasts with collagen. Fibroblasts produce both the collagen fibers and the ground substance. Mast cells release histamine, plasma cells make antibody, and macrophages engulf debris.

Question 212. A described electron micrograph shows a bundle of long parallel fibrils with a repeating banded pattern across them. Which protein are those fibrils made of, and what does the banding represent? The answer is collagen, and the banding shows how the molecules overlap in regular steps. That regular overlap is what gives collagen its great tensile strength.

Question 213. Which statement about a tendon and a ligament is correct: a tendon joins muscle to bone and a ligament joins bone to bone, both join muscle to muscle, both lie inside a joint, or both are elastic cartilage? The answer is that a tendon joins muscle to bone and a ligament joins bone to bone. Both are dense regular connective tissue. A ligament holds two bones together.

Question 214. Which type of connective tissue forms the dermis of the skin and the fibrous capsule of an organ, with collagen bundles running in many directions? The answer is dense irregular connective tissue. Its bundles are tightly packed but not parallel, so the tissue resists pulling from any direction. Dense regular tissue has all its bundles in line instead.

Question 215. Which of these features separates connective tissue from epithelium: it is usually rich in blood vessels, it always sits on a basement membrane, it is avascular, or its cells form tight sheets? The answer is that it is usually rich in blood vessels. Epithelium is the avascular tissue, and it rests on a basement membrane. Cartilage is the connective tissue that lacks vessels.

Question 216. A constructed illustration: a person whose diet has long lacked fresh fruit and vegetables is described as having bleeding gums and wounds that will not heal. Which protein of connective tissue is deficient? The answer is collagen. Collagen synthesis needs vitamin C, so weak fibers form and the tissues that depend on them give way. This illustration is described to show the role of collagen.

Question 217. Which of these explains why loose connective tissue lies beneath most epithelia: it lets fluid and cells pass from the vessels to the epithelium, it bears the weight of the epithelium, it forms a waterproof barrier, or it gives rigid support? The answer is that it lets fluid and cells pass. That layer carries the capillaries that feed the avascular epithelium above it. White cells also travel through it.

Question 218. A described preparation of a tendon shows long wavy bundles running the length of the field, with rows of flattened nuclei between them and little ground substance. Which tissue is shown, and how does its structure serve its function? The answer is dense regular connective tissue. Its parallel bundles resist pull along one line, which is what a tendon needs. Few vessels run within it, so it heals slowly.

Question 219. Which tissue consists of a firm rubbery matrix with cells enclosed in small spaces, and has no blood vessels of its own? The answer is cartilage. Its cells, the chondrocytes, lie in lacunae within the matrix they secreted. Because it has no vessels, it is fed by diffusion from its surface.

Question 220. What is the name of the cell that produces and maintains the matrix of cartilage? The answer is the chondrocyte. It lies in a small cavity called a lacuna. While it is still laying down new matrix in growing cartilage, the same cell is called a chondroblast, and once the matrix closes around it, it becomes a chondrocyte.

Question 221. Which of these cartilages is the most widespread in the body and covers the ends of the long bones: hyaline cartilage, elastic cartilage, fibrocartilage, or bone? The answer is hyaline cartilage. It gives the smooth articular surface of joints and forms the costal cartilages and the rings of the windpipe. Elastic cartilage is found in the ear and the epiglottis.

Question 222. Which type of cartilage contains a dense network of elastic fibers and is found in the external ear and the epiglottis? The answer is elastic cartilage. Its fibers let it bend and then spring back into shape. Hyaline cartilage is far commoner, and fibrocartilage carries thick collagen bundles instead.

Question 223. A described section shows a smooth glassy matrix with rounded cells sitting in clear spaces, alone or in small groups, and no visible fibers. Which tissue is shown? The answer is hyaline cartilage. Its matrix looks glassy because the collagen fibers within it are too fine to be seen in an ordinary preparation. The cells of each group are the descendants of one parent cell.

Question 224. Which type of cartilage forms the intervertebral discs and the pubic symphysis: hyaline cartilage, elastic cartilage, fibrocartilage, or dense fibrous tissue alone? The answer is fibrocartilage. It carries thick bundles of collagen and resists both compression and pulling. It is the cartilage that comes closest to dense connective tissue.

Question 225. In the trachea, which tissue forms the rings that hold the airway open, and where are those rings incomplete? The answer is hyaline cartilage, and the rings are open at the back. The open side faces the esophagus, which lies behind the trachea. That gap is why a swallowed mouthful can bulge into the airway.

Question 226. A described cartilage has no blood vessels and is fed by diffusion from its covering. Which of these follows: it heals slowly after injury, it heals faster than bone, it cannot grow after birth, or it is replaced by muscle? The answer is that it heals slowly. Diffusion must carry every nutrient from the surface. Cartilage has no vessels of its own to bring them in.

Question 227. A constructed illustration: a joint is described as stiff and painful, and the smooth lining of its surfaces has been worn away. Which tissue has been lost in that description? The answer is hyaline cartilage, the articular cartilage of the joint. It has no blood vessels and almost no capacity to regrow. The bone beneath it is left exposed.

Question 228. Which statement about the growth of cartilage is correct: it grows both from within and from its surface, it grows only from within, it grows only at its surface, or it cannot grow after birth? The answer is that it grows both from within and from its surface. Interstitial growth divides the cells inside the matrix. Appositional growth adds new layers at the edge.

Question 229. A described specimen of an intervertebral disc shows an outer ring of parallel collagen bundles and an inner core of softer matrix with scattered cells. Which tissues form the ring and the core? The answer is fibrocartilage in the ring, called the annulus fibrosus, and a gel-like core, the nucleus pulposus. The core is a remnant of the notochord of the embryo.

Question 230. True or false: the surface of a moving joint is covered by hyaline cartilage, and that cartilage has no perichondrium over the articular surface. The answer is true. Articular cartilage is hyaline cartilage that has no fibrous covering where it faces the joint cavity. It is nourished instead by the synovial fluid that bathes it.

Question 231. Which of these pairs belongs together: elastic cartilage with the external ear, hyaline cartilage with the intervertebral disc, fibrocartilage with the tracheal rings, or bone with the epiglottis? The answer is elastic cartilage with the external ear. Hyaline cartilage forms the tracheal rings, fibrocartilage forms the intervertebral discs, and the epiglottis is made of elastic cartilage as well.

Question 232. Name the three types of cartilage and give one site for each, spoken as a sentence. The answer is hyaline cartilage at the ends of the long bones, elastic cartilage in the external ear, and fibrocartilage in the intervertebral discs. All three have cells in lacunae and a matrix they secrete, and none of the three has vessels of its own.

Question 233. Which tissue is described as a hard matrix of mineral salts deposited around collagen fibers, with cells enclosed in small spaces that communicate with one another? The answer is bone tissue. The mineral gives it hardness and the collagen lets it bend rather than shatter. The small spaces are the lacunae, joined by narrow canaliculi.

Question 234. What is the name of the mature bone cell that lies in a lacuna and maintains the matrix around it? The answer is the osteocyte. Its long processes run through the narrow canaliculi to reach its neighbors. Osteoblasts build new matrix, and osteoclasts break the old matrix down.

Question 235. Which of these cells is responsible for resorbing bone: the osteoblast, the osteocyte, the osteoclast, or the chondrocyte? The answer is the osteoclast. It is a large cell with several nuclei that dissolves both the mineral and the collagen. The osteoblast lays down new matrix instead, and the chondrocyte belongs to cartilage.

Question 236. Which cell of bone lays down the soft unmineralized matrix called osteoid before it hardens? The answer is the osteoblast. It sits on the surface of the bone it is building and secretes collagen and ground substance around itself. Once the matrix closes around it, it becomes an osteocyte.

Question 237. Which statement about the periosteum is correct: it covers the outer surface of a bone except at the joints and where tendons attach, it lines the marrow cavity, it covers the articular cartilage, or it has no vessels? The answer is that it covers the outer surface except at the joints. The endosteum lines the marrow cavity. It also brings vessels and bone-forming cells to the surface.

Question 238. What is the name of the thin membrane that lines the inside of the marrow cavity and covers the inner surfaces of the bone? The answer is the endosteum. It is a single layer of cells that includes both osteoblasts and osteoclasts. Like the periosteum, it can lay down bone or remove it.

Question 239. A described ground section of bone shows concentric rings of matrix around a central channel, with dark spaces between the rings. What is the unit shown, and what is the central channel called? The answer is an osteon, also called a Haversian system, with the Haversian canal at its center. It carries a small artery, a vein and a nerve. The dark spaces hold the osteocytes.

Question 240. Which of these describes compact bone rather than spongy bone: it is organized into osteons, it is a mesh of trabeculae, it has large spaces filled with marrow, or it forms the interior of the vertebral bodies? The answer is that it is organized into osteons. Spongy bone is a mesh of trabeculae with marrow between them. It fills the ends of the long bones.

Question 241. In a long bone, which part lies between the two ends and contains the marrow cavity? The answer is the diaphysis, which is the shaft. It is a cylinder of compact bone around the marrow. The two ends are the epiphyses, each covered by articular cartilage and separated from the shaft in the growing bone by the epiphyseal plate.

Question 242. Which statement about the blood supply of a long bone is correct: the periosteal vessels and the nutrient artery both supply it, only the nutrient artery supplies it, the marrow has no vessels, or the articular cartilage carries the main artery? The answer is that both the periosteal vessels and the nutrient artery supply it. The nutrient artery enters through a foramen in the shaft. The periosteal vessels enter the outer surface.

Question 243. A constructed illustration: a child's long bone is injured at its growing end, and growth in length stops. Which structure has been damaged in the description? The answer is the epiphyseal plate, the layer of cartilage that lengthens the bone. Growth in length happens there. Growth in width continues from the periosteum.

Question 244. A described radiograph of a growing child's knee shows a dark band across the end of the thigh bone and another across the end of the shin bone, with a small separate bone in front. Which structure do the dark bands represent, and what is that separate bone? The answer is the epiphyseal plates, and the separate bone is the patella. The plates look dark because cartilage is not mineralized.

Question 245. Which of these pairs a bone cell with its action: the osteoclast breaks down bone, the osteoblast breaks down bone, the osteocyte builds new matrix, or the osteoclast becomes an osteocyte? The answer is that the osteoclast breaks down bone. The osteoblast builds matrix and the osteocyte maintains it. An enclosed osteoblast becomes an osteocyte and not an osteoclast.

Question 246. Which structure lies immediately deep to the periosteum on the outer surface of the shaft of a long bone: compact bone, spongy bone, the marrow cavity, or the endosteum? The answer is compact bone. The periosteum covers it, and the compact layer surrounds the marrow cavity. Spongy bone lies inside the ends of the bone.

Question 247. A constructed illustration: a described fracture heals through a soft stage and then a hard stage, and the hard swelling is later reshaped. Which tissue forms the hard stage? The answer is bone, laid down first as a callus of woven bone and later replaced by mature bone. Woven bone has irregular collagen and is weak. It is rebuilt along the lines of force.

Question 248. Which statement about the mineral of bone is correct: it is a calcium phosphate salt laid along and between the collagen fibers, it is pure calcium carbonate, it lies only on the surface, or it fills the marrow cavity? The answer is that it is a calcium phosphate salt laid along and between the collagen fibers. The mineral gives hardness and the collagen gives resilience. Together they let it bend rather than crack.

Question 249. True or false: spongy bone is weaker than compact bone simply because it contains fewer cells. The answer is false. Spongy bone is arranged as a mesh of trabeculae that follow the lines of force, and that arrangement gives it great strength for its weight. Its cells are as numerous as those of compact bone.

Question 250. A described section of the shaft of a long bone shows, from the outside inward, a fibrous layer, parallel rings around central channels, and a cavity crossed by fine strands. Name the three layers in order. The answer is the periosteum, compact bone and the endosteum. The rings around the central channels are the osteons. The strands crossing the cavity are trabeculae of spongy bone.

Question 251. Which of these explains why a bone that is not used loses mass: the balance between the osteoblasts and the osteoclasts shifts toward removal, the bone cells stop making collagen, the matrix dissolves, or the marrow fills with cartilage? The answer is that the balance shifts toward removal. Mechanical load stimulates new bone. A limb that is not used is rebuilt less and loses mass.

Question 252. A growing long bone has a plate of cartilage across each end. Which of these lies immediately on the shaft side of that plate: the metaphysis, the epiphysis, the articular cartilage, or the marrow of the epiphysis? The answer is the metaphysis, the flared part of the shaft next to the plate. The epiphysis lies on the other side. The articular cartilage covers its free surface.

Question 253. Which of these pairs belongs together: the osteoblast with bone formation, the osteocyte with bone resorption, the osteoclast with cartilage growth, or the chondrocyte with mineral deposition? The answer is the osteoblast with bone formation. The osteocyte maintains the matrix, the osteoclast resorbs it, and the chondrocyte makes cartilage rather than bone.

Question 254. Name the three kinds of cell found in bone tissue and say what each one does, spoken as a sentence. The answer is the osteoblast, which builds new matrix, the osteocyte, which maintains the matrix around it, and the osteoclast, which resorbs bone. All three are active throughout life, so bone is rebuilt continuously.

Question 255. Which of the three muscle types is described as having cross striations, many nuclei in each fiber, and voluntary control? The answer is skeletal muscle. Its fibers are long cylinders with many nuclei at the edge, and its striations are easy to see under the microscope. Cardiac muscle is also striated, but it is not under voluntary control.

Question 256. Which muscle type is found only in the wall of the heart and is described as striated and involuntary? The answer is cardiac muscle. Its cells are joined end to end at intercalated discs and branch to form a network. It contracts rhythmically throughout life without any conscious control, and it cannot be rested.

Question 257. Which of these muscle types has no cross striations: skeletal muscle, cardiac muscle, smooth muscle, or all three? The answer is smooth muscle. It is found in the walls of the gut, the bladder and the blood vessels. Its cells are spindle shaped and each one has a single central nucleus.

Question 258. What is the name of the long multinucleated cell of skeletal muscle that runs the length of the muscle? The answer is the muscle fiber, also called a myofiber. It is formed during development by the fusion of many separate cells, which is why it carries several nuclei. Its nuclei lie just beneath the cell membrane.

Question 259. Which of these stores oxygen inside a muscle fiber and gives the tissue its red color: myoglobin, collagen, glycogen, or the sarcolemma? The answer is myoglobin. It holds oxygen within the fiber so that the muscle can work briefly before new blood arrives. Glycogen is the stored fuel, and the sarcolemma is the membrane of the fiber.

Question 260. A described microscope field from the wall of the small intestine shows elongated cells with a single central nucleus each, packed side by side, with no cross striations and no branching. Which muscle type is shown? The answer is smooth muscle. Its cells are spindle shaped and thickest in the middle. It contracts slowly and can hold the contraction.

Question 261. What is the name of the fine connective tissue sheath that wraps an individual muscle fiber in skeletal muscle? The answer is the endomysium. It is the finest of the three sheaths of a muscle, and it carries the capillaries that feed each fiber. The perimysium wraps bundles of fibers, and the epimysium wraps the whole muscle.

Question 262. Which of these describes a motor unit: one motor neuron and all the fibers it supplies, one fiber and all the neurons reaching it, one bundle of fibers, or one tendon with its muscle? The answer is one motor neuron and all the fibers it supplies. The fibers of a single unit are scattered through the muscle. Its size sets how fine the muscle control can be.

Question 263. Within a skeletal muscle, which sheath lies immediately around a bundle of fibers: the endomysium, the perimysium, the epimysium, or the sarcolemma? The answer is the perimysium. It wraps each bundle, which is called a fascicle, and carries the vessels and the nerves into the muscle. The epimysium is the outer covering of the whole muscle.

Question 264. A described section of the heart wall shows short branching cells joined end to end by dark bands, with one nucleus in each cell and faint cross striations. Which tissue is shown, and what are the dark bands called? The answer is cardiac muscle, and the bands are the intercalated discs. They hold the cells together. They also pass the impulse quickly from cell to cell.

Question 265. Which statement about the nuclei of the three muscle types is correct: skeletal fibers have several nuclei near the edge, cardiac cells have several in the middle, smooth cells have several each, or all three have one nucleus? The answer is that skeletal fibers have several near the edge. Cardiac and smooth muscle cells each have one nucleus. That nucleus lies in the middle of the cell.

Question 266. A constructed illustration: a described patch of the stomach wall loses its muscle, and that part of the wall becomes permanently weak and bulges. Which muscle type has been lost in the description? The answer is smooth muscle. The stomach wall has three layers of it, and a gap in that coat lets the wall give way. The other two muscle types do not occur there.

Question 267. In a skeletal muscle, which connective tissue layer lies between two neighboring bundles of fibers: the endomysium, the perimysium, the epimysium, or the deep fascia? The answer is the perimysium. Bundles of fibers are called fascicles, and the perimysium wraps each bundle. The deep fascia lies outside the epimysium and wraps the whole muscle.

Question 268. Which of these explains why cardiac muscle contracts as a single unit: its cells are joined by intercalated discs that pass the impulse quickly, its fibers are multinucleated, its cells have no striations, or it is supplied by many separate motor units? The answer is that the intercalated discs pass the impulse quickly. The heart therefore behaves as one unit. It needs no separate nerve ending for every cell.

Question 269. True or false: smooth muscle contraction is under conscious control and is therefore described as voluntary. The answer is false. Smooth muscle is involuntary, and it is found in the walls of the hollow organs and of the blood vessels. Skeletal muscle is the voluntary type, and cardiac muscle is involuntary as well.

Question 270. A described electron micrograph of skeletal muscle shows a repeating pattern of thick and thin filaments arranged in regular bands along the fiber. What is the functional unit of that repeating pattern called? The answer is the sarcomere, the segment between one Z line and the next. The thick and the thin filaments slide past one another when the fiber shortens.

Question 271. A constructed illustration: a cut divides the fibers of a skeletal muscle, but the fine sheath around each fiber survives. Which sheath is described, and does skeletal muscle regenerate well? The answer is the endomysium, and skeletal muscle regenerates only poorly. The surviving sheath can guide a few fibers across the gap, but scar tissue usually fills it instead.

Question 272. Which of these pairs belongs together: cardiac muscle with intercalated discs, skeletal muscle with a single central nucleus, smooth muscle with many nuclei in each fiber, or smooth muscle with cross striations? The answer is cardiac muscle with intercalated discs. Skeletal fibers carry several nuclei, smooth muscle cells have one, and smooth muscle has no striations.

Question 273. Which cell of nervous tissue is described as the conducting cell, with a cell body, dendrites and a long axon? The answer is the neuron. It receives signals on its dendrites and its cell body and sends them away along its axon. The supporting cells of nervous tissue are the glial cells.

Question 274. In the peripheral nervous system, which supporting cell wraps a single axon and forms the myelin sheath around it? The answer is the Schwann cell. Each one wraps a short segment of one axon. In the central nervous system the same work is done by the oligodendrocyte, which can myelinate several axons at once.

Question 275. Which type of cell forms the myelin sheath around axons in the central nervous system: the oligodendrocyte, the Schwann cell, the astrocyte, or the ependymal cell? The answer is the oligodendrocyte. One of them can myelinate several axons at the same time. The Schwann cell does the same work for the nerves outside the brain and the spinal cord.

Question 276. Which large star-shaped glial cell has processes that end on blood vessels and on neurons and helps to form the barrier between blood and nervous tissue? The answer is the astrocyte. Its processes cover the vessels of the brain and take part in the blood-brain barrier. It also helps to repair nervous tissue after injury.

Question 277. Which feature of a neuron fixes the direction in which its signal travels: the axon conducts away from the cell body and the dendrites toward it, the dendrites conduct away from the cell body, the cell body has no processes, or the myelin conducts toward it? The answer is that the axon conducts away from the cell body and the dendrites conduct toward it. The dendrites and the cell body receive the signal. The axon alone carries it away.

Question 278. A described section of the spinal cord shows a central mass of gray material shaped like a butterfly, ringed by white material, with a narrow channel at its center. Which substance is the gray material, and what is the channel? The answer is gray matter, which holds the cell bodies, and the channel is the central canal. The white matter outside carries the long tracts and owes its color to myelin.

Question 279. Which of these pairs a part of nervous tissue with what it contains: gray matter with cell bodies, white matter with cell bodies, gray matter with long myelinated tracts, or white matter with the central canal? The answer is gray matter with cell bodies. The cell bodies of neurons lie in the gray matter. White matter is white because of the myelin on the axons within it.

Question 280. In a peripheral nerve, which connective tissue sheath lies immediately around a single axon and its Schwann cell? The answer is the endoneurium. Around it, groups of axons form a fascicle wrapped in the perineurium. The whole nerve is wrapped in the epineurium, which is the thickest of the three.

Question 281. A constructed illustration: a nerve in the hand is divided completely, and sensation returns slowly from the cut toward the fingers over many months. What is happening along the nerve? The answer is that the axons are regrowing along the surviving sheaths. The endoneurial tubes guide them. This regrowth happens in the peripheral nerves only.

Question 282. Which statement about the myelin sheath is correct: supporting cells wrap the axon and speed the impulse, the neuron makes it itself, it slows the impulse, or it lies inside the axon? The answer is that supporting cells wrap it and it speeds the impulse. The gaps between the wrapped segments are the nodes of Ranvier. The sheath also insulates the axon from its neighbors.

Question 283. A described preparation of a peripheral nerve shows a bundle of axons, each wrapped in a spiral of many layers of membrane, with dark nuclei lying between the axons. What is the wrapping, and which cell made it? The answer is the myelin sheath, and it was made by the Schwann cell. Each Schwann cell wraps one segment of one axon.

Question 284. In the brain, which cells line the cavities of the ventricles and help to move the cerebrospinal fluid? The answer is the ependymal cells. They form a single layer that lines the ventricles and the central canal of the spinal cord. The cerebrospinal fluid itself is made by the capillaries of the choroid plexus.

Question 285. True or false: neurons in the central nervous system divide readily to replace those lost after injury. The answer is false. Mature neurons do not divide, and the central nervous system has very little capacity to replace them. That is why the loss of nerve cells in the brain and the spinal cord is usually permanent.

Question 286. Which of these explains why a nerve fiber with a thick myelin sheath carries a signal faster than one with a thin sheath: the impulse jumps between the gaps, the sheath makes the axon wider, the sheath carries the impulse itself, or it stops the axon using energy? The answer is that the impulse jumps between the gaps. That jumping is called saltatory conduction. Each jump renews the impulse.

Question 287. A constructed illustration: an injury divides a nerve in the arm cleanly, and sensation returns over many months, while the same injury in the spinal cord leaves a permanent loss. Which property of the two tissues explains the difference? The answer is the ability of peripheral axons to regrow along the surviving sheaths. The endoneurial tubes guide them. The central nervous system forms scar tissue instead.

Question 288. Which of these pairs belongs together: the Schwann cell with the myelin of a peripheral axon, the oligodendrocyte with a peripheral axon, the astrocyte with the myelin of the brain, or the ependymal cell with the myelin of a nerve? The answer is the Schwann cell with the myelin of a peripheral axon. The oligodendrocyte myelinates axons in the central nervous system. The astrocyte supports and the ependymal cell lines the cavities.

Question 289. Which of the four basic tissues is described as cells packed closely together, resting on a basement membrane, with almost no material between them? The answer is epithelium. It covers surfaces, lines cavities and forms glands. Its cells are joined to one another at their sides, which is why it forms a continuous sheet.

Question 290. Which of these forms the outer layer of the skin and the lining of the mouth: epithelium, connective tissue, muscle tissue, or nervous tissue? The answer is epithelium. It covers the surface of the body and lines every cavity that opens onto it. The tissue immediately beneath it is connective tissue.

Question 291. A described microscope field shows a single layer of flat cells with flattened nuclei resting on a thin basement membrane, with loose connective tissue below. Which tissue is shown? The answer is simple squamous epithelium. Its thinness allows gases and fluids to pass across it quickly. It lines the air sacs of the lung and the inner surface of the blood vessels.

Question 292. Which type of epithelium is described as having several layers, with flat cells at the surface and taller cells beneath them? The answer is stratified squamous epithelium. Its many layers protect the surface where wear is heavy. The skin and the lining of the mouth and the esophagus are made of it.

Question 293. Which of these epithelia is best suited to moving a film of mucus along a tube: ciliated columnar epithelium, simple squamous epithelium, stratified squamous epithelium, or transitional epithelium? The answer is ciliated columnar epithelium. Its cilia beat in one direction and carry the film along. It lines the air passages and the uterine tube.

Question 294. A constructed illustration: a surface that must stretch as a hollow organ fills is lined by an epithelium whose cells change shape as the organ empties and fills again. Which epithelium is described, and where is it found? The answer is transitional epithelium, and it lines the urinary bladder and the ureters. When the organ fills, the cells flatten. When it empties, they become rounded again.

Question 295. Which statement about glands is correct: an exocrine gland keeps a duct to the surface and an endocrine gland does not, both keep a duct, neither keeps a duct, or an endocrine gland secretes onto the skin? The answer is that an exocrine gland keeps a duct and an endocrine gland does not. The endocrine gland passes its product into the blood. The exocrine gland pours it onto a surface.

Question 296. A described section shows a tube lined by a single layer of tall cells, some of which reach the basement membrane and some of which do not, with nuclei at several levels. Which epithelium is shown? The answer is pseudostratified columnar epithelium. It looks layered, but every cell rests on the basement membrane. The air passages are lined by it.

Question 297. Spatial reasoning: a described section is cut across a tube, so the tube appears as a circle with its lining all round. What is that section called, and what would a cut along the length show? The answer is a transverse section, and a cut along the length is a longitudinal section. The transverse view shows the wall as a closed ring. The longitudinal view opens it out.

Question 298. Which of these best separates a section of dense regular connective tissue from one of smooth muscle: wavy bundles with few nuclei against spindle cells with a central nucleus each, both showing cross striations, the muscle having no nuclei, or the connective tissue having many nuclei? The answer is wavy bundles with few nuclei against spindle cells with a central nucleus each. One of the two shows cross striations and the other does not. One is mostly matrix and the other is mostly cell.

Question 299. Name the four basic tissue types of the body and say in one sentence what each one does. The answer is epithelium, which covers and lines, connective tissue, which supports and binds, muscle tissue, which contracts, and nervous tissue, which conducts. Every organ of the body is built from these four in different proportions.

Question 300. Spatial reasoning: in a described section of the gut wall, which layer lies between the epithelium that lines the lumen and the muscle that moves the wall? The answer is the connective tissue layer of the wall, with its vessels and nerves. It lies outside the epithelium and inside the muscle. Contraction of the muscle narrows the lumen.

To summarize, the body is built from four basic tissues: epithelium, which covers and lines, connective tissue, which supports and binds, muscle tissue, which contracts, and nervous tissue, which conducts. Connective tissue is mostly extracellular matrix, made by fibroblasts, with collagen for strength, elastic fibers for recoil and reticular fibers for delicate nets, and its looser and denser forms differ in the way their fibers are arranged. Cartilage has cells in lacunae, no vessels of its own, and three varieties, hyaline, elastic and fibrocartilage, while bone adds a mineralized matrix and the osteoblast, the osteocyte and the osteoclast. Skeletal muscle is striated and voluntary with many nuclei in each fiber, cardiac muscle is striated, involuntary and joined by intercalated discs, and smooth muscle is unstriated and involuntary. Nervous tissue conducts through neurons and is supported by glial cells, of which the Schwann cell myelinates peripheral axons and the oligodendrocyte myelinates central ones.
