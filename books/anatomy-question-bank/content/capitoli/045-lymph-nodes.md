# Chapter 45 — Lymph Nodes

This chapter examines the lymph nodes and the thoracic duct, the filters and the great collecting vessel of the lymphatic system. It describes what a lymph node is built from, where the named groups of nodes lie, what each group drains, and how lymph travels from the tissues through the nodes and into the thoracic duct. The chapter holds one hundred questions, and every one of them gives its answer immediately after the question and then explains why that answer is right, so nothing has to be held in mind until the end. The clinical items are constructed illustrations rather than real patients, and the measurements are ordinary textbook ranges offered only to describe normal structure. This is educational material, and it supports rather than replaces an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 4401. Which fibrous coat forms the outer covering of a lymph node, and what does it send inward? The answer is the capsule, which sends trabeculae inward. From its inner surface, septa pass into the cortex and carry blood vessels with them. The trabeculae divide the cortex into compartments.

Question 4402. Roughly how many lymph nodes are present in the adult body: 50 to 60, 150 to 200, 500 to 600, or 5,000 to 6,000? The answer is 500 to 600. They lie along the courses of the lymphatic vessels, often in groups where several vessels meet. The largest single collection lies in the mesentery of the small intestine.

Question 4403. A described section through a lymph node shows a dark outer zone of rounded masses and a paler central zone. Which part is the outer zone, and what are the masses? The answer is the cortex, and the masses are the lymphoid follicles. The paler central zone is the medulla, which contains cords and sinuses.

Question 4404. At which surface of a lymph node do the afferent lymphatic vessels enter, and how does their number compare with the vessel that leaves? The answer is the convex surface, while a single efferent vessel leaves at the hilum. Lymph enters widely and leaves by one narrow route, which slows its flow through the node.

Question 4405. Where does the efferent lymphatic vessel leave a lymph node: at the convex surface, at the hilum, through the capsule, or through a trabecula? The answer is at the hilum. The hilum is the concave notch where the capsule is drawn in, and the artery and vein of the node use the same place.

Question 4406. At the hilum of a lymph node, the efferent lymphatic vessel leaves beside which other structures? The answer is the artery and the vein of the node. Blood vessels enter and leave through the hilum, while lymph enters at the convex surface, so the two streams cross the node in opposite directions.

Question 4407. Which space does lymph enter first after crossing the capsule: the medullary sinus, the subcapsular sinus, the cortical sinus, or the trabecular sinus? The answer is the subcapsular sinus, just deep to the capsule. From there lymph seeps through the cortical sinuses toward the medulla and leaves by the efferent vessel at the hilum.

Question 4408. Which zone of a lymph node lies between the follicles of the cortex and the medulla, and which lymphocytes gather there? The answer is the paracortex, or deep cortex, where T lymphocytes gather. The follicles above it are the home of B lymphocytes, so the two zones hold different cells.

Question 4409. Which cells fill the follicles of the cortex: T lymphocytes, B lymphocytes, plasma cells, or macrophages? The answer is B lymphocytes. An active follicle shows a pale germinal center where those cells multiply, while plasma cells lie in the medullary cords and T lymphocytes in the paracortex.

Question 4410. True or false: lymph from most tissues passes through at least one lymph node before it returns to the blood. The answer is true. The nodes lie along the lymphatic vessels and act as filters, so particles in the lymph meet the macrophages and lymphocytes of a node. Some lymph passes through two or more nodes in series.

Question 4411. Which group of lymph nodes forms the largest single collection in the body, and where does it lie? The answer is the mesenteric nodes, between the two layers of the mesentery of the small intestine. They lie along the branches of the superior mesenteric artery and receive lymph from the jejunum and the ileum.

Question 4412. About how many nodes lie in the mesentery of the small intestine: 10 to 20, 100 to 150, 300 to 400, or 1,000 to 1,500? The answer is 100 to 150. They are arranged in tiers, with small nodes near the bowel wall and larger ones near the root of the mesentery, where the lymph joins the intestinal trunk.

Question 4413. A described lymphangiogram of the neck shows a chain of nodes along the internal jugular vein, deep to the sternocleidomastoid muscle. Which group is shown? The answer is the deep cervical nodes. They form a chain from the base of the skull to the root of the neck, while the superficial cervical nodes lie along the external jugular vein.

Question 4414. A constructed illustration: a person with a sore throat has a tender node below the angle of the mandible, at the level of the hyoid bone. Which node is enlarged? The answer is the jugulodigastric node, also called the tonsillar node. It lies at the junction of the posterior belly of the digastric muscle with the internal jugular vein and enlarges early in tonsillitis.

Question 4415. What is the name of the large deep cervical node lying at the junction of the posterior belly of the digastric muscle with the internal jugular vein? The answer is the jugulodigastric node. It is also called the tonsillar node because it drains the palatine tonsil, and it enlarges early in a throat infection.

Question 4416. Which muscle lies immediately above the jugulodigastric node, and which large vein lies deep to it? The answer is the posterior belly of the digastric muscle above the node, with the internal jugular vein deep to it. Because the node drains the tonsil, its enlargement points to the throat.

Question 4417. The tip of the tongue drains first to which group of nodes: the submental nodes, the submandibular nodes, the jugulodigastric node, or the occipital nodes? The answer is the submental nodes, which lie under the chin between the anterior bellies of the digastric muscles. The posterior third of the tongue drains instead to the deep cervical nodes.

Question 4418. A constructed illustration: an older adult has weight loss and early fullness after meals, and a hard node is found in the left supraclavicular fossa. Which organ should be examined first, and which vessel explains the pattern? The answer is the stomach, by way of the thoracic duct. Lymph from the abdominal organs ascends in the duct to the left venous angle just above that node.

Question 4419. Which left supraclavicular node, named after the physician who described it, may enlarge when a cancer of the stomach spreads along the thoracic duct? The answer is the node of Virchow. It lies in the left supraclavicular fossa behind the sternal head of the sternocleidomastoid muscle, and its enlargement is a sign of disease that has travelled far from its origin.

Question 4420. Where do the submandibular nodes lie, and toward which group does their lymph pass? The answer is in the submandibular triangle, below the body of the mandible and beside the submandibular gland, and their lymph passes to the deep cervical nodes. They receive lymph from the front of the tongue, the floor of the mouth, and the teeth and gums.

Question 4421. Which group of axillary nodes lies along the axillary vein on the lateral wall of the axilla: the pectoral group, the subscapular group, the lateral group, or the apical group? The answer is the lateral group, also called the humeral group. It lies along the axillary vein and receives most of the lymph from the upper limb.

Question 4422. Which group of axillary nodes lies along the lateral thoracic vessels behind the pectoralis major, and what does it drain? The answer is the pectoral group, also called the anterior group. It receives lymph from the anterior chest wall and from a large part of the breast.

Question 4423. A described dissection of the posterior wall of the axilla shows a row of nodes along the subscapular vessels. Which group is shown, and what does it drain? The answer is the subscapular or posterior group. It drains the back of the chest and the shoulder region, and its efferent vessels pass to the central and apical nodes.

Question 4424. Which group of axillary nodes lies at the apex of the axilla above the upper border of the pectoralis minor, and into which trunk do its vessels drain? The answer is the apical group, whose lymph passes into the subclavian trunk. It forms the highest tier of the axillary nodes, medial to the axillary vein.

Question 4425. A constructed illustration: a tumor of the upper outer quadrant of the breast is found together with nodes in the axilla. About how much of the lymph of the breast reaches the axillary nodes: one quarter, one half, three quarters, or all of it? The answer is about three quarters. The rest passes chiefly to the parasternal nodes beside the internal thoracic vessels.

Question 4426. The lymph of the upper limb drains chiefly into which group of nodes: the cervical nodes, the axillary nodes, the inguinal nodes, or the mediastinal nodes? The answer is the axillary nodes. The superficial vessels of the limb run along its medial and lateral borders and end in that group, and a deep set follows the main vessels to the same nodes.

Question 4427. A described lymphangiogram of the upper limb shows a single small node just above and medial to the elbow, beside the basilic vein. Which node is shown? The answer is the epitrochlear node, also called the cubital node. It receives lymph from the ulnar side of the hand and the medial side of the forearm.

Question 4428. Which node lies 2 to 3 centimeters above the medial epicondyle of the humerus, beside the basilic vein? The answer is the epitrochlear node. It is the only constant node of the upper limb below the axilla, and its enlargement often follows an infection of the hand.

Question 4429. The epitrochlear node lies in the superficial fascia on which side of the arm, and to which group do its efferent vessels pass? The answer is the medial side of the arm, and its efferents pass to the lateral group of axillary nodes. It sits above the medial epicondyle beside the basilic vein.

Question 4430. Which nodes receive lymph from the ulnar side of the hand and the medial side of the forearm: the apical nodes of the axilla, the epitrochlear node, the supraclavicular nodes, or the parasternal nodes? The answer is the epitrochlear node. From it the lymph passes to the lateral group of axillary nodes.

Question 4431. A constructed illustration: a puncture wound on the little finger becomes infected, and a tender node appears above and medial to the elbow. Which node is inflamed, and where does its lymph go next? The answer is the epitrochlear node, whose lymph passes to the lateral axillary nodes. The infection follows the lymphatic vessels that run with the basilic vein.

Question 4432. Which group of nodes lies in the superficial fascia of the groin just below the inguinal ligament? The answer is the superficial inguinal nodes. They receive lymph from the lower limb and from the superficial tissues of the lower abdominal wall, the perineum and the external genitalia.

Question 4433. Into which two groups are the superficial inguinal nodes arranged: medial and lateral, horizontal and vertical, deep and superficial, or upper and lower? The answer is a horizontal group and a vertical group. The horizontal group lies below the inguinal ligament and drains the superficial tissues of the abdomen and the perineum, and the vertical group lies along the great saphenous vein.

Question 4434. The deep inguinal nodes lie immediately medial to which vessel, and within which sheath? The answer is the femoral vein, within the femoral sheath. They lie beneath the deep fascia, and their efferents pass through the femoral canal to the external iliac nodes.

Question 4435. Which node is the highest of the deep inguinal group and lies in the femoral canal just below the inguinal ligament? The answer is the node of Cloquet, also called the node of Rosenmuller. It lies medial to the femoral vein at the entrance to the femoral canal.

Question 4436. Where does the node of Cloquet lie: in the popliteal fossa, in the femoral canal medial to the femoral vein, in the adductor canal, or beside the great saphenous vein? The answer is in the femoral canal, medial to the femoral vein. It is the highest node of the deep inguinal group.

Question 4437. A described cross section taken just below the inguinal ligament shows the femoral vein within a fibrous sheath, with a small node on the medial side of the vein inside that sheath. Which node is shown? The answer is the node of Cloquet, the highest deep inguinal node. Nodes inside the femoral sheath are deep inguinal nodes, while those outside it in the superficial fascia are superficial inguinal nodes.

Question 4438. Which structures do the popliteal nodes chiefly drain: the deep parts of the leg and the foot, the skin of the thigh, the perineum, or the buttock? The answer is the deep parts of the leg and the foot. They lie in the popliteal fossa around the popliteal vessels and also receive lymph from the knee joint.

Question 4439. Which group of nodes lies in the fat of the popliteal fossa, around the popliteal artery and vein, and receives lymph from the deep parts of the leg? The answer is the popliteal nodes. There are usually 4 to 6 of them, and their efferents ascend to the deep inguinal nodes.

Question 4440. A constructed illustration: a deep infection of the calf muscles is followed by a tender swelling in the popliteal fossa. Which nodes are involved, and where does their lymph go next? The answer is the popliteal nodes, whose lymph passes to the deep inguinal nodes. The deep tissues of the leg drain to them, while the skin drains to the superficial inguinal nodes.

Question 4441. True or false: superficial lymph from the foot drains mainly to the superficial inguinal nodes rather than to the popliteal nodes. The answer is true. The superficial tissues of the lower limb drain along the great saphenous vein to the superficial inguinal nodes, except on the lateral side of the foot and leg, which follows the small saphenous vein to the popliteal nodes.

Question 4442. Which nodes receive lymph from the medial half of the breast: the axillary nodes, the parasternal nodes, the popliteal nodes, or the deep cervical nodes? The answer is the parasternal nodes, which lie along the internal thoracic vessels. Lymph from them passes to the bronchomediastinal trunk.

Question 4443. The parasternal nodes lie along the internal thoracic vessels. Behind which structures do they lie, and on which side of the sternum? The answer is behind the costal cartilages and lateral to the sternum. They receive lymph from the medial part of the breast and from the anterior chest wall.

Question 4444. Which lymph nodes lie at the root of the mesentery, around the first part of the superior mesenteric artery, and receive the lymph gathered by the mesenteric nodes? The answer is the superior mesenteric nodes. Their efferent vessels join the intestinal trunk, which ends in the cisterna chyli.

Question 4445. A described operation field shows a chain of nodes lying on the front of the abdominal aorta above the origin of the renal arteries, with lymphatic vessels running up from the kidneys and the gonads. Which group is shown? The answer is the para-aortic or lumbar nodes. Their efferents form the lumbar trunks, which end in the cisterna chyli.

Question 4446. Which trunks join to form the cisterna chyli: the right and left lumbar trunks with the intestinal trunk, the right and left jugular trunks, the bronchomediastinal trunks, or the subclavian trunks? The answer is the right and left lumbar trunks together with the intestinal trunk. The lumbar trunks drain the lower limbs and the pelvis, and the intestinal trunk drains the gut.

Question 4447. Which of these pairs belongs together: the axillary nodes with the lower limb, the popliteal nodes with the deep calf, the cervical nodes with the foot, or the inguinal nodes with the head? The answer is the popliteal nodes with the deep calf. The axillary nodes drain the upper limb, and the inguinal nodes drain the lower limb and the perineum.

Question 4448. Which of these pairs belongs together: the submental nodes with the tip of the tongue, the submandibular nodes with the tip of the tongue, the occipital nodes with the tip of the tongue, or the supraclavicular nodes with the tip of the tongue? The answer is the submental nodes with the tip of the tongue. The submandibular nodes lie further back and drain the front of the tongue.

Question 4449. Which of these pairs belongs together: the epitrochlear node with the radial forearm, the node of Cloquet with the axilla, the jugulodigastric node with the tonsil, or the popliteal nodes with the buttock? The answer is the jugulodigastric node with the tonsil, which is why it is named the tonsillar node. The epitrochlear node drains the ulnar side of the forearm, and the node of Cloquet lies deep in the groin.

Question 4450. Which of these pairs belongs together: the subscapular nodes with the anterior chest wall, the pectoral nodes with the back of the chest, the lateral nodes with the upper limb, or the central nodes with the lower limb? The answer is the lateral nodes with the upper limb. The pectoral nodes drain the anterior chest wall, and the subscapular nodes drain the back of the chest.

Question 4451. Name two groups of lymph nodes of the neck, spoken as a list. The answer is the submental nodes and the deep cervical nodes. The submental nodes lie under the chin between the anterior bellies of the digastric muscles, and the deep cervical nodes form a chain along the internal jugular vein.

Question 4452. Name two structures that enter or leave a lymph node at its hilum, spoken as a list. The answer is the efferent lymphatic vessel and the blood vessels. The artery and vein of the node use the hilum, while the afferent lymphatic vessels enter on the convex surface.

Question 4453. What is the chief work of a lymph node: to pump lymph toward the heart, to filter lymph and give lymphocytes a place to meet foreign material, to make red blood cells, or to store fat? The answer is that it filters lymph and gives lymphocytes a place to meet foreign material. Macrophages in the sinuses remove particles, and lymphocytes respond to what the lymph carries.

Question 4454. What is the name of the milky fluid that fills the lymphatic vessels of the small intestine after a fatty meal? The answer is chyle. The fat absorbed from the gut enters the lymphatic vessels called lacteals and gives the lymph its milky look.

Question 4455. The lymphatic vessels called lacteals are found in which structure: the villi of the small intestine, the crypts of the colon, the alveoli of the lung, or the glomeruli of the kidney? The answer is the villi of the small intestine. Each villus holds a central lacteal that absorbs the products of fat digestion, which is why intestinal lymph is milky after a meal.

Question 4456. A described section of a node taken from a person who died during a bacterial infection shows large pale germinal centers in the follicles and widened sinuses full of cells. Which change is shown? The answer is reactive follicular hyperplasia, the normal response of a node to infection. The germinal centers enlarge as B lymphocytes multiply, and the sinuses fill with macrophages.

Question 4457. A constructed illustration: a child with a sore throat has a small, tender, mobile node in the upper neck, and the node settles when the throat settles. What is the likely nature of the node? The answer is reactive enlargement of a cervical node in response to infection. Tenderness and mobility point to inflammation rather than to a tumor.

Question 4458. Which feature of an enlarged lymph node favors a malignant cause rather than an infection: tenderness on pressure, warmth of the skin over it, a hard and fixed node, or a node that shrinks as the infection resolves? The answer is a hard and fixed node. Infection usually makes a node tender, warm and mobile.

Question 4459. True or false: the right side of the head and the neck drains through the right lymphatic duct into the right venous angle. The answer is true. The right jugular trunk joins that duct, which ends where the right internal jugular and right subclavian veins meet.

Question 4460. Which venous junction receives the lymph of the right upper limb, and by which duct does it arrive? The answer is the right venous angle, by way of the right lymphatic duct. In many people that duct is absent, and its three trunks open separately into the angle.

Question 4461. Which statement about the right lymphatic duct is correct: it is present in every person, it carries lymph from the whole body, it is a short vessel that may be absent, or it is the upper end of the thoracic duct? The answer is that it is a short vessel, about 1 to 2 centimeters long, which may be absent. It drains only the right upper quadrant, and its trunks may open separately.

Question 4462. A described dissection of the small bowel mesentery shows small nodes close to the bowel wall and larger nodes near the root of the mesentery. What are these two sets? The answer is the juxta-intestinal nodes near the bowel and the superior mesenteric nodes near the root. Lymph filters through them in that order and then enters the intestinal trunk.

Question 4463. How does blood reach the tissue of a lymph node: by entering at the hilum and running along the trabeculae, by entering at the convex surface with the afferent lymphatics, by way of the subcapsular sinus, or by capillaries that pierce the capsule? The answer is that the artery enters at the hilum and its branches follow the trabeculae. Lymph enters at the convex surface instead.

Question 4464. A described section of a lymph node shows septa of connective tissue running inward from the capsule and dividing the outer part of the node into compartments. What are these septa called? The answer is the trabeculae, and they divide the cortex into compartments. They carry blood vessels into the substance of the node.

Question 4465. A constructed illustration: a person in a region where a mosquito-borne worm is common develops a leg that is swollen and firm and does not go down with rest. Which structures are blocked? The answer is the lymphatic vessels and nodes of the limb, a condition called lymphedema. Care of such a limb belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 4466. In which part of a lymph node do the high endothelial venules lie, the vessels through which lymphocytes re-enter the node from the blood: the follicles, the subcapsular sinus, the paracortex, or the medullary sinuses? The answer is the paracortex, the deep cortex between the follicles and the medulla. Lymphocytes leave the blood through the tall cells that line those venules.

Question 4467. Which cells line the sinuses of a lymph node and remove particulate matter from the lymph as it passes? The answer is the macrophages. They lie in the subcapsular sinus, the cortical sinuses and the medullary sinuses, and they present what they take up to the lymphocytes of the node.

Question 4468. In the medulla of a lymph node, between which spaces do the medullary cords lie, and which cells fill them? The answer is between the medullary sinuses, and the cords are filled with lymphocytes and plasma cells. Lymph flows through the sinuses that surround the cords.

Question 4469. A described cut surface of a lymph node shows a rounded outer border and a single concave notch where the capsule is drawn in. What is that notch called, and which structures use it? The answer is the hilum, through which the efferent lymphatic vessel and the blood vessels pass. The afferent vessels enter on the convex surface.

Question 4470. Which structure of a lymph node lies deepest, nearest the hilum: the lymphoid follicles, the subcapsular sinus, the medullary sinuses, or the outer cortex? The answer is the medullary sinuses. Lymph enters at the convex surface and travels inward, and it reaches the medullary sinuses last before leaving at the hilum.

Question 4471. A constructed illustration: an older adult has a hard, fixed node in the left supraclavicular fossa, and no infection is found in the neck. Which region should be examined first, and which vessel explains the finding? The answer is the abdomen, particularly the stomach, with the thoracic duct explaining it. Investigations belong to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 4472. A described specimen of the floor of the mouth shows two small nodes lying just below the body of the mandible, beside the submandibular gland. Which nodes are shown? The answer is the submandibular nodes. They drain the front of the tongue, the floor of the mouth and the teeth, and their efferent vessels pass to the deep cervical nodes.

Question 4473. Which group of lymph nodes receives lymph from the upper limb and from most of the breast: the cervical nodes, the axillary nodes, the inguinal nodes, or the mediastinal nodes? The answer is the axillary nodes. They lie in the axilla around the axillary vessels, and their lymph passes to the subclavian trunk.

Question 4474. Which lymph nodes lie beside the trachea and at its bifurcation, receiving lymph from the lungs? The answer is the tracheobronchial nodes, which include the paratracheal nodes and those below the bifurcation. On the left their lymph ends in the thoracic duct, and on the right in the right lymphatic duct.

Question 4475. True or false: a normal lymph node is usually too small to be felt through the skin. The answer is true. Most nodes are only a few millimeters across when they are healthy, and they become easy to feel when infection or a tumor enlarges them.

Question 4476. What is the name of the largest lymphatic vessel in the body, which carries lymph from both lower limbs, the abdomen, the left thorax, the left upper limb and the left side of the head and neck? The answer is the thoracic duct. It begins in the abdomen at the cisterna chyli and ends in the root of the neck.

Question 4477. About how long is the thoracic duct in an adult: 5 to 8 centimeters, 12 to 15 centimeters, 38 to 45 centimeters, or 90 to 100 centimeters? The answer is 38 to 45 centimeters. It runs from the upper part of the abdomen, through the chest, to the root of the neck.

Question 4478. What is the name of the dilated lower end of the thoracic duct, which receives the lumbar and intestinal trunks? The answer is the cisterna chyli. It lies at the level of the first and second lumbar vertebrae, and from it the thoracic duct ascends to the left venous angle.

Question 4479. At which level does the cisterna chyli lie: at the twelfth thoracic vertebra, at the first and second lumbar vertebrae, at the third lumbar vertebra, or in the pelvis? The answer is the first and second lumbar vertebrae. It lies in front of the vertebral bodies, behind the aorta and to its right.

Question 4480. The thoracic duct begins behind the right crus of the diaphragm. Between which two vessels does it lie at that point? The answer is the aorta and the azygos vein. It passes through the aortic opening of the diaphragm behind the aorta, with the azygos vein to its right.

Question 4481. A described lymphangiogram shows a thin, beaded vessel ascending through the back of the chest from the upper abdomen to the base of the neck. Which vessel is shown? The answer is the thoracic duct. Its beaded look comes from the many valves along its length.

Question 4482. Through which opening of the diaphragm does the thoracic duct pass from the abdomen into the chest: the caval opening, the esophageal opening, the aortic opening, or the opening for the sympathetic trunk? The answer is the aortic opening. The duct passes behind the aorta at the level of the twelfth thoracic vertebra.

Question 4483. In the lower part of the chest, the thoracic duct lies behind which structure, and which vessels lie on its left and on its right? The answer is that it lies behind the esophagus, with the aorta on its left and the azygos vein on its right. Near the fourth thoracic vertebra it crosses to the left side of the chest.

Question 4484. A described transverse section of the chest at the level of the eighth thoracic vertebra shows a small thin-walled vessel lying between the aorta and the azygos vein, behind the esophagus. Which vessel is it? The answer is the thoracic duct. It lies against the vertebral bodies at that level.

Question 4485. At about which level does the thoracic duct cross from the right side of the chest to the left: the second thoracic vertebra, the fourth or fifth thoracic vertebra, the eighth thoracic vertebra, or the twelfth thoracic vertebra? The answer is the fourth or fifth thoracic vertebra, at the level of the sternal angle. Below that level the duct lies on the right of the midline.

Question 4486. Which lymphatic vessel receives the lymph from the intercostal nodes and from the posterior mediastinal nodes? The answer is the thoracic duct. The intercostal nodes drain the back of the chest wall, and the posterior mediastinal nodes lie beside the esophagus and the aorta.

Question 4487. At which venous junction does the thoracic duct end, and which two veins form that junction? The answer is the left venous angle, formed by the left internal jugular vein and the left subclavian vein. Its lymph therefore enters the blood a short distance above the heart.

Question 4488. Which of these regions sends its lymph through the thoracic duct: the right upper limb, the right side of the head and neck, the left lower limb, or the right side of the chest? The answer is the left lower limb. The thoracic duct drains both lower limbs, the abdomen, the left thorax, the left upper limb and the left side of the head and neck.

Question 4489. Which of these does not send its lymph through the thoracic duct: the left lung, the right side of the head and neck, the small intestine, or the left lower limb? The answer is the right side of the head and neck, whose lymph reaches the right venous angle by the right lymphatic duct. The left lung drains by the left bronchomediastinal trunk.

Question 4490. A described specimen at the root of the neck shows a duct ending in the angle between the left internal jugular and left subclavian veins, with three short trunks joining it just before it ends. What are the duct and the trunks? The answer is the thoracic duct, joined by the left jugular, left subclavian and left bronchomediastinal trunks.

Question 4491. A constructed illustration: after an operation in the back of the chest, milky fluid collects in the left pleural cavity over several days. Which structure has been injured, and what is the collection called? The answer is the thoracic duct, and the collection is a chylothorax. Management belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 4492. A constructed illustration: a person with obstruction of the lymphatic channels of the abdomen passes urine that looks like milk. Which channel is involved? The answer is the thoracic duct and its abdominal tributaries, and the condition is chyluria. Care belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 4493. True or false: the thoracic duct carries the lymph of the left upper limb. The answer is true. The left subclavian trunk gathers the lymph of that limb and joins the thoracic duct at its upper end, while the right upper limb drains by the right subclavian trunk.

Question 4494. Which of these pairs belongs together: the left jugular trunk with the right side of the head and neck, the intestinal trunk with the lower limbs, the lumbar trunks with the gut, or the left bronchomediastinal trunk with the left lung? The answer is the left bronchomediastinal trunk with the left lung. The left jugular trunk drains the left side of the head and neck.

Question 4495. Name the three trunks that join the upper end of the thoracic duct, spoken as a list. The answer is the left jugular trunk, the left subclavian trunk and the left bronchomediastinal trunk. They carry lymph from the left side of the head and neck, from the left upper limb, and from the left side of the chest.

Question 4496. A described sagittal section through the back of the chest shows a thin-walled tube running upward in front of the vertebral column and behind the esophagus. On which side of the midline does it lie below, and on which side above? The answer is the right side below and the left side above. The change takes place near the fourth thoracic vertebra.

Question 4497. Which statement about the valves of the thoracic duct is correct: they are absent, they lie only in the cisterna chyli, they keep lymph moving toward the veins and stop blood from entering the duct, or they close the duct during exercise? The answer is that they keep lymph moving toward the veins and stop blood from entering the duct. The valve at its termination guards against backflow.

Question 4498. Which lymphatic vessel lies immediately behind the left subclavian artery as it approaches the left venous angle at the root of the neck? The answer is the thoracic duct. Having crossed to the left in the chest, it ascends behind the left subclavian artery and ends a short distance above that point.

Question 4499. A described section of a vessel wall shows a thin layer of endothelium over elastic fibers and smooth muscle, with valves at intervals, and the vessel carries a milky fluid. Which vessel is it? The answer is the thoracic duct. Its wall is thinner than that of a vein of similar size, and no red blood cells lie within it.

Question 4500. A described dissection of the upper abdomen shows, in place of a single sac, a plexus of small vessels at the level of the first lumbar vertebra, behind the aorta. Which structure is described? The answer is the cisterna chyli, which in many people is a plexus rather than a sac. Its form varies widely.

To summarize, lymph nodes are filters set along the lymphatic vessels, built around a capsule that sends trabeculae inward, with afferent vessels entering the convex surface and a single efferent vessel leaving at the hilum. Lymph enters the subcapsular sinus, passes through the cortical and medullary sinuses and leaves at the hilum, meeting B lymphocytes in the follicles, T lymphocytes in the paracortex and macrophages in the sinuses. The named groups drain their own territories: the deep cervical chain drains the head and the neck, the axillary nodes drain the upper limb and most of the breast, the parasternal nodes drain the medial half of the breast, the mesenteric nodes drain the gut, the inguinal nodes drain the lower limb and the perineum, and the popliteal nodes drain the deep parts of the leg. The thoracic duct begins at the cisterna chyli at the first and second lumbar vertebrae, ascends behind the esophagus between the aorta and the azygos vein, crosses to the left at about the fourth thoracic vertebra and ends at the left venous angle, draining both lower limbs, the abdomen, the left thorax, the left upper limb and the left side of the head and neck, while the right upper quadrant drains by the right lymphatic duct.
