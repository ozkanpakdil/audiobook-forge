# Chapter 64 — Pleura

The pleura is the serous membrane that wraps each lung and lines the inside of the chest, and the diaphragm is the muscular sheet that forms the floor of the chest and the chief muscle of breathing. This chapter takes them in turn, first the two layers of pleura with their recesses, their nerve supply and their surface markings, then the diaphragm with its parts, its three great openings and its action. It holds one hundred questions, and each one gives its answer immediately after the question and then explains why that answer is right, so nothing has to be held in mind until the end. This is educational material, and it supports rather than replaces an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 6301. Which serous membrane is wrapped tightly over the surface of each lung and dips into every fissure? The answer is the visceral pleura. It cannot be separated from the lung without tearing the tissue beneath it. The parietal pleura lines the chest wall instead.

Question 6302. What is the name of the narrow, fluid-filled interval between the visceral pleura and the parietal pleura? The answer is the pleural cavity. In health it is only a potential space, because the two layers lie in contact. A film of serous fluid between them lets them slide on each other during breathing.

Question 6303. Which of these is not one of the named parts of the parietal pleura: the costal part, the mediastinal part, the diaphragmatic part, or the pulmonary part? The answer is the pulmonary part. The parietal layer is divided by the surfaces it lines into costal, mediastinal, diaphragmatic and cervical parts. The layer applied to the lung is the visceral pleura.

Question 6304. Which part of the parietal pleura covers the side of the pericardium and the great vessels in the middle of the chest? The answer is the mediastinal pleura. It hangs as a curtain between the lung and the heart. The costal pleura lies farther out, against the ribs.

Question 6305. Which part of the parietal pleura rises above the first rib into the root of the neck: the costal part, the mediastinal part, the cervical part, or the diaphragmatic part? The answer is the cervical part, or cupola. A fibrous sheet, the suprapleural membrane, strengthens it from above. That membrane is attached to the inner border of the first rib and to the transverse process of the seventh cervical vertebra.

Question 6306. Which space of the pleural cavity fills first when fluid collects in an upright person: the costomediastinal recess, the costodiaphragmatic recess, the pulmonary ligament, or the pleural sleeve? The answer is the costodiaphragmatic recess. It is the lowest and most dependent part of the cavity, so fluid gathers there first. On a chest radiograph it appears as a dense shadow that blots out the angle between the diaphragm and the chest wall.

Question 6307. Which of these spaces is a true pleural recess: the costomediastinal recess, the aortic recess, the subphrenic recess, or the coronary recess? The answer is the costomediastinal recess, where the costal and mediastinal pleurae meet behind the sternum. The costodiaphragmatic recess is the other named one. The subphrenic space lies below the diaphragm and outside the chest.

Question 6308. The pleura lining the inner surface of the ribs is supplied by which nerves? The answer is the intercostal nerves. They are the segmental nerves of that wall, and the pleura covering it shares them. The mediastinal pleura and the central part of the diaphragmatic pleura are supplied by the phrenic nerve instead.

Question 6309. Irritation of the central part of the diaphragmatic pleura is felt where, and by which nerve is that sensation carried? The answer is the tip of the shoulder, carried by the phrenic nerve. The phrenic nerve comes from the third, fourth and fifth cervical segments. The skin over the shoulder is supplied by the same segments, so the brain misreads the source.

Question 6310. Is the visceral pleura sensitive to pain? The answer is no. It carries autonomic nerves that follow the bronchi and the vessels, and these do not convey sharp pain. Pleuritic pain comes from the parietal layer, which is richly supplied by somatic nerves.

Question 6311. A constructed illustration: a young adult describes a sharp pain in the side of the chest that worsens on a deep breath and eases when breathing is shallow. Which layer of pleura is inflamed? The answer is the parietal pleura. It is the sensitive layer, and stretching it with each deep breath provokes the pain. The visceral pleura can be diseased without causing pain at all.

Question 6312. A described upright chest radiograph shows a dense shadow at the base of one lung whose upper border curves upward toward the axilla. What does that curve represent? The answer is the upper surface of a pleural effusion. Free fluid settles at the base, and its surface rises higher against the chest wall than near the mediastinum. The lung above recoils away from it.

Question 6313. Which layer lies between the costal parietal pleura and the inner surface of the ribs? The answer is the endothoracic fascia, with the intercostal muscles outside it. This loose fibrous layer allows the pleura to move on the chest wall. Beyond the muscles lie the ribs themselves.

Question 6314. The lower border of the pleura is described in the midaxillary line. At which rib does it lie: the eighth, the tenth, the eleventh, or the twelfth? The answer is the tenth rib. The reflection then runs down and back to reach the twelfth rib beside the vertebral column. At the midclavicular line the pleura reaches only the eighth rib.

Question 6315. True or false: the lower border of the lung lies about two ribs higher than the lower border of the pleura. The answer is true. The pleura turns back below the lung and so forms the costodiaphragmatic recess. That gap of about two ribs is the space into which the lung expands on deep inspiration.

Question 6316. What is the name of the serous membrane that lines the inner surface of the thoracic wall, the mediastinum and the diaphragm? The answer is the parietal pleura. It is continuous with the visceral layer at the root of the lung. The two layers together form a closed sac.

Question 6317. Where does the apex of the lung, covered by pleura, lie in relation to the clavicle? The answer is that it rises about 2 to 3 centimeters above the medial third of the clavicle. The cervical pleura therefore reaches into the root of the neck. A wound above the clavicle can open the pleural cavity.

Question 6318. A described chest radiograph shows one side of the chest uniformly bright with no lung markings, and the lung itself is shrunken toward the middle. What has happened? The answer is a pneumothorax. Air has entered the pleural cavity and the elastic recoil of the lung has collapsed it. The visceral pleura lies against the collapsed lung.

Question 6319. A constructed illustration: fluid is to be drawn from the pleural cavity through the chest wall. Why is the needle passed just above the upper border of the rib below? The answer is that the intercostal nerves and vessels run in a groove on the under surface of each rib. Passing above the lower rib keeps the needle away from them, and the management belongs to the treating clinician.

Question 6320. Which of these pairs belongs together: the midclavicular line with the tenth rib, the midaxillary line with the eighth rib, the scapular line with the eleventh rib, or the vertebral column with the sixth rib? The answer is the scapular line with the eleventh rib. The pleural reflection reaches the tenth rib in the midaxillary line and the eighth in the midclavicular line.

Question 6321. What is the name of the double fold of pleura that hangs below the root of the lung? The answer is the pulmonary ligament. It is formed where the visceral and parietal layers meet and continue downward. It allows the structures of the root to move a little during breathing.

Question 6322. Which pleura follows the oblique and horizontal fissures of the lung: the costal, the mediastinal, the visceral, or the diaphragmatic? The answer is the visceral pleura. It is applied to the lung surface and therefore dips into every fissure between the lobes. The parietal layer stays on the wall and knows nothing of the fissures.

Question 6323. Why is the costomediastinal recess larger on the left than on the right? The answer is the cardiac notch of the left lung. The heart occupies that notch, so the anterior border of the left lung lies farther from the midline. The left pleura therefore extends further across the front of the pericardium.

Question 6324. A described upright chest radiograph shows a straight horizontal line across the lower chest with a bright zone above it and a dense zone below. What is shown? The answer is a hydropneumothorax, with air above the fluid. Air rises and fluid sinks, so the boundary between them is flat. A collection of fluid alone would show a curved upper border instead.

Question 6325. Name the two named recesses of the pleural cavity, spoken as a list. The answer is the costodiaphragmatic recess and the costomediastinal recess. The first lies at the bottom of the cavity, between the diaphragm and the chest wall. The second lies behind the sternum, where the costal and mediastinal pleurae meet.

Question 6326. Where are the visceral and parietal layers of the pleura continuous with each other? The answer is at the root of the lung. The pleura leaves the mediastinum there and turns onto the lung as a sleeve. Below the root the two layers continue down as the pulmonary ligament.

Question 6327. The visceral pleura takes its blood supply mainly from which vessels: the intercostal arteries, the bronchial arteries, the internal thoracic arteries, or the pulmonary arteries? The answer is the bronchial arteries. They follow the bronchi and supply the tissue around them. The intercostal and internal thoracic arteries supply the parietal layer.

Question 6328. True or false: in a healthy person the pleural cavity contains a small amount of air. The answer is false. It contains only a thin film of serous fluid, and the two pleural layers lie in contact. Air in the pleural cavity is a pneumothorax and separates the layers.

Question 6329. A constructed illustration: a chest wound lets air into the pleural cavity with each breath, and later the heart and the trachea are pushed toward the opposite side. Why does that shift occur? The answer is that pressure in the injured pleural cavity has risen above atmospheric pressure. The trapped air presses the mediastinum across, and the management belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 6330. A described dissection of the neck above the clavicle shows a dome of pleura covered by a fibrous sheet and crossed in front by vessels. Which pleura is shown, and what lies immediately in front of it? The answer is the cervical pleura, with the subclavian vessels in front. The fibrous sheet above it is the suprapleural membrane.

Question 6331. Lymph from the costal parietal pleura drains mainly to which nodes: the pulmonary nodes, the intercostal and internal thoracic nodes, the hilar nodes, or the deep cervical nodes? The answer is the intercostal and internal thoracic nodes. They lie with the vessels that supply the same wall. Lymph from the visceral pleura drains instead to the pulmonary nodes along the bronchi.

Question 6332. Where does the phrenic nerve lie in relation to the root of the lung? The answer is in front of it. The nerve descends through the thorax on the side of the pericardium and crosses the front of the root. The vagus nerve passes behind the root instead.

Question 6333. What is the name of the fibrous sheet that covers the dome of the cervical pleura and is attached to the first rib? The answer is the suprapleural membrane. It also gains attachment to the transverse process of the seventh cervical vertebra. It helps to keep the cupola of the pleura in place during breathing.

Question 6334. Which structures lie immediately outside the costal parietal pleura: the ribs and their periosteum only, the endothoracic fascia and the intercostal muscles, the parietal peritoneum, or the pulmonary ligament? The answer is the endothoracic fascia and the intercostal muscles. The fascia is the loose layer against the pleura. Beyond the muscles lie the ribs and their periosteum.

Question 6335. A constructed illustration: after a chest infection, thick pus collects in one pleural cavity and the lung on that side cannot expand. What is that collection called, and which layer lines it? The answer is an empyema of the pleura, lined by the parietal pleura. The pus lies between the two pleural layers. Treatment belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 6336. A described specimen shows a lung encased in a thick gray-white sheet of tumor that follows the surface and fills the fissures, with the lung tissue compressed inside. From which layer did this tumor arise? The answer is the pleura, most often the parietal layer. Such a tumor is called a mesothelioma. The lung is compressed rather than invaded at first.

Question 6337. Which of these pairs belongs together: the costal pleura with the phrenic nerve, the mediastinal pleura with the intercostal nerves, the central diaphragmatic pleura with the phrenic nerve, or the visceral pleura with the intercostal nerves? The answer is the central diaphragmatic pleura with the phrenic nerve. The costal pleura is supplied by the intercostal nerves and the visceral pleura by autonomic nerves.

Question 6338. Which part of the parietal pleura is applied to the inner surface of the ribs and the intercostal spaces: the costal part, the mediastinal part, the cervical part, or the diaphragmatic part? The answer is the costal part. It is the largest part and forms the outer wall of the pleural cavity. It continues over the diaphragm below as the diaphragmatic pleura.

Question 6339. Which of the two layers of pleura is sensitive: the visceral layer, the parietal layer, both, or neither? The answer is the parietal layer. It is supplied by somatic nerves and is richly sensitive. The visceral layer carries autonomic nerves and cannot feel a cut.

Question 6340. Where does the pleural reflection lie in relation to the twelfth rib near the vertebral column? The answer is that it crosses the twelfth rib. The pleura reaches its lowest point at the back, close to the spine. The lung above it ends about two ribs higher.

Question 6341. Name two arteries that supply the parietal pleura. The answer is the intercostal arteries and the internal thoracic artery. The intercostal arteries reach the costal pleura from behind. The internal thoracic artery and its branches supply the pleura near the front of the chest.

Question 6342. Which statement about the pleura of the right lung is correct: it has a horizontal fissure that the pleura follows, it has a cardiac notch, it has only one fissure, or it lacks a costomediastinal recess? The answer is that it has a horizontal fissure that the pleura follows. The right lung has three lobes and two fissures. The cardiac notch belongs to the left lung.

Question 6343. A described computed tomography scan of the chest shows two thin-walled spaces, one on each side of a central mass, each containing lung tissue. What are these spaces, and what lines them? The answer is the pleural cavities, each lined by parietal pleura and closed by visceral pleura over the lung. The central mass is the mediastinum, holding the heart and the great vessels.

Question 6344. A constructed illustration: after a rib fracture, air escapes from a torn lung into the tissues of the chest wall and the neck, and crackling can be felt under the skin. Which layer has the air entered? The answer is the subcutaneous tissue of the chest wall. The air has also entered the pleural cavity first. The crackling is called subcutaneous emphysema.

Question 6345. True or false: the visceral pleura can be stripped from the lung surface as a complete sheet, leaving the lung intact. The answer is false. It is fused to the lung and sends fine septa into the tissue, so it tears when pulled away. Only the parietal pleura can be separated from the chest wall with ease.

Question 6346. What is the name given to the sleeve of pleura that surrounds the structures entering and leaving the lung? The answer is the pleural sleeve at the root of the lung. It is the point where the parietal pleura becomes the visceral layer. The pulmonary ligament continues downward from it.

Question 6347. Which vessels lie immediately in front of the cervical pleura as it rises into the root of the neck? The answer is the subclavian artery and vein. They cross the cupola in front of it and leave a groove on its surface. The first rib and the suprapleural membrane lie below and above it.

Question 6348. Which part of the parietal pleura lies directly against the fibrous pericardium: the costal part, the mediastinal part, the cervical part, or the diaphragmatic part? The answer is the mediastinal part. On the left it covers most of the side of the pericardium. The phrenic nerve runs down between the pericardium and that pleura.

Question 6349. In a described dissection, a needle passes through the chest wall in the midaxillary line just above the seventh rib. Which structures has it crossed last before entering the pleural cavity? The answer is the intercostal muscle and the endothoracic fascia. The parietal pleura lies immediately inside them. A needle placed much lower risks the costodiaphragmatic recess and the abdomen below.

Question 6350. Disease of the lung itself causes no sharp pain, while disease of the chest wall pleura is painful. Which single difference explains this: the thickness of the two layers, their nerve supply, their distance from the lung, or their blood supply? The answer is their nerve supply. The parietal pleura carries somatic nerves that convey sharp pain. The visceral pleura carries only autonomic nerves, which do not.

Question 6351. Which muscle forms the floor of the thoracic cavity and the roof of the abdominal cavity? The answer is the diaphragm. It is a dome-shaped sheet of muscle with a central tendon. Its fibers arise from the sternum, the lower ribs and the upper lumbar vertebrae, and all of them insert into that tendon.

Question 6352. Which part of the sternum gives origin to the sternal part of the diaphragm? The answer is the xiphoid process. Two small muscular slips arise from its back and run up to the central tendon. They leave a small gap on each side, the sternocostal triangle.

Question 6353. Which of these is not an origin of the diaphragm: the xiphoid process, the inner surfaces of the lower six ribs, the bodies of the upper lumbar vertebrae, or the medial border of the clavicle? The answer is the medial border of the clavicle. The clavicle gives origin to no part of the diaphragm. The other three are the sternal, costal and lumbar origins.

Question 6354. Which crus of the diaphragm is longer and reaches the higher lumbar vertebrae: the right, the left, both equally, or neither? The answer is the right crus. It arises from the bodies of the upper three lumbar vertebrae and their discs. The left crus arises from the upper two and is shorter.

Question 6355. Which structures pass through the aortic opening of the diaphragm: the aorta and the esophagus, the aorta, the thoracic duct and the azygos vein, the inferior vena cava only, or the esophagus and the vagus nerves? The answer is the aorta, the thoracic duct and the azygos vein. The opening lies at the level of the twelfth thoracic vertebra, between the two crura.

Question 6356. A described chest radiograph shows two curved domes forming the lower border of the lung fields, with the right one lying higher than the left. What are these shadows, and why is the right one higher? The answer is the two halves of the diaphragm, the right raised by the liver beneath it. Both domes move down on inspiration.

Question 6357. A constructed illustration: a person has repeated sudden involuntary contractions of the diaphragm followed by closure of the glottis. What is this, and which nerve carries the irritation? The answer is hiccups, and the irritation travels in the phrenic nerve. It often follows a full stomach or a sudden change of temperature.

Question 6358. Which structures pass through the esophageal opening of the diaphragm: the esophagus alone, the esophagus and the thoracic duct, the esophagus and the two vagal trunks, or the esophagus and the aorta? The answer is the esophagus and the two vagal trunks. Small esophageal branches of the left gastric vessels and lymphatics travel with them. The opening lies at the level of the tenth thoracic vertebra.

Question 6359. In which part of the diaphragm does the opening for the inferior vena cava lie? The answer is the central tendon. The opening lies a little to the right of the midline, at the level of the eighth thoracic vertebra. The inferior vena cava is bound to its margins.

Question 6360. Spoken from above downward, in what order do the three great openings of the diaphragm lie? The answer is the caval opening first, then the esophageal opening, then the aortic opening. The vena cava passes at the eighth thoracic level, the esophagus at the tenth and the aorta at the twelfth. The caval opening also lies furthest forward.

Question 6361. Which structures pierce the muscular crura of the diaphragm rather than passing through a named opening: the splanchnic nerves, the thoracic duct, the azygos vein, or the inferior vena cava? The answer is the splanchnic nerves. The greater, lesser and least splanchnic nerves pass through the crura on each side. The thoracic duct and the azygos vein pass through the aortic opening instead.

Question 6362. A described sagittal section through the trunk shows three gaps in the diaphragm, one in front within the tendon, one in the middle of the muscle, and one at the back behind the muscle. Which structures pass through the front and the back gaps? The answer is the inferior vena cava in front and the aorta behind. The middle gap carries the esophagus and the vagal trunks.

Question 6363. Which of these pairs belongs together: the caval opening with the tenth thoracic vertebra, the esophageal opening with the eighth, the aortic opening with the twelfth, or the caval opening with the twelfth? The answer is the aortic opening with the twelfth thoracic vertebra. The caval opening lies at the eighth level and the esophageal opening at the tenth.

Question 6364. What is the name of the fibrous arch that joins the two crura of the diaphragm in the midline above the aorta? The answer is the median arcuate ligament. The aorta passes behind it to enter the abdomen. The two crura and this arch together form the aortic opening.

Question 6365. Over which muscle does the medial arcuate ligament of the diaphragm arch? The answer is the psoas major. The ligament is a thickening of the fascia over that muscle. It runs from the body of the first lumbar vertebra to its transverse process.

Question 6366. Over which muscle does the lateral arcuate ligament of the diaphragm arch? The answer is the quadratus lumborum. It runs from the transverse process of the first lumbar vertebra to the twelfth rib. The subcostal nerve and vessels pass behind it on their way to the abdominal wall.

Question 6367. Which nerve supplies the motor power of the diaphragm? The answer is the phrenic nerve. There is one on each side, and together they supply the whole muscular sheet. It reaches the muscle from above, descending through the neck and then through the chest. The intercostal nerves supply only sensation to the outer part of the muscle.

Question 6368. The outer part of the diaphragm receives sensory fibers from which nerves: the phrenic nerve only, the lower intercostal nerves, the vagus nerve, or the sympathetic trunk? The answer is the lower intercostal nerves. They supply the peripheral rim of the muscle. The phrenic nerve supplies sensation to the central part.

Question 6369. True or false: the phrenic nerve is the only nerve that supplies the diaphragm, both for movement and for sensation. The answer is false. The phrenic nerve supplies all of the movement and the sensation of the central part. The outer rim also receives sensory fibers from the lower intercostal nerves.

Question 6370. Name the three parts of the origin of the diaphragm, spoken as a list. The answer is the sternal part, the costal part and the lumbar part. The sternal part arises from the xiphoid process, the costal part from the lower six ribs and their cartilages, and the lumbar part from the crura and the arcuate ligaments.

Question 6371. A described transverse section through the lower chest shows the esophagus in the midline with a nerve trunk on each side of it, and the aorta behind and to the left of the midline. At which level was the section taken? The answer is the level of the tenth thoracic vertebra, where the esophagus passes through the diaphragm. The two nerve trunks are the vagal trunks.

Question 6372. Which vessels pass through the small gap between the sternal and costal parts of the diaphragm: the internal thoracic artery, the superior epigastric artery, the musculophrenic artery, or the inferior epigastric artery? The answer is the superior epigastric artery. It continues the internal thoracic artery downward into the abdominal wall. That gap is the sternocostal triangle.

Question 6373. A constructed illustration: after an operation in the lower neck, one half of the diaphragm no longer descends on inspiration. Which nerve has been injured, and where does it arise? The answer is the phrenic nerve, which arises from the third, fourth and fifth cervical segments. It crosses the neck in front of the scalenus anterior muscle, where it is exposed.

Question 6374. From which spinal segments does the phrenic nerve arise? The answer is the third, fourth and fifth cervical segments. Its fibers leave the neck high up and travel a long way down to the diaphragm. This long course explains why the nerve is at risk in operations on the neck.

Question 6375. Does the left phrenic nerve pass through the same opening as the right one? The answer is no. The right phrenic nerve passes through the opening for the inferior vena cava in the central tendon. The left phrenic nerve pierces the muscular part of the left dome on its own.

Question 6376. Through which opening does the right phrenic nerve reach the abdomen: the caval opening, the esophageal opening, the aortic opening, or the sternocostal triangle? The answer is the caval opening. It travels with the inferior vena cava through the central tendon. The left phrenic nerve pierces the left dome separately.

Question 6377. A described fluoroscopic study shows one dome of the diaphragm moving upward while the other moves downward when the person sniffs sharply. What does this indicate? The answer is paralysis of the dome that rises. A paralyzed hemidiaphragm is pushed up by the pressure in the abdomen when the healthy side contracts. The healthy dome descends as it should.

Question 6378. Which of these pairs belongs together: the posterolateral defect of the diaphragm with the right side, the parasternal defect with the left side, the pleuroperitoneal canal with the posterolateral defect, or the sternocostal triangle with the aortic opening? The answer is the pleuroperitoneal canal with the posterolateral defect. That defect is more common on the left.

Question 6379. What effect does the contraction of the costal part of the diaphragm have on the lower ribs: it raises them, it lowers them, it pulls them inward, or it leaves them unchanged? The answer is that it raises them. With the central tendon fixed, the fibers pull the ribs upward and outward. This widens the chest in its transverse diameter.

Question 6380. A constructed illustration: a newborn baby has difficulty breathing, and a radiograph shows loops of intestine in the left side of the chest, with the heart pushed to the right. Which developmental defect is illustrated? The answer is a posterolateral defect of the diaphragm, through the pleuroperitoneal canal. The abdominal contents have entered the chest.

Question 6381. True or false: the aorta pierces the muscular part of the diaphragm and carries a sleeve of muscle with it. The answer is false. The aorta passes behind the diaphragm, between the two crura, and does not pierce its muscle. The opening lies behind the muscle, at the level of the twelfth thoracic vertebra.

Question 6382. A described dissection of the diaphragm from the abdominal side shows a broad tendon in the middle, radiating muscle fibers around it, and two muscular legs descending behind. What are the three structures shown? The answer is the central tendon, the muscular part and the crura. The tendon lies in the middle, and the crura descend to the upper lumbar vertebrae.

Question 6383. Which structure passes behind the lateral arcuate ligament of the diaphragm: the sympathetic trunk, the subcostal nerve, the splanchnic nerves, or the superior epigastric artery? The answer is the subcostal nerve. It runs behind the ligament on its way to the abdominal wall. The sympathetic trunk passes behind the medial arcuate ligament instead.

Question 6384. To which structure above is the central tendon of the diaphragm fused? The answer is the fibrous pericardium. The two are united, so the heart moves with the diaphragm. This is one reason the heart descends a little on deep inspiration. The serous pericardium inside is free, so the heart can still beat within its fibrous sac.

Question 6385. Into which structure do all the muscle fibers of the diaphragm insert? The answer is the central tendon. It is a thin, strong, aponeurotic sheet shaped like a clover leaf. The inferior vena cava passes through it, a little to the right of the midline.

Question 6386. Which muscle is the chief muscle of quiet inspiration: the external intercostal, the diaphragm, the rectus abdominis, or the serratus anterior? The answer is the diaphragm. Its contraction lowers the domes and increases the vertical diameter of the chest. The external intercostals assist by raising the ribs.

Question 6387. A described pair of chest radiographs shows the domes of the diaphragm at a low level, with the ribs sloping steeply upward in the second film. What does the second film show? The answer is the chest at full inspiration. The diaphragm has descended and the ribs have been raised and everted. In the first film the chest was at rest.

Question 6388. A constructed illustration: a collection of pus lies between the right dome of the diaphragm and the liver, and the person has pain at the tip of the right shoulder. Why is the pain felt there? The answer is that the phrenic nerve supplies the central part of the diaphragm. The nerve comes from the third, fourth and fifth cervical segments, the same segments as the skin over the shoulder.

Question 6389. Why does the phrenic nerve arise from the cervical spinal cord: the muscle develops in the neck and drags its nerve down, the nerve branches from the vagus, the muscle grows from the lumbar somites, or the nerve descends from the chest? The answer is that the muscle develops in the neck and carries its nerve supply down as it migrates. This is a developmental explanation for an adult arrangement.

Question 6390. Which of these pairs belongs together: the inferior vena cava with the aortic opening, the esophagus with the caval opening, the aorta with the aortic opening, or the thoracic duct with the caval opening? The answer is the aorta with the aortic opening. The thoracic duct and the azygos vein pass through the same opening in its company.

Question 6391. Which of these structures passes through the diaphragm without using any of its three named openings: the esophagus, the inferior vena cava, the sympathetic trunk, or the thoracic duct? The answer is the sympathetic trunk. It slips behind the medial arcuate ligament. The thoracic duct uses the aortic opening.

Question 6392. What are the two muscular legs called that descend from the diaphragm to the upper lumbar vertebrae? The answer is the crura. The right crus is the longer of the two. Each crus is attached to the bodies of the lumbar vertebrae and to the discs between them, and together they bound the aortic opening behind.

Question 6393. Within the central tendon, on which side of the midline does the opening for the inferior vena cava lie, and where does it lie in relation to the muscular part of the diaphragm? The answer is that it lies to the right of the midline and within the tendon, above the muscle. The vena cava runs almost vertically to reach it. The esophageal opening lies behind and below it.

Question 6394. A constructed illustration: in an older adult, part of the stomach lies beside the esophagus in the chest, having rolled up through the esophageal opening, while the junction of the esophagus and the stomach remains below it. Which kind of hernia is illustrated? The answer is a paraesophageal hernia. In the sliding type the junction itself moves upward instead.

Question 6395. Which statement about the openings of the diaphragm explains why the esophageal opening is the one most likely to widen with age: it lies in the central tendon, it lies between the crura in muscular tissue that can stretch, it lies behind the muscle, or it is bounded by bone? The answer is that it lies between the crura in muscular tissue that can stretch.

Question 6396. A described chest radiograph shows the right dome of the diaphragm much higher than the left, and on screening it rises further on inspiration while the left descends. Which side is paralyzed, and in which direction does it move? The answer is the right dome, moving paradoxically upward. The left dome is working normally.

Question 6397. A described specimen of the diaphragm viewed from below shows a wide central sheet, radiating muscle fibers, and three gaps. Which gap lies furthest to the right, and which openings are bounded by muscle? The answer is that the caval opening lies furthest to the right. The esophageal and aortic openings are bounded by muscle and by the crura between them.

Question 6398. Which organ lies immediately below the right dome of the diaphragm and molds it into a higher curve: the stomach, the liver, the spleen, or the left kidney? The answer is the liver. The right dome is molded over its upper surface and therefore sits higher than the left one. The stomach and the spleen lie below the left dome.

Question 6399. The diaphragm develops from several sources. Which part of it develops from the septum transversum: the crura, the central tendon, the costal part, or the arcuate ligaments? The answer is the central tendon. The pleuroperitoneal membranes form much of the rest of the muscle, and the crura grow from the dorsal mesentery of the esophagus.

Question 6400. Irritation of the outer rim of the diaphragm is felt in the chest wall on the same side, while irritation of the central part is felt at the shoulder. What single fact explains the difference? The answer is that the two regions have different sensory nerves. The outer rim is supplied by the lower intercostal nerves, which supply the chest wall too. The central part is supplied by the phrenic nerve.

To summarize, the pleura is a closed serous sac with a visceral layer fused to the lung and a parietal layer named costal, mediastinal, diaphragmatic and cervical, and the thin film of fluid between the layers lets them slide. Only the parietal layer is sensitive, supplied by the intercostal nerves over the chest wall and by the phrenic nerve over the mediastinum and the center of the diaphragm, which is why irritation there is felt at the shoulder tip. Fluid gathers first in the costodiaphragmatic recess, and the pleural reflection lies about two ribs below the lung. The diaphragm arises from the xiphoid process, the lower six ribs and the upper lumbar vertebrae, and inserts into the central tendon, with the caval opening at the eighth thoracic level, the esophageal opening at the tenth and the aortic opening at the twelfth. Its phrenic nerve comes from the third, fourth and fifth cervical segments and descends a long way, and the crura, the arcuate ligaments and the sternocostal triangle explain where the remaining structures cross.
