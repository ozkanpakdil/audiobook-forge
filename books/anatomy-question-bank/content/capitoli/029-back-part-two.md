# Chapter 29 — Back, Part Two

This chapter finishes the muscles of the back and then crosses the chest wall, and it deals with the two groups that move the trunk and the ribs. It begins with the transversospinalis group, the deep sheet of short oblique muscles that runs from a transverse process below to a spinous process above, and works through the semispinalis, the multifidus and the rotatores. It then turns to the pectoral muscles, the pectoralis major, the pectoralis minor, the subclavius and the serratus anterior, and it ends with the intercostal muscles, their three layers and the neurovascular bundle they enclose. It holds one hundred questions, and each one gives its answer immediately after the question and then explains why that answer is right, so nothing has to be held in mind until the end. This is educational material, and it supports rather than replaces an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 2801. Which group of deep muscles of the back takes its name from its two attachments, running from a transverse process below to a spinous process above, and lies in the gutter beside the vertebral spines? The answer is the transversospinalis group. Its fibers pass upward and medially, and it lies deep to the erector spinae, filling the groove between the transverse processes and the spines. It is arranged in three layers.

Question 2802. Which three muscles make up the transversospinalis group: the splenius, the erector spinae and the latissimus dorsi, the semispinalis, the multifidus and the rotatores, the trapezius, the rhomboid muscles and the levator scapulae, or the interspinales, the intertransversarii and the levatores costarum? The answer is the semispinalis, the multifidus and the rotatores. They form three layers from superficial to deep, and each deeper layer spans fewer vertebral levels than the one above it.

Question 2803. A described dissection of the neck shows the largest muscle of the semispinalis group, a thick rounded band running from the upper thoracic vertebrae to the base of the skull between the nuchal lines. Which muscle is it? The answer is the semispinalis capitis. It forms the rounded bulge felt below the external occipital protuberance, and the greater occipital nerve pierces it.

Question 2804. A described specimen shows a series of short muscles in the thoracic region, each running from the transverse process of one vertebra to the spinous process of the vertebra one or two levels above it. Which muscles are shown, and what is their action? The answer is the rotatores. They are best developed in the thoracic region and assist in turning the vertebral column to the opposite side.

Question 2805. Which muscle lies immediately deep to the erector spinae in the lumbar region, filling the gutter on each side of the spinous processes and taking origin from the mammillary processes of the lumbar vertebrae? The answer is the multifidus. It lies between the erector spinae behind it and the vertebral laminae in front of it, and the rotatores lie deeper still.

Question 2806. From which bony points does the lumbar multifidus take origin: the transverse processes of the cervical vertebrae alone, the mammillary processes of the lumbar vertebrae together with the sacrum and the hip bone, the bodies of the thoracic vertebrae, or the costal cartilages of the lower ribs? The answer is the mammillary processes of the lumbar vertebrae together with the sacrum and the hip bone. Its fibers then pass upward and medially.

Question 2807. A described illustration: after a long period of poor posture and repeated lifting, a person has wasting of the deep muscle beside the lumbar spines, and the lower back feels unstable on movement. Which muscle is described, and what is its normal action? The answer is the multifidus, which steadies and extends the vertebral column and is the chief stabilizer of the lumbar segments.

Question 2808. True or false: the rotatores are best developed in the thoracic region of the vertebral column. The answer is true. The thoracic part of the column carries the most rotatores, where each bundle spans one or two segments. In the lumbar region the multifidus takes their place as the dominant deep muscle of the back.

Question 2809. Which part of the semispinalis group arises from the transverse processes of the upper thoracic vertebrae and ends on the spinous processes of the second to the fifth cervical vertebrae? The answer is the semispinalis cervicis. It lies deep to the semispinalis capitis, and it extends the cervical part of the vertebral column and turns it to the opposite side.

Question 2810. Which of these pairs belongs together: the multifidus with the mammillary processes of the lumbar vertebrae, the semispinalis capitis with the coracoid process, the rotatores with the mastoid process, or the semispinalis cervicis with the first rib? The answer is the multifidus with the mammillary processes of the lumbar vertebrae. The other three attachments belong to muscles of the shoulder girdle and the neck.

Question 2811. When the semispinalis and the multifidus contract on one side only, toward which side do they turn the face and the trunk: the same side as the contraction, the opposite side, either side equally, or neither side, because they do not rotate the column at all? The answer is the opposite side. They run upward and medially, so they pull the spines toward the far side.

Question 2812. Which muscle lies immediately deep to the semispinalis capitis as that muscle passes from the upper thoracic vertebrae to the base of the skull? The answer is the semispinalis cervicis. It lies in front of and below the capitis part in the back of the neck, and the multifidus lies deeper still. All three belong to the same group.

Question 2813. A described section through the back of the neck shows a large muscle pierced in its lower part by a nerve that then runs upward over the scalp toward the top of the head. Which muscle and which nerve are described? The answer is the semispinalis capitis and the greater occipital nerve. That nerve is the medial branch of the posterior ramus of the second cervical spinal nerve.

Question 2814. Which part of the transversospinalis group is best developed in the lumbar region, where it forms the largest mass of the deep muscles of the lower back and covers the laminae on each side? The answer is the multifidus. Its lumbar fibers are the thickest in the group, and they take origin from the sacrum and the mammillary processes.

Question 2815. How many vertebral levels does a typical fascicle of the multifidus cross: one level, two to about four levels, six to eight levels, or the whole length of the vertebral column? The answer is two to about four levels. The rotatores cross only one or two levels, and the semispinalis crosses four to six, so the deeper the muscle the shorter its span.

Question 2816. Name two muscles of the transversospinalis group that extend the vertebral column when both of their sides act together. The answer is the multifidus and the semispinalis. Both of them also turn the column to the opposite side when one side acts, and they are supplied by the posterior rami of the spinal nerves.

Question 2817. A described illustration: a person bends forward before lifting a heavy load and feels a sudden deep pain beside the lumbar spines, with spasm of the muscle there. Which muscle is most likely strained, and where does it attach below? The answer is the multifidus, which attaches to the sacrum and the mammillary processes of the lumbar vertebrae.

Question 2818. In a transverse section through the lumbar region, the transversospinalis group occupies a defined zone. Between which two structures does the multifidus lie, and where does it sit relative to the spinous process? The answer is that it lies between the erector spinae behind and the vertebral laminae in front, in the gutter lateral to the spinous process.

Question 2819. Which statement about the nerve supply of the deep muscles of the back is correct: the transversospinalis group is supplied by the anterior rami of the spinal nerves, by the posterior rami, by the sympathetic trunk, or by the accessory nerve? The answer is the posterior rami. Each posterior ramus divides into branches and supplies the deep muscles of its own segment.

Question 2820. A described dissection shows the semispinalis thoracis as a thin sheet passing from the transverse processes of the lower thoracic vertebrae to the spinous processes of the upper thoracic and lower cervical vertebrae. How many segments do its fibers span, and what is its action? The answer is four to six segments, and it extends the thoracic part of the column.

Question 2821. Which muscle of the transversospinalis group lies deepest and is the shortest of the three, running from one transverse process to the spinous process of the vertebra one or two levels above, and is thickest in the thoracic region? The answer is the rotatores. They extend the column and are richly supplied with receptors for position sense.

Question 2822. Which muscle of the deep back takes origin from the transverse processes of the vertebrae from the sacrum upward and inserts on the spinous process of a vertebra two to four levels above, so that its bundles overlap like tiles? The answer is the multifidus. It is the middle layer of the transversospinalis group and the strongest of the three.

Question 2823. Which structure lies immediately superficial to the transversospinalis group in the back, forming the thick longitudinal ridge felt on either side of the midline? The answer is the erector spinae. The transversospinalis group lies in the gutter deep to it, and the rotatores form the deepest layer of the back muscles.

Question 2824. True or false: the greater occipital nerve is the medial branch of the posterior ramus of the second cervical spinal nerve, and it pierces the semispinalis capitis on its way to the scalp. The answer is true. The nerve runs upward through the muscle and then supplies the skin of the back of the head. Its compression is a described cause of headache.

Question 2825. A described illustration: in a person with long-standing lumbar pain, the deep muscle beside the spines is found to be pale and wasted, with fatty tissue replacing its fibers on one side. Which muscle is described, and why does the finding matter? The answer is the multifidus, whose wasting leaves the lumbar segments less steady and is a described reason for pain to persist.

Question 2826. A described illustration: a needle placed beside the lumbar spines for a local block is said to lie where the posterior ramus of a spinal nerve runs. Which muscles does that ramus supply as it passes? The answer is the deep muscles of the back, including the multifidus and the rotatores. The block itself belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 2827. Which part of the transversospinalis group forms the rounded bulge that can be felt at the back of the neck just below the occiput, and what is its main action? The answer is the semispinalis capitis, which extends the head and turns the face to the opposite side. The greater occipital nerve pierces it on its course to the scalp.

Question 2828. A described radiograph of the lumbar spine shows the spinous processes in a neat row down the midline, with a smooth gutter on each side of them. Which deep muscle fills those gutters, and does the radiograph show the muscle itself? The answer is the multifidus. The film shows the bone that the muscle lies against, not the muscle.

Question 2829. Which of these muscles does not belong to the transversospinalis group: the semispinalis capitis, the multifidus, the rotatores, or the spinalis thoracis? The answer is the spinalis thoracis, which belongs to the erector spinae. It lies closest to the spinous processes in the erector mass and runs between the lower thoracic and upper thoracic spines.

Question 2830. Name the three layers of the transversospinalis group in order from superficial to deep, and say how many vertebral levels each typically crosses. The answer is the semispinalis, the multifidus and the rotatores. The semispinalis crosses four to six levels, the multifidus two to four, and the rotatores only one or two.

Question 2831. Which of these pairs belongs together: the rotatores with the thoracic region, the multifidus with the cervical region only, the semispinalis capitis with the lumbar region, or the transversospinalis group with the anterior rami of the spinal nerves? The answer is the rotatores with the thoracic region, where they are best developed and most numerous.

Question 2832. Which arteries supply the deep muscles of the back: the anterior intercostal arteries, the dorsal branches of the posterior intercostal and lumbar arteries, the internal thoracic arteries, or the superior epigastric arteries? The answer is the dorsal branches of the posterior intercostal and lumbar arteries. They follow the posterior rami of the spinal nerves into the muscles they supply.

Question 2833. Which muscle lies immediately medial to the multifidus in the back, forming a slender band that runs between the spinous processes of adjacent vertebrae? The answer is the spinalis, the medial column of the erector spinae. It lies superficial to the multifidus and is best developed in the thoracic region, not in the lumbar region.

Question 2834. When the transversospinalis muscles of one side contract alone, to which side do they bend the trunk, and what rotation do they add? The answer is that they bend the trunk to the same side and turn it to the opposite side. Extension of the column is the effect when both sides act together, as in standing upright.

Question 2835. Which thick sheet of fibrous tissue encloses the erector spinae and the transversospinalis group in the lower back and also gives attachment to the latissimus dorsi and the gluteal muscles? The answer is the thoracolumbar fascia. It forms a strong sleeve around the deep muscles and is a landmark in the surgery of the lumbar region.

Question 2836. A described magnetic resonance image of the lumbar region shows a band of muscle lying directly against the vertebral laminae on each side, much thinner on the left than on the right. Which muscle is shown, and why does its bulk matter? The answer is the multifidus, whose thickness is a described marker of segmental steadiness in the lower back.

Question 2837. A described illustration: during an operation on the lower back, a small nerve is cut as it crosses the lamina, and the muscle beside the spine on that side later wastes. Which nerve has been divided, and which muscle has lost its supply? The answer is a posterior ramus of a spinal nerve, and the multifidus of that segment.

Question 2838. Which of these muscles both extends the vertebral column and turns it to the opposite side: the iliocostalis, the longissimus, the multifidus, or the latissimus dorsi? The answer is the multifidus. The iliocostalis and the longissimus bend the column to the same side, and the latissimus dorsi moves the arm and not the column.

Question 2839. True or false: the transversospinalis group lies deep to the erector spinae and is therefore not visible until the erector spinae has been removed. The answer is true. The group occupies the gutter between the transverse processes and the spines, and the long mass of the erector spinae covers it from the sacrum to the skull.

Question 2840. Which layer of the transversospinalis group lies immediately deep to the semispinalis and immediately superficial to the rotatores, and is the thickest of the three in the lumbar region? The answer is the multifidus. It fills the gutter beside the spines and sends its bundles upward and medially across two to four segments before they end.

Question 2841. Which muscle of the deep back is described in the brief as the chief stabilizer of adjacent vertebrae, holding one segment steady against the next during movement? The answer is the multifidus, with the rotatores assisting it. These muscles are short, span few segments, and are among the most richly innervated muscles in the body.

Question 2842. A described dissection after removal of the erector spinae shows a fan of muscle arising from the sacrum, the hip bone and the lumbar mammillary processes and reaching upward to the thoracic and cervical spines, with the longest bundles in the middle of the fan. Which muscle is shown? The answer is the multifidus.

Question 2843. Which structure lies immediately anterior to the multifidus in the lumbar region, separating it from the vertebral canal and the spinal cord within it? The answer is the vertebral lamina, together with the ligamentum flavum that joins one lamina to the next. The thoracolumbar fascia and the latissimus dorsi lie behind the muscle instead.

Question 2844. Which of these is the main postural role of the transversospinalis group: to swing the arm, to hold adjacent vertebrae steady against one another, to raise the ribs in inspiration, or to bend the head forward? The answer is to hold adjacent vertebrae steady. That is why the group is rich in position-sensing receptors and is arranged in short segmental bundles.

Question 2845. Which muscle of the transversospinalis group is the most superficial of its three layers and the only one whose fibers reach as high as the skull? The answer is the semispinalis, whose capitis part ends on the occipital bone between the nuchal lines. The multifidus reaches only the cervical spines, and the rotatores stop within a segment or two.

Question 2846. A described illustration: after a deep injury to the back, a person reports an area of numbness in a narrow strip of skin beside the spine, with weakness of the deep muscles on the same side. Which nerve has been injured, and what is its normal territory? The answer is a posterior ramus of a spinal nerve, which supplies the deep back muscles and the overlying skin of that segment.

Question 2847. Which layer of the transversospinalis group is poorly developed or absent in the lumbar region, being best seen in the thoracic and cervical parts of the column: the semispinalis, the multifidus, the rotatores brevis, or the rotatores longi? The answer is the semispinalis, which is replaced in the lower back by the bulk of the multifidus.

Question 2848. A described dissection of the back of the neck shows three muscles of the semispinalis group lying one behind another, the largest reaching the skull and the smallest ending on the cervical spines. Which three muscles are shown, and what is the shared direction of their fibers? The answer is the semispinalis capitis, cervicis and thoracis, all running upward and medially.

Question 2849. Which statement about the deep muscles of the back is correct: they are supplied by the anterior rami of the spinal nerves, they are arranged in layers that span progressively fewer segments as they lie deeper, they lie superficial to the latissimus dorsi, or they all insert on the ribs? The answer is that they span fewer segments as they lie deeper, ending with the rotatores.

Question 2850. Which muscle of the transversospinalis group is the shortest and spans only the interval between one vertebra and the vertebra above it: the semispinalis, the multifidus, the rotatores brevis, or the erector spinae? The answer is the rotatores brevis. Its longer companion, the rotatores longi, spans two levels and is also confined mostly to the thoracic region.

Question 2851. The large fan-shaped muscle of the front of the chest has two heads and ends on the upper part of the humerus. On which part of the humerus does its tendon insert? The answer is the lateral lip of the intertubercular sulcus. The muscle forms the anterior fold of the armpit and gives the chest its broad outline.

Question 2852. Which two parts make up the pectoralis major: a clavicular head and a sternocostal head, a costal head and a vertebral head, a humeral head and a scapular head, or a superficial head and a deep head? The answer is a clavicular head and a sternocostal head. The sternocostal part also takes fibers from the sheath of the rectus abdominis below.

Question 2853. Which small triangular muscle of the chest lies deep to the pectoralis major, arises from the third, fourth and fifth ribs near their cartilages, and inserts on the coracoid process of the scapula? The answer is the pectoralis minor. It draws the scapula forward and downward, and it also tilts the lower angle of the scapula backward.

Question 2854. Which part of the axillary artery lies immediately behind the pectoralis minor, and what does that relation define for the surgeon? The answer is the second part of the artery. The muscle divides the artery into three parts, and the cords of the brachial plexus are named from their position around that second part.

Question 2855. A described dissection of the front of the chest shows a thick muscle with two heads, one from the clavicle and one from the sternum and the upper costal cartilages, ending in a flat tendon that twists onto the humerus. Which muscle is shown, and which groove receives the tendon? The answer is the pectoralis major, whose tendon enters the intertubercular sulcus.

Question 2856. Which nerves supply the pectoralis major: the long thoracic nerve alone, the lateral and medial pectoral nerves, the thoracodorsal nerve, or the dorsal scapular nerve? The answer is the lateral and medial pectoral nerves. The lateral pectoral nerve supplies the clavicular part, and the medial pectoral nerve reaches the sternocostal part.

Question 2857. A described illustration: after an operation in the armpit, the medial border of the scapula stands away from the chest wall when the person pushes the hand against a wall. Which nerve has been injured, and which muscle has lost its supply? The answer is the long thoracic nerve and the serratus anterior, whose slips no longer hold the scapula flat.

Question 2858. True or false: when the scapula is held fixed, the pectoralis minor can help to raise the ribs during inspiration. The answer is true. With the shoulder girdle held steady, the muscle pulls upward on the third, fourth and fifth ribs, so it is counted among the accessory muscles of respiration.

Question 2859. Which small muscle lies in the groove on the under surface of the clavicle, running from the first rib and its cartilage to the bone above it, and what is its action? The answer is the subclavius. It steadies the clavicle and draws it downward, and it is supplied by the nerve to the subclavius from the upper trunk of the brachial plexus.

Question 2860. Which of these pairs belongs together: the serratus anterior with the long thoracic nerve, the pectoralis major with the thoracodorsal nerve, the subclavius with the accessory nerve, or the pectoralis minor with the dorsal scapular nerve? The answer is the serratus anterior with the long thoracic nerve, which runs down on its outer surface and is at risk in operations on the armpit.

Question 2861. Which nerve passes through the pectoralis minor on its way to the pectoralis major: the lateral pectoral nerve, the medial pectoral nerve, the long thoracic nerve, or the intercostobrachial nerve? The answer is the medial pectoral nerve, which pierces the muscle before ending in the deep surface of the pectoralis major.

Question 2862. A described dissection shows a muscle with a series of finger-like slips from the outer surfaces of the upper ribs, passing backward around the chest wall to the deep surface of the scapula. Which muscle is described, and what is its main action? The answer is the serratus anterior, which pulls the scapula forward and rotates it so that the arm can be raised.

Question 2863. A described section through the chest wall below the armpit shows a muscle lying directly on the ribs, covered in front by the scapula and behind by the latissimus dorsi. Which muscle is shown, and which nerve runs on its surface? The answer is the serratus anterior, with the long thoracic nerve running down on it.

Question 2864. Which of these structures pierces the clavipectoral fascia: the cephalic vein, the median cubital vein, the axillary nerve, or the radial nerve? The answer is the cephalic vein. It pierces the fascia just below the clavicle to join the axillary vein, and it is accompanied through the same sheet by the thoracoacromial artery and its branches.

Question 2865. Name two muscles that pull the scapula forward on the chest wall, and say which of them is the more powerful. The answer is the serratus anterior and the pectoralis minor. The serratus anterior is the stronger of the two, and the pectoralis minor also tilts the scapula so that its lower angle swings backward.

Question 2866. Which statement about the clavicular head of the pectoralis major is correct: it extends the arm at the shoulder, it flexes the arm at the shoulder, it rotates the arm laterally, or it raises the scapula? The answer is that it flexes the arm at the shoulder. The sternocostal part instead brings the arm down and across the chest.

Question 2867. Which structure lies immediately anterior to the second part of the axillary artery and separates that part from the skin of the chest wall? The answer is the pectoralis minor. The first part of the artery lies above the muscle, and the third part lies below and lateral to it, where the arm begins.

Question 2868. In the chest wall, the pectoralis minor and the serratus anterior lie in different planes. How are the two muscles placed relative to each other, and what lies between them? The answer is that the pectoralis minor lies anterior and the serratus anterior lies posterior, with the ribs and the intercostal muscles between the two.

Question 2869. Which sheet of fascia stretches from the clavicle above to the floor of the armpit below, encloses the pectoralis minor, and is pierced by the cephalic vein? The answer is the clavipectoral fascia. Its lower part forms the suspensory ligament of the armpit, which holds the axillary contents against the chest wall.

Question 2870. Which nerve supplies the subclavius: the nerve to the subclavius from the upper trunk of the brachial plexus, the long thoracic nerve, the medial pectoral nerve, or the suprascapular nerve? The answer is the nerve to the subclavius, a small branch of the upper trunk of the brachial plexus that reaches the muscle below the clavicle.

Question 2871. Which triangle of the chest wall is bounded above by the clavicle and below by the upper border of the pectoralis major, and carries the cephalic vein in its floor? The answer is the deltopectoral triangle. The vein runs upward there before it pierces the clavipectoral fascia to enter the axillary vein.

Question 2872. Which statement about the lateral pectoral nerve is correct: it passes above the pectoralis minor, it pierces the pectoralis minor, it runs down on the serratus anterior, or it supplies the latissimus dorsi? The answer is that it passes above the pectoralis minor. It then reaches the deep surface of the pectoralis major along with the pectoral branch of the thoracoacromial artery.

Question 2873. Which two muscles bound the deltopectoral groove, and which vein runs in that groove: the deltoid and the pectoralis major with the cephalic vein, the pectoralis major and the pectoralis minor with the basilic vein, the deltoid and the trapezius with the external jugular vein, or the serratus anterior and the latissimus dorsi with the axillary vein? The answer is the deltoid and the pectoralis major, with the cephalic vein.

Question 2874. A described illustration: a newborn is found on one side to be missing the lower, sternocostal part of the pectoralis major, and the hand on the same side is small with short fingers. Which structure is deficient, and what is this described pattern called? The answer is the sternocostal head of the pectoralis major, in the described pattern known as Poland sequence.

Question 2875. Which layer of the intercostal muscles has fibers that run downward and forward, in the direction of the hands placed in the pockets, and reaches from the tubercle of the rib behind to the costal cartilage in front? The answer is the external intercostal. Its fibers lift the ribs when they contract, and it is the outermost of the three layers.

Question 2876. Within the costal groove of a rib, which structure lies lowest, nearest the inner edge of the bone: the intercostal vein, the intercostal artery, the intercostal nerve, or the collateral branch? The answer is the intercostal nerve. From above downward the groove holds the vein first, then the artery, and then the nerve.

Question 2877. A described cross section of an intercostal space shows three layers of muscle with a bundle of vessels and a nerve lying between the middle layer and the deepest one. Which layer is the deepest, and what is the bundle called? The answer is the innermost intercostal, and the bundle is the intercostal neurovascular bundle.

Question 2878. Between which two layers of the chest wall does the intercostal neurovascular bundle run, and why does that position matter? The answer is between the internal intercostal and the innermost intercostal muscles. That neurovascular plane carries the bundle around the chest wall, and it lies deep to the internal intercostal, so the vessels are shielded from the outer surface.

Question 2879. Where the external intercostal muscle stops short in front, what replaces it between the costal cartilages: the external intercostal membrane, the internal intercostal membrane, the clavipectoral fascia, or the supraspinous ligament? The answer is the external intercostal membrane. The internal intercostal layer is replaced behind by the internal intercostal membrane instead.

Question 2880. True or false: the fibers of the internal intercostal muscles run downward and backward, at right angles to those of the external intercostals. The answer is true. They run from the floor of the costal groove above to the upper border of the rib below, sloping back toward the vertebral column, and they depress the ribs in forced expiration.

Question 2881. Which is the deepest and most incomplete layer of the intercostal muscles, lying inside the internal intercostals and separated from them by the neurovascular bundle? The answer is the innermost intercostal. Its fibers run in the same downward and backward direction as the internal intercostals, and it is best developed in the middle of the chest wall.

Question 2882. Which of these pairs belongs together: the external intercostal with elevation of the ribs, the internal intercostal with elevation of the ribs, the transversus thoracis with elevation of the ribs, or the innermost intercostal with movement of the arm? The answer is the external intercostal with elevation of the ribs, which it lifts toward the one above it.

Question 2883. Which thin muscles lie on the inner surface of the lower posterior chest wall and run in the same direction as the internal intercostals but skip over one or more ribs: the levatores costarum, the subcostal muscles, the serratus anterior, or the transversus thoracis? The answer is the subcostal muscles, found near the lower ribs at the back.

Question 2884. A described illustration: a chest drain is passed through the chest wall into the pleural cavity. Which layers of the intercostal muscles must the operator pass through, in order from the outside inward? The answer is the external intercostal, then the internal intercostal, then the innermost intercostal. The procedure belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 2885. Which small muscles arise from the transverse processes of the lower cervical and upper thoracic vertebrae and run downward and laterally to the rib below, lifting it? The answer is the levatores costarum. They occupy the spaces behind the intercostal muscles and are supplied by the posterior rami of the spinal nerves.

Question 2886. A described dissection of one intercostal space shows three muscle layers, the outermost running downward and forward, the next running downward and backward, and the deepest running in the same direction as the second but lying inside it. Which three muscles are shown? The answer is the external, the internal and the innermost intercostal muscles.

Question 2887. The intercostal nerves are the anterior rami of which spinal nerves: the cervical nerves, the first to the eleventh thoracic nerves, the lumbar nerves, or the sacral nerves? The answer is the first to the eleventh thoracic spinal nerves. The twelfth thoracic nerve is the subcostal nerve, which runs below the twelfth rib into the abdominal wall.

Question 2888. Which artery gives off the anterior intercostal arteries of the upper six intercostal spaces, and where does that artery itself descend? The answer is the internal thoracic artery. It runs down behind the costal cartilages just lateral to the sternum, and its branches pass sideways in the spaces to meet the posterior intercostal arteries.

Question 2889. Which arteries supply the posterior intercostal spaces of the first and second ribs: the descending thoracic aorta, the supreme intercostal artery from the costocervical trunk, the internal thoracic artery directly, or the musculophrenic artery? The answer is the supreme intercostal artery, a branch of the costocervical trunk of the subclavian artery.

Question 2890. A described illustration: after several ribs are broken in two places, the loose segment of the chest wall moves inward while the rest of the chest moves outward during inspiration. Which muscles can no longer hold that segment steady, and what is this described pattern called? The answer is the intercostal muscles, and the described pattern is a flail chest.

Question 2891. Which thin sheet of muscle lies on the inner surface of the front of the chest wall, running from the lower part of the sternum upward and outward to the costal cartilages? The answer is the transversus thoracis. Its fibers run in the same direction as the internal intercostals, and it draws the ribs and the cartilages downward.

Question 2892. The internal thoracic artery ends by dividing into which two branches: the superior epigastric and the musculophrenic arteries, the inferior epigastric and the external iliac arteries, the pericardiacophrenic and the bronchial arteries, or the thyrocervical and the costocervical trunks? The answer is the superior epigastric and the musculophrenic arteries.

Question 2893. A described cross section of the chest wall shows a needle passing between two ribs into the pleural cavity. Which part of the rib should the needle pass close to, and why? The answer is the upper border of the rib below, because the neurovascular bundle runs in the costal groove of the rib above and lies out of reach there.

Question 2894. Which of these pairs belongs together: the internal thoracic artery with the anterior intercostal arteries, the descending thoracic aorta with the anterior intercostal arteries, the subclavian artery with the intercostal nerves, or the azygos vein with the intercostal nerves? The answer is the internal thoracic artery with the anterior intercostal arteries.

Question 2895. A described illustration: a drain is to be placed in the fifth intercostal space. Which point of entry keeps clear of the intercostal vessels and nerve, and why? The answer is the middle of the space just above the rib below, because the bundle of that space runs in the costal groove of the rib above and turns upward behind.

Question 2896. Which intercostal nerve has no lateral cutaneous branch and sends a large branch to the brachial plexus: the first thoracic nerve, the second thoracic nerve, the sixth thoracic nerve, or the twelfth thoracic nerve? The answer is the first thoracic nerve. It runs in the first intercostal space, and its fibers reach the arm through the lower trunk of the plexus.

Question 2897. A described dissection of an intercostal space shows the main bundle running in the costal groove of the rib above and a smaller bundle running along the upper border of the rib below. What is the smaller bundle called, and what does it contain? The answer is the collateral bundle, holding a collateral branch of the intercostal nerve with its artery and vein.

Question 2898. Which structure runs in the costal groove of each rib below the intercostal artery, and from which spinal nerves does it come? The answer is the intercostal nerve, which comes from the anterior ramus of a thoracic spinal nerve. It is the lowest structure in the groove, so it is the one most often injured by a fracture.

Question 2899. A described illustration: after a fracture of a lower rib, a person has a band of numbness in the chest wall and weakness of the abdominal muscles on that side. Which nerves are involved, and where do they run? The answer is the lower intercostal nerves, which run in the costal grooves and then continue downward into the abdominal wall.

Question 2900. A described section of the chest wall in the midaxillary line shows, from the outside inward, the external intercostal, then the internal intercostal, then a bundle of vessels and a nerve, then the innermost intercostal, and finally the pleura. Which layer lies directly against the pleura? The answer is the innermost intercostal, with the bundle lying above it in the same space.

To summarize, the transversospinalis group is the deepest layer of the back muscles, running from a transverse process up to a spinous process in three layers, the semispinalis, the multifidus and the rotatores, all supplied by the posterior rami of the spinal nerves, extending the trunk and turning it to the opposite side. The semispinalis is the longest and reaches the skull as the semispinalis capitis, which the greater occipital nerve pierces, while the multifidus is the thickest in the lumbar region and is the chief stabilizer of the lower back, and the rotatores are the shortest and are best developed in the thoracic region. The pectoral muscles move the shoulder girdle and the arm: the pectoralis major adducts and medially rotates the arm, the pectoralis minor draws the scapula forward and downward and, with the scapula fixed, raises the ribs, the subclavius steadies the clavicle, and the serratus anterior, supplied by the long thoracic nerve, pulls the scapula forward. The intercostals form three layers, the external running downward and forward to lift the ribs and the internal and innermost running downward and backward to lower them, with the neurovascular bundle lying between the two deepest layers and the vein, the artery and the nerve arranged in that order from above downward in the costal groove.
