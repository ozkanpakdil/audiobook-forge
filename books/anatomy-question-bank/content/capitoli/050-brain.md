# Chapter 50 — Brain

This chapter gathers what a listener needs to hold about the surface of the cerebral hemispheres and about the small folded brain that sits beneath the tentorium. It moves through the gyri, the ridges of cortex that carry the named functions, then through the sulci that divide them, and finally through the cerebellum, its lobules, its peduncles and its deep nuclei. It holds one hundred questions, and each one gives its answer immediately after the question and then explains why that answer is right, so nothing has to be carried forward in memory. The landmarks named here are the ones a clinician points to when a scan, a stroke or a seizure has to be explained aloud, and they make the rest of the nervous system easier to learn. This is educational material, and it supports rather than replaces an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 4901. Which gyrus of the frontal lobe lies immediately anterior to the central sulcus? The answer is the precentral gyrus. It carries the primary motor cortex, whose fibers descend to move the opposite side of the body. The body is represented along it upside down, with the foot highest and the face lowest.

Question 4902. Which of these gyri lies between the superior frontal sulcus and the inferior frontal sulcus: the superior frontal gyrus, the middle frontal gyrus, the inferior frontal gyrus, or the precentral gyrus? The answer is the middle frontal gyrus. The superior frontal gyrus lies above its own sulcus. The inferior frontal gyrus lies below the inferior frontal sulcus, and the precentral gyrus lies behind them all.

Question 4903. Which gyrus of the parietal lobe lies immediately behind the central sulcus, and what does it receive? The answer is the postcentral gyrus, which receives general sensation from the opposite side of the body. It carries the primary somatosensory cortex. Its map of the body mirrors the motor map in front of it, with the foot highest and the face lowest.

Question 4904. Which of these areas lies in the posterior part of the inferior frontal gyrus, usually of the left hemisphere: the visual cortex, the motor speech area, the auditory cortex, or the sensory speech area? The answer is the motor speech area, named for Broca. Damage there leaves speech effortful and sparse while understanding stays relatively preserved. The sensory speech area lies further back in the temporal lobe.

Question 4905. A described view of the left hemisphere shows the lateral sulcus opened to reveal two or three small ridges across the upper surface of the temporal lobe. Which gyri are shown, and what do they serve? The answer is the transverse temporal gyri. They carry the primary auditory cortex and lie hidden inside the lateral sulcus on the upper surface of the superior temporal gyrus.

Question 4906. Which gyrus of the temporal lobe runs forward from the temporal pole just below the lateral sulcus? The answer is the superior temporal gyrus. Its posterior part holds the sensory speech area named for Wernicke. The middle and inferior temporal gyri lie below it, separated by the superior and inferior temporal sulci.

Question 4907. Which of these gyri arches directly over the corpus callosum: the fusiform gyrus, the parahippocampal gyrus, the cingulate gyrus, or the precentral gyrus? The answer is the cingulate gyrus. It sweeps from front to back above the corpus callosum as part of the limbic lobe. The parahippocampal gyrus lies far below it on the medial surface of the temporal lobe.

Question 4908. The uncus is the hooked medial tip of which gyrus, and where exactly does it lie? The answer is the parahippocampal gyrus, at its anterior and medial end. It lies in the temporal lobe just in front of the hippocampus. Because it sits close to the medial edge of the tentorium, it can be pressed upon when the brain swells.

Question 4909. Which gyrus of the orbital surface of the frontal lobe lies immediately medial to the olfactory sulcus? The answer is the straight gyrus, also called the gyrus rectus. It runs from front to back beside the midline. The olfactory tract lies in the sulcus just lateral to it, and the orbital gyri lie lateral to that.

Question 4910. A described view of the medial surface of the occipital lobe shows a long gyrus lying below a deep sulcus and above the collateral sulcus. Which gyrus is described? The answer is the lingual gyrus. It lies between the calcarine sulcus above and the collateral sulcus below. Its anterior end joins the parahippocampal gyrus.

Question 4911. Which gyrus lies between the collateral sulcus and the occipitotemporal sulcus on the inferior surface of the hemisphere? The answer is the fusiform gyrus, also called the lateral occipitotemporal gyrus. It lies lateral to the collateral sulcus. The lingual gyrus lies medial to that same sulcus.

Question 4912. Which of these named parts lies between the parieto-occipital sulcus and the calcarine sulcus: the cuneus, the lingual gyrus, the precuneus, or the uncus? The answer is the cuneus. It is a wedge of occipital cortex on the medial surface. The precuneus lies in front of the parieto-occipital sulcus and belongs to the parietal lobe.

Question 4913. A constructed illustration: a patient speaks in short effortful phrases and repeats a single word with great effort, while following simple spoken commands well. Which gyrus of which hemisphere is most likely injured? The answer is the posterior inferior frontal gyrus of the left hemisphere. That region holds the motor speech area. The preserved understanding points away from the sensory speech area behind it.

Question 4914. A constructed illustration: a patient cannot name the fingers of the hand, cannot do simple arithmetic, cannot write, and confuses right and left. Which gyrus of the dominant parietal lobe is involved? The answer is the angular gyrus. The four findings together are described as the syndrome named for Gerstmann. The angular gyrus lies in the inferior parietal lobule around the end of the superior temporal sulcus.

Question 4915. Which folded sheet of cortex lies hidden in the floor of the lateral sulcus, covered by the frontal, parietal and temporal opercula? The answer is the insula. It is drawn out of sight by the overgrowth of the surrounding opercula during development. A small part of it can still be seen in the depth of the sulcus.

Question 4916. True or false: in the normal brain the cingulate gyrus lies above the corpus callosum and below the cingulate sulcus. The answer is true. It arches over the corpus callosum from the front to the back. The sulcus of the corpus callosum separates it from the commissure beneath, and the cingulate sulcus separates it from the medial frontal gyrus above.

Question 4917. Which of these gyri forms the medial wall of the temporal lobe and joins the uncus at its front: the fusiform gyrus, the superior temporal gyrus, the parahippocampal gyrus, or the middle temporal gyrus? The answer is the parahippocampal gyrus. It lies medial to the collateral sulcus and above the hippocampal sulcus. The uncus is its anterior hooked end.

Question 4918. A described view of the orbital surface of the frontal lobe shows an H-shaped groove dividing the cortex into four named strips. Which gyri are those strips? The answer is the orbital gyri. They are named medial, anterior, posterior and lateral. The straight gyrus lies medial to the olfactory sulcus, separate from all four.

Question 4919. Which gyrus curls around the posterior end of the lateral sulcus, and which gyrus curls around the posterior end of the superior temporal sulcus beneath it? The answer is the supramarginal gyrus above and the angular gyrus below. Both belong to the inferior parietal lobule. The angular gyrus is the more posterior and inferior of the two.

Question 4920. Which lobule of the medial surface surrounds the medial end of the central sulcus, and which two body regions does it serve? The answer is the paracentral lobule, serving the foot and the leg. It carries the parts of the motor and sensory maps that lie over the top of the hemisphere. It also holds the cortical region concerned with control of the bladder.

Question 4921. Which slender band of cortex lies between the fimbria and the parahippocampal gyrus, its margin cut into small notches? The answer is the dentate gyrus. It belongs to the hippocampal formation. The notches give it the tooth-like appearance that its name describes, and it curls into the hippocampus behind the uncus.

Question 4922. Name two gyri that lie on the medial surface of the occipital lobe, spoken as a list. The answer is the cuneus and the lingual gyrus. The cuneus lies above the calcarine sulcus and the lingual gyrus below it. Both belong to the occipital lobe, and the calcarine sulcus separates them.

Question 4923. Which of these gyri lies in front of the parieto-occipital sulcus on the medial surface: the cuneus, the precuneus, the lingual gyrus, or the fusiform gyrus? The answer is the precuneus. It belongs to the parietal lobe, not the occipital lobe. The cuneus and the lingual gyrus both lie behind that sulcus.

Question 4924. A described view of the insula shows a small central groove dividing it into a larger front part with several short ridges and a smaller back part with one long ridge. Which sulcus and which gyri are described? The answer is the central sulcus of the insula, with the short gyri in front and the long gyrus behind. The island is ringed by the circular sulcus.

Question 4925. A constructed illustration: a patient speaks fluently and at length, but the words are jumbled and meaningless, and the patient does not seem to notice. Which region of which gyrus is most likely injured? The answer is the posterior part of the superior temporal gyrus of the left hemisphere. That region holds the sensory speech area. Fluent but empty speech with poor understanding is its signature.

Question 4926. Which gyrus forms the upper border of the lateral surface of the frontal lobe, running forward above the superior frontal sulcus? The answer is the superior frontal gyrus. Its medial part continues onto the medial surface as the medial frontal gyrus. Its posterior portion on the medial side holds the supplementary motor area.

Question 4927. Which of these pairs is correctly matched: the straight gyrus lateral to the olfactory sulcus, the straight gyrus medial to the olfactory sulcus, the straight gyrus on the lateral surface, or the straight gyrus in the occipital lobe? The answer is the straight gyrus lying medial to the olfactory sulcus. It lies on the orbital surface beside the midline. The olfactory tract runs in that sulcus.

Question 4928. In the medial wall of the hemisphere, which structure lies immediately below the cingulate gyrus, and which lies immediately above it? The answer is the corpus callosum below, with the medial frontal gyrus above. The sulcus of the corpus callosum and the cingulate sulcus mark those two boundaries. The cingulate gyrus therefore sits in a groove of its own.

Question 4929. Which gyrus occupies the lower margin of the lateral surface of the temporal lobe, below the inferior temporal sulcus? The answer is the inferior temporal gyrus. It runs forward to the temporal pole. Behind it the cortex continues into the lateral occipital region, where the boundary between the lobes is only arbitrary.

Question 4930. A described view of the inferior parietal region shows two curved gyri stacked around the ends of two sulci. Which lobule and which two gyri are shown? The answer is the inferior parietal lobule, made of the supramarginal gyrus and the angular gyrus. The supramarginal gyrus arches around the end of the lateral sulcus. The angular gyrus arches around the end of the superior temporal sulcus.

Question 4931. Which of these gyri forms the anterior and medial hook of the temporal lobe that lies beside the amygdala: the fusiform gyrus, the uncus, the cingulate gyrus, or the dentate gyrus? The answer is the uncus. It is the medial, hooked end of the parahippocampal gyrus. The amygdala lies just above and behind it in the temporal lobe.

Question 4932. Which of these pairs belongs together: the precentral gyrus with the primary visual cortex, the postcentral gyrus with the primary motor cortex, the calcarine cortex with the primary auditory cortex, or the transverse temporal gyri with the primary auditory cortex? The answer is the transverse temporal gyri with the primary auditory cortex. The precentral gyrus carries motor cortex and the postcentral gyrus carries somatosensory cortex.

Question 4933. Which gyrus lies between the superior temporal sulcus and the inferior temporal sulcus on the lateral surface? The answer is the middle temporal gyrus. It lies below the superior temporal gyrus. The inferior temporal gyrus lies below it in turn, and the three are stacked like bands along the side of the temporal lobe.

Question 4934. Which band of white matter lies along the ventricular surface of the hippocampus, and into which structure does the dentate gyrus roll behind it? The answer is the alveus, with the dentate gyrus rolling into the hippocampus. The alveus gathers the fibers that leave the hippocampus. They continue as the fimbria and then as the fornix.

Question 4935. A constructed illustration: a patient cannot move the right face or the right arm and has lost speech, while the right leg is barely affected. Which part of the cortex is deprived of blood? The answer is the lower part of the left precentral gyrus. The face and the hand occupy its lowest part, above the lateral sulcus. The leg area lies highest and is spared.

Question 4936. A described view of the lateral surface shows a deep groove running downward and forward toward the lateral sulcus, with a broad gyrus on each side. Which sulcus is described? The answer is the central sulcus. It separates the frontal lobe in front from the parietal lobe behind. It usually stops just short of the lateral sulcus.

Question 4937. Which of these sulci separates the superior parietal lobule from the inferior parietal lobule: the postcentral sulcus, the intraparietal sulcus, the superior temporal sulcus, or the cingulate sulcus? The answer is the intraparietal sulcus. It runs backward through the parietal lobe. The postcentral sulcus lies in front of it and bounds the postcentral gyrus.

Question 4938. Along the medial surface of the occipital lobe, which gyrus lies immediately above the calcarine sulcus, and which lies immediately below it? The answer is the cuneus above and the lingual gyrus below. The calcarine sulcus separates them. Both banks of that sulcus carry the primary visual cortex, which is why a lesion there removes the opposite half of the visual field.

Question 4939. Which groove runs across the lateral surface of the hemisphere, separating the temporal lobe below from the frontal and parietal lobes above? The answer is the lateral sulcus, also called the lateral fissure. Its floor is formed by the insula. The middle cerebral artery runs within it.

Question 4940. Which of these structures lies in the floor of the lateral sulcus: the corpus callosum, the insula, the cingulate gyrus, or the dentate gyrus? The answer is the insula. The opercula of the frontal, parietal and temporal lobes grow over it and hide it. The middle cerebral artery and its branches course along the surface of the insula.

Question 4941. True or false: the central sulcus separates the precentral gyrus from the postcentral gyrus and marks the boundary between the frontal and parietal lobes. The answer is true. The precentral gyrus lies in front of it and the postcentral gyrus behind it. That single groove therefore separates the motor cortex from the somatosensory cortex.

Question 4942. A described medial view shows a groove that begins below the corpus callosum at the front, arches above it, and ends by turning upward toward the top of the hemisphere. Which sulcus is described? The answer is the cingulate sulcus, whose upturned end is the marginal sulcus. It separates the cingulate gyrus from the medial frontal gyrus. The precuneus lies behind that end.

Question 4943. Which of these sulci marks the boundary between the parietal lobe and the occipital lobe on the medial surface: the collateral sulcus, the parieto-occipital sulcus, the calcarine sulcus, or the olfactory sulcus? The answer is the parieto-occipital sulcus. It runs downward and forward on the medial surface. It separates the precuneus in front from the cuneus behind.

Question 4944. Which sulcus lies on the medial surface of the occipital lobe and separates the cuneus from the lingual gyrus? The answer is the calcarine sulcus. The primary visual cortex lines both of its banks. A lesion that destroys the cortex around it removes vision from the opposite half of the visual field.

Question 4945. A constructed illustration: a patient can write normally but cannot read a word, and the eyes cannot carry what is seen in the right half of the visual field to the reading cortex. Which two structures are injured together? The answer is the left occipital cortex and the splenium of the corpus callosum. The combination spares writing while cutting off the reading input. The pattern is called alexia without agraphia.

Question 4946. Which of these grooves separates the cingulate gyrus from the medial frontal gyrus above it: the sulcus of the corpus callosum, the cingulate sulcus, the marginal sulcus, or the collateral sulcus? The answer is the cingulate sulcus. The sulcus of the corpus callosum lies below the cingulate gyrus instead. The marginal sulcus is the ascending end of the cingulate sulcus.

Question 4947. Which landmark on the inferior margin of the hemisphere, taken with a line drawn to the parieto-occipital sulcus, marks the front edge of the occipital lobe? The answer is the preoccipital notch. The line completes the boundary that the sulcus begins. Everything behind that line belongs to the occipital lobe.

Question 4948. A described medial view shows a thin curved groove hugging the upper surface of the great commissure and following it from the rostrum to the splenium. Which sulcus is described, and what does it separate? The answer is the sulcus of the corpus callosum. It separates the corpus callosum below from the cingulate gyrus above. It follows the curve of the commissure for its whole length.

Question 4949. Name two sulci that lie on the medial surface of the cerebral hemisphere, spoken as a list. The answer is the cingulate sulcus and the calcarine sulcus. The cingulate sulcus arches above the corpus callosum, and the calcarine sulcus lies in the occipital lobe. The parieto-occipital sulcus lies on the same surface and is a third.

Question 4950. Which of these sulci lies between the superior temporal gyrus and the middle temporal gyrus: the inferior temporal sulcus, the superior temporal sulcus, the lateral sulcus, or the collateral sulcus? The answer is the superior temporal sulcus. The lateral sulcus lies above the superior temporal gyrus. The inferior temporal sulcus lies one gyrus further down.

Question 4951. Which of these pairs belongs together: the lateral sulcus with the boundary between the hemispheres, the central sulcus with the boundary between the frontal and parietal lobes, the calcarine sulcus with the boundary between the temporal and parietal lobes, or the parieto-occipital sulcus with the boundary between the temporal gyri? The answer is the central sulcus, which separates the frontal and parietal lobes.

Question 4952. Which sulcus separates the superior temporal gyrus from the middle temporal gyrus? The answer is the superior temporal sulcus. It runs backward along the lateral surface of the temporal lobe. Its posterior end is surrounded by the angular gyrus in the parietal lobe.

Question 4953. Which of these sulci lies between the parahippocampal gyrus and the fusiform gyrus on the inferior surface: the collateral sulcus, the hippocampal sulcus, the olfactory sulcus, or the circular sulcus? The answer is the collateral sulcus. It runs from front to back along the inferior surface. The fusiform gyrus lies lateral to it and the lingual gyrus medial to it.

Question 4954. A described dissection of the temporal lobe shows a groove on its medial surface separating the hippocampus and dentate gyrus from the parahippocampal gyrus beside them. Which sulcus is shown? The answer is the hippocampal sulcus. It lies behind the uncus and follows the curve of the hippocampus. The fimbria lies on the other side of the hippocampus.

Question 4955. A constructed illustration: a patient reports brief episodes of an unpleasant smell that no one else can detect, followed by a few seconds of lip smacking and a dreamy stare. Which medial temporal structure is most likely the origin? The answer is the uncus of the parahippocampal gyrus. Such episodes are described as uncinate fits. The uncus lies in the temporal lobe beside the amygdala.

Question 4956. Which of these sulci contains the olfactory tract and lies lateral to the straight gyrus on the orbital surface: the orbital sulcus, the olfactory sulcus, the cingulate sulcus, or the collateral sulcus? The answer is the olfactory sulcus. It runs from front to back on the orbital surface of the frontal lobe. The straight gyrus lies medial to it.

Question 4957. The precuneus lies immediately in front of which sulcus, and which gyrus lies immediately behind that same sulcus? The answer is the precuneus in front of the parieto-occipital sulcus, with the cuneus behind it. Both lie on the medial surface. The marginal sulcus bounds the precuneus at its front.

Question 4958. Which sulcus of the frontal lobe runs downward and forward just in front of the precentral gyrus? The answer is the precentral sulcus. It forms the anterior boundary of the precentral gyrus. The superior and inferior frontal sulci lie further forward on the lateral surface.

Question 4959. Which of these structures occupies the longitudinal fissure between the two hemispheres: the falx cerebri, the tentorium cerebelli, the corpus callosum, or the insula? The answer is the falx cerebri. It is a fold of dura that descends between the hemispheres. The corpus callosum lies in the depth of the fissure and joins the two halves.

Question 4960. True or false: the lateral sulcus separates the temporal lobe from the frontal lobe and the parietal lobe. The answer is true. The temporal lobe lies below the sulcus, and the frontal and parietal lobes lie above it. The insula forms the floor of the groove between them.

Question 4961. A described view of the orbital surface of the frontal lobe shows a groove shaped like the letter H, with four strips of cortex around it. Which sulcus is shown? The answer is the orbital sulcus. Its crossbar and two limbs divide the orbital gyri into medial, anterior, posterior and lateral parts. The olfactory sulcus lies medial to the whole pattern.

Question 4962. Which sulcus forms the posterior boundary of the postcentral gyrus? The answer is the postcentral sulcus. It lies behind the postcentral gyrus on the lateral surface of the parietal lobe. Beyond it lies the superior parietal lobule, divided further back by the intraparietal sulcus.

Question 4963. Which of these sulci rings the insula and separates it from the opercula: the central sulcus of the insula, the circular sulcus, the hippocampal sulcus, or the calcarine sulcus? The answer is the circular sulcus. The central sulcus of the insula divides the island into a front and a back part. The opercula of the frontal, parietal and temporal lobes cover the ring.

Question 4964. A constructed illustration: a blockage of one artery removes the cortex of the lateral surface of the left hemisphere, and the patient loses speech and the use of the right face and arm. Which artery is blocked, and in which groove does it run? The answer is the middle cerebral artery, running in the lateral sulcus. Its branches supply the insula and the opercular cortex.

Question 4965. Which notch on the inferior margin of the hemisphere helps to define the anterior limit of the occipital lobe? The answer is the preoccipital notch. A line drawn from it to the parieto-occipital sulcus completes the boundary. The notch itself lies on the inferior margin of the temporal and occipital junction.

Question 4966. A described view of the medial occipital surface shows a deep sulcus with a thin stripe of cortex along both banks, thicker than the surrounding cortex. Which sulcus is shown, and what does the stripe contain? The answer is the calcarine sulcus, whose banks contain the primary visual cortex. The stripe is the striate cortex. Damage on one side removes the opposite half of the visual field.

Question 4967. Which two structures lie immediately on either side of the sulcus of the corpus callosum? The answer is the corpus callosum below and the cingulate gyrus above. The sulcus follows the curve of the commissure from front to back. The indusium griseum lies on the upper surface of the corpus callosum itself.

Question 4968. Which narrow midline part of the cerebellum joins its two hemispheres and carries the lobules named lingula, culmen and uvula? The answer is the vermis. It lies in the midline between the hemispheres. Each of its lobules corresponds to a named lobule in the hemisphere beside it.

Question 4969. Which of these fissures separates the small anterior lobe of the cerebellum from the large posterior lobe: the horizontal fissure, the primary fissure, the posterolateral fissure, or the calcarine sulcus? The answer is the primary fissure. It runs across the superior surface of the cerebellum. The posterolateral fissure separates the flocculonodular lobe instead.

Question 4970. Name the three pairs of cerebellar peduncles and the part of the brainstem each one joins, spoken as a list. The answer is the inferior peduncles joining the medulla, the middle peduncles joining the pons, and the superior peduncles joining the midbrain. The middle pair is much the largest. The superior pair carries most of the output of the deep nuclei.

Question 4971. A described view of the superior surface of the cerebellum shows a narrow midline strip and, on each side, a large four-sided lobule just behind the primary fissure. Which lobules are shown? The answer is the culmen in the midline with the quadrangular lobule beside it. The quadrangular lobule is the hemispheric part corresponding to the culmen. The declive and the simple lobule lie behind.

Question 4972. Which of these lobes of the cerebellum consists of the nodulus and the two flocculi: the anterior lobe, the posterior lobe, the flocculonodular lobe, or the vermis? The answer is the flocculonodular lobe. It is separated from the rest of the cerebellum by the posterolateral fissure. It is concerned with balance and with the movements of the eyes.

Question 4973. Which rounded lobule of the cerebellum lies on the inferior surface of each hemisphere, close to the foramen magnum? The answer is the tonsil. It lies medial and inferior, beside the brainstem. When the brain swells, the tonsils can be pushed down through the foramen magnum.

Question 4974. True or false: a lesion of one cerebellar hemisphere produces weakness and loss of coordination on the same side of the body. The answer is true. The output of the cerebellum crosses in the superior cerebellar peduncle and crosses again in the corticospinal tract. Two crossings leave the signs on the same side as the lesion.

Question 4975. Which of these deep cerebellar nuclei is the largest and lies farthest to the side in each hemisphere: the fastigial nucleus, the globose nucleus, the emboliform nucleus, or the dentate nucleus? The answer is the dentate nucleus. Its folded, tooth-like band of gray matter lies near the roof of the fourth ventricle. The fastigial nucleus lies most medially, in the vermis.

Question 4976. A described section across the cerebellum shows four masses of gray matter on each side, embedded in the white matter a short distance from the midline. Which nuclei are shown? The answer is the fastigial, globose, emboliform and dentate nuclei. The fastigial lies nearest the midline. The globose and emboliform are often grouped together, and the dentate lies farthest out.

Question 4977. Which structure lies immediately above the cerebellar tonsils, and through which opening do the tonsils descend when the pressure inside the skull rises? The answer is the foramen magnum, with the medulla oblongata lying immediately in front of the tonsils. The tonsils are the parts that herniate. They press on the medulla, which can stop breathing.

Question 4978. Which small tufted lobule of the cerebellum hangs from the inferior surface of each middle cerebellar peduncle? The answer is the flocculus. It belongs to the flocculonodular lobe along with the nodulus. It receives input from the vestibular apparatus of the inner ear.

Question 4979. Which of these cerebellar peduncles is the largest and connects the cerebellum to the pons: the inferior peduncle, the middle peduncle, the superior peduncle, or the peduncle of the flocculus? The answer is the middle cerebellar peduncle. It carries the fibers that reach the cerebellum from the cerebral cortex by way of the pons. The inferior peduncle joins the medulla and the superior joins the midbrain.

Question 4980. A constructed illustration: a patient with raised pressure inside the skull becomes drowsy and then stops breathing, and the cerebellar tonsils lie below the foramen magnum. Which structure is compressed, and why is that dangerous? The answer is the medulla oblongata, which lies immediately in front of the tonsils. The medulla holds the centers that drive breathing and control the heart. Its management belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 4981. A described side view of the cerebellum shows a long fissure running around its margin, separating a superior group of lobules from an inferior group. Which fissure is shown? The answer is the horizontal fissure. It runs along the margin between the superior and inferior surfaces of the posterior lobe. The folium and the superior semilunar lobule lie above it.

Question 4982. Which hemispheric lobule of the cerebellum corresponds to the uvula of the vermis: the tonsil, the biventral lobule, the superior semilunar lobule, or the flocculus? The answer is the tonsil. The biventral lobule corresponds to the pyramis. The flocculus corresponds to the nodulus, and the simple lobule corresponds to the declive.

Question 4983. Which of these pairs belongs together: the nodulus with the flocculus, the culmen with the tonsil, the uvula with the quadrangular lobule, or the declive with the biventral lobule? The answer is the nodulus with the flocculus. The culmen corresponds to the quadrangular lobule, the uvula to the tonsil, and the declive to the simple lobule.

Question 4984. Which lobule of the vermis lies between the uvula above it and the nodulus below it, forming the lowest part of the vermis? The answer is the nodulus. It lies at the bottom of the vermis and joins the two flocculi by the peduncle of the flocculus. Together the nodulus and the flocculi form the flocculonodular lobe.

Question 4985. Which of these deep cerebellar nuclei lies in the midline of the vermis, nearest the roof of the fourth ventricle: the dentate nucleus, the fastigial nucleus, the emboliform nucleus, or the globose nucleus? The answer is the fastigial nucleus. It lies in the vermis, one on each side of the midline. The dentate nucleus lies farthest to the side.

Question 4986. A described section through the cerebellar cortex shows three layers, the outermost sparsely populated, the middle a single row of large flask-shaped cells, and the innermost densely packed. Which layers are shown, and which cells form the middle row? The answer is the molecular layer, the Purkinje cell layer and the granular layer. The Purkinje cells form that row. They are the only cells whose fibers leave the cortex.

Question 4987. Through which part of the brainstem does the superior cerebellar peduncle enter, and where does most of its output end? The answer is the midbrain, ending in the thalamus of the opposite side after crossing. The crossing lies in the midbrain. The thalamus then relays the signal to the motor cortex.

Question 4988. Which lobule of the cerebellar hemisphere lies between the inferior semilunar lobule and the tonsil on the inferior surface? The answer is the biventral lobule, also called the gracile lobule. It corresponds to the pyramis of the vermis. The tonsil lies medial to it and the inferior semilunar lobule above it.

Question 4989. Which of these signs belongs to a lesion of one cerebellar hemisphere: weakness of the opposite arm, loss of coordination of the same arm, loss of pain sensation on the same side, or loss of vision? The answer is loss of coordination of the same arm. Cerebellar signs appear on the same side as the lesion. Weakness and sensory loss from the cortex appear on the opposite side.

Question 4990. True or false: the flocculonodular lobe is concerned with balance and with the control of eye movements. The answer is true. It receives fibers from the vestibular apparatus of the inner ear. Its damage produces unsteadiness and nystagmus. It is the oldest part of the cerebellum in evolutionary terms.

Question 4991. The flocculus of each hemisphere is joined to which midline lobule, and by which band of fibers? The answer is the nodulus, joined by the peduncle of the flocculus. The two flocculi and the nodulus together form the flocculonodular lobe. That lobe sits behind the posterolateral fissure.

Question 4992. Which of these tumors arises at the cerebellopontine angle and damages the nerve of hearing: a tumor of the dentate nucleus, a tumor of the vestibular nerve, a tumor of the optic nerve, or a tumor of the olfactory tract? The answer is a tumor of the vestibular nerve, described as a vestibular schwannoma. It grows where the facial and vestibulocochlear nerves leave the brainstem. Hearing loss in one ear is usually the first sign.

Question 4993. Which of these pairs belongs together: the dentate nucleus with the vermis, the fastigial nucleus with the lateral hemisphere, the dentate nucleus with the lateral hemisphere, or the emboliform nucleus with the flocculus? The answer is the dentate nucleus with the lateral hemisphere. The fastigial nucleus lies in the vermis. The dentate nucleus sits far out among the fibers of the hemisphere.

Question 4994. A constructed illustration: a person has had headache and neck pain for years, and imaging shows the cerebellar tonsils below the foramen magnum as two pointed pegs. Which developmental arrangement is described? The answer is a Chiari malformation. The tonsils are displaced downward through the opening because the back of the skull is small. Headache made worse by coughing is common, and the decision about treatment belongs to the treating clinician.

Question 4995. A constructed illustration: a patient suddenly has vertigo, unsteady walking and a severe headache, with clumsiness of the left arm and nystagmus but preserved strength. Which part of the brain and which artery are involved? The answer is the left cerebellar hemisphere, supplied by the left posterior inferior cerebellar artery. The cerebellar signs lie on the same side as the lesion. Management belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 4996. Which of these pairs belongs together: the flocculonodular lobe with skilled limb movement, the vermis with balance of the trunk, the lateral hemisphere with eye movements, or the anterior lobe with speech? The answer is the vermis with balance of the trunk. The flocculonodular lobe serves balance and eye movements. The lateral hemispheres serve the coordination of skilled movement of the limbs.

Question 4997. A section is cut in the midline through the whole brain from front to back. Which part of the cerebellum will appear in that section, and what will lie in front of it? The answer is the vermis, with the midbrain, the pons and the medulla in front of it. A midline section passes through no cerebellar hemisphere. The fourth ventricle lies between the brainstem and the cerebellum.

Question 4998. Which of these structures forms the branching white core of each cerebellar hemisphere, visible on a cut surface: the arbor vitae, the corpus callosum, the alveus, or the fimbria? The answer is the arbor vitae. Its branches carry fibers into the many thin leaves of the cerebellar cortex. The gray cortex lies on the surface of every leaf.

Question 4999. Which large cell of the cerebellar cortex is the only source of its outgoing fibers, and what kind of signal does it send? The answer is the Purkinje cell. Its fiber leaves the cortex and ends in the deep cerebellar nuclei. Its signal is inhibitory, so it quiets the cells beneath it rather than exciting them.

Question 5000. A section is cut across the cerebellum in the coronal plane. Which structures will be seen, and how are they arranged? The answer is the vermis in the middle, a hemisphere on each side, and the deep nuclei in the white matter. The dentate nucleus will appear farthest from the midline. The fourth ventricle lies above, between the cerebellum and the brainstem.

To summarize, the lateral surface of the hemisphere is organized by three grooves a listener can place at once: the central sulcus, which separates the precentral motor gyrus from the postcentral sensory gyrus, the lateral sulcus, whose floor holds the insula, and the calcarine sulcus on the medial surface of the occipital lobe, whose banks carry the primary visual cortex. The named gyri follow those grooves: the superior, middle and inferior frontal gyri, with the motor speech area in the posterior inferior frontal gyrus of the left hemisphere, the superior temporal gyrus holding the auditory cortex and the sensory speech area, the cingulate gyrus arching over the corpus callosum, the parahippocampal gyrus ending in the uncus, and the orbital and medial surfaces carrying the straight gyrus, the orbital gyri and the precuneus. The cerebellum answers to the same method: the vermis in the midline and a hemisphere on each side, the primary fissure dividing anterior from posterior lobe, the posterolateral fissure cutting off the flocculonodular lobe of balance, the horizontal fissure, and the tonsil, biventral lobule, flocculus and central lobule each paired with a named lobule of the vermis. The deep nuclei, the fastigial, globose, emboliform and dentate, sit in the white matter of the arbor vitae, the Purkinje cell is the only output of the cortex, and the middle peduncle is the largest of the three. Because the cerebellar pathway crosses twice, its signs fall on the same side as the lesion, and because the tonsils lie beside the foramen magnum, their herniation compresses the medulla.
