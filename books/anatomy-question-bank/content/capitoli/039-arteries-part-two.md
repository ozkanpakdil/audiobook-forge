# Chapter 39 — Arteries, Part Two

This chapter continues the journey from the heart towards the tissues by following the arteries of the arm below the elbow and the arteries of the lower limb from the pelvis to the foot. It covers the radial artery and the ulnar artery with the arches they form in the hand, the common iliac, external iliac and internal iliac arteries, the femoral artery and its deep branch, the popliteal artery behind the knee, and the anterior tibial, posterior tibial and peroneal arteries of the leg. It holds one hundred questions, and each one gives its answer immediately after the question and then explains why that answer is right, so nothing has to be held in mind until the end. Several items describe what an arteriogram or a specimen would show, because a listener cannot look at a picture. This is educational material, and it supports rather than replaces an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 3801. Which artery is the smaller of the two terminal branches of the brachial artery, beginning at the level of the neck of the radius? The answer is the radial artery. It arises in the cubital fossa and runs down the lateral side of the forearm. The ulnar artery is the larger of the two terminal branches.

Question 3802. At the wrist, is the radial pulse felt just medial to the flexor carpi radialis tendon, just lateral to it, deep to the palmaris longus tendon, or behind the ulnar styloid process? The answer is just lateral to the tendon of the flexor carpi radialis. The artery rests there on the lower end of the radius, where its wall can be pressed against bone.

Question 3803. Which muscle tendon lies immediately medial to the radial artery in the lower part of the forearm? The answer is the tendon of the flexor carpi radialis. The artery lies between that tendon and the tendon of the brachioradialis. Both tendons can be felt at the wrist, with the artery between them.

Question 3804. Which artery crosses the floor of the anatomical snuffbox, running over the scaphoid and the trapezium? The answer is the radial artery. It passes between the tendons that bound the snout-shaped hollow on either side. From there it turns into the palm to form the deep palmar arch.

Question 3805. A constructed illustration: a needle is placed in the radial artery at the wrist to sample arterial blood, and blood is obtained. Which structure lies immediately deep to the artery at that point and gives the needle a firm backing? The answer is the lower end of the radius. The artery lies on the bone there, which is why it can be compressed against it.

Question 3806. Which of these branches of the radial artery arises near the elbow and runs upward to join the network around the elbow joint: the radial recurrent artery, the princeps pollicis artery, the dorsal carpal branch, or the superficial palmar branch? The answer is the radial recurrent artery. It ascends between the brachioradialis and the brachialis and joins the profunda brachii artery. The other three lie far distal to the elbow.

Question 3807. A described arteriogram of the hand shows an arch in the palm formed mainly by a vessel that has turned around the lateral border of the wrist, with a smaller contribution from the medial side. Which arch is described? The answer is the deep palmar arch. The radial artery forms most of it, and the deep palmar branch of the ulnar artery completes it.

Question 3808. Which artery of the hand arises from the radial artery and runs along the thumb to its tip: the princeps pollicis artery, the radialis indicis artery, the superficial palmar arch, or the anterior interosseous artery? The answer is the princeps pollicis artery. The radialis indicis artery runs to the index finger instead. The anterior interosseous artery lies in the forearm.

Question 3809. True or false: in the upper part of the forearm the radial artery lies deep to the brachioradialis muscle. The answer is true. It passes down under cover of that muscle before it reaches the lateral side of the wrist. In the lower forearm it becomes superficial and easy to feel.

Question 3810. Which nerve runs down the forearm lateral to the radial artery in its middle third and later emerges to supply the skin of the back of the hand? The answer is the superficial branch of the radial nerve. It lies lateral to the artery under cover of the brachioradialis. It becomes superficial near the wrist.

Question 3811. Which arch of the hand is formed mainly by the ulnar artery with a small contribution from the radial artery: the deep palmar arch, the superficial palmar arch, the dorsal carpal arch, or the plantar arch? The answer is the superficial palmar arch. The deep palmar arch is the one formed mainly by the radial artery. The plantar arch belongs to the foot.

Question 3812. A constructed illustration: before a procedure on the hand, a clinician presses on both arteries at the wrist and asks the person to clench and then open the fist. Which two vessels are being compressed in that described test? The answer is the radial artery and the ulnar artery. The test checks that each artery can supply the hand on its own.

Question 3813. Which artery of the forearm is the main vessel of the lateral side and ends in the palm by taking part in the deep palmar arch? The answer is the radial artery. It runs from the cubital fossa along the lateral border of the forearm. Its terminal part turns around the first metacarpal into the deep palm.

Question 3814. Which structure forms the lateral boundary of the anatomical snuffbox, with the radial artery passing deep to it: the extensor pollicis longus tendon, the extensor pollicis brevis tendon, the flexor carpi radialis tendon, or the extensor digitorum tendon? The answer is the extensor pollicis brevis tendon. It lies with the abductor pollicis longus tendon on the lateral side. The extensor pollicis longus tendon forms the medial boundary.

Question 3815. A described transverse section of the lower forearm shows one artery lying on the anterior surface of a bone, close to a tendon that runs to the wrist, with another artery deeper between the muscles. Which artery lies on the bone? The answer is the radial artery. It rests on the lower end of the radius, lateral to the flexor carpi radialis tendon.

Question 3816. Name two branches of the radial artery that lie in the hand, spoken as a list. The answer is the princeps pollicis artery and the radialis indicis artery. The first dorsal metacarpal artery may also be named. All three arise as the radial artery turns into the deep palm.

Question 3817. Which artery, present in a small number of people, arises high in the arm from the axillary or the brachial artery and then follows the usual course of the radial artery in the forearm? The answer is a high origin of the radial artery. The variant matters when a pulse is sought near the elbow. The usual origin is at the neck of the radius.

Question 3818. In a described cross section of the distal forearm, the artery that can be pressed against the radius lies on the lateral side of the section. Which artery is it, and how does it lie relative to the artery on the medial side? The answer is the radial artery, which lies lateral to the ulnar artery. The ulnar artery runs on the medial side beside the ulnar nerve.

Question 3819. Which artery is the larger of the two terminal branches of the brachial artery, beginning at the neck of the radius and passing down the medial side of the forearm? The answer is the ulnar artery. It gives off the common interosseous artery in the upper forearm. It reaches the hand at the wrist beside the ulnar nerve.

Question 3820. In the upper part of its course, which muscle does the ulnar artery pass beneath: the pronator teres, the brachioradialis, the flexor carpi radialis, or the supinator? The answer is the pronator teres. It passes beneath that muscle and then between the two layers of the flexor muscles. The brachioradialis overlies the radial artery instead.

Question 3821. In the lower forearm, which nerve lies immediately medial to the ulnar artery? The answer is the ulnar nerve. The two run together towards the wrist, with the artery on the lateral side of the nerve. Both pass into the hand beside the pisiform bone.

Question 3822. The common interosseous artery divides into which two vessels: the anterior and posterior interosseous arteries, the radial and ulnar recurrent arteries, the princeps pollicis and radialis indicis arteries, or the medial and lateral plantar arteries? The answer is the anterior and posterior interosseous arteries. The anterior one runs down the front of the interosseous membrane. The posterior one passes through the membrane to the back of the forearm.

Question 3823. A constructed illustration: a wound at the medial side of the wrist divides a vessel that lies just lateral to the pisiform bone. Which artery is likely injured, and which nerve lies beside it? The answer is the ulnar artery, with the ulnar nerve beside it. Both pass through the short canal at the wrist on their way into the hand.

Question 3824. A described dissection of the front of the wrist shows a vessel and a nerve passing together superficial to the flexor retinaculum on the medial side. Which two structures are shown? The answer is the ulnar artery and the ulnar nerve. They enter the hand beside the pisiform bone. The median nerve lies deeper, beneath the retinaculum.

Question 3825. Which artery forms the superficial palmar arch in the hand? The answer is the ulnar artery. The arch is completed on the lateral side by the superficial palmar branch of the radial artery. From the arch arise the common palmar digital arteries. The arch lies just beneath the palmar aponeurosis.

Question 3826. At the wrist, the ulnar artery passes through a short canal. Which structure forms the roof of that canal: the flexor retinaculum, the palmar aponeurosis, the extensor retinaculum, or the abductor pollicis brevis? The answer is the flexor retinaculum. The canal lies between that retinaculum and the pisiform bone. The extensor retinaculum lies on the back of the wrist.

Question 3827. True or false: in the lower forearm the ulnar nerve lies on the medial side of the ulnar artery. The answer is true. The two travel together towards the wrist in that order, nerve medially and artery laterally. They keep the same relation as they pass into the hand beside the pisiform bone.

Question 3828. Which branch of the ulnar artery turns deeply into the palm to complete the deep palmar arch: the deep palmar branch, the superficial palmar branch, the anterior interosseous artery, or the dorsal carpal branch? The answer is the deep palmar branch. It passes between the hypothenar muscles into the deep palm. The superficial palmar branch belongs to the radial artery.

Question 3829. Which of these pairs belongs together: the radial artery with the deep palmar arch, the radial artery with the superficial palmar arch, the ulnar artery with the arch on the top of the foot, or the ulnar artery with the anterior tibial artery? The answer is the radial artery with the deep palmar arch. The ulnar artery forms the superficial palmar arch.

Question 3830. A constructed illustration: a person who uses the heel of the hand as a hammer develops pain and pale fingers on the medial side of the hand. Which artery is compressed against the hook of the hamate bone? The answer is the ulnar artery. It passes through the canal at the wrist against that bone. The illustration is a teaching example, and management belongs to the treating clinician.

Question 3831. A described specimen shows an artery in the upper forearm dividing into two vessels that run along the front and the back of the membrane between the two forearm bones. Which artery has divided? The answer is the common interosseous artery, dividing into the anterior and the posterior interosseous arteries. It is a branch of the ulnar artery.

Question 3832. In the lower forearm, which muscle lies immediately deep to the ulnar artery and separates it from the interosseous membrane? The answer is the pronator quadratus. The artery rests on that muscle as it approaches the wrist. The flexor digitorum profundus lies deep to the artery higher up.

Question 3833. Which artery runs down the back of the forearm between the superficial and the deep extensor muscles, accompanied by the posterior interosseous nerve? The answer is the posterior interosseous artery. It reaches the back of the forearm through the opening between the oblique cord and the interosseous membrane. It is a branch of the common interosseous artery.

Question 3834. Which artery runs down the front of the interosseous membrane between the flexor digitorum profundus and the flexor pollicis longus? The answer is the anterior interosseous artery. It is a branch of the common interosseous artery. It ends in the lower forearm by passing behind the pronator quadratus.

Question 3835. Name two arteries of the forearm that arise from the ulnar artery, spoken as a list. The answer is the common interosseous artery and the anterior ulnar recurrent artery. The posterior ulnar recurrent artery may also be named. All three arise in the upper part of the forearm.

Question 3836. At which level does the abdominal aorta divide into the two common iliac arteries? The answer is the level of the fourth lumbar vertebra. The division lies a little to the left of the midline. The small median sacral artery continues downward from that point.

Question 3837. Into which two vessels does each common iliac artery divide: the external and internal iliac arteries, the femoral and popliteal arteries, the renal and suprarenal arteries, or the obturator and gluteal arteries? The answer is the external and internal iliac arteries. The division lies in front of the sacroiliac joint. The obturator and gluteal arteries are branches of the internal iliac artery.

Question 3838. Which ligament does the external iliac artery pass beneath as it leaves the pelvis to enter the thigh? The answer is the inguinal ligament. The artery runs along the pelvic brim and then turns forward under the ligament. Beyond it, the vessel is named the femoral artery.

Question 3839. Into which two divisions does the internal iliac artery split after its short course into the pelvis? The answer is an anterior division and a posterior division. The posterior division supplies the buttock and the back of the pelvic wall. The anterior division supplies the pelvic viscera and the perineum.

Question 3840. Which of these arteries arises from the posterior division of the internal iliac artery: the superior gluteal artery, the obturator artery, the internal pudendal artery, or the inferior vesical artery? The answer is the superior gluteal artery. The iliolumbar and the lateral sacral arteries arise there too. The other three belong to the anterior division.

Question 3841. Which artery leaves the pelvis through the obturator foramen and supplies the muscles on the medial side of the thigh: the internal pudendal artery, the obturator artery, the inferior gluteal artery, or the middle rectal artery? The answer is the obturator artery. It also sends a small branch to the head of the femur. The internal pudendal artery leaves through the greater sciatic foramen.

Question 3842. A described arteriogram of the pelvis shows a short trunk dividing into an anterior and a posterior group of branches, with one branch passing backward through the greater sciatic foramen below the piriformis muscle. Which artery is that branch? The answer is the inferior gluteal artery. The superior gluteal artery leaves above the piriformis. Both come from the internal iliac artery.

Question 3843. A described arteriogram shows a vessel running upward and laterally towards the anterior abdominal wall above the inguinal ligament. Which artery is shown, and from which vessel does it arise? The answer is the inferior epigastric artery, arising from the external iliac artery just above the inguinal ligament. It ascends behind the rectus abdominis muscle.

Question 3844. Besides the inferior epigastric artery, which other named branch does the external iliac artery give just above the inguinal ligament: the deep circumflex iliac artery, the superior gluteal artery, the middle sacral artery, or the superior vesical artery? The answer is the deep circumflex iliac artery. It runs laterally along the iliac crest. The other three arise from the aorta or from the internal iliac artery.

Question 3845. True or false: the external iliac artery becomes the femoral artery after it passes beneath the inguinal ligament. The answer is true. The change of name happens as the vessel crosses the ligament. The femoral artery then enters the femoral triangle of the thigh.

Question 3846. Which tube crosses in front of the common iliac artery at the point where that artery divides? The answer is the ureter. It crosses the vessel at the pelvic brim as it descends towards the bladder. The ureter lies in front of the artery at that crossing.

Question 3847. Which of these pairs belongs together: the superior gluteal artery with the buttock, the obturator artery with the foot, the internal pudendal artery with the kidney, or the iliolumbar artery with the hand? The answer is the superior gluteal artery with the buttock. It leaves the pelvis above the piriformis and supplies the gluteal muscles. The obturator artery supplies the medial thigh instead.

Question 3848. Which artery passes out of the pelvis above the piriformis muscle through the greater sciatic foramen: the inferior gluteal artery, the superior gluteal artery, the internal pudendal artery, or the obturator artery? The answer is the superior gluteal artery. The inferior gluteal artery leaves below the piriformis. The internal pudendal artery leaves below it as well.

Question 3849. A constructed illustration: after a severe pelvic injury a person becomes pale and the pelvis feels unstable, and bleeding is thought to come from branches of the internal iliac artery. Which vessel supplies most of the injured structures in that described region? The answer is the internal iliac artery, whose branches supply the pelvic walls and the viscera. Control of such bleeding belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 3850. Which small unpaired artery arises from the back of the abdominal aorta just above its division into the two common iliac arteries? The answer is the median sacral artery. It runs down the front of the sacrum. It is the continuation of the embryonic dorsal aorta.

Question 3851. A described cross section of the pelvis shows two large vessels lying against the medial wall of the iliac fossa, one on each side, with a tube crossing in front of each of them. Which vessels are shown? The answer is the two common iliac arteries and their divisions. The ureters cross them at the pelvic brim. The external iliac artery continues towards the thigh.

Question 3852. After the internal iliac artery is tied off in an emergency, blood still reaches the pelvic organs. Through which route does it arrive: through the median sacral and the superior rectal arteries, through the pulmonary artery, through the portal vein, or through the thoracic duct? The answer is through the median sacral and the superior rectal arteries, which anastomose with the internal iliac branches. The external iliac branches also contribute.

Question 3853. Which artery is the direct continuation of the external iliac artery after it passes beneath the inguinal ligament? The answer is the femoral artery. It begins at the mid-inguinal point and enters the femoral triangle. Its pulse can be felt there against the head of the femur.

Question 3854. In the femoral triangle, in what order do the femoral nerve, the femoral artery and the femoral vein lie from lateral to medial: nerve, artery, vein; artery, vein, nerve; vein, artery, nerve; or nerve, vein, artery? The answer is nerve, artery, vein. The nerve lies outside the femoral sheath. The artery and the vein lie inside it.

Question 3855. Which large vein lies immediately medial to the femoral artery in the femoral triangle? The answer is the femoral vein. It lies inside the same fibrous sheath as the artery. The femoral nerve lies lateral to the artery and outside the sheath. Above the inguinal ligament the same vein is named the external iliac vein.

Question 3856. Which large branch of the femoral artery arises about 4 centimeters below the inguinal ligament and supplies the deep muscles of the thigh: the profunda femoris artery, the inferior epigastric artery, the popliteal artery, or the obturator artery? The answer is the profunda femoris artery, also called the deep artery of the thigh. It gives the medial and the lateral circumflex femoral arteries.

Question 3857. A constructed illustration: a long catheter is passed into the femoral artery at the groin so that the heart can be examined from within. Which artery has been entered, and what is the name of the vessel at that point? The answer is the femoral artery, entered at the mid-inguinal point. It continues upward as the external iliac artery. The procedure itself belongs to the treating clinician.

Question 3858. A described arteriogram of the upper thigh shows a main trunk dividing into a vessel that continues downward and a large deep branch that turns backward among the muscles. Which two vessels are shown? The answer is the femoral artery and the profunda femoris artery. The deep branch is the main supply of the thigh muscles. It also gives the circumflex femoral arteries.

Question 3859. Which artery supplies the head and neck of the femur through small branches that run along the neck within the retinacula: the medial circumflex femoral artery, the lateral circumflex femoral artery, the obturator artery, or the inferior gluteal artery? The answer is the medial circumflex femoral artery. Interruption of them is a recognized cause of avascular change after a fracture.

Question 3860. True or false: the femoral pulse is felt at the mid-inguinal point, halfway between the pubic symphysis and the anterior superior iliac spine. The answer is true. The artery can be pressed against the head of the femur there. The mid-inguinal point lies a little medial to the midpoint of the inguinal ligament.

Question 3861. Through which opening in a muscle does the femoral artery leave the adductor canal to become the popliteal artery? The answer is the adductor hiatus, the opening in the adductor magnus muscle. The artery passes through it into the back of the knee. The femoral vein passes through the same opening.

Question 3862. A described cross section of the thigh shows a large vessel lying in a tunnel whose roof is formed by a long strap-like muscle and whose walls are formed by muscles on either side. Which artery lies in that tunnel? The answer is the femoral artery in the adductor canal. The saphenous nerve and the femoral vein lie with it.

Question 3863. Which artery of the lower thigh, a branch of the femoral artery or of the profunda femoris, accompanies the saphenous nerve and helps supply the knee joint: the descending genicular artery, the inferior epigastric artery, the middle genicular artery, or the anterior tibial artery? The answer is the descending genicular artery. It arises just above the adductor hiatus. The middle genicular artery comes from the popliteal artery instead.

Question 3864. Which nerve lies in the adductor canal beside the femoral artery and supplies the vastus medialis muscle? The answer is the nerve to vastus medialis. The saphenous nerve also lies in the canal. Both are branches of the femoral nerve. The artery, the vein and the two nerves run together towards the opening in the adductor magnus.

Question 3865. Which of these pairs belongs together: the profunda femoris artery with the deep muscles of the thigh, the inferior epigastric artery with the foot, the popliteal artery with the forearm, or the dorsalis pedis artery with the buttock? The answer is the profunda femoris artery with the deep muscles of the thigh. It is the main artery of that compartment.

Question 3866. Which branches of the profunda femoris artery pierce the adductor magnus muscle to reach the back of the thigh and supply its muscles? The answer is the perforating arteries, usually three in number. The third of them is the continuation of the profunda itself. They form an important collateral path when the femoral artery is blocked above them.

Question 3867. A described dissection of the front of the thigh shows three structures lying in a row from lateral to medial inside a fibrous sheath, with a fourth structure just outside the sheath on the lateral side. Which four structures are described? The answer is the femoral nerve outside the sheath, then the femoral artery, the femoral vein and the femoral canal inside it.

Question 3868. In a described cross section of the upper thigh, the femoral vein lies medial to the femoral artery and the femoral nerve lies lateral to the artery. Which of the three occupies the most lateral position? The answer is the femoral nerve. From lateral to medial the order is nerve, artery and vein. The femoral canal is the most medial space of the group.

Question 3869. Which artery does the femoral artery become after it passes through the adductor hiatus: the popliteal artery, the anterior tibial artery, the profunda femoris artery, or the obturator artery? The answer is the popliteal artery. The name changes as the vessel enters the back of the knee. The anterior tibial artery is one of the two branches into which the popliteal artery divides.

Question 3870. A constructed illustration: a deep cut in the femoral triangle bleeds heavily and the pulse beyond it is lost. Which artery is most likely divided, and why is the bleeding so brisk? The answer is the femoral artery. It is large and lies close to the skin at the mid-inguinal point. Control of such bleeding belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 3871. Which artery begins at the adductor hiatus and ends at the lower border of the popliteus muscle? The answer is the popliteal artery. It runs down the back of the knee behind the joint. It divides into the anterior and the posterior tibial arteries.

Question 3872. Which structure lies immediately in front of the popliteal artery: the femur and the capsule of the knee joint, the popliteal vein, the tibial nerve, or the small saphenous vein? The answer is the femur and the capsule of the knee joint. The artery is the deepest structure in the popliteal fossa. The vein and the nerve lie behind it.

Question 3873. Which vein lies immediately behind the popliteal artery in the popliteal fossa? The answer is the popliteal vein. It lies superficial to the artery, that is, nearer the skin. The tibial nerve lies behind the vein. Artery, vein and nerve lie one behind another against the bone.

Question 3874. Which branch of the popliteal artery pierces the oblique popliteal ligament to reach the interior of the knee joint: the middle genicular artery, the superior medial genicular artery, the anterior tibial artery, or the sural artery? The answer is the middle genicular artery. It supplies the cruciate ligaments and the synovial membrane. The sural arteries supply the heads of the gastrocnemius.

Question 3875. A described radiograph of the lower thigh shows a fracture line just above the knee with the lower fragment displaced backward. Which vessel is at risk because it lies against the bone at that level? The answer is the popliteal artery. It lies directly against the lower end of the femur and the knee capsule. The displacement can stretch or tear it.

Question 3876. A described arteriogram of the knee region shows five small branches wrapping around the lower end of the femur and the upper end of the tibia and joining one another in a network. What is that network called? The answer is the genicular anastomosis around the knee. It links the popliteal artery with branches of the femoral artery above.

Question 3877. At which level does the popliteal artery end by dividing: at the lower border of the popliteus muscle, at the middle of the thigh, at the ankle, or at the neck of the fibula? The answer is at the lower border of the popliteus muscle. There it divides into the anterior and the posterior tibial arteries. The level lies a little below the knee joint.

Question 3878. True or false: the popliteal artery is the most superficial structure in the popliteal fossa. The answer is false. It is the deepest structure there, lying against the bone and the joint capsule. The popliteal vein and then the tibial nerve lie behind it.

Question 3879. A constructed illustration: a knee is dislocated backward and the pulse at the ankle disappears. Why is the popliteal artery especially vulnerable in that described injury? The answer is that the artery is tethered above at the adductor hiatus and below at the lower border of the popliteus, so it cannot move with the joint. The injury is described only as an illustration. Management belongs to the treating clinician.

Question 3880. Which of these pairs belongs together: the popliteal artery with the back of the knee, the popliteal artery with the front of the elbow, the popliteal artery with the palm of the hand, or the popliteal artery with the sole of the foot? The answer is the popliteal artery with the back of the knee. The brachial artery lies at the front of the elbow.

Question 3881. Which terminal branch of the popliteal artery passes forward through the opening in the upper part of the interosseous membrane? The answer is the anterior tibial artery. It reaches the front of the leg through that opening. The posterior tibial artery stays behind.

Question 3882. In some people the popliteal artery divides into its two terminal branches far above the knee. Which vessel then lies in an unusual place: the anterior tibial artery, the dorsalis pedis artery, the medial plantar artery, or the peroneal artery? The answer is the anterior tibial artery, which may then run in front of the popliteus muscle. This high division is a normal variant.

Question 3883. A described dissection of the back of the knee shows a diamond-shaped space bounded above by two muscles from the thigh and below by the two heads of a muscle of the calf, with vessels and nerves lying in fat within it. Which space is described? The answer is the popliteal fossa. Its floor is the femur, the knee capsule and the popliteus.

Question 3884. Which muscle forms the lower boundary of the course of the popliteal artery, the muscle at whose lower border the artery divides? The answer is the popliteus. It lies in the floor of the popliteal fossa. The artery ends at its lower border.

Question 3885. Name two branches of the popliteal artery that take part in the network around the knee, spoken as a list. The answer is the superior medial genicular artery and the inferior lateral genicular artery. The middle genicular artery and the other genicular arteries may also be named. All of them join the anastomosis around the joint.

Question 3886. Which artery begins at the lower border of the popliteus muscle, passes through the interosseous membrane and runs down the front of the leg? The answer is the anterior tibial artery. It is the smaller of the two terminal branches of the popliteal artery. It ends at the ankle as the dorsalis pedis artery.

Question 3887. Which artery continues the anterior tibial artery across the front of the ankle onto the top of the foot: the dorsalis pedis artery, the posterior tibial artery, the peroneal artery, or the medial plantar artery? The answer is the dorsalis pedis artery. Its pulse can be felt on the top of the foot. The posterior tibial artery passes behind the medial malleolus instead.

Question 3888. In the lower part of the leg, between which two tendons does the anterior tibial artery lie as it approaches the ankle? The answer is between the tendons of the extensor hallucis longus and the extensor digitorum longus. Higher up it lies between the tibialis anterior and the extensor digitorum longus. The deep peroneal nerve runs beside it.

Question 3889. Where is the posterior tibial pulse normally felt: behind the medial malleolus, in front of the lateral malleolus, on the top of the foot, or in the popliteal fossa? The answer is behind the medial malleolus. The artery lies there between the malleolus and the tendo calcaneus. The pulse on the top of the foot belongs to the dorsalis pedis artery.

Question 3890. A constructed illustration: a fracture of the shaft of the tibia is complicated by a tense and painful anterior compartment of the leg. Which artery of that compartment is compressed? The answer is the anterior tibial artery. It runs in the anterior compartment with the deep peroneal nerve. Management of the compartment belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 3891. A described arteriogram of the lower leg shows one vessel descending behind the medial malleolus and another passing forward above the ankle. Which two arteries are shown? The answer is the posterior tibial artery and the anterior tibial artery. The posterior one continues into the sole of the foot. The anterior one continues as the dorsalis pedis artery.

Question 3892. Which artery arises from the posterior tibial artery and runs down beside the fibula, giving a perforating branch above the ankle: the peroneal artery, the anterior tibial artery, the obturator artery, or the descending genicular artery? The answer is the peroneal artery, also called the fibular artery. It is the largest branch of the posterior tibial artery. Its perforating branch pierces the interosseous membrane above the ankle.

Question 3893. Which of these pairs belongs together: the posterior tibial artery with the sole of the foot, the posterior tibial artery with the back of the hand, the anterior tibial artery with the buttock, or the peroneal artery with the face? The answer is the posterior tibial artery with the sole of the foot. It divides into the medial and the lateral plantar arteries.

Question 3894. Which artery of the leg can be identified in a described dissection by its course beside the fibula and by a terminal branch that pierces the interosseous membrane above the ankle? The answer is the peroneal artery. Its perforating branch joins the network at the ankle and can replace the dorsalis pedis artery. That variant explains why a foot pulse may be felt when the anterior tibial artery is small.

Question 3895. A constructed illustration: after a crush injury of the leg the anterior compartment is tight, the deep peroneal nerve is failing, and yet the dorsalis pedis pulse is still felt. Why can that be? The answer is that the peroneal artery may keep the foot supplied through its perforating branch while the anterior tibial artery is compressed. A pulse therefore does not exclude raised pressure in the compartment.

Question 3896. In some people the dorsalis pedis pulse cannot be felt at all. Which explanation fits best: the artery may be replaced by the perforating branch of the peroneal artery, it is absent in everyone with heart disease, it appears only during exercise, or the anterior tibial artery never forms? The answer is replacement by the perforating branch of the peroneal artery. That replacement is a normal variant.

Question 3897. In the tarsal tunnel behind the medial malleolus, which structure lies between the tendons of the flexor digitorum longus and the flexor hallucis longus? The answer is the posterior tibial artery, with the tibial nerve beside it. The tunnel is roofed by the flexor retinaculum. The artery divides within it into the medial and the lateral plantar arteries.

Question 3898. A described cross section of the calf shows a deep vessel lying behind the tibia on the posterior surface of a muscle that covers the interosseous membrane, with the soleus and the gastrocnemius behind it. Which artery is shown? The answer is the posterior tibial artery. It runs downward deep to the soleus on the tibialis posterior. The peroneal artery lies nearer the fibula.

Question 3899. Into which two arteries does the posterior tibial artery divide in the sole of the foot: the medial and lateral plantar arteries, the dorsalis pedis and arcuate arteries, the anterior and posterior tibial arteries, or the peroneal and sural arteries? The answer is the medial and lateral plantar arteries. The lateral plantar artery is the larger and helps form the plantar arch.

Question 3900. Which artery completes the plantar arch in the sole of the foot by joining the lateral plantar artery: the deep plantar branch of the dorsalis pedis artery, the superficial palmar branch of the radial artery, the anterior tibial artery, or the medial plantar artery? The answer is the deep plantar branch of the dorsalis pedis artery. It passes into the sole between the heads of the first dorsal interosseous muscle.

To summarize, the radial and the ulnar arteries continue the brachial artery below the elbow, the radial running on the lateral side of the forearm to form the deep palmar arch and the ulnar crossing to the medial side to form the superficial palmar arch. The abdominal aorta divides at the fourth lumbar vertebra into the common iliac arteries, each of which divides into an external iliac artery that becomes the femoral artery and an internal iliac artery whose branches supply the pelvic walls, the pelvic viscera and the buttock. The femoral artery passes through the femoral triangle and the adductor canal to become the popliteal artery at the adductor hiatus, and the popliteal artery, the deepest structure behind the knee, ends at the lower border of the popliteus as the anterior and the posterior tibial arteries. The anterior tibial artery reaches the front of the leg through the interosseous membrane and continues as the dorsalis pedis artery, while the posterior tibial artery runs behind the medial malleolus to divide into the plantar arteries and gives the peroneal artery beside the fibula. At the groin the femoral nerve lies lateral to the femoral artery and the femoral vein medial to it, and behind the knee the popliteal vein lies between the artery and the tibial nerve.
