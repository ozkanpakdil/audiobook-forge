# Chapter 84 — Eye

This chapter walks around the eye itself, the globe that turns light into a signal the brain can read, and it takes the listener through the coats of that globe and the contents they enclose. It begins with the eyeball as a whole, then covers the cornea, the sclera, the choroid, the retina and finally the lens. It holds one hundred questions, and each one gives its answer immediately after the question and then explains why that answer is right, so nothing has to be held in mind until the end. The eye is the one part of the body where living nerve tissue and blood vessels can be seen directly, and every structure named here supports that view. This is educational material, and it supports rather than replaces an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 8301. The wall of the eyeball is built of three concentric coats. What is the name of the outermost coat, and which two parts does it have? The answer is the fibrous coat, made of the sclera and the cornea. The sclera is the opaque white part that covers most of the surface. The cornea is the transparent part at the front, and this tough coat holds the shape of the eye.

Question 8302. Which of these fills the large space behind the lens and in front of the retina: the aqueous humor, the vitreous body, the cerebrospinal fluid, or the tears? The answer is the vitreous body. It is a clear gel that fills the posterior segment of the eyeball and presses the retina against the wall.

Question 8303. The lens divides the interior of the eye into an anterior segment and a posterior segment. Which structures lie in the anterior segment, and what fills the posterior segment? The answer is that the aqueous humor and the iris lie in the anterior segment, and the vitreous body fills the posterior segment.

Question 8304. A described magnetic resonance imaging scan of the orbits shows a rounded structure with three concentric bands of different brightness and a clear oval body near its front. Which organ is shown, and which coat lies between the other two? The answer is the eyeball, with the vascular coat between the fibrous coat and the retina. The clear oval body is the lens.

Question 8305. The eyeball lies in the bony orbit. What is the name of the fatty tissue that cushions it there and allows it to move freely? The answer is the orbital fat. It fills the space around the globe inside the cone of muscles. The eyeball itself is not fixed to the bone.

Question 8306. A constructed illustration: a blunt blow to the eye causes blood to layer in the space between the cornea and the iris. Which space has filled, and which structures are the likely source of the blood? The answer is the anterior chamber, with blood coming from the iris and the ciliary body. That space normally holds clear aqueous humor.

Question 8307. At which places are the coats of the eye firmly attached to one another: at the fovea, at the optic disc and the ora serrata, at the cornea, or at the equator? The answer is at the optic disc and the ora serrata. The choroid is fixed to the sclera at those two places, while the retina is fixed to the pigment epithelium more widely.

Question 8308. The optic axis passes through the centers of the cornea and the posterior pole, while the visual axis runs from the point being looked at to the fovea. How do the two lines differ? The answer is that they are not the same line. The visual axis lies a little to the nasal side of the optic axis, and the two cross at the nodal point.

Question 8309. Which coat of the eye carries the iris, the ciliary body and the choroid, and by what name is that whole coat known? The answer is the vascular coat, also called the uvea. It lies between the fibrous coat outside and the retina inside. The iris is its most anterior part and the choroid its most posterior part.

Question 8310. The vitreous body lies immediately behind which structure, and immediately in front of which coat of the eye? The answer is behind the lens and in front of the retina. It fills the posterior segment, which is the larger of the two segments of the eyeball. The retina is pressed against the wall by it.

Question 8311. Which of these best describes the shape of the normal adult eyeball: a perfect sphere, a sphere a little longer from front to back than across, a flattened disc, or a cone? The answer is a sphere a little longer from front to back than across. Its diameter is about 24 millimeters in each direction. The cornea bulges slightly in front of that rounded shape.

Question 8312. True or false: the three coats of the eye are the same length, so all three reach the front of the eye together. The answer is false. The sclera becomes the cornea at the front, the choroid continues forward as the ciliary body, and the retina ends at the ora serrata. Each coat therefore ends at a different line.

Question 8313. A described section of the front of the eye shows one space between the cornea and the iris and a narrow slit between the iris and the lens, both filled with the same clear fluid. Which two chambers are described, and why is one a slit? The answer is the anterior chamber and the posterior chamber. The posterior chamber is narrow because the iris lies close to the lens.

Question 8314. Which clear fluid fills the chambers in front of the lens, and which two structures does it nourish? The answer is the aqueous humor, which nourishes the cornea and the lens. Neither of those structures has blood vessels of its own, so both depend on this fluid. It is made by the ciliary body and leaves the eye at the angle between the cornea and the iris.

Question 8315. Which structure secretes the fluid that fills the chambers in front of the lens: the corneal endothelium, the ciliary epithelium, the iris stroma, or the lens capsule? The answer is the ciliary epithelium. It covers the ciliary processes, and the fluid it makes nourishes the cornea and the lens. The corneal endothelium removes fluid from the eye rather than making it.

Question 8316. Name the three coats of the eyeball in order from outside inward, and say which of them reaches the very front of the eye. The answer is the fibrous coat, the vascular coat and the neural coat. Only the fibrous coat reaches the front, where it becomes the transparent cornea.

Question 8317. Which of these pairs belongs together: the sclera with the transparent front of the eye, the choroid with the pigmented vascular layer behind the retina, the retina with the white outer coat, or the iris with the gel behind the lens? The answer is the choroid with the pigmented vascular layer behind the retina. The sclera is the white outer coat and the retina the neural coat.

Question 8318. Which transparent structure forms the window at the front of the eye, and what lies immediately behind it? The answer is the cornea, with the aqueous humor of the anterior chamber behind it. It is the front part of the fibrous coat and is continuous with the sclera at its edge. It has no blood vessels, so it stays clear.

Question 8319. How many layers does the cornea have: two, three, five, or eight? The answer is five. They are the epithelium, the anterior limiting membrane, the stroma, the posterior limiting membrane and the endothelium. The epithelium is the outermost of the five and the endothelium the innermost, and the stroma forms most of the thickness.

Question 8320. A described section through the front of the eye shows a transparent layer that is thicker in the middle than at its edge, with a single layer of flat cells on its inner surface. Which structure is described, and what is that inner layer? The answer is the cornea, and the inner layer is the endothelium. The endothelium pumps fluid out of the cornea and keeps it clear.

Question 8321. A constructed illustration: a contact lens is worn for far too long, and the cornea becomes swollen and cloudy over its whole surface. Which layer has failed in its work? The answer is the endothelium. It normally pumps fluid out of the stroma, and when it cannot, the cornea takes up water and loses its transparency.

Question 8322. What is the name of the ring-shaped junction where the cornea meets the sclera, and what lies in its depth? The answer is the limbus, with the trabecular meshwork in its depth. Aqueous humor leaves the eye through that meshwork. The stem cells that renew the corneal surface also lie at the limbus.

Question 8323. Which of these statements about the cornea is correct: it is the most sensitive part of the eye, it is richly supplied with blood vessels, it is opaque, or it contains the rods and cones? The answer is that it is the most sensitive part of the eye. It is packed with nerve endings from the ophthalmic division of the trigeminal nerve.

Question 8324. Immediately deep to the corneal epithelium lies which layer, and immediately deep to the corneal endothelium lies which space? The answer is the anterior limiting membrane deep to the epithelium, and the anterior chamber deep to the endothelium. The stroma lies between the two limiting membranes. The endothelium therefore has aqueous humor on its inner face.

Question 8325. True or false: the cornea is the main refracting surface of the eye. The answer is true. It provides about two thirds of the total refractive power of the eye. The lens provides the rest and can change its power, but the cornea cannot.

Question 8326. Which of these statements about the corneal endothelium is correct: it is a single layer of cells, it is several cells thick, it lies beneath the epithelium, or it plays no part in keeping the cornea clear? The answer is that it is a single layer of cells. It lines the inner surface of the cornea, next to the aqueous humor.

Question 8327. A described view of an eye shows a cornea that is cloudy over its whole surface, with fine new vessels growing in from the limbus. What has happened to the cornea, and why has it lost its clarity? The answer is that it has been scarred and has grown new vessels. Scar tissue and vessels scatter light instead of letting it pass straight through.

Question 8328. Which layer of the cornea lies between the two limiting membranes and forms most of its thickness? The answer is the stroma. It is made of many thin layers of collagen fibers lying in regular sheets. That regular arrangement is what lets the cornea stay clear.

Question 8329. A constructed illustration: the corneal reflex is absent on one side while it is normal on the other. Which nerve carries the sensory limb of that reflex? The answer is the ophthalmic division of the trigeminal nerve, with the surface of the cornea touched. The motor limb is carried by the facial nerve to the muscle that closes the lids.

Question 8330. Which of these pairs belongs together: the corneal epithelium with the tear film, the corneal stroma with the endothelium, the corneal endothelium with the iris, or the limbus with the fovea? The answer is the corneal epithelium with the tear film. The epithelium is kept moist and supplied with oxygen by the tears.

Question 8331. Which of these explains why the cornea refracts more light than the lens, although the two have a similar curve: it is denser, it meets air while the lens is bathed in fluid, it is thicker, or it has more cells? The answer is that it meets air while the lens is bathed in fluid. The change in refractive index there is greater than at the lens.

Question 8332. Which structure forms the white outer coat of the eye, and what is its main job? The answer is the sclera. It is a tough fibrous layer that keeps the shape of the eyeball and protects what lies inside. The muscles that move the eye are attached to it.

Question 8333. The sclera is thinnest at which of these places: around the optic nerve, just behind the insertions of the rectus muscles, at the equator, or at the limbus? The answer is just behind the insertions of the rectus muscles. The tendons of those muscles blend with the sclera there, over its thinnest part. The sclera is thickest at the back, around the optic nerve.

Question 8334. Which coat lies immediately deep to the sclera at the back of the eye, and which coat lies immediately inside that one? The answer is the choroid deep to the sclera, with the retina inside the choroid. The coats are stacked from outside inward as the sclera, the choroid and the retina. At the front the sclera continues as the cornea.

Question 8335. A described section through the wall of the eye at the back shows a thick white fibrous layer pierced by a bundle of nerve fibers and by several small vessels around it. Which coat is pierced, and which opening does the nerve use? The answer is the sclera, pierced by the optic nerve through the scleral canal.

Question 8336. Where does the optic nerve leave the eyeball, and what is the opening through the sclera called? The answer is at the optic disc, through the scleral canal. The disc lies about 3 millimeters to the nasal side of the posterior pole. The fibers of the retina converge there to form the nerve.

Question 8337. A constructed illustration: an eye is painful and inflamed, and the white coat looks thin and bluish. Which coat is affected, and why does the blue color appear? The answer is the sclera, and the bluish tinge appears because it has become thin enough to let the dark choroid show through. The illustration is described only.

Question 8338. Which of these structures passes through the sclera at the back of the eye: the optic nerve, the trochlea, the levator palpebrae superioris, or the lacrimal gland? The answer is the optic nerve. It leaves the eye through the sclera and carries the fibers of the retina toward the brain. The other structures lie in the orbit or in the eyelid.

Question 8339. Name two kinds of vessel that pierce the sclera around the optic nerve. The answer is the short posterior ciliary arteries and the long posterior ciliary arteries. The short ones supply the choroid, and the long ones run forward to the front of the eye. Small veins also leave the eye through the sclera.

Question 8340. A described dissection of the front of the eye shows a narrow channel running around the eye in the deepest part of the junction between the cornea and the sclera. What is that channel, and what flows along it? The answer is the venous channel called the canal of Schlemm, which carries aqueous humor away from the eye.

Question 8341. Which of these statements about the fibers of the sclera is correct: they are arranged irregularly in all directions, they lie in regular parallel sheets, they are evenly spaced, or they form no bundles? The answer is that they are arranged irregularly in all directions. That irregular arrangement scatters light and makes the sclera white.

Question 8342. Which coat lies immediately between the sclera and the retina, and which retinal layer rests against it? The answer is the choroid, with the pigment epithelium of the retina against it. That epithelium is the outermost layer of the retina. The choroid supplies the outer part of the retina with blood.

Question 8343. A described view of the back of the eye shows a pale round disc with vessels radiating from it, and beside the disc a dark crescent. What is that crescent, and what does it show about the sclera? The answer is a crescent of choroid and retinal pigment, seen where the scleral ring around the disc is incomplete.

Question 8344. Which of these pairs belongs together: the sclera with the opaque outer coat, the cornea with the vascular coat, the choroid with the fibrous coat, or the retina with the middle coat? The answer is the sclera with the opaque outer coat. The cornea is the transparent front of that same fibrous coat.

Question 8345. Which layer of the eye lies between the sclera and the retina, and what kind of tissue is it? The answer is the choroid, a dark and vascular layer. It is the posterior part of the vascular coat. It nourishes the outer part of the retina and is firmly joined to the pigment epithelium inside.

Question 8346. Which of these describes the blood supply of the choroid: a single artery, a rich network of vessels arranged in layers, a capillary bed supplied by branches of the central retinal artery, or no supply of its own? The answer is a rich network of vessels arranged in layers. The innermost layer is a dense bed of capillaries, fed by the short posterior ciliary arteries.

Question 8347. Immediately outside the choroid lies which coat, and immediately inside it lies which layer of the retina? The answer is the sclera outside and the pigment epithelium inside. The choroid is firmly joined to that epithelium. This is why the retina lifts away from the wall as a sheet when it detaches.

Question 8348. A described section of the back of the eye shows a dark layer packed with vessels, thicker at the back than at the front, ending at a scalloped line. What is that line, and what lies just beyond it? The answer is the ora serrata, and beyond it the ciliary body continues forward. The retina ends at the same scalloped line.

Question 8349. What is the name of the dark pigment in the choroid that absorbs stray light inside the eye? The answer is melanin. It lies in the pigment cells of the choroid. By absorbing light it stops reflections that would blur the picture formed on the retina.

Question 8350. A constructed illustration: a patient notices a sudden curtain across the vision, and a sheet of retina is found lifted from the wall of the eye. Which two layers have come apart, and which layer stays where it is? The answer is the neural retina and the pigment epithelium, which have separated. The pigment epithelium stays with the choroid, and fluid collects in the space between.

Question 8351. Which layer of the choroid lies closest to the retina: the layer of large vessels, the layer of medium vessels, the layer of capillaries, or the suprachoroidal space? The answer is the layer of capillaries. It lies immediately outside the pigment epithelium and is fed by the short posterior ciliary arteries. The suprachoroidal space lies on the outer side of the choroid, beside the sclera.

Question 8352. A described dissection shows a thin brown layer peeled from the inner surface of the sclera, loosely joined to it except at two fixed points. Which layer is it, and where is it fixed? The answer is the choroid, fixed at the optic nerve behind and at the ora serrata in front. The space between it and the sclera is the suprachoroidal space.

Question 8353. Which arteries supply the choroid, and which supply the iris and the ciliary body? The answer is that the posterior ciliary arteries supply the choroid, and the anterior ciliary arteries supply the iris and the ciliary body. The short posterior ciliary arteries pierce the sclera around the optic nerve.

Question 8354. Which of these structures lies between the retina and the sclera: the cornea, the choroid, the lens, or the vitreous body? The answer is the choroid. It lies between the retina inside and the sclera outside. The lens and the vitreous body lie in the middle of the eye, in front of the retina.

Question 8355. A described study of the vessels of the eye shows one set of arteries fanning out behind the eye and another running forward toward the front. Which two systems are shown, and which parts of the vascular coat do they supply? The answer is the posterior ciliary arteries for the choroid and the anterior ciliary arteries for the iris and the ciliary body.

Question 8356. True or false: the choroid is loosely joined to the sclera but firmly joined to the retina. The answer is true. Only the suprachoroidal space and a few vessels and nerves join the choroid to the sclera, whereas its attachment to the pigment epithelium is much firmer. The choroid is also firmly fixed to the sclera at the optic nerve and at the ora serrata.

Question 8357. Name the three parts of the vascular coat of the eye, spoken from front to back. The answer is the iris, the ciliary body and the choroid. All three are continuous with one another. Together they form the uvea, the middle coat of the eye.

Question 8358. Which of these functions belongs to the choroid: it changes the shape of the lens, it absorbs stray light and nourishes the outer retina, it moves the eye, or it makes the aqueous humor? The answer is that it absorbs stray light and nourishes the outer retina. Its pigment stops light from scattering inside the eye.

Question 8359. Which coat of the eye contains the light-sensitive cells, and what are those cells called? The answer is the retina, and its light-sensitive cells are the rods and the cones. The rods work in dim light and the cones in bright light and in color. The retina is the innermost of the three coats.

Question 8360. Where in the retina are the cones packed most densely: at the optic disc, at the fovea, at the ora serrata, or at the equator? The answer is at the fovea. The fovea lies at the center of the macula and gives the sharpest vision. The optic disc has no light-sensitive cells at all.

Question 8361. A described section of the retina shows a layer of rods and cones on its outer side and a layer of nerve fibers on its inner side, next to the vitreous body. Which layer is outermost, and which way does light travel through them? The answer is that the rods and cones lie outermost, against the pigment epithelium, and light must cross the nerve fiber layer first.

Question 8362. Which part of the retina has no light-sensitive cells and is therefore called the blind spot? The answer is the optic disc. It is the place where the fibers of the optic nerve leave the eye. The retinal vessels also enter and leave there.

Question 8363. Which of these structures lies at the center of the macula: the optic disc, the fovea, the ora serrata, or the ciliary body? The answer is the fovea. The macula is the small area of the retina that gives the sharpest vision, and the fovea is its center. The optic disc lies about 3 millimeters to its nasal side.

Question 8364. Which retinal layer rests directly against the pigment epithelium, and which retinal layer faces the vitreous body? The answer is that the rods and cones rest against the pigment epithelium, while the layer of nerve fibers faces the vitreous body. Light therefore crosses the whole thickness of the retina before it reaches the receptors.

Question 8365. A constructed illustration: pressure inside the skull is raised, and the disc at the back of the eye is swollen with blurred margins. Why does that pressure reach the eye? The answer is that the sheath around the optic nerve carries cerebrospinal fluid from the skull to the back of the eye, so the disc swells.

Question 8366. What is the name of the small central area of the retina that gives the sharpest vision, and what is its center called? The answer is the macula, and its center is the fovea. The fovea contains cones only. Around the macula the retina holds a mixture of rods and cones.

Question 8367. Which cells of the retina work in dim light: the cones, the rods, the ganglion cells, or the pigment cells? The answer is the rods. They are far more numerous than the cones and respond to very little light. They do not separate colors, so a dim scene looks gray.

Question 8368. A described section through the retina at the fovea shows the layers pushed to the side so that the receptors lie near the surface, with a thick rim of cells around them. Why is vision sharpest at that point? The answer is that the cones there are packed tightly and light reaches them with little scattering.

Question 8369. Which vessels supply the inner part of the retina: the choriocapillaris, branches of the central retinal artery, the anterior ciliary arteries, or the suprachoroidal vessels? The answer is branches of the central retinal artery. That artery enters the eye at the optic disc and divides into branches that run in the nerve fiber layer.

Question 8370. A described view of the retina shows a delicate pattern of vessels radiating from a pale round area and crossing the inner surface of the retina. Which vessels are these, and from where do they come? The answer is the branches of the central retinal artery and its veins, radiating from the optic disc.

Question 8371. Which of these orders of the retinal layers runs from outside inward: pigment epithelium, rods and cones, then nerve fibers; rods and cones, pigment epithelium, then nerve fibers; nerve fibers, rods and cones, then pigment epithelium; or pigment epithelium, nerve fibers, then rods and cones? The answer is the pigment epithelium, then the rods and cones, then the nerve fibers. That order is why light crosses the nerve fibers first.

Question 8372. True or false: the retina has two separate blood supplies, one for its outer part and one for its inner part. The answer is true. The outer part, including the receptors, is nourished by the choriocapillaris of the choroid, and the inner part by branches of the central retinal artery. Those two systems meet but do not overlap.

Question 8373. A constructed illustration: an older patient has lost central vision in one eye while the outer parts of the field are still seen. Which part of the retina is affected, and which cells are most affected there? The answer is the macula, and the cones at the fovea are affected first.

Question 8374. Which part of the retina has no blood vessels crossing its center, and why does that help vision? The answer is the fovea, whose center is free of vessels. If vessels crossed it, they would scatter light before it reached the cones. Its supply comes from the choriocapillaris behind it.

Question 8375. Which of these statements about the rods and cones is correct: the rods are more numerous and work in dim light, the cones are more numerous, the rods give color vision, or the cones work only in dim light? The answer is that the rods are more numerous and work in dim light. Cones are concentrated at the fovea and give sharp colored vision.

Question 8376. A described view shows pale bundles of fibers arching above and below a darker central area and running toward a pale round disc. What are those bundles, and why do they curve? The answer is the nerve fiber layer of the retina, whose fibers run to the optic disc. They curve around the macula because they cannot pass through the fovea.

Question 8377. A line is drawn from the center of the cornea straight back through the pupil to the retina. Which structures does that line cross, in order from front to back? The answer is the cornea, the aqueous humor of the anterior chamber, the pupil, the lens, the vitreous body and finally the retina. That line is the optic axis.

Question 8378. Which cells of the retina send their fibers out of the eye to form the optic nerve? The answer is the ganglion cells, whose axons form the nerve fiber layer. The fibers gather at the optic disc and leave the eye there. Their cell bodies lie in the ganglion cell layer.

Question 8379. Which of these statements about the fovea is correct: it contains cones only, it contains rods only, it is the place where the optic nerve leaves the eye, or it is the thickest part of the retina? The answer is that it contains cones only. Each cone there is joined to a single bipolar cell, which gives fine detail.

Question 8380. Which retinal layer lies immediately inside the pigment epithelium, and which cells are found in it? The answer is the layer of rods and cones, which holds the outer parts of the receptor cells. The pigment epithelium wraps around the tips of those receptors. That close contact is what is lost when the retina detaches.

Question 8381. A constructed illustration: a patient with long-standing raised blood pressure has a swollen disc and small hemorrhages that follow the bundles of nerve fibers in the retina. Why do those hemorrhages take that shape? The answer is that the fibers of the nerve fiber layer run in bundles toward the optic disc, and blood tracks along them.

Question 8382. Which part of the retina lies farthest forward, ending at a scalloped edge near the equator of the eye? The answer is the front edge of the retina, which ends at the ora serrata. Beyond that line the ciliary body continues forward. The retina does not reach the front of the eye.

Question 8383. Which of these explains why the optic disc is a blind spot: it holds no receptors, it has no blood supply, it lies too far forward, or it is covered by the choroid? The answer is that the disc holds no receptors, only the axons of the ganglion cells on their way out of the eye. The brain fills in the missing part of the picture, so the gap is not noticed.

Question 8384. Which of these pairs belongs together: the macula with the sharpest vision, the optic disc with the sharpest vision, the ora serrata with color vision, or the fovea with the blind spot? The answer is the macula with the sharpest vision. The fovea lies at its center and holds the greatest density of cones.

Question 8385. Which of these structures separates the neural retina from the choroid: the sclera, the pigment epithelium, the vitreous body, or the ciliary body? The answer is the pigment epithelium. It is the outermost layer of the retina and lies against the choroid. The neural retina can separate from it, and that separation is a retinal detachment.

Question 8386. Which structure of the eye changes its shape to focus on objects at different distances, and what is that change called? The answer is the lens, and the change is accommodation. The ciliary muscle controls it. When that muscle contracts, the lens becomes rounder and the eye focuses on something near.

Question 8387. Which of these holds the lens in place: the suspensory ligament, the optic nerve, the canal of Schlemm, or the ora serrata? The answer is the suspensory ligament. Its fibers run from the ciliary body to the equator of the lens. They keep the lens centered behind the pupil.

Question 8388. A described section through the front of the eye shows a flattened oval body with a dense center, hung by fine strands from a ring of tissue. Which structure is described, and what is the ring? The answer is the lens, suspended from the ciliary body. The fine strands form the suspensory ligament, and the dense center is the nucleus.

Question 8389. Which part of the lens lies in the center, and which part surrounds it? The answer is that the nucleus lies in the center and the cortex surrounds it. The lens grows through life by adding new fibers to the outside. That is why the nucleus is its oldest part.

Question 8390. Which of these statements about the lens is correct: it supplies about one third of the power of the eye and can be changed, it supplies most of the power, it has no refractive power, or it refracts more than the cornea? The answer is that it supplies about one third of the power of the eye and can be changed. The lens adjusts its power to focus on near objects.

Question 8391. A constructed illustration: the pupil of one eye looks white instead of black, and that eye does not see well. Which structure lies directly behind the pupil, and how does the loss of its clarity explain the appearance? The answer is the lens. A clear lens lets light pass, so the pupil looks black, while a cloudy lens scatters light back and looks white.

Question 8392. Immediately behind the iris and the pupil lies which structure, and which clear gel lies immediately behind that? The answer is the lens behind the iris and the vitreous body behind the lens. The posterior chamber, filled with aqueous humor, lies between the iris and the lens. The vitreous body fills the rest of the eye behind the lens.

Question 8393. Which muscle changes the shape of the lens, and what happens to the lens when that muscle contracts? The answer is the ciliary muscle, and the lens becomes rounder and thicker. That change increases its power for near vision. When the muscle relaxes, the lens flattens again for distance.

Question 8394. Which of these is the transparent structure that lies immediately behind the pupil: the cornea, the lens, the retina, or the choroid? The answer is the lens. It lies behind the iris and the pupil and in front of the vitreous body. The cornea lies in front of the pupil.

Question 8395. True or false: the lens goes on adding new fibers throughout life, so its center is its oldest part. The answer is true. New fibers are laid down at the edge of the lens, so it grows larger and less elastic with age.

Question 8396. A constructed illustration: a person cannot see distant objects clearly, and the eye is longer than usual from front to back. Where does the image form, and which structure inside the eye could change the focus? The answer is that the image forms in front of the retina, and the only structure that can change the focus is the lens.

Question 8397. Which of these describes the fibers of the lens correctly: they lose their nuclei as they mature and fill the lens, they are replaced every year, they contain blood vessels, or they are the same cells as the corneal epithelium? The answer is that they lose their nuclei as they mature and fill the lens. They are never replaced.

Question 8398. Which of these pairs belongs together: the ciliary muscle with the shape of the lens, the suspensory ligament with the size of the pupil, the iris with the shape of the lens, or the cornea with the shape of the lens? The answer is the ciliary muscle with the shape of the lens. The iris controls the size of the pupil instead.

Question 8399. Which of these structures is found between the iris and the vitreous body: the cornea, the lens, the retina, or the sclera? The answer is the lens. It lies behind the iris, and the vitreous body lies behind it. The cornea lies in front of the iris.

Question 8400. Which of these explains why the lens must change shape to focus on a near object: the distance from the lens to the retina changes, the refractive power of the eye must increase, the pupil must widen, or the cornea must flatten? The answer is that the refractive power of the eye must increase. The lens does that by becoming rounder.

To summarize, the eyeball is built of three concentric coats: the fibrous coat of sclera and cornea outside, the vascular coat of choroid, ciliary body and iris in the middle, and the neural coat of retina inside. The cornea is the transparent front window, without blood vessels but richly supplied with nerves, and its endothelium pumps fluid out to keep it clear. The sclera is the tough white outer coat, thinnest behind the insertions of the rectus muscles and pierced at the back by the optic nerve. The choroid is the dark vascular layer between the sclera and the retina, and the retina carries the rods and cones, with the fovea at the center of the macula for sharp vision and the optic disc for the blind spot. The lens lies behind the pupil and the iris, held by the suspensory ligament, and the ciliary muscle changes its shape to add power for near vision.
