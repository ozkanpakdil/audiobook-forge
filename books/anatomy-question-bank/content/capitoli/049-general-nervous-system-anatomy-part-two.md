# Chapter 49 — General Nervous System Anatomy, Part Two

This chapter continues the general anatomy of the nervous system by looking at the supporting cells that fill it, the roots by which its nerves leave the spinal cord, and the ganglia in which its nerve cell bodies gather, and it then turns to the cerebrum and its lobes. It covers the glial cells, the dorsal and ventral roots of the spinal nerves, the sensory and autonomic ganglia, and the hemispheres, lobes and deep masses of the brain. It holds one hundred questions, and each one gives its answer at once, in the same paragraph, before the reason for that answer. The structures are described as they are in the ordinary body, and where a common variation exists it is noted in the reason. This is educational material, and it supports rather than replaces an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 4801. Which star-shaped supporting cell is the most numerous in the central nervous system, sending many processes out among the nerve cells and the blood vessels? The answer is the astrocyte. Some of its processes end in flat feet on the walls of capillaries. It helps to nourish the nerve cells and to keep the barrier around them tight.

Question 4802. Which of these cells wraps an axon in myelin inside the brain and the spinal cord: the astrocyte, the oligodendrocyte, the ependymal cell, or the microglial cell? The answer is the oligodendrocyte. One oligodendrocyte sends a process to each of several axons and myelinates a segment of every one of them.

Question 4803. Which cells form a single layer that lines the ventricles of the brain and the central canal of the spinal cord and helps to produce the cerebrospinal fluid? The answer is the ependymal cells. Where the lining is folded over a tuft of vessels, in the choroid plexus, they secrete most of it.

Question 4804. Which small cells of the nervous system multiply after an injury, gather at the damaged place and clear away the debris? The answer is the microglial cells. They are phagocytic, and they do not arise from the same embryonic sheet as the nerve cells. They come from mesoderm and enter the nervous system with the blood vessels before birth.

Question 4805. A described peripheral nerve is wrapped by a chain of cells, and each of them forms a single segment of myelin around a single axon. Which cell is described: the astrocyte, the Schwann cell, the oligodendrocyte, or the satellite cell? The answer is the Schwann cell. It serves one segment of one axon outside the central nervous system.

Question 4806. Which flattened cells form a capsule around the nerve cell body inside a ganglion, in the way that astrocytes surround nerve cell bodies in the brain? The answer is the satellite cells. They lie closely around each cell body in the ganglion. Their task matches that of the astrocyte, but it is carried out in the peripheral nervous system.

Question 4807. True or false: the astrocyte helps to build the barrier that keeps many substances in the blood out of the brain tissue. The answer is true. The barrier itself is made by tight junctions between the cells that line the capillary. The foot plates of the astrocytes press against that capillary and signal it to stay tight.

Question 4808. In the developing nervous system, which elongated cells stretch from the inner lining to the outer surface and guide the young nerve cells to their final place? The answer is the radial glial cells. Their long processes form a ladder across the thickness of the wall. Most of them later shorten and become astrocytes.

Question 4809. Three of these cell types arise from the same embryonic sheet as the nerve cells themselves, and one does not: the astrocytes, the oligodendrocytes, the ependymal cells, and the microglial cells. The answer is the microglial cell, which arrives with the blood vessels from mesoderm. The other three are all derived from the wall of the neural tube.

Question 4810. A constructed illustration: a young adult has attacks of numbness, blurred vision and unsteady walking, and the myelin of the brain and spinal cord is under attack. Which cells are the target? The answer is the oligodendrocytes. Loss of their myelin slows conduction along the central pathways, while the peripheral nerves wrapped by Schwann cells are usually spared.

Question 4811. Which glial cell sends foot plates that lie immediately against the outer wall of a capillary in the brain, and what does that position let it do? The answer is the astrocyte. Its end feet cover much of the capillary surface. From there it signals the lining cells to keep their tight junctions and helps to carry nutrients into the tissue.

Question 4812. Which single cell of the central nervous system can myelinate segments of as many as fifty different axons at one time: the astrocyte, the ependymal cell, the microglial cell, or the oligodendrocyte? The answer is the oligodendrocyte. It reaches several neighboring fibers with separate processes, and each process wraps one segment. A Schwann cell serves one axon only.

Question 4813. A described microscopic preparation shows a star-shaped cell with many radiating processes, one of which ends in a broad flat foot on the wall of a small vessel while the others lie among nerve fibers. Which cell is shown? The answer is the astrocyte. The foot on the vessel identifies it. A myelin-forming cell would wrap an axon instead.

Question 4814. After an injury to the brain, which glial cell multiplies and knits its processes together to wall off the damaged area: the ependymal cell, the astrocyte, the Schwann cell, or the satellite cell? The answer is the astrocyte. It forms a scar in the nervous tissue. The scar helps to seal the injury, but it also blocks the regrowth of central axons.

Question 4815. Which statement about the myelin-forming cells is correct: one Schwann cell myelinates several axons, the oligodendrocyte lies only in peripheral nerves, one Schwann cell myelinates one segment of one axon, or myelin is made by the microglial cells? The answer is that one Schwann cell myelinates a segment of a single axon. The oligodendrocyte of the brain and cord serves several axons instead.

Question 4816. Which of these lies at the meeting point of two Schwann cells along a peripheral axon: the node of Ranvier, the motor end plate, the synaptic cleft, or the dorsal root? The answer is the node of Ranvier, the short bare gap of membrane where the impulse is renewed. The impulse leaps from one node to the next, so conduction is fast.

Question 4817. Name two kinds of supporting cell in the brain and the spinal cord that do not make myelin, spoken as a list. The answer is the astrocytes and the ependymal cells, and the microglial cells belong in the same group. Astrocytes support and scar, and ependymal cells line the cavities. Myelin in the central nervous system comes from the oligodendrocytes alone.

Question 4818. A constructed illustration: an imaging study of an adult shows a rapidly growing tumor in one cerebral hemisphere, and it is described as arising from the supporting cells that nourish nerve cells. Which cell of origin is meant? The answer is the astrocyte. Tumors of astrocytes are the most common malignant tumors of the brain in adults, and they spread by infiltration.

Question 4819. A described electron micrograph shows one cell body with several processes, and each process spirals around a different axon to form a dark ring of myelin. In which part of the nervous system does this cell work, and why can it serve several axons? The answer is the central nervous system, and the cell is an oligodendrocyte.

Question 4820. Which supporting cell would be found wrapped around the nerve cell bodies inside a dorsal root ganglion: the astrocyte, the ependymal cell, the satellite cell, or the oligodendrocyte? The answer is the satellite cell. The other three belong to the central nervous system, and a ganglion lies outside it. The satellite cell is the peripheral counterpart of the astrocyte.

Question 4821. Which root of a spinal nerve carries the fibers that leave the spinal cord to supply the muscles, and in which direction do those fibers run? The answer is the ventral root, also called the anterior root, and its fibers run outward. It is built of motor fibers that begin in the large cells of the anterior horn.

Question 4822. Which of these describes the dorsal root of a spinal nerve: it carries motor fibers away from the cord, it carries sensory fibers toward the cord, it carries both kinds of fiber, or it carries no nerve fibers at all? The answer is that it carries sensory fibers toward the cord. The motor fibers travel outward in the ventral root.

Question 4823. What is the name of the swelling on the dorsal root of a spinal nerve that holds the cell bodies of the sensory fibers? The answer is the dorsal root ganglion. It lies in the intervertebral foramen beside the root. No synapse takes place inside it, because the sensory cell body merely relays the impulse onward to the cord.

Question 4824. How many pairs of spinal nerves leave the spinal cord, and how are they grouped: twenty-one pairs, thirty-one pairs, forty-one pairs, or twelve pairs? The answer is thirty-one pairs, made up of eight cervical, twelve thoracic, five lumbar, five sacral and one coccygeal pair. The first cervical nerve leaves above the first cervical vertebra.

Question 4825. In the cervical region, does a spinal nerve leave the vertebral canal above or below the vertebra of the same number, and where does the first cervical nerve run relative to the vertebral artery? The answer is above the vertebra of the same number, and the first cervical nerve runs behind the vertebral artery. Each cervical nerve takes the number of the vertebra below it.

Question 4826. The nerves of the upper limb are formed by the ventral rami of which spinal nerves: the first to the fourth cervical, the fifth cervical to the first thoracic, the first to the twelfth thoracic, or the first to the fifth lumbar? The answer is the fifth cervical to the first thoracic. Those five rami form the brachial plexus.

Question 4827. After a spinal nerve leaves the intervertebral foramen it divides into two branches. Which of them supplies the deep muscles and the skin of the back: the ventral ramus, the dorsal ramus, the gray ramus, or the white ramus? The answer is the dorsal ramus, the smaller posterior branch.

Question 4828. In an adult, the solid spinal cord ends at about which vertebral level, and why is a lumbar puncture made below the third lumbar vertebra? The answer is that the cord ends at the first or second lumbar vertebra, so below the third the canal holds only the loose roots of the cauda equina. A needle placed there can push those roots aside instead of striking the cord.

Question 4829. A described dissection of the lower vertebral canal shows the solid cord ending in a point, with a bundle of nerve roots descending beyond it like the tail of a horse. What is that bundle called, and what does each root in it become? The answer is the cauda equina, and each root runs on to its own intervertebral foramen.

Question 4830. A constructed illustration: a person has pain shooting down the back of the leg after lifting a heavy load, and a disc between the fourth and fifth lumbar vertebrae is described as pressing on one nerve root. Which root is affected? The answer is the fifth lumbar root. A disc herniates backward and outward, where it meets the root leaving at the level below.

Question 4831. True or false: a ganglion that holds sensory nerve cell bodies sits on the ventral root of a spinal nerve. The answer is false. The swelling of nerve cell bodies sits on the dorsal root, which carries fibers toward the cord. The ventral root carries motor fibers outward and bears no such ganglion.

Question 4832. The denticulate ligament anchors the spinal cord to the dura at the side. Does the dorsal root lie in front of that ligament or behind it, and where does the ventral root lie? The answer is that the dorsal root lies behind the ligament and the ventral root in front of it.

Question 4833. A described cross section of the spinal cord shows a butterfly of gray matter with a broad front horn and a slender back horn, and two roots leaving on each side. Which root carries fibers toward the cord, and which carries them away? The answer is that the thinner dorsal root behind carries fibers toward the cord, and the ventral root carries them away.

Question 4834. Which of these pairs belongs together: the dorsal root with the back muscles, the ventral root with the skin of the back, the dorsal ramus with the deep muscles of the back, or the ventral ramus with the joints alone? The answer is the dorsal ramus with the deep muscles of the back. The dorsal root carries sensory fibers, the ventral root carries motor fibers, and the ventral ramus supplies the limbs.

Question 4835. Name the two roots that join to form a spinal nerve, and say what each of them carries. The answer is the ventral root, which carries motor fibers outward, and the dorsal root, which carries sensory fibers inward. The dorsal root bears the ganglion that holds the sensory cell bodies. The two roots meet in the intervertebral foramen.

Question 4836. In a dorsal root ganglion, each nerve cell body gives off a single short process that then divides into two long branches. What name is given to that shape, and where does one branch end? The answer is a pseudounipolar cell. One branch reaches the skin, a muscle or a joint, and the other enters the spinal cord.

Question 4837. In which kind of ganglion does a nerve fiber actually make a synapse with a second nerve cell: the dorsal root ganglion, an autonomic ganglion, a sensory ganglion of a cranial nerve, or the trigeminal ganglion? The answer is an autonomic ganglion. There the fiber that left the central nervous system hands its message to a second cell.

Question 4838. What name is given to the row of ganglia that lies on each side of the vertebral column and is joined into a chain from the neck to the pelvis? The answer is the sympathetic chain, whose ganglia are called paravertebral. A ganglion of the same system in front of the aorta, such as the celiac ganglion, is called prevertebral.

Question 4839. Which of these autonomic ganglia lies in front of the abdominal aorta rather than beside the vertebral column: the superior cervical ganglion, the celiac ganglion, the stellate ganglion, or the otic ganglion? The answer is the celiac ganglion. It lies around the origin of the celiac artery and serves the upper abdominal organs.

Question 4840. Which plexus lies immediately around the origin of the celiac artery from the abdominal aorta, and which two ganglia lie within it? The answer is the celiac plexus, with the celiac ganglia on either side. Sympathetic and parasympathetic fibers pass through it to the stomach, the liver, the spleen and the pancreas. Pain from those organs travels back along the same route.

Question 4841. Which large sensory ganglion lies in a pocket of dura on the front of the petrous part of the temporal bone, at the root of the fifth cranial nerve? The answer is the trigeminal ganglion. Its three divisions arise from it to supply the face, the teeth and the muscles of chewing. It is the sensory ganglion of the largest cranial nerve.

Question 4842. Which of these cranial ganglia holds the cell bodies of the parasympathetic fibers that constrict the pupil: the trigeminal ganglion, the ciliary ganglion, the geniculate ganglion, or the spiral ganglion? The answer is the ciliary ganglion, which lies in the orbit behind the eye. The trigeminal ganglion is sensory, the geniculate serves the facial nerve, and the spiral ganglion serves hearing.

Question 4843. True or false: the deep gray masses called the basal ganglia are true ganglia lying outside the central nervous system. The answer is false. The caudate nucleus, the putamen and the globus pallidus lie deep inside each cerebral hemisphere. The name is a historical one, and a true ganglion lies outside the central nervous system.

Question 4844. A constructed illustration: a person has a band of painful blisters on one side of the chest that stops at the midline. Which ganglion is meant? The answer is a dorsal root ganglion of a thoracic spinal nerve. The virus travels out along the sensory fibers, and the rash follows one segment.

Question 4845. Which of these pairs belongs together: the dorsal root ganglion with a synapse, an autonomic ganglion with sensory cell bodies, the sympathetic chain with a synapse outside the central nervous system, or a sensory ganglion with motor fibers? The answer is the sympathetic chain with a synapse outside the central nervous system. A dorsal root ganglion holds sensory cell bodies and no synapse.

Question 4846. A described dissection shows a chain of small swellings beside the vertebral bodies in the neck, each joined to a spinal nerve by two short connections. What is the chain, and what are the two connections? The answer is the sympathetic chain, joined to each spinal nerve by a gray and a white ramus. The white ramus carries fibers that have just left the cord.

Question 4847. Which small parasympathetic ganglion hangs just below the foramen ovale and sends its fibers to the parotid gland? The answer is the otic ganglion. It is attached to the mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve, which passes through that opening. Its fibers come from the ninth cranial nerve and join it along the way.

Question 4848. In the neck, does the sympathetic chain lie in front of the carotid sheath or behind it, and what lies immediately behind the chain? The answer is that the chain lies behind the carotid sheath, and the vertebral column lies behind the chain. It rests on the long muscles at the front of the neck, medial to the vagus nerve.

Question 4849. Which statement about the dorsal root ganglion is correct: it contains multipolar cells, it contains no synapse, it lies inside the brain or spinal cord, or it carries motor fibers outward? The answer is that it contains no synapse. Its cells are pseudounipolar sensory cells that relay the impulse straight into the cord.

Question 4850. Name the two great groups into which ganglia are divided by the presence or absence of a synapse, and give one example of each. The answer is the sensory ganglia, such as a dorsal root ganglion, and the autonomic ganglia, such as the celiac ganglion. The sensory ganglion holds cell bodies and no synapse, while the autonomic ganglion holds a synapse.

Question 4851. Which deep groove divides the cerebrum into a left and a right hemisphere, and what sheet of tissue lies within it? The answer is the longitudinal fissure, and the falx cerebri lies in it. The falx is a fold of dura that dips down between the two hemispheres. The two halves are joined below by the corpus callosum.

Question 4852. Which thick band of white matter crosses the midline below the longitudinal fissure and joins the two cerebral hemispheres? The answer is the corpus callosum. Its fibers are commissural, which means that they cross from one side to the other. It is the largest bridge of its kind in the brain.

Question 4853. Which groove runs downward and forward across the upper lateral surface of a hemisphere and separates the frontal lobe from the parietal lobe? The answer is the central sulcus. It lies between the precentral gyrus in front and the postcentral gyrus behind. Those two gyri carry the motor and the sensory maps of the body.

Question 4854. Which deep cleft on the side of a hemisphere separates the temporal lobe below from the frontal and parietal lobes above? The answer is the lateral sulcus, also called the lateral fissure. Its floor is formed by the insula, a lobe that is hidden from the surface. The cleft runs backward and upward from the front of the temporal region.

Question 4855. Which lobe of the cerebrum lies behind the parietooccipital sulcus at the back of the head: the frontal lobe, the parietal lobe, the occipital lobe, or the temporal lobe? The answer is the occipital lobe. Its chief task is vision, and its primary visual cortex lies on the medial surface. The temporal lobe lies below the lateral sulcus and serves hearing.

Question 4856. Which gyrus lies immediately in front of the central sulcus and holds the large cells that begin the pathway to the skeletal muscles? The answer is the precentral gyrus, which is the primary motor cortex. Its cells are arranged along the gyrus in the order of the body. The body is represented upside down, with the face lowest.

Question 4857. Which gyrus lies immediately behind the central sulcus and receives the signals of touch and position from the opposite side of the body? The answer is the postcentral gyrus, the primary somatosensory cortex. It mirrors the motor map in front of it. The hand and the face take up a very large part of it.

Question 4858. Which area in the lower part of the frontal lobe, usually on the left, plans the movements of speech, so that damage there leaves a person able to understand words but unable to speak them? The answer is the motor speech area of Broca. It lies just in front of the lower end of the precentral gyrus.

Question 4859. Which area at the junction of the temporal and parietal lobes, usually on the left, is needed to understand spoken and written language: the precentral gyrus, the area of Wernicke, the area of Broca, or the calcarine cortex? The answer is the area of Wernicke. Damage there leaves speech fluent but empty of meaning.

Question 4860. On the medial surface of which lobe, and around which sulcus, does the primary visual cortex lie? The answer is the occipital lobe, around the calcarine sulcus. The upper bank of the sulcus receives the upper part of the opposite visual field. The lower bank receives the lower part.

Question 4861. A described preparation shows a gyrus running along the upper surface of the temporal lobe, hidden inside the cleft that separates that lobe from the frontal and parietal lobes. Which gyrus is shown, and what does it receive? The answer is the superior temporal gyrus, which carries the primary auditory cortex. Each ear sends fibers to both hemispheres.

Question 4862. Which of these is a lobe of the cerebrum that cannot be seen from the surface until the lips of the lateral sulcus are pulled apart: the occipital lobe, the insula, the parietal lobe, or the cingulate gyrus? The answer is the insula, which lies in the floor of the lateral sulcus and is covered by the opercula. The cingulate gyrus belongs to the limbic lobe.

Question 4863. Which compact band of white matter lies between the thalamus and the caudate nucleus on its inner side and the lentiform nucleus on its outer side, and carries the motor fibers from the cortex? The answer is the internal capsule. Above it those fibers fan out as the corona radiata.

Question 4864. Which large egg-shaped mass of gray matter lies deep in each hemisphere beside the third ventricle and relays almost every sensory pathway to the cortex: the putamen, the thalamus, the amygdala, or the hippocampus? The answer is the thalamus. Every sense except smell passes through it on the way to the cortex. The internal capsule runs along its lateral border.

Question 4865. Which part of the brain lies below and in front of the thalamus, forms the floor of the third ventricle, and controls body temperature, hunger and the hormones of the pituitary gland? The answer is the hypothalamus. A stalk carries its orders down to the pituitary.

Question 4866. Which three deep masses of gray matter are grouped together as the basal ganglia, and what kind of movement do they help to smooth? The answer is the caudate nucleus, the putamen and the globus pallidus, and they help to smooth voluntary movement. They receive fibers from the whole cortex and send them back through the thalamus.

Question 4867. A constructed illustration: a person suddenly cannot move the right arm or the right leg and cannot speak, though the muscles of the face work on both sides. An artery supplying the lateral surface of the left hemisphere is blocked. Which artery is it? The answer is the middle cerebral artery.

Question 4868. A constructed illustration: after an artery on the medial surface of one hemisphere is blocked, a person loses power and feeling in the opposite leg and foot, while the arm and the face are spared. Which artery is involved? The answer is the anterior cerebral artery, which supplies the medial surface as far back as the parietal lobe.

Question 4869. A constructed illustration: a person reports that one half of the world has vanished from vision in both eyes, and the damage lies at the back of the brain. Which artery supplies the damaged region? The answer is the posterior cerebral artery, which supplies the occipital lobe and its visual cortex. Damage there removes the opposite half of the visual field in both eyes.

Question 4870. Which statement about the motor map on the precentral gyrus is correct: the face lies at the top, the foot lies at the top, the hand lies at the bottom, or the body is represented the right way up? The answer is that the foot lies at the top. The map is upside down, so the face lies lowest.

Question 4871. A described computed tomography section through the brain shows two dark fluid-filled spaces near the midline, and beside each of them a comma-shaped mass of gray matter. Which structures are described? The answer is the lateral ventricles and the heads of the caudate nuclei. The head of the caudate nucleus forms part of the floor of the anterior horn.

Question 4872. A described magnetic resonance imaging section through the brain shows two parallel gyri running down the upper surface with a deep groove between them, and a horizontal cleft entering from the side. Which gyri and which groove are described? The answer is the precentral and postcentral gyri, with the central sulcus between them. The horizontal cleft is the lateral sulcus.

Question 4873. A described section across the brain shows two fluid-filled cavities lying side by side, separated in the midline by a thin partition, with a third cavity between the two thalami below them. Which cavities are described, and what separates the upper pair? The answer is the two lateral ventricles, separated by the septum pellucidum, with the third ventricle below.

Question 4874. Which plane of section cuts across both hemispheres from side to side and shows the corpus callosum above the lateral ventricles, and which plane instead separates the two hemispheres along the midline? The answer is a coronal plane for the first, and a sagittal plane for the second. That is the section used to compare the two sides of the brain.

Question 4875. True or false: the left hemisphere always carries the speech areas. The answer is false, although the left side is the dominant one in most people. In a minority the right hemisphere holds the speech areas, and in others the two sides share the work. Handedness gives only a rough guide.

Question 4876. Which of these pairs belongs together: the occipital lobe with hearing, the temporal lobe with vision, the frontal lobe with the planning of movement, or the parietal lobe with smell? The answer is the frontal lobe with the planning of movement. The occipital lobe serves vision, the temporal lobe serves hearing, and the parietal lobe receives touch and position sense.

Question 4877. Which of these pairs belongs together: the precentral gyrus with touch, the postcentral gyrus with touch, the superior temporal gyrus with vision, or the cortex around the calcarine sulcus with hearing? The answer is the postcentral gyrus with touch. The precentral gyrus holds the motor cortex, the superior temporal gyrus serves hearing, and the calcarine cortex serves vision.

Question 4878. Fibers that link the same region of the two hemispheres, such as the two visual areas, are called which of these: projection fibers, association fibers, commissural fibers, or capsular fibers? The answer is commissural fibers, and the corpus callosum is built of them. Association fibers link one region of a hemisphere with another region on the same side.

Question 4879. A described preparation of a hemisphere with the cortex stripped away shows a broad fan of fibers rising from a narrow band between two deep gray masses and spreading out toward the surface. Which structures are described? The answer is the corona radiata above and the internal capsule below. The fibers run in both directions between the cortex and the lower centers.

Question 4880. A constructed illustration: a person whose behavior has changed, who jokes inappropriately and neglects one side of the body, is found to have a tumor at the front of one hemisphere. Which lobe is involved, and which part of it is disturbed? The answer is the frontal lobe, and the prefrontal cortex is disturbed.

Question 4881. A constructed illustration: a person can see the parts of an object and describe them, yet cannot recognize what the object is, and the damage lies in the occipital lobe. Which cortex is injured, and why is sight itself unaffected? The answer is the visual association cortex around the primary visual area. The picture still arrives, but its meaning cannot be attached to it.

Question 4882. A described lateral view of a hemisphere shows a long cleft running backward from the front, a groove descending toward it from above and behind, and a short groove at the back. Which three grooves are described? The answer is the lateral sulcus, the central sulcus and the parietooccipital sulcus. The lateral sulcus separates the temporal lobe below from the rest.

Question 4883. Which gyrus lies in the parietal lobe at the end of the superior temporal sulcus, where the pathways for reading and for language meet: the cingulate gyrus, the angular gyrus, the superior temporal gyrus, or the precentral gyrus? The answer is the angular gyrus. Damage on the dominant side can disturb reading.

Question 4884. Which part of the frontal lobe lies in front of the motor and premotor areas and is concerned with planning, judgment and the control of impulses? The answer is the prefrontal cortex. It has no direct part in moving a muscle. Its wide connections with the rest of the brain let it weigh an action before it is taken.

Question 4885. Which area lies just in front of the precentral gyrus and prepares a sequence of movements before the primary motor cortex carries it out: the premotor area, the postcentral gyrus, the angular gyrus, or the calcarine cortex? The answer is the premotor area. It occupies the back of the frontal lobe beside the motor strip. It plans patterns of movement rather than single muscles.

Question 4886. Which small arteries, branches of the middle cerebral artery, pass through the base of the brain to supply the internal capsule and the basal ganglia, and why is their blockage so disabling? The answer is the lenticulostriate arteries. They are fine vessels with little overlap between their territories, so a block in one can cut the motor fibers in the internal capsule.

Question 4887. Which thin partition of nervous tissue separates the two anterior horns of the lateral ventricles in the midline? The answer is the septum pellucidum. It stretches between the corpus callosum above and the fornix below. It forms the medial wall of each lateral ventricle.

Question 4888. Which fringe of vascular tissue lies in the floor of each lateral ventricle and secretes cerebrospinal fluid into it: the falx cerebri, the choroid plexus, the tentorium cerebelli, or the septum pellucidum? The answer is the choroid plexus. It is built of ependymal cells over a core of blood vessels. It continues into the third and the fourth ventricles, where it adds more fluid.

Question 4889. Which statement about the cerebral cortex is correct: it lies deep to the white matter, it is a thin sheet of gray matter on the surface, it contains no nerve cell bodies, or it is present only in the occipital lobe? The answer is that it is a thin sheet of gray matter on the surface. The white matter lies beneath it.

Question 4890. A described photograph of the surface of the brain shows winding ridges separated by narrow grooves that branch over the whole surface. What are the ridges and the grooves called, and why does the surface take this form? The answer is gyri for the ridges and sulci for the grooves. The folding greatly increases the area of the cortex within the skull.

Question 4891. Which lobe of the cerebrum lies immediately above the roof of the orbit and carries the olfactory bulb and tract on its under surface? The answer is the frontal lobe. The olfactory tract runs along the orbital surface below it. Smell is the only sense that reaches the cortex without first passing through the thalamus.

Question 4892. True or false: the corpus callosum is the only bridge of fibers joining the two cerebral hemispheres. The answer is false. The anterior commissure and the commissure of the hippocampus also cross the midline, though they are far smaller. The corpus callosum is much the largest of the three.

Question 4893. Which of these pairs belongs together: the motor speech area with the occipital lobe, the sensory speech area with the frontal lobe, the visual cortex with the occipital lobe, or the auditory cortex with the parietal lobe? The answer is the visual cortex with the occipital lobe. The auditory cortex lies in the temporal lobe.

Question 4894. A constructed illustration: after a deep midline operation, a person can pick up an object in the left hand but cannot name it, while an object in the right hand is named at once. Which structure was divided, and why does the difficulty appear on the left? The answer is the corpus callosum. The left hand is served by the right hemisphere, which has lost its link to the speech areas.

Question 4895. A described view of the medial surface of a hemisphere shows a curved band of fibers above a C-shaped gyrus, and a deep sulcus running backward at the lower edge. Which structures are described? The answer is the corpus callosum with the cingulate gyrus above it, and the calcarine sulcus behind. The cingulate gyrus arches over the callosum within the limbic lobe.

Question 4896. In the body of a lateral ventricle, which structure forms the floor and which forms the roof? The answer is that the thalamus and the fornix form the floor, and the corpus callosum forms the roof. The caudate nucleus lies in the lateral wall of the ventricle. The choroid plexus hangs along the floor beside the fornix.

Question 4897. A described view of the medial surface shows a C-shaped gyrus arching immediately above the corpus callosum and continuing backward toward the temporal lobe. Which gyrus is described, and to which lobe is it assigned? The answer is the cingulate gyrus, which belongs to the limbic lobe. Behind it continues as the parahippocampal gyrus.

Question 4898. Three of these structures lie in the temporal lobe: the hippocampus, the primary auditory cortex, and the superior temporal gyrus. Which one does not? The answer is the angular gyrus, which lies in the parietal lobe. The hippocampus lies in the medial part of the temporal lobe. Damage to the angular gyrus on the dominant side disturbs reading.

Question 4899. Which fibers sweep forward into the temporal lobe before turning back to the occipital cortex, and what defect follows damage to them there? The answer is the optic radiation. Its lower fibers loop forward into the temporal lobe before turning backward. Damage to them removes the upper part of the opposite visual field.

Question 4900. A described section through a hemisphere shows the central sulcus running downward and forward, with a thick gyrus in front of it and another behind. Which lobe lies in front of that groove, and which two functions are represented in the two gyri beside it? The answer is the frontal lobe, with the motor cortex in front and the somatosensory cortex behind.

To summarize, the supporting cells each have one clear task: the astrocyte nourishes and scars, the oligodendrocyte myelinates several axons in the brain and cord, the Schwann cell myelinates a single axon outside them, the ependymal cell lines the cavities, and the microglial cell clears debris. A spinal nerve is formed by a ventral root of motor fibers and a dorsal root of sensory fibers, and the dorsal root carries the swelling that holds the sensory cell bodies. Ganglia hold either sensory cell bodies with no synapse, as in a dorsal root ganglion, or the synapse of an autonomic pathway, as in the sympathetic chain. The cerebrum is divided by the longitudinal fissure into two hemispheres joined by the corpus callosum, and its surface is marked by the central sulcus between the frontal and parietal lobes, by the lateral sulcus above the temporal lobe, and by the calcarine sulcus behind. The motor and sensory maps lie in the precentral and postcentral gyri, the speech areas lie in the frontal and temporal lobes of the dominant hemisphere, and the deep masses are packed around the internal capsule.
