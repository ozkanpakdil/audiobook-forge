# Chapter 15 — Upper Limb Bones, Part Two

This chapter continues the survey of the upper limb bones and then turns to the ring of bone that carries the weight of the trunk into the lower limb. The first half examines the metacarpals of the palm and the phalanges of the fingers and the thumb; the second half examines the three bones that fuse to form each hip bone, the ilium, the ischium and the pubis. It holds one hundred questions, and every question gives its answer at once and then explains why that answer is right, so nothing has to be held in mind until the end. Everything is described in words, because the listener cannot see a bone, a radiograph or a specimen. This is educational material, and it supports rather than replaces an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 1401. Which five bones form the skeleton of the palm of the hand, lying between the wrist above and the fingers below? The answer is the metacarpals. Each is a long bone with a base that meets the carpus, a shaft, and a head that forms a knuckle. They are numbered from the first at the thumb side to the fifth at the little finger side.

Question 1402. What name is given to a bone of the hand that has a flattened base turned toward the wrist, a long shaft, and a rounded head that forms a knuckle at the back of the hand? The answer is a metacarpal. The base carries facets for the carpal bones and for its neighbors. The head carries the rounded surface of the knuckle joint.

Question 1403. How many metacarpal bones does one hand contain: four, five, six, or seven? The answer is five. They run side by side through the palm, so every finger and the thumb has a metacarpal of its own. The first belongs to the thumb and the fifth to the little finger.

Question 1404. Which bone of the palm lies between the trapezium above and the first phalanx of the thumb below? The answer is the first metacarpal. It is the only metacarpal that meets the trapezium, and its head carries the joint with the first phalanx of the thumb. Its shaft is set apart from the other four.

Question 1405. Which carpal bone receives the base of the third metacarpal: the trapezium, the trapezoid, the capitate, or the hamate? The answer is the capitate. It is the largest bone of the carpus. The trapezoid receives the second metacarpal, and the hamate receives the fourth and the fifth.

Question 1406. Which carpal bone lies immediately proximal to the base of the first metacarpal, and what kind of joint do the two form? The answer is the trapezium, and the two form the saddle joint of the thumb. That joint lies at the base of the thumb, separate from the wrist joint. Its shape is what lets the thumb swing across the palm.

Question 1407. Which metacarpal base carries a pointed process that rises between the bones of the wrist? The answer is the third metacarpal, which lies in the middle of the hand. The pointed process is its styloid process, and it can be felt at the back of the wrist.

Question 1408. A described radiograph of the hand shows one metacarpal set apart from the other four, with its shaft turned so that its palmar surface faces the fingers. Which bone is shown, and what does that position tell you? The answer is the first metacarpal, held in opposition. No other metacarpal lies at that angle.

Question 1409. A constructed illustration: after a punch against a hard wall, the back of the hand is swollen over the little finger and its knuckle has lost its outline. Which structure is injured? The answer is the neck of the fifth metacarpal. The head of the bone is tilted toward the palm by the pull of the muscles crossing the joint. The treating clinician decides the management.

Question 1410. The rounded bumps seen in a row across the back of a clenched fist are formed by which parts: the bases of the metacarpals, the heads of the metacarpals, the shafts of the metacarpals, or the first phalanges? The answer is the heads of the metacarpals. Each head is rounded and carries the joint with the first phalanx of its digit.

Question 1411. True or false: the first metacarpal is the only metacarpal whose growth plate lies at its base rather than at its head. The answer is true. In the first metacarpal the second center of ossification appears at the base, as it does in the phalanges. In the other four metacarpals it appears at the head.

Question 1412. Two small sesamoid bones lie in the tendons at the front of the thumb, one on each side of a joint. Which joint do they lie beside, and which tendons hold them? The answer is the metacarpophalangeal joint of the thumb, with the tendons of the short flexor of the thumb and the adductor of the thumb. They glide over the joint.

Question 1413. Which type of synovial joint is the metacarpophalangeal joint: a hinge joint, a saddle joint, a condyloid joint, or a plane joint? The answer is a condyloid joint. It allows flexion, extension, abduction, adduction and a small amount of rotation. The joint at the base of the thumb is the saddle joint of the hand.

Question 1414. A described cross section through the middle of the palm shows the five metacarpal shafts in an arch, with the middle raised and its two ends lower. Which two metacarpals lie lowest, and why does the arch matter? The answer is the first and the fifth. The arch lets the palm cup around an object, with the second and third metacarpals as its keystone.

Question 1415. A constructed illustration: a person falls onto the outstretched thumb, and its base is painful and deformed just beyond the wrist. Which bone and which joint are involved? The answer is the first metacarpal and its carpometacarpal joint with the trapezium. A break there is unstable because the long abductor muscle of the thumb pulls the shaft away from the small basal fragment. The treating clinician decides the management.

Question 1416. In a hand held in the anatomical position, with the palm facing forward, which metacarpal lies immediately lateral to the fourth metacarpal? The answer is the third metacarpal. In that position the thumb and its metacarpal point laterally, and the bones are numbered from the thumb side inward. The third therefore lies between the second and the fourth.

Question 1417. Which muscles fill the spaces between the shafts of the metacarpals, and where do their tendons go? The answer is the interosseous muscles of the hand. They arise from the sides of the metacarpals. Their tendons cross the back of the hand to reach the bases of the first phalanges.

Question 1418. Which metacarpal is the shortest and thickest of the five, and which digit does it carry: the first, the second, the third, or the fifth? The answer is the first, which carries the thumb. Its greater thickness suits the powerful pinch of the thumb. The fifth metacarpal is the most slender of the five and the one most often broken.

Question 1419. A described radiograph of an adult hand shows five metacarpals in the palm. The outermost of them on one side carries a digit that has only two phalanges. Which metacarpal is that? The answer is the first metacarpal. It carries the thumb, the only digit with two phalanges instead of three.

Question 1420. Which of these pairs belongs together: the first metacarpal with the capitate, the second metacarpal with the trapezoid, the third metacarpal with the hamate, or the fifth metacarpal with the trapezium? The answer is the second metacarpal with the trapezoid. The third meets the capitate, the fourth and fifth meet the hamate, and the first meets the trapezium.

Question 1421. Name two bones with which the base of the fifth metacarpal articulates, spoken as a list. The answer is the hamate and the fourth metacarpal. Its base carries a facet above for the hamate and one beside it for the fourth metacarpal. That gives the little finger a little more movement.

Question 1422. Which statement about the metacarpals is correct: the fourth and fifth share the hamate, the first carries its growth plate at the head, the second is the longest of the five, or the heads of the metacarpals form the wrist joint? The answer is that the fourth and fifth share the hamate. The first has its growth plate at the base, and the third is the longest.

Question 1423. A constructed illustration: a fall forces the thumb sharply away from the index finger, and the side of the thumb joint that faces the index finger is tender and unstable. Which structure is torn? The answer is the collateral ligament on that side of the metacarpophalangeal joint of the thumb. Forced movement in that direction tears it. The treating clinician decides the management.

Question 1424. Which of these statements about the metacarpophalangeal joints is correct: they are hinge joints that allow only flexion and extension, they are condyloid joints that also allow abduction and adduction, their collateral ligaments are tight when the fingers are straight, or they lack a capsule? The answer is that they are condyloid joints that also allow abduction and adduction. Their collateral ligaments are tight in flexion, not in extension.

Question 1425. How many phalanges does one hand contain altogether, counting the thumb? The answer is fourteen. Each of the four fingers has three, and the thumb has two. That gives twelve for the fingers and two more for the thumb. They are the last bones of the upper limb.

Question 1426. How many phalanges does the thumb contain, and what are they called? The answer is two, the proximal phalanx and the distal phalanx. The thumb has no middle phalanx. The other four digits each have a proximal, a middle and a distal phalanx.

Question 1427. What are the three phalanges of a finger called, in order from the knuckle to the tip: proximal, middle and distal; upper, middle and lower; first, second and third; or basal, middle and terminal? The answer is proximal, middle and distal. The proximal phalanx meets the metacarpal, the middle lies between, and the distal carries the tip of the finger.

Question 1428. A described radiograph of the thumb shows the metacarpal, then a joint, and then only two phalanges. Which two phalanges are shown, and which one is missing? The answer is the proximal phalanx and the distal phalanx, with no middle phalanx. The joint between the two is the interphalangeal joint of the thumb.

Question 1429. Which type of joint are the interphalangeal joints of the fingers: hinge joints, saddle joints, condyloid joints, or pivot joints? The answer is hinge joints. Each allows flexion and extension in one plane and nothing else. A finger therefore has two hinge joints in series, one on each side of its middle phalanx.

Question 1430. Which phalanx of a finger ends not in a rounded head but in a broad, roughened tuberosity, and what rests upon that tuberosity? The answer is the distal phalanx. Its flattened end gives the fingertip its shape, and the nail and the pulp rest upon it.

Question 1431. The base of the middle phalanx carries two shallow facets that meet the rounded head of the bone above it. Which bone's head is that, and what kind of joint is formed? The answer is the head of the proximal phalanx, forming the proximal interphalangeal joint. The two facets sit astride the pulley, which keeps the joint a pure hinge.

Question 1432. True or false: the thumb has a middle phalanx like every other digit. The answer is false. The thumb has only a proximal phalanx and a distal phalanx, and therefore only one interphalangeal joint. The four fingers each have three phalanges and two interphalangeal joints.

Question 1433. The collateral ligaments of the metacarpophalangeal joints are tightest in which position: when the fingers are straight, when the fingers are fully bent, when the fingers are spread apart, or when the hand is turned palm upward? The answer is when the fingers are fully bent. Each runs from the back of the metacarpal head to the front of the phalangeal base, so bending winds it tight.

Question 1434. Which of these pairs belongs together: the distal phalanx with its broad tuberosity, the middle phalanx with the metacarpal head, the proximal phalanx of the thumb with two pulleys, or the distal phalanx of the thumb with the trapezium? The answer is the distal phalanx with its broad tuberosity. The proximal phalanx meets the metacarpal head, and the trapezium meets a metacarpal.

Question 1435. A described radiograph of the index finger shows a break across the broad tuft of the distal phalanx, with the fragments held in line. Which phalanx is broken, and why do such breaks usually stay in place? The answer is the distal phalanx. Fibrous septa run from the skin to the bone and splint the fragments, so the break rarely displaces.

Question 1436. Which structure lies immediately distal to the head of the first metacarpal? The answer is the base of the proximal phalanx of the thumb. The two meet at the metacarpophalangeal joint of the thumb. On each side of that joint lies a collateral ligament, and the sesamoid bones lie in front of it.

Question 1437. On which sides of an interphalangeal joint do its collateral ligaments lie? The answer is one on each side, the medial and the lateral, joining the head of one phalanx to the base of the next. They are tight in every position and hold the hinge steady.

Question 1438. Which phalanx of a finger is the longest and which is the shortest? The answer is that the proximal phalanx is the longest and the distal phalanx the shortest. The middle phalanx lies between them in length. The thumb is the exception, since its proximal phalanx is short and stout.

Question 1439. A described dissection of a finger shows a fibrous sheet spreading over the back of the proximal phalanx and receiving the tendons of the forearm and of the hand. What is that sheet, and what does it do? The answer is the extensor expansion. It covers the back of the phalanges and lets one tendon bend the knuckle and straighten the joints beyond it.

Question 1440. A described radiograph shows one hand in full, with all its bones counted in a row. How many bones does an adult hand contain, and how are they divided? The answer is twenty-seven: eight carpals, five metacarpals and fourteen phalanges. The phalanges make up more than half of them. Only the thumb departs from the count of three phalanges.

Question 1441. Which phalanx receives the tendon of the deep flexor muscle of the fingers, and on which surface of that phalanx does it end? The answer is the distal phalanx, on the palmar surface at its base. The deep flexor muscle is the one that bends the tip of the finger.

Question 1442. A constructed illustration: a ball strikes the tip of an outstretched finger, and the tip is left drooping and cannot be straightened while the rest of the finger moves well. Which structure is injured? The answer is the extensor tendon where it attaches to the base of the distal phalanx. The pull of the tendon has avulsed it. The treating clinician decides the management.

Question 1443. A constructed illustration: a player grabs a jersey, the ring finger is pulled hard while already bent, and the tip can no longer be bent at all. Which structure has given way? The answer is the tendon of the deep flexor muscle of the fingers, torn from the base of the distal phalanx. It is the only tendon that bends the tip. The treating clinician decides the management.

Question 1444. True or false: the proximal interphalangeal joint of a finger permits a small amount of sideways movement, which is why a finger can be spread. The answer is false. The interphalangeal joints are hinges and allow movement in one plane only. Spreading the fingers happens at the metacarpophalangeal joints, where the fingers join the hand.

Question 1445. Which of these pairs belongs together: the metacarpophalangeal joint with the head of a metacarpal, the interphalangeal joint with the trapezium, the carpometacarpal joint of the thumb with a phalanx, or the wrist joint with the head of the fifth metacarpal? The answer is the metacarpophalangeal joint with the head of a metacarpal. The carpometacarpal joint of the thumb is formed with the trapezium.

Question 1446. A described specimen of the thumb shows a joint between two phalanges whose surfaces are shaped like a pulley and whose movement is confined to a single plane. Which joint is shown, and which movements does it allow? The answer is the interphalangeal joint of the thumb, which allows flexion and extension only. The wide arc of the thumb belongs to the joint at its base.

Question 1447. The rounded head of the proximal phalanx of a finger is received by the base of which bone, and what is that joint called? The answer is the middle phalanx, and the joint is the proximal interphalangeal joint. The head of the middle phalanx is received in turn by the base of the distal phalanx.

Question 1448. Which statement about a break of the tuft of the distal phalanx is correct: it usually stays in place because the skin and fibrous septa splint it, it always displaces, it is a break of the middle phalanx, or it separates the extensor tendon? The answer is that it usually stays in place because the skin and fibrous septa splint it. Such breaks are closed injuries.

Question 1449. Which movements does the metacarpophalangeal joint of a finger allow, and which movement does it lack? The answer is that it allows flexion, extension, abduction, adduction and a small amount of rotation. It cannot spin the finger on its own long axis the way the shoulder spins the arm. The interphalangeal joints beyond it are plain hinges.

Question 1450. Which feature of the head of a proximal phalanx keeps the interphalangeal joint in a single plane: a central ridge that fits a groove in the base beyond it, a flat surface with no ridge, a ball that sits in a socket, or a pair of sesamoid bones? The answer is a central ridge that fits a groove, called a pulley. It allows flexion and extension but no sideways swing.

Question 1451. Which of the three bones that fuse to form the hip bone is the largest, and where does it lie? The answer is the ilium, which forms the upper and widest part of the bone. Its broad upper part is the wing, and its lower part joins the other two bones in the socket of the hip.

Question 1452. Which border of the hip bone can be followed with the fingers from the front to the back, forming the rim of the pelvis? The answer is the iliac crest. It is the upper border of the wing of the ilium and runs in a gentle curve. It ends in front at the anterior superior iliac spine and behind at the posterior superior iliac spine.

Question 1453. The highest point of the iliac crest lies at the level of which vertebra: the twelfth thoracic, the second lumbar, the fourth lumbar, or the first sacral? The answer is the fourth lumbar vertebra. That level is used to find the space between the spines for a lumbar puncture.

Question 1454. A described radiograph of the back of the pelvis shows an ear-shaped patch of joint surface on each side of the sacrum, gripping it like a pair of hands. Which bone carries that surface, and which joint does it form? The answer is the ilium, and the surface forms the sacroiliac joint. Its ear shape interlocks the two bones.

Question 1455. Which smooth, concave surface on the inner side of the ilium lodges a muscle that runs down to the thigh: the iliac fossa, the iliac tuberosity, the arcuate line, or the gluteal surface? The answer is the iliac fossa, which lodges the iliacus muscle. That muscle fills the hollow and joins the psoas muscle to bend the hip.

Question 1456. The anterior superior iliac spine gives attachment to which two structures: the inguinal ligament and the sartorius muscle, the sacrotuberous ligament and the gluteus maximus, the rectus femoris and the inguinal ligament, or the iliacus and the psoas? The answer is the inguinal ligament and the sartorius muscle. The anterior inferior spine below it gives attachment to the straight head of the rectus femoris.

Question 1457. A constructed illustration: an adolescent kicks a football hard and feels a sudden pain at the front of the hip, and a small fragment of bone lies just above the hip joint. Which bony point has given way, and which muscle pulled it off? The answer is the anterior inferior iliac spine, pulled off by the straight head of the rectus femoris. The treating clinician decides the management.

Question 1458. Which bony point lies at the lower end of the front border of the ilium, just below the anterior superior iliac spine? The answer is the anterior inferior iliac spine. It gives attachment to the straight head of the rectus femoris muscle of the thigh.

Question 1459. The greater sciatic notch is converted into the greater sciatic foramen by which two ligaments: the inguinal ligament and the sacrospinous ligament, the sacrospinous ligament and the sacrotuberous ligament, the sacrotuberous ligament and the iliofemoral ligament, or the pubofemoral ligament and the ischiofemoral ligament? The answer is the sacrospinous ligament and the sacrotuberous ligament. The notch itself lies below the posterior inferior iliac spine.

Question 1460. A described radiograph of the hip bone of a young child shows three separate bones meeting in the socket of the hip, with a band of cartilage between them shaped like a letter Y. Which bones are shown, and where do they meet? The answer is the ilium, the ischium and the pubis, meeting in the acetabulum, where they fuse as growth ends.

Question 1461. The socket of the hip joint has a gap at its lower margin, bridged by a ligament so that a small opening remains. Which structure passes through that opening to reach the head of the femur? The answer is the round ligament of the head of the femur, carrying a small artery. The gap is the acetabular notch.

Question 1462. Which of these pairs belongs together: the anterior superior iliac spine with the inguinal ligament, the posterior superior iliac spine with the rectus femoris, the iliac fossa with the gluteus maximus, or the iliac crest with the sacrospinous ligament? The answer is the anterior superior iliac spine with the inguinal ligament. The iliac fossa lodges the iliacus, and the crest gives attachment to the abdominal muscles.

Question 1463. True or false: the strong ligaments that bind the sacrum to the ilium are attached to the rough area just behind the ear-shaped joint surface of the ilium. The answer is true. That rough area is the iliac tuberosity. The ligaments that fill the space between it and the sacrum lock the two bones together, so that very little movement is possible.

Question 1464. Which part of the socket of the hip joint is formed by the ilium: the superior part, the anterior part, the posterior inferior part, or the floor of the notch? The answer is the superior part, rather more than two fifths of the socket. The ischium forms the posterior inferior part and the pubis the anterior part. The three meet in the floor of the socket.

Question 1465. Which ridge on the inner surface of the ilium runs downward and forward to join the pubis, marking the brim of the pelvis? The answer is the arcuate line. It divides the inner surface of the hip bone into a broad upper part and a narrow lower part. The pelvic cavity lies below it and the greater pelvis above.

Question 1466. A described specimen of the outer surface of the ilium shows three curved ridges running from the back of the bone toward the front. What are these ridges, and what do they separate? The answer is the gluteal lines, which mark the areas of origin of the gluteus minimus, the gluteus medius and the gluteus maximus.

Question 1467. A constructed illustration: a young sprinter feels a sudden tearing pain at the front of the hip while the leg is driven backward hard, and a small piece of bone lies just below the front spine of the ilium. Which muscle has pulled it off? The answer is the sartorius, from the anterior superior iliac spine. The treating clinician decides the management.

Question 1468. Which statement about the ilium is correct: its inner surface above the arcuate line forms part of the greater pelvis, its outer surface gives origin to the hamstring muscles, its lower part is excluded from the acetabulum, or its crest lies at the level of the second sacral segment? The answer is that its inner surface above the arcuate line forms part of the greater pelvis. The hamstrings arise from the ischial tuberosity.

Question 1469. Which part of the hip bone bears the weight of the body when a person sits: the iliac crest, the anterior superior iliac spine, the ischial tuberosity, or the pubic tubercle? The answer is the ischial tuberosity, the rough lower end of the ischium. The muscles of the back of the thigh arise from its upper part. The sacrotuberous ligament also attaches there.

Question 1470. Which small pointed projection of the hip bone separates the greater sciatic notch above from the lesser sciatic notch below? The answer is the ischial spine. It gives attachment to the sacrospinous ligament, which runs from it to the sacrum. The pudendal nerve hooks around it to reach the perineum.

Question 1471. The hamstring muscles arise from which part of the hip bone: the iliac crest, the anterior superior iliac spine, the ischial tuberosity, or the pubic tubercle? The answer is the ischial tuberosity. The semitendinosus, the semimembranosus and the long head of the biceps femoris all arise there. They cross the hip and the knee and bend both joints.

Question 1472. Which nerve leaves the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen, hooks around the ischial spine, and returns through the lesser sciatic foramen: the sciatic nerve, the pudendal nerve, the obturator nerve, or the femoral nerve? The answer is the pudendal nerve. It curls around the sacrospinous ligament at the spine to reach the perineum. The sciatic nerve does not return.

Question 1473. A described radiograph of the pelvis taken from the front shows, on each side below the socket of the hip, a small pointed spur of bone. Which structure is that spur, and which two openings does it separate? The answer is the ischial spine, which separates the greater sciatic foramen above from the lesser sciatic foramen below.

Question 1474. The lesser sciatic notch lies between which two bony points? The answer is the ischial spine above and the ischial tuberosity below. The sacrospinous and sacrotuberous ligaments cross the notch and close it into the lesser sciatic foramen. Through that foramen pass the tendon of the obturator internus and the pudendal nerve.

Question 1475. Which of these statements about the ischium is correct: its spine gives attachment to the sacrospinous ligament, its tuberosity gives origin to the sartorius, its ramus forms the upper border of the obturator foramen, or its body forms the whole socket of the hip? The answer is that its spine gives attachment to the sacrospinous ligament. Its ramus forms the lower border of the obturator foramen.

Question 1476. True or false: the two bony points on which the body rests when sitting are the ischial tuberosities. The answer is true. They lie at the lower end of each hip bone and are covered by a bursa and a pad of fat. When the hip is flexed, they move relative to the skin and the gluteus maximus slides off them.

Question 1477. A constructed illustration: a young sprinter feels a sudden tearing pain deep in the buttock while stretching for a long stride, and a fragment of bone is seen beside the lower part of the ischium. Which structure has been pulled away, and by which muscles? The answer is the growing tip of the ischial tuberosity, pulled by the hamstrings. The treating clinician decides the management.

Question 1478. Which band of fibrous tissue runs from the back of the ilium and the sacrum down to the ischial tuberosity? The answer is the sacrotuberous ligament. It reaches the lower end of the ischium behind and below the ischial spine. With the sacrospinous ligament it closes the sciatic notches into foramina.

Question 1479. Which of these structures passes through the lesser sciatic foramen: the sciatic nerve, the tendon of the obturator internus, the femoral artery, or the superior gluteal nerve? The answer is the tendon of the obturator internus, which turns around the lesser sciatic notch to reach the buttock. The pudendal nerve and its vessels also pass through that foramen. The sciatic nerve uses the greater foramen.

Question 1480. Which process of the ischium runs forward and upward to meet the pubis and so close the lower part of the ring of bone around the obturator foramen? The answer is the ramus of the ischium. It joins the inferior ramus of the pubis below the obturator foramen.

Question 1481. Which statement about the ischial tuberosities is correct: they move relative to the skin when the hip is flexed, they lie in front of the hip joint, they form the front part of the socket of the hip, or they give origin to the sartorius? The answer is that they move relative to the skin when the hip is flexed. The gluteus maximus slides off them when the hip bends.

Question 1482. A described dissection of the buttock shows a small fluid-filled sac lying between a bony point of the ischium and the skin, where the weight of the body presses in sitting. Which structure is described? The answer is the bursa over the ischial tuberosity. It is a normal cushion there, and it can become inflamed by long pressure.

Question 1483. A described radiograph of the pelvis of a growing child shows a separate scale of bone at the lower end of the ischium, still divided from the rest by a line of cartilage. Why is that piece so easily torn away by a sudden pull? The answer is that it is a growing tip and its cartilage is weaker than the tendon that pulls on it.

Question 1484. Name two bony points of the ischium that the examining hand can reach, spoken as a list. The answer is the ischial tuberosity and the ischial spine. The tuberosity is easily felt beneath the buttock when the hip is flexed. The spine is reached only through the wall of the pelvis.

Question 1485. Which joint lies in the midline at the front of the pelvis, joining the two hip bones to each other? The answer is the pubic symphysis. It is a secondary cartilaginous joint between the bodies of the two pubic bones. A disc of fibrocartilage lies between them, and only a little movement is possible.

Question 1486. Which small bony point at the front of the pelvis gives attachment to the medial end of the inguinal ligament? The answer is the pubic tubercle. It lies at the outer end of the pubic crest, on the upper border of the body of the pubis.

Question 1487. The obturator canal, the small opening at the upper lateral angle of that foramen, transmits which structures: the femoral nerve and vessels, the obturator nerve and vessels, the sciatic nerve, or the tendon of the obturator internus? The answer is the obturator nerve and vessels. They pass from the pelvis into the medial part of the thigh. The obturator membrane closes the rest of the foramen.

Question 1488. Which part of the socket of the hip joint is formed by the pubis: the superior part, the anterior part, the posterior part, or the floor of the notch alone? The answer is the anterior part, about one fifth of the socket. The ilium forms the superior part and the ischium the posterior inferior part. The three meet in the floor of the socket.

Question 1489. A described radiograph of the front of the pelvis shows a large oval opening on each side below and in front of the socket of the hip. Which two bones bound that opening? The answer is the pubis and the ischium. The rami of the pubis bound it in front and above, and the ramus of the ischium behind and below.

Question 1490. Which joint lies immediately medial to the pubic tubercle and the pubic crest? The answer is the pubic symphysis, the joint between the two pubic bones in the midline. The crest is the ridge on the upper border of the body of the pubis, and the tubercle is its outer end.

Question 1491. The subpubic angle, the arch below the pubic symphysis, is wider in which pelvis and why: wider in the male pelvis because its bones are heavier, wider in the female pelvis because it must allow the birth of a child, equal in both, or absent in the female? The answer is that it is wider in the female pelvis. In the male pelvis the arch is narrow and pointed.

Question 1492. Which type of joint is the pubic symphysis: a synovial joint with a cavity, a secondary cartilaginous joint with a disc between the bones, a fibrous joint with no movement at all, or a saddle joint? The answer is a secondary cartilaginous joint with a disc between the bones. It allows only slight movement, which increases late in pregnancy.

Question 1493. A constructed illustration: after a fall, a person has pain in the front of the pelvis, and a break runs through the superior and inferior rami of the pubis. Why is the pelvic ring usually broken in two places? The answer is that it is a closed loop, so a break in one part must be accompanied by trouble elsewhere. The treating clinician decides the management.

Question 1494. A described radiograph of the pelvis shows the brim of the pelvis as a continuous line running from the sacrum forward and around to the pubic symphysis. Which ridges make up that line? The answer is the arcuate line of the ilium, the pecten pubis on the superior ramus, and the pubic crest. Together they form the line that divides the greater pelvis from the lesser.

Question 1495. Which of these pairs belongs together: the pubic tubercle with the medial end of the inguinal ligament, the pecten pubis with the tendon of the rectus femoris, the inferior ramus with the sacrotuberous ligament, or the body of the pubis with the gluteus medius? The answer is the pubic tubercle with the medial end of the inguinal ligament. The pecten pubis gives attachment to the pectineal ligament.

Question 1496. Name the three bones that meet in the socket of the hip joint, spoken as a list. The answer is the ilium, the ischium and the pubis. They meet in the floor of the acetabulum at a band of cartilage shaped like a letter Y, which closes when growth ends. The three together form one hip bone.

Question 1497. The brim of the pelvis is described as a plane that is neither horizontal nor vertical. How is it directed in the standing body, and what does it separate? The answer is that it is tilted forward and downward, and it separates the greater pelvis above from the lesser pelvis below. It runs from the sacral promontory behind to the top of the pubic symphysis in front.

Question 1498. Which statement about the pubis is correct: its superior ramus carries the obturator groove on its under surface, its inferior ramus meets the ilium, its crest lies at the level of the second sacral segment, or its body is the largest part of the hip bone? The answer is that its superior ramus carries the obturator groove. It transmits the obturator vessels and nerve.

Question 1499. Which two muscles arise from the two surfaces of the membrane that closes the obturator foramen, one inside the pelvis and one outside? The answer is the obturator internus within the pelvis and the obturator externus on the outer side. The internus turns around the lesser sciatic notch to reach the buttock. The externus lies on the outer surface of the membrane.

Question 1500. By what route does the weight of the trunk pass through the pelvis to the hip joint of the standing leg? The answer is from the sacrum through the sacroiliac joint into the ilium, and then down the bone above the socket into the femur. The pubis and the ischium act mainly as ties.

To summarize, the five metacarpals form the palm, each with a base at the wrist, a shaft, and a head that makes a knuckle, and the first is the shortest and thickest, carries its growth plate at its base, and joins the trapezium at a saddle joint that lets the thumb oppose the fingers. The others meet the trapezoid, the capitate and the hamate, and the third carries the styloid process. One hand holds fourteen phalanges, three in each finger and two in the thumb, and the interphalangeal joints are hinges while the metacarpophalangeal joints are condyloid and their collateral ligaments tighten as the fingers bend. Each hip bone is built from three bones that meet in the socket of the hip: the ilium, which forms the upper part and carries the iliac crest, the ear-shaped sacroiliac surface, the iliac fossa and the gluteal lines; the ischium, which carries the ischial spine, the lesser sciatic notch and the tuberosity that takes the weight in sitting; and the pubis, which carries the pubic crest and tubercle, the pecten pubis, the obturator foramen and the symphysis. The pelvic ring is a closed loop, so a break in one part is accompanied by trouble elsewhere in the ring, and the brim of the pelvis, tilted forward and downward, divides the greater pelvis from the lesser.
