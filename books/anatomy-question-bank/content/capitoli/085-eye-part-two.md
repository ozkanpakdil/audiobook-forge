# Chapter 85 — Eye, Part Two

This chapter continues the study of the eye by moving through the iris, the ciliary body, the vitreous body, the optic nerve, and the muscles that turn the globe. It holds one hundred questions, and each one states its answer immediately after the question and then explains why that answer is right, so nothing has to be held in mind until the end. The structures described here work together as one optical and mechanical system, and the questions follow them in that order. This is educational material, and it supports rather than replaces an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 8401. Which part of the eye is the colored diaphragm that hangs in front of the lens and has a hole at its center? The answer is the iris. The hole is the pupil, through which light passes to reach the lens. The iris forms the most anterior part of the uveal tract.

Question 8402. Which two spaces does the iris separate: the anterior and posterior chambers, the posterior chamber and the vitreous body, the anterior chamber and the vitreous body, or the vitreous body and the subretinal space? The answer is the anterior and posterior chambers. The anterior chamber lies between the cornea and the iris, and the posterior chamber is the ring behind the iris.

Question 8403. Which muscle of the iris is a ring of circular fibers that narrows the pupil when it contracts? The answer is the sphincter pupillae. Its fibers encircle the pupillary margin, so shortening them draws the margin inward like a purse string. It is also called the pupillary constrictor.

Question 8404. Which sheet of the iris is arranged in radial fibers that run from the root toward the pupillary margin and widen the pupil when it contracts? The answer is the dilator pupillae. Its fibers run outward like the spokes of a wheel, so shortening them pulls the margin open. It lies deep to the stroma.

Question 8405. Which fibers make the pupil narrow when light falls on the eye: sympathetic fibers from the superior cervical ganglion, parasympathetic fibers carried by the third cranial nerve, fibers of the fourth cranial nerve, or fibers of the sixth cranial nerve? The answer is parasympathetic fibers carried by the third cranial nerve. They reach the sphincter through the short ciliary nerves.

Question 8406. Which fibers widen the pupil in dim light: parasympathetic fibers of the third cranial nerve, sympathetic fibers from the superior cervical ganglion, fibers of the optic nerve, or fibers of the fourth cranial nerve? The answer is sympathetic fibers from the superior cervical ganglion. They run with the internal carotid artery and reach the iris through the long ciliary nerves.

Question 8407. True or false: the pupil looks black because the iris contains no pigment at all. The answer is false. Every iris has a pigmented posterior epithelium, and the black appearance comes from light being absorbed inside the eye. The pigment in the front of the iris is what makes the eye brown, hazel, green, or blue.

Question 8408. Which vessels form the major arterial circle at the root of the iris: the long posterior ciliary arteries and the anterior ciliary arteries, the short posterior ciliary arteries alone, the central retinal artery and its branches, or the ophthalmic artery and the angular artery? The answer is the long posterior ciliary arteries and the anterior ciliary arteries. The circle lies in the ciliary body near the iris root.

Question 8409. Which structure lies immediately behind the pupillary margin of the iris, so that the margin rests lightly against it? The answer is the lens. The posterior chamber is only a narrow ring between the iris and the lens, and the pupillary margin glides over the front of the lens as the pupil changes size. The cornea lies in front of the iris.

Question 8410. The outer edge of the iris joins the ciliary body at the thinnest part of the iris. What is that junction called, and why does it matter? The answer is the iris root, which tears more easily than the rest of the iris. A blunt blow to the eye can detach the iris there, an injury described as iridodialysis.

Question 8411. A described optical section through the front of the eye shows a thin pigmented sheet hanging from the side wall, with a clear space in front and a clear round body behind. What does the sheet represent? The answer is the iris. The clear space is the anterior chamber, and the round body behind is the lens.

Question 8412. A described close-up of a normal iris shows a raised circular ridge partway between the root and the pupil, with pits and radiating ridges on its outer side. What is that ridge? The answer is the collarette, which marks the junction of the pupillary zone and the ciliary zone. The pits are the crypts, and the outer zone carries the contraction furrows.

Question 8413. A constructed illustration: a person has a drooping upper lid, an eye that turns outward and downward, and a pupil on that side that is wide and does not narrow in light. Which muscle of the iris has lost its nerve supply? The answer is the sphincter pupillae. The third cranial nerve carries the parasympathetic fibers to it.

Question 8414. A constructed illustration: one pupil is smaller than the other, the upper lid on that side droops slightly, and the skin of that side of the face does not sweat. Which muscle of the iris has failed, and under whose control does it normally lie? The answer is the dilator pupillae, which is supplied by sympathetic fibers. Losing that supply leaves the sphincter unopposed.

Question 8415. Which of these pairs belongs together: the sphincter pupillae with widening the pupil, the dilator pupillae with narrowing the pupil, the collarette with the junction of the two zones of the iris, or the iris root with the center of the pupil? The answer is the collarette with the junction of the two zones. The sphincter narrows the pupil and the dilator widens it.

Question 8416. Name the two muscles of the iris and say in which direction each one moves the pupil. The answer is the sphincter pupillae, which narrows the pupil, and the dilator pupillae, which widens it. The sphincter is a ring of circular fibers and the dilator is a sheet of radial fibers.

Question 8417. A described transverse section through the front of an eye shows a thin pigmented sheet whose peripheral edge is continuous with a triangular mass of tissue behind it, with a narrow gap between the two at the angle. Which two structures are continuous there? The answer is the iris and the ciliary body, joined at the iris root. The gap is the iridocorneal angle.

Question 8418. A described illustration: a person has small irregular pupils that do not narrow when a light is shone into either eye, yet they do narrow when the person looks at a near object. Which part of the reflex pathway is at fault: the optic nerve, the sphincter pupillae, the midbrain region that links them, or the ciliary muscle? The answer is the midbrain region that links them. The near response reaches the sphincter by a separate route.

Question 8419. Why does an eye with very little pigment in the front of the iris still look blue: because the iris epithelium is blue, because the blood within the iris is blue, because short wavelengths of light are scattered back by the fine texture of the stroma, or because the lens is blue? The answer is that short wavelengths are scattered back by the fine texture of the stroma. Brown eyes simply carry more melanin there.

Question 8420. Which layer of the iris continues backward as the pigmented epithelium of the ciliary body and the retina, and where does it lie? The answer is the posterior pigmented epithelium of the iris, the deepest of the iris layers. It faces the posterior chamber and rests against the lens at the pupillary margin.

Question 8421. Which part of the eye is the ring of tissue between the root of the iris and the retina, containing both muscle and secretory epithelium? The answer is the ciliary body. In section it is triangular, with its base against the sclera and its apex toward the lens. It belongs to the uveal tract.

Question 8422. What happens to the lens when the ciliary muscle contracts: it becomes flatter for distant vision, it becomes more convex for near vision, it moves into the anterior chamber, or it does not change? The answer is that it becomes more convex for near vision. Contraction slackens the suspensory fibers, and the elastic lens rounds up.

Question 8423. What are the many small ridges on the inner surface of the ciliary body that produce the fluid filling the front of the eye? The answer is the ciliary processes. They are covered by a double layer of epithelium whose inner layer secretes aqueous humor. They lie in the pars plicata.

Question 8424. What is the name of the ring of fine fibers running from the ciliary body to the capsule of the lens and holding the lens in place? The answer is the zonule of Zinn, also called the suspensory ligament. Tension in these fibers keeps the lens flattened until the ciliary muscle contracts.

Question 8425. Which nerve supply makes the ciliary muscle contract for near vision: sympathetic fibers from the superior cervical ganglion, parasympathetic fibers carried by the third cranial nerve, fibers of the fourth cranial nerve, or fibers of the optic nerve? The answer is parasympathetic fibers carried by the third cranial nerve. They relay in the ciliary ganglion and reach the muscle by the short ciliary nerves.

Question 8426. Where does aqueous humor go as it leaves the anterior chamber: through the trabecular meshwork into the canal of Schlemm, into the vitreous body, into the optic nerve, or into the choroid? The answer is through the trabecular meshwork into the canal of Schlemm. From there it passes to the aqueous veins.

Question 8427. True or false: aqueous humor is produced in the anterior chamber and then flows backward through the pupil. The answer is false. It is produced by the epithelium of the ciliary processes into the posterior chamber, and it flows forward through the pupil into the anterior chamber before leaving at the angle.

Question 8428. Which two structures does the zonule of Zinn connect, and what does the connection allow? The answer is the ciliary body and the capsule of the lens. When the ciliary muscle contracts, the ring narrows and the fibers slacken, so the lens becomes more convex.

Question 8429. In which tissue does the canal of Schlemm lie, and at which part of the globe is it found? The answer is the sclera at the corneoscleral limbus, at the iridocorneal angle. Aqueous humor reaches it after passing through the trabecular meshwork, and from the canal it drains into the aqueous veins.

Question 8430. A described section through the wall of the eye shows a triangular mass of tissue with its base against the sclera and its apex toward the lens, its inner surface thrown into many small ridges. What is this tissue, and what covers its inner surface? The answer is the ciliary body, with the ciliary processes on its inner surface.

Question 8431. A described section of the iridocorneal angle shows fluid passing between beams of connective tissue into a thin-walled channel buried in the sclera. Which two structures are shown? The answer is the trabecular meshwork and the canal of Schlemm. This is the main route by which aqueous humor leaves the eye.

Question 8432. A described section of the inner wall of the eye shows the retina ending in a wavy line, with a smooth dark zone beyond it and ridges beginning further forward. What is the wavy line called, and what lies beyond it? The answer is the ora serrata, beyond which lie the pars plana and then the ciliary processes.

Question 8433. A constructed illustration: a medication that blocks the parasympathetic supply to the eye leaves the pupil wide and makes reading blurred for a few hours. Which muscle has been paralyzed, and why does reading suffer? The answer is the ciliary muscle, which can no longer change the shape of the lens. The pupil is wide for the same reason.

Question 8434. A constructed illustration: a middle-aged person must hold reading material farther away, although the pupils react normally and the eye is otherwise healthy. Which structure has lost its elasticity, and what is the effect? The answer is the lens, which can no longer become convex enough for near vision. The ciliary muscle still contracts normally.

Question 8435. Which of these pairs belongs together: the ciliary muscle with the production of aqueous humor, the ciliary epithelium with accommodation, the zonule of Zinn with suspension of the lens, or the canal of Schlemm with the secretion of aqueous humor? The answer is the zonule of Zinn with suspension of the lens. The ciliary muscle acts in accommodation.

Question 8436. Name the two routes by which aqueous humor leaves the eye, spoken as a sentence. The answer is the trabecular route and the uveoscleral route. Most of the fluid passes through the trabecular meshwork into the canal of Schlemm, while a smaller amount seeps through the ciliary muscle and the sclera.

Question 8437. Which group of fibers in the ciliary muscle pulls on the scleral spur and helps keep the trabecular meshwork open: the circular fibers, the radial fibers, the longitudinal fibers, or the fibers of the dilator pupillae? The answer is the longitudinal fibers, which run forward from the choroid to the scleral spur.

Question 8438. Which three structures make up the uveal tract: the iris, the ciliary body and the choroid, the cornea, the iris and the lens, the retina, the choroid and the sclera, or the iris, the lens and the vitreous body? The answer is the iris, the ciliary body and the choroid. These three are continuous with one another.

Question 8439. A constructed illustration: in one eye the outflow of aqueous humor is blocked at the angle, the globe becomes hard, and the optic nerve head becomes cupped. Which structures are involved, and why does sight fail? The answer is the trabecular meshwork and the optic nerve, whose fibers are damaged by the raised pressure. The ciliary epithelium continues to secrete fluid.

Question 8440. Why does the epithelium covering the ciliary body consist of two layers, one pigmented and one not? The answer is that the two layers are the two walls of the optic cup. The outer wall becomes the pigmented layer, and the inner wall becomes the non-pigmented layer that secretes aqueous humor.

Question 8441. Which structure is the transparent gel that fills the large chamber behind the lens and in front of the retina? The answer is the vitreous body. It occupies about four fifths of the volume of the globe and gives the eye its shape. It is also called the vitreous humor.

Question 8442. Of what is the vitreous body mainly made: water with fine collagen fibers and hyaluronic acid, blood plasma with red cells, a meshwork of elastic fibers, or a thin film of aqueous humor? The answer is water with fine collagen fibers and hyaluronic acid. The gel is about ninety-nine per cent water, and its few cells lie in the cortex.

Question 8443. Which two things does the vitreous body lack, unlike almost every other tissue of the eye? The answer is blood vessels and nerves. It is nourished by diffusion from the tissues around it, mainly the ciliary body and the retina. Its avascular nature helps it stay clear.

Question 8444. What is the name of the narrow channel that runs through the vitreous body from the optic disc to the back of the lens? The answer is the hyaloid canal, also called the canal of Cloquet. It is the remains of the path taken by the hyaloid artery in the fetus.

Question 8445. Where is the vitreous body attached most firmly to the wall of the eye: at the optic disc, at the macula, at the vitreous base over the ora serrata, or at the posterior pole? The answer is at the vitreous base over the ora serrata. There the gel is fused with the retina and the pars plana, and the attachment lasts for life.

Question 8446. Which statement describes a main function of the vitreous body: it refracts light more than the cornea does, it holds the retina against the wall of the eye, it produces aqueous humor, or it changes shape for near vision? The answer is that it holds the retina against the wall of the eye. It also transmits light and cushions the globe.

Question 8447. True or false: the vitreous body is renewed continuously, in the same way as the aqueous humor. The answer is false. The vitreous body is formed before birth and is never replaced. Its gel slowly liquefies with age, which is one reason floaters become common later in life.

Question 8448. Which structure lies immediately in front of the vitreous body, separated from it by the thin hyaloid membrane? The answer is the lens, together with the ring of fibers of the zonule. The vitreous body fills the space behind the lens and in front of the retina.

Question 8449. The vitreous base straddles which two structures, and why does that matter? The answer is the ora serrata, where the retina behind it meets the pars plana of the ciliary body in front. Because the gel is fused to both, traction there can tear the retina.

Question 8450. A described section of an eye shows a clear gel filling the space behind the lens, with a narrow channel running through it from the lens to the optic disc. What is the channel, and what ran through it before birth? The answer is the hyaloid canal, which carried the hyaloid artery.

Question 8451. A described specimen of an aging eye shows the gel collapsed away from the back wall, with a small ring-shaped opacity that had lain around the optic disc before it detached. What has happened? The answer is a posterior vitreous detachment, the ring being the part of the gel that had been attached around the disc.

Question 8452. A described microscopic section of the outer part of the vitreous body shows fine collagen fibers and a few scattered cells but no blood vessels. What region is shown, and what are the cells? The answer is the cortex of the vitreous body, and the cells are the hyalocytes that lie near its surface.

Question 8453. A constructed illustration: after a blow to the eye, a person sees many dark specks drifting across the vision, and a red haze lies behind the lens. Which structure contains the blood? The answer is the vitreous body. Because it is a gel, blood within it clears slowly, and the specks are shadows of the drifting clot.

Question 8454. A constructed illustration: a person suddenly sees flashes of light and a shower of floaters, and a shadow begins to rise across the side of the vision. Which sequence of events in the vitreous is described? The answer is separation of the gel from the retina with a tear, which lets fluid lift the retina from the wall. The treating clinician decides what follows.

Question 8455. Which of these pairs belongs together: the vitreous base with the optic disc, the hyaloid canal with the remnant of the hyaloid artery, the cortex of the vitreous with the retinal pigment epithelium, or the vitreous body with the production of aqueous humor? The answer is the hyaloid canal with the remnant of the hyaloid artery.

Question 8456. Name two places, other than the vitreous base, where the vitreous body is attached to the retina. The answer is the margin of the optic disc and the region of the macula. Attachments are also found along the larger retinal vessels, and all of them weaken with age.

Question 8457. Which change in the vitreous body explains why floaters become more common with age: the gel becomes firmer and shrinks away from the lens, the gel liquefies and forms pockets while its fibers clump, the gel fills with blood vessels, or the gel is replaced by aqueous humor? The answer is that the gel liquefies and forms pockets while its fibers clump.

Question 8458. Why does the vitreous body bend light far less than the cornea does: because it lies behind the lens, because its refractive index is close to that of water and of the tissues around it, because it contains no collagen, or because light does not pass through it? The answer is that its refractive index is close to that of water. Light is bent mainly at the cornea.

Question 8459. In the embryo a vessel runs through the developing vitreous body from the optic disc to the lens and then disappears. What is that vessel, and what does it leave behind? The answer is the hyaloid artery, which leaves behind the hyaloid canal. If part of it persists, a fine strand may remain behind the lens.

Question 8460. When the retina detaches, fluid from the vitreous collects in a space that does not exist in the healthy eye. What is that space called, and which two layers does it separate? The answer is the subretinal space, which separates the retina from the retinal pigment epithelium. The fluid reaches it through a tear.

Question 8461. Which cranial nerve carries the fibers that leave the retina and run to the brain, and what kind of fibers are they? The answer is the second cranial nerve, the optic nerve, which carries special sensory fibers for sight. Its fibers arise from the ganglion cells of the retina and pass backward to the optic chiasm.

Question 8462. From which cells of the retina do the fibers of the optic nerve arise: the rods, the cones, the bipolar cells, or the ganglion cells? The answer is the ganglion cells. Their axons run along the inner surface of the retina, converge at the optic disc, and leave the eye as the optic nerve.

Question 8463. At which point in the retina do the fibers of the optic nerve leave the eye, and why is that point blind? The answer is the optic disc, where the fibers converge and turn backward. It contains no rods or cones, so it forms the blind spot.

Question 8464. Which vessels enter and leave the eye at the optic disc and supply the inner layers of the retina? The answer is the central retinal artery and the central retinal vein. They run inside the optic nerve for part of their course and branch on the surface of the disc.

Question 8465. The optic nerve is wrapped in which coverings, and what fills the space between them: a single fibrous sheath with lymph, the three meninges with cerebrospinal fluid, a layer of fat, or the capsule of the orbit? The answer is the three meninges, with cerebrospinal fluid between the arachnoid and the pia. That space is continuous with the space around the brain.

Question 8466. Which two structures pass through the optic canal: the optic nerve and the ophthalmic artery, the optic nerve and the oculomotor nerve, the optic nerve and the trochlear nerve, or the optic nerve and the abducens nerve? The answer is the optic nerve and the ophthalmic artery. The artery lies inferolateral to the nerve.

Question 8467. True or false: the optic nerve is a peripheral nerve, built like the nerves that supply the limbs. The answer is false. It is a tract of the central nervous system, and its fibers are myelinated by the same cells that myelinate the brain. That is why it is wrapped in meninges.

Question 8468. Which small collection of nerve cells lies immediately lateral to the optic nerve in the orbit, between the nerve and the lateral rectus muscle? The answer is the ciliary ganglion. The parasympathetic fibers for the pupil relay there before reaching the eye through the short ciliary nerves.

Question 8469. At the optic chiasm, which fibers cross to the opposite side, and which stay on the same side? The answer is that fibers from the nasal half of each retina cross, while fibers from the temporal half stay on the same side. This is why a lesion of the chiasm removes the outer halves of both visual fields.

Question 8470. A described axial section through the orbit shows a band of tissue running from the back of the globe to the apex of the orbit, lying in the middle of the cone of muscles. Which structure is shown? The answer is the optic nerve. It is surrounded by orbital fat and lies free within the muscle cone.

Question 8471. A described scan shows a mass growing upward from the region of the sella turcica and pressing on the structure above it. Which structure is compressed, which fibers are lost first, and what does the person notice? The answer is the optic chiasm, whose crossing fibers are lost first, removing the outer half of each visual field.

Question 8472. A described view of the back of the eye shows a disc whose margins are blurred and raised, with the vessels riding over the swelling and no cupping. What does this appearance indicate? The answer is swelling of the optic disc from raised pressure within the skull, because the sheath of the nerve communicates with the space around the brain.

Question 8473. A constructed illustration: a person loses the sight of one eye suddenly and without pain, and the retina looks pale except for a cherry-red spot at the macula. Which vessel is blocked? The answer is the central retinal artery. The macula stays red because the choroid beneath it still shows through the thin retina there.

Question 8474. A constructed illustration: a young person has blurred vision in one eye and pain when the eye is moved. Which structure is inflamed, and why does movement hurt? The answer is the optic nerve. Its sheath is supplied by sensory nerves and is tethered within the orbit, so moving the eye pulls on the inflamed nerve.

Question 8475. Which of these pairs belongs together: the optic disc with the crossing of fibers, the optic chiasm with the blind spot, the lateral geniculate body with the relay of visual fibers, or the optic radiation with the retina? The answer is the lateral geniculate body with the relay of visual fibers. From it the fibers pass to the occipital cortex.

Question 8476. A described examination: when a light is swung from one eye to the other, the pupil narrows on the first side and widens on the second, and the second eye also sees less well. Which structure is at fault? The answer is the optic nerve of the second eye. Its signal is weaker, so the pupil dilates when the light reaches it.

Question 8477. Why does the optic disc swell when pressure inside the skull rises: because the nerve carries no sheath, because the space within its sheath communicates with the space around the brain, because the nerve produces cerebrospinal fluid, or because the retina secretes fluid? The answer is that the space within its sheath communicates with the space around the brain.

Question 8478. Which fibers of the right optic nerve remain on the right side at the chiasm, and where do they end? The answer is the fibers from the temporal half of the right retina, which end in the right lateral geniculate body. Fibers from the nasal half cross to the left side.

Question 8479. In an axial section through the orbit, in which direction does the optic nerve run from the back of the globe, and through which opening does it leave the orbit? The answer is backward and medially, through the optic canal. Because the two canals converge, the nerves form a shape like an X as they approach the chiasm.

Question 8480. The retina and the optic nerve develop from an outgrowth of the embryonic brain. Which adult features of the optic nerve follow from that origin: its covering of meninges and its myelin, its supply from the facial artery, its bony canal, or its union with the ciliary body? The answer is its covering of meninges and its myelin. A structure derived from the brain keeps its coverings.

Question 8481. Which muscle turns the eye directly outward, away from the nose? The answer is the lateral rectus. It arises from the common tendinous ring at the apex of the orbit and inserts on the lateral side of the globe. Its opponent, the medial rectus, turns the eye inward.

Question 8482. Which nerve supplies the lateral rectus muscle: the third cranial nerve, the fourth cranial nerve, the sixth cranial nerve, or the optic nerve? The answer is the sixth cranial nerve, the abducens nerve. It has the longest intracranial course of the nerves that move the eye, so it is often affected by raised pressure within the skull.

Question 8483. Which muscle of the eye sends its tendon through a fibrous pulley attached to the frontal bone on the medial wall of the orbit? The answer is the superior oblique. The pulley is the trochlea. After passing through it, the tendon turns backward and outward to reach the globe.

Question 8484. Which muscle arises from the floor of the orbit just lateral to the lacrimal groove and turns the eye upward when the eye is turned inward? The answer is the inferior oblique. It runs backward and outward below the globe, and it is the only muscle of the orbit that does not arise from the apex.

Question 8485. Which set of actions belongs to the superior rectus when the eye is in the primary position: elevation, intorsion and adduction; depression, extorsion and abduction; pure elevation; or pure abduction? The answer is elevation, intorsion and adduction. Because the muscle approaches the globe at an angle, its pull is not purely upward.

Question 8486. Which muscles arise from the common tendinous ring at the apex of the orbit: the four rectus muscles, the four rectus muscles and the superior oblique, all six muscles that move the eye, or the two oblique muscles alone? The answer is the four rectus muscles. The superior oblique arises above and medial to the ring, and the inferior oblique from the floor.

Question 8487. True or false: the medial rectus muscle is supplied by the sixth cranial nerve. The answer is false. It is supplied by the third cranial nerve, like the superior and inferior rectus muscles and the inferior oblique. The sixth cranial nerve supplies only the lateral rectus.

Question 8488. Which muscle lies immediately deep to the levator palpebrae superioris, so that the two are often described together as a single sheet? The answer is the superior rectus. The levator lies above it, arising from the roof of the orbit, and both run forward to the globe and the lid.

Question 8489. Which muscle lies immediately above the floor of the orbit and is at risk when the floor is fractured and the contents of the orbit are displaced downward? The answer is the inferior rectus. It runs forward just above the bone, so it may be caught in the break, and the eye then cannot look upward.

Question 8490. A described axial section through both orbits shows a muscle running forward along the medial wall of each orbit, separated from the nasal cavity by a thin plate of bone. Which muscle is shown? The answer is the medial rectus. It lies against the ethmoid air cells, which is why surgery there can injure it.

Question 8491. A described coronal section through the orbits shows, around the optic nerve on each side, a ring of four muscles set at the four points of the compass. Which muscles form the ring? The answer is the four rectus muscles, superior, inferior, medial and lateral. The oblique muscles lie outside this cone.

Question 8492. A described specimen of the orbit viewed from above shows a rounded tendon passing through a small fibrous ring on the medial wall and then turning sharply backward. Which muscle is shown? The answer is the superior oblique, and the ring is the trochlea. No other muscle of the orbit changes direction in this way.

Question 8493. A constructed illustration: a person cannot turn one eye outward, and objects appear as two images side by side. Which muscle is weak, and which nerve is at fault? The answer is the lateral rectus, supplied by the sixth cranial nerve. The images are separated horizontally because the eyes no longer point together.

Question 8494. A constructed illustration: a person sees two images, one above the other, and the separation is worst when going down stairs or when the head is tilted to one side. Which muscle is weak? The answer is the superior oblique, the main depressor when the eye is turned inward, so its weakness shows most in that position.

Question 8495. Which of these pairs belongs together: the lateral rectus with the third cranial nerve, the superior oblique with the fourth cranial nerve, the medial rectus with the sixth cranial nerve, or the inferior oblique with the optic nerve? The answer is the superior oblique with the fourth cranial nerve. The lateral rectus is supplied by the sixth and the medial rectus by the third.

Question 8496. Which muscle of the orbit arises from the roof of the orbit above the common tendinous ring and lies immediately beneath the bone: the superior rectus, the levator palpebrae superioris, the superior oblique, or the medial rectus? The answer is the levator palpebrae superioris, which raises the upper lid. The superior rectus lies immediately below it.

Question 8497. Which set of actions belongs to the inferior oblique: elevation, extorsion and abduction; depression, intorsion and adduction; pure depression; or pure intorsion? The answer is elevation, extorsion and abduction. Like the superior oblique, it approaches the globe from behind, so its action depends on the position of the eye.

Question 8498. A constructed illustration: a person tilts the head to one side and the eye on that side rises while the other eye stays level, producing a vertical double image. Which muscle is weak on the side that rises? The answer is the superior oblique. In the tilting test the weak muscle fails to hold the eye steady.

Question 8499. In an axial section through an orbit, which muscles lie on either side of the optic nerve, and how do they relate to the walls of the orbit? The answer is the medial rectus against the medial wall and the lateral rectus against the lateral wall. The nerve lies in the middle of the cone between them.

Question 8500. Which muscle of the orbit arises from the orbital surface of the maxilla, passes below the globe and inserts on its upper and outer surface behind the equator? The answer is the inferior oblique. It is the only muscle of the eye that arises from the front of the orbit.

To summarize, the iris is the pigmented diaphragm in front of the lens, and its two muscles set the size of the pupil: the sphincter pupillae narrows it under parasympathetic fibers of the third cranial nerve, and the dilator pupillae widens it under sympathetic fibers. The ciliary body lies between the iris root and the ora serrata, and its muscle changes the shape of the lens for near vision while its epithelium secretes aqueous humor, which leaves the eye at the iridocorneal angle through the trabecular meshwork and the canal of Schlemm. The vitreous body is an avascular gel formed before birth that holds the retina against the wall and is never replaced, and its strongest attachment is the vitreous base over the ora serrata. The optic nerve is a tract of the brain wrapped in meninges, and it runs backward and medially through the optic canal, so raised pressure within the skull can swell its disc. The six muscles that move the eye arise from the apex or the floor of the orbit, and the fourth and sixth cranial nerves supply the superior oblique and the lateral rectus while the third supplies the rest.
