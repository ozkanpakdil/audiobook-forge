# Chapter 114 — Structure and Function

This chapter treats shape as the explanation of what a structure does and of how it fails. It moves through bone, cartilage, joints, muscle, tendon, skin, vessels, heart, lymphatics, airway, gut, urinary tract and glands, asking what each form is for. It holds one hundred questions, and every answer follows its question immediately with the reason it is right. The last twenty-five items turn that reasoning toward injury, asking which structure a described mechanism puts at risk. This is educational material, and it does not replace an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 11301. Which kind of bone tissue forms the hard outer shell of a long bone, and which fills the interior? The answer is that cortical bone forms the shell and cancellous bone fills the interior. Cortical bone is dense and resists bending. Cancellous bone is a network of thin plates that resists compression where load enters at a joint.

Question 11302. What is the advantage of a long bone shaft built as a hollow tube: it is lighter for the same strength, it is heavier but stiffer, it stores more marrow, or it grows faster? The answer is that it is lighter for the same strength. Material far from the center resists bending better than material at the axis, and the marrow cavity costs almost nothing.

Question 11303. A described section of a long bone shows a dense outer layer, a spongy interior at both ends, and a central cavity of fatty tissue. Which parts are shown? The answer is the cortex, the cancellous bone of the ends, and the marrow cavity. The fat is yellow marrow, which replaces red marrow in the shaft.

Question 11304. A described specimen shows thin plates of bone arranged in arches that cross one another near a joint. What is this arrangement, and what decides the direction of the plates? The answer is cancellous bone, whose plates follow the lines of stress. When loading changes, the plates slowly remodel along the new lines.

Question 11305. Why do the plates in the neck of the femur cross in two opposing arcs: to resist tension above and compression below, to fill space, to carry marrow, or to speed healing? The answer is to resist tension above and compression below. The upper arc carries the pull of the pelvis, and the lower arc carries the body weight into the shaft.

Question 11306. By what path does the weight of the body leave the pelvis and enter the shaft of the femur? The answer is through the head and the neck, by way of the arches of cancellous bone, into the shaft. The load then joins the thick inner cortex of the shaft.

Question 11307. Which of these pairs belongs together: hyaline cartilage with the knee joint surface, fibrocartilage with the outer ear, elastic cartilage with the intervertebral disc, or hyaline cartilage with the pubic symphysis? The answer is hyaline cartilage with the knee joint surface. The outer ear is elastic, and the disc and the pubic symphysis are fibrocartilage.

Question 11308. What is the name of the smooth, glassy cartilage that covers the ends of bones inside a joint? The answer is hyaline cartilage. It has no blood vessels and no nerves, so it cannot feel pain and heals poorly. Synovial fluid keeps it slippery, which is why a joint moves almost without friction.

Question 11309. A described radiograph shows the neck of the femur running upward and inward from the shaft to the head. In which plane must the limb lie for that angle to appear true? The answer is the frontal plane. That plane divides the body into front and back parts, and any other view foreshortens the neck.

Question 11310. Which of these is a ball and socket joint: the shoulder, the elbow, the ankle, or the joint between the atlas and the axis? The answer is the shoulder. Its rounded head sits in a shallow socket and moves in every direction, including rotation. The elbow and the ankle are hinges.

Question 11311. What are the two parts of an intervertebral disc, and which lies in the center? The answer is the annulus fibrosus around the outside and the nucleus pulposus in the center. The annulus is built of rings of fibrocartilage. The nucleus is a soft core that spreads compression onto those rings.

Question 11312. A described midline section of the lower spine shows a disc whose soft center has been pushed backward. What has happened, and what lies in that space? The answer is a disc herniation, and the space holds a spinal nerve root. The soft center escapes through a tear in the outer rings and presses on the root.

Question 11313. Where does the nucleus pulposus lie within a disc, and which way does it move when the spine bends? The answer is that it lies slightly behind the center and moves backward. Bending forward squeezes the front of the disc and pushes the core backward, which is why a disc usually bulges backward.

Question 11314. How does a sesamoid bone such as the patella change the action of the muscle that runs through it: it lengthens the lever arm, it shortens the muscle, it adds a joint, or it has no effect? The answer is that it lengthens the lever arm by holding the tendon away from the joint. The tendon then pulls at a better angle, so the same force turns the joint more.

Question 11315. Which of these is a sesamoid bone, a small bone embedded in a tendon where it crosses a joint: the patella, the talus, the clavicle, or the calcaneus? The answer is the patella, the largest sesamoid bone. It lies in the tendon of the quadriceps at the front of the knee.

Question 11316. A described specimen from the knee shows two C-shaped pads of fibrocartilage resting on the flat upper surface of the tibia. What are they, and what do they do? The answer is the menisci. They deepen the flat tibial surface, spread the load of the femur, and help absorb shock.

Question 11317. In which of these places does fibrocartilage form the main tissue: the articular surface of the hip, the intervertebral disc, the epiglottis, or the trachea? The answer is the intervertebral disc. Fibrocartilage resists compression and tearing at once, which is what a disc must do. The hip surface is hyaline cartilage.

Question 11318. A described section through the knee shows a wedge of tissue between the femur and the tibia, with fibers in two directions. What is it, and why does its shape matter? The answer is a meniscus, whose wedge turns the flat tibial surface into a shallow socket. The two fiber directions resist tearing and squeezing.

Question 11319. Where does the medial meniscus sit in relation to the medial collateral ligament of the knee? The answer is that the meniscus lies deep to the ligament and is attached to it. The deep fibers of the ligament blend with the outer edge of the meniscus, so a tear of one often tears the other.

Question 11320. Which of these pairs belongs together: a hinge joint with movement in one plane, a saddle joint with movement in no plane, a pivot joint with gliding only, or a ball and socket joint with no movement? The answer is a hinge joint with movement in one plane. A pivot joint turns about one axis, and a saddle joint moves in two planes.

Question 11321. What is the name of the ring of fibrocartilage attached to the rim of the shoulder socket? The answer is the glenoid labrum. It deepens the shallow socket and gives the head of the humerus a better fit. It also gives attachment to the capsule.

Question 11322. Why does the shoulder dislocate more often than the hip: the socket is shallower, the ligaments are absent, the capsule is thicker, or the bone is smaller? The answer is the shallower socket. The glenoid is a small, flat dish that allows wide movement at the cost of stability, while the acetabulum is a deep cup.

Question 11323. True or false: the labrum of the shoulder is a rim of hyaline cartilage that deepens the socket. The answer is false. The labrum is a rim of fibrocartilage attached to the bony edge of the glenoid. It widens the contact area and adds stability.

Question 11324. A described membrane lines the inside of a joint capsule but stops at the edge of the cartilage. What is it, and what does it produce? The answer is the synovial membrane. It secretes the fluid that lubricates the joint and feeds the cartilage, which has no blood vessels.

Question 11325. Which statement about a pivot joint is correct: it allows rotation about one axis, it allows gliding in two planes, it permits no movement, or it allows movement in three planes? The answer is rotation about one axis. The joint between the atlas and the axis lets the head turn, and the joints of the forearm turn the palm.

Question 11326. A described section through the hip joint shows a ring of fibrocartilage around the rim of the socket. What is the ring, and how does it work? The answer is the acetabular labrum. It deepens the cup, grips the head, and helps seal the fluid inside, whose film resists the head being pulled out.

Question 11327. What kind of joint is formed where two bones are united by a pad of fibrocartilage that allows only slight movement: a hinge joint, a cartilaginous joint, a pivot joint, or a saddle joint? The answer is a cartilaginous joint. The joints between the vertebral bodies and between the pubic bones are of this kind.

Question 11328. A described section through a joint in front of the ear shows a disc of fibrocartilage dividing the cavity into two spaces. Which joint is shown, and what does the disc allow? The answer is the temporomandibular joint. The disc lets the jaw both hinge and slide forward.

Question 11329. Which joint lies between the first cervical vertebra and the second, and what movement does it allow: the head to nod, the head to turn, the shoulder to circle, or the jaw to open? The answer is the joint between the atlas and the axis, which turns the head. Nodding happens higher up.

Question 11330. Name two joints that allow rotation only, spoken as a list. The answer is the joint between the atlas and the axis, and the joint between the radius and the ulna near the elbow. Both are pivot joints, turning about a single long axis. Together they turn the head and the palm.

Question 11331. A described spindle-shaped structure lies within a skeletal muscle, wrapped in a capsule and supplied by a nerve. What is it, and what does it report? The answer is a muscle spindle. It lies parallel to the ordinary fibers and senses how far and how fast the muscle stretches, which adjusts its tension.

Question 11332. Which part of a sarcomere shortens when a muscle contracts: the thick filaments, the thin filaments, the distance between the two sets of filaments, or the Z lines themselves? The answer is the distance between the two sets of filaments. The filaments keep their own length and slide past one another.

Question 11333. A described microscopic section of muscle shows long parallel fibers with fine cross bands and many nuclei at the edge. Which kind of muscle is shown? The answer is skeletal muscle. The cross bands are the regular overlap of thick and thin filaments, and the many nuclei show fused cells.

Question 11334. At the place where a tendon joins a bone, which tissue lies between the two, and why does it matter? The answer is fibrocartilage that gradually changes from tendon into bone. That gradual change spreads the pull over a wider area and removes a sharp corner where a tendon would tear away.

Question 11335. A described cord of dense regular connective tissue joins the end of a muscle to a bone. What is this structure? The answer is a tendon. Its fibers are packed in parallel in the direction of pull, which makes it very strong and almost inextensible.

Question 11336. Which of these pairs belongs together: epimysium with the whole muscle, perimysium with a single fiber, endomysium with a bundle of fibers, or epimysium with a single fiber? The answer is epimysium with the whole muscle. The perimysium wraps a bundle, and the endomysium wraps each fiber.

Question 11337. How does a pennate muscle differ from a parallel muscle of the same size: it develops more force but shortens less, it develops less force but shortens more, it moves the joint further, or it has no tendon? The answer is more force and less shortening. More fibers fit into the same volume because they attach at an angle to the tendon.

Question 11338. Name two muscles whose fibers run at an angle to their tendon, spoken as a list. The answer is the rectus femoris of the thigh and the deltoid of the shoulder. A muscle of this kind is called pennate, and it trades distance for force.

Question 11339. Which of these pairs belongs together: a multipennate muscle with the deltoid, a parallel muscle with the gastrocnemius, a bipennate muscle with the biceps brachii, or a circular muscle with the rectus abdominis? The answer is a multipennate muscle with the deltoid. The gastrocnemius is bipennate and the biceps brachii is parallel.

Question 11340. In most limb muscles the effort is applied between the joint and the load, which makes them what kind of lever: first class, second class, third class, or fourth class? The answer is third class. The muscle pulls between the fulcrum of the joint and the weight. This favors speed and range over force, which is why limbs move quickly.

Question 11341. Why does the middle part of the deltoid raise the arm with the greatest effect? The answer is that its fibers lie farthest from the axis of the joint, so its lever arm is longest. A longer lever arm gives more turning effect for the same force, and the other parts lie nearer the axis.

Question 11342. A described section of a muscle shows fibers converging from one side onto a tendon on the other side. What arrangement is shown, and what does it do to the force? The answer is a unipennate arrangement. The fibers join the tendon at an angle, so more of them fit and it pulls harder.

Question 11343. A described specimen shows a broad flat sheet of tendon that spreads over the side of the abdomen and joins the muscle to the midline. What is this sheet? The answer is an aponeurosis. It gives a wide area for fibers to attach and spreads their pull over a broad surface, as in the palm.

Question 11344. Why can the calf muscles lift the whole body weight at the ankle: the load lies between the fulcrum and the effort, the fulcrum lies between the load and the effort, the effort lies between the load and the fulcrum, or no lever is involved? The answer is that the load lies between the fulcrum and the effort. The toes are the fulcrum, and the calf pulls on the heel behind the ankle.

Question 11345. True or false: a pennate muscle can shorten further than a parallel muscle with the same length of fiber. The answer is false. A pennate muscle develops more force but shortens less, because its fibers pull at an angle to the tendon. The parallel muscle gives up force for a longer range.

Question 11346. Which of these pairs belongs together: intercalated discs with cardiac muscle, many nuclei at the edge of the fiber with smooth muscle, spindle-shaped cells with skeletal muscle, or voluntary control with cardiac muscle? The answer is intercalated discs with cardiac muscle. Smooth muscle cells are spindle-shaped with one central nucleus.

Question 11347. Why does the impulse travel faster through the conducting fibers of the heart than through the ordinary muscle: the fibers are larger, the fibers are wrapped in fat, they release more calcium, or they are shorter? The answer is that the fibers are larger. A wider fiber carries current more easily along its inside, and its junctions pass the impulse on quickly.

Question 11348. A described chest radiograph shows a dense white shadow behind the sternum, with dark lungs on either side and the ribs crossing in front. What does the shadow represent, and why is it white? The answer is the heart and the mediastinum. Dense tissue absorbs more of the beam than air does.

Question 11349. A described organ is bean-shaped, has a capsule, an outer zone crowded with round nodules, and a center with cords of cells. What organ is it, and what does it do? The answer is a lymph node. It filters the lymph passing through it and gives lymphocytes a place to meet what they must answer.

Question 11350. Where in a lymph node do B lymphocytes gather, and where do T lymphocytes gather? The answer is that B lymphocytes gather in the outer cortex and T lymphocytes in the deep cortex just inside it. The medulla at the center holds macrophages and plasma cells.

Question 11351. What are the two tissues of the spleen, and what does each do: red pulp filters blood and white pulp fights infection, red pulp stores iron and white pulp makes antibodies, red pulp filters lymph and white pulp stores iron, or red pulp makes antibodies and white pulp filters blood? The answer is that red pulp filters blood and white pulp fights infection. White pulp surrounds small arteries and holds lymphocytes.

Question 11352. True or false: the thymus is largest in adult life, when the immune system is most active. The answer is false. The thymus is largest in childhood and then slowly shrinks as its tissue is replaced by fat. It is where T lymphocytes are trained, and most of that work is done early.

Question 11353. A described collection of lymphoid tissue sits in the wall of the throat, with folds of tissue and deep crypts on its surface. What is it, and where is it placed? The answer is a tonsil. It sits at the entrance to the throat, where it samples what is breathed and swallowed. Its crypts give a large surface for that contact.

Question 11354. A described section of a small organ shows a fibrous capsule sending bands inward, a dark outer zone of round nodules, and a pale center with cords of cells. What organ is shown? The answer is a lymph node, and the nodules of its outer zone are lymphatic nodules. The bands from the capsule are trabeculae.

Question 11355. Where in the lung does the exchange of oxygen and carbon dioxide take place: the trachea, the bronchi, the bronchioles, or the alveoli? The answer is the alveoli. Their walls are one cell thick and lie against the capillaries, so gas crosses a very short distance. The airways above them only carry air.

Question 11356. Why does the lung of a premature infant tend to collapse: the alveoli are too few, the surfactant that lowers surface tension is not yet made, the airways are too wide, or the diaphragm is weak? The answer is that the surfactant is not yet made. Surface tension in the film lining each alveolus pulls its walls together, and surfactant reduces that tension.

Question 11357. A described section of an airway shows a wall held open by C-shaped plates of cartilage, with a band of smooth muscle behind them. Which part of the airway is shown? The answer is a bronchus. The plates keep the airway from collapsing during a forced breath, and the muscle can narrow the tube.

Question 11358. Why does the dome of the diaphragm flatten when it contracts, and what does that do to the chest? The answer is that its fibers run from the central tendon to the lower ribs and the vertebral column, so shortening pulls the center down and the ribs up. That enlarges the chest from top to bottom.

Question 11359. In which direction do the fibers of the external intercostal muscles run, and what does that do: downward and forward, so they raise the ribs, downward and backward, so they lower the ribs, straight down, or around the chest? The answer is downward and forward, so they raise the ribs. The internal intercostal fibers run the other way.

Question 11360. A described section through the side wall of the nose shows three curled shelves of bone covered by moist membrane, with air spaces between them. What are these shelves, and what are they for? The answer is the conchae. They increase the surface over which air passes, so it is warmed, moistened, and cleaned.

Question 11361. A described flap of elastic cartilage stands at the root of the tongue and folds over the opening of the voice box during swallowing. What is it? The answer is the epiglottis. Its elastic cartilage springs upright again when swallowing ends, and it keeps food out of the airway.

Question 11362. True or false: the wall of the small intestine is built of four coats, one of which is a muscle layer that moves the contents along. The answer is true. From the outside inward the coats are the serous coat, the muscle coat, the submucous coat, and the mucous membrane. The muscle coat has two layers.

Question 11363. What are the finger-like projections of the lining of the small intestine for: to increase the surface for absorbing food, to move the contents along, to make enzymes, or to store fat? The answer is to increase the surface for absorbing food. Each projection is covered with cells whose free edge carries even finer folds.

Question 11364. A described ring of thickened circular muscle guards the outlet of the stomach and opens only at intervals. What is it, and what does it do? The answer is the pyloric sphincter. It holds food in the stomach long enough to be mixed, then releases it in small amounts.

Question 11365. A described transverse section through the upper abdomen shows a large organ on the right and a smaller one on the left. Which plane was used, and where do the two organs lie? The answer is a transverse plane, and the section shows the liver on the right and the spleen on the left. It divides the body into upper and lower parts.

Question 11366. A described microscopic section of the liver shows six-sided units, each with a vessel at its center and plates of cells running outward like the spokes of a wheel. What are these units, and what runs between the plates? The answer is the liver lobules. Between the plates run the channels that carry bile outward and the spaces where blood is processed.

Question 11367. Why can the pancreas be described as two glands in one: it makes enzymes and also makes hormones, it stores bile and releases it, it filters blood and makes lymph, or it absorbs food and makes acid? The answer is that it makes enzymes and also makes hormones. Most of the gland pours enzymes into a duct, while small clusters release hormones into the blood.

Question 11368. A described section of the kidney shows a round tuft of capillaries pushed into a cup-shaped capsule. What is the tuft called, and where is it found? The answer is the glomerulus, and it lies in the cortex of the kidney. Blood is filtered through its walls into the capsule, and the fluid becomes urine.

Question 11369. Why does the loop of Henle let the kidney make urine more concentrated than the blood: its two limbs carry fluid in opposite directions and build a salt gradient, it stores water, it makes red cells, or it reabsorbs protein? The answer is the opposite directions of flow. Salt is pumped out of the ascending limb, which water cannot leave.

Question 11370. A described section of the kidney shows an outer zone of round bodies and a deeper zone of triangular areas with stripes running toward the center. Which parts are shown, and what do the stripes mean? The answer is the cortex outside and the medulla inside, with the renal pyramids within the medulla. The stripes are parallel tubules and vessels.

Question 11371. True or false: the ureter moves urine to the bladder by gravity alone. The answer is false. Its wall carries smooth muscle in two layers, and waves of contraction push the urine along whatever the position of the body. The same waves are felt as colic when the tube is blocked.

Question 11372. What is the name of the smooth muscle of the wall of the urinary bladder? The answer is the detrusor muscle. Its fibers run in several directions and contract as one to empty the bladder. As the bladder fills, the fold of membrane at its base flattens out.

Question 11373. What is the structural unit of the thyroid gland: a follicle lined by cells and filled with stored secretion, a cluster of nerve cells, a duct opening onto the skin, or a knot of capillaries? The answer is a follicle lined by cells and filled with stored secretion. The cells take iodine from the blood and build the hormones.

Question 11374. Why does a tumor of the adrenal medulla raise the blood pressure in sudden attacks, while a tumor of the cortex does not? The answer is that the medulla releases its hormones straight into the blood, while the cortex releases steroids that act more slowly. The medulla is part of the sympathetic nervous system.

Question 11375. Name two features of the small intestine that increase its surface area, spoken as a list. The answer is the villi of its lining and the circular folds of its wall. Each villus is covered with cells whose free border carries fine projections, and together they multiply the surface many times.

Question 11376. A constructed illustration: a player plants a foot and turns sharply, and the knee gives way with a sudden pop and fast swelling. Which ligament inside the knee is most likely torn? The answer is the anterior cruciate ligament. It stops the tibia from sliding forward on the femur, and a twist with the foot fixed loads it most.

Question 11377. Which ligament is most often torn when the ankle turns inward: the anterior talofibular ligament, the deltoid ligament, the calcaneofibular ligament, or the spring ligament? The answer is the anterior talofibular ligament. It is the weakest part of the outer complex and lies first in the path of the turning foot.

Question 11378. A described specimen shows a strong triangular ligament running from the medial malleolus down to the bones of the foot on the inner side. Which structure is it, and what movement does it resist? The answer is the deltoid ligament, which resists turning the sole outward. It is stronger than the ligaments on the outer side.

Question 11379. A described radiograph of the wrist shows a crack across the lower end of the radius, with the fragment tilted backward. What injury is shown? The answer is a break of the lower end of the radius with backward displacement, the break of a fall on the outstretched hand. The hand is driven back, and the fragment follows.

Question 11380. A constructed illustration: an older person has pain when raising the arm sideways through the middle of the arc, and the pain eases once the arm is high. Which structure is inflamed? The answer is the supraspinatus tendon, which passes beneath the arch over the shoulder. There the tendon is pressed hardest against the arch.

Question 11381. Why is the ulnar nerve so easily injured behind the inner side of the elbow: it lies in a shallow groove with little covering, it lies deep under muscle, it passes through a bony tunnel, or it runs beside the artery? The answer is that it lies in a shallow groove. A knock on the medial epicondyle tingles in the little finger.

Question 11382. A constructed illustration: a deep cut across the front of the wrist leaves the thumb unable to oppose and the palm numb on its outer half. Which nerve is most likely cut? The answer is the median nerve. It lies just under the skin in the middle of the wrist, where the branch to the thumb muscles leaves it.

Question 11383. How does the radial nerve relate to the shaft of the humerus, and why does that matter after a break? The answer is that the nerve lies in a spiral groove on the back of the shaft, close against the bone. A break across the middle can press on or tear it, and the wrist then cannot be lifted.

Question 11384. A constructed illustration: a blow strikes the outer side of a fixed knee, and the joint opens on the inner side, with pain along the inner joint line. Which two structures are injured together, and why? The answer is the medial collateral ligament and the medial meniscus. The deep fibers of the ligament are attached to the meniscus.

Question 11385. Why does bleeding into a joint after a ligament tear make the whole joint swell so quickly: the synovial membrane is rich in blood vessels, the cartilage bleeds, the bone bleeds, or the fluid is secreted? The answer is that the synovial membrane is rich in blood vessels. Blood irritates the membrane, which then secretes more fluid.

Question 11386. A constructed illustration: after a fall on the outstretched hand, a person has tenderness in the hollow at the base of the thumb and pain when the wrist is loaded. Which bone is most likely broken? The answer is the scaphoid. It lies in the floor of that hollow and bridges the two rows of carpal bones.

Question 11387. A described radiograph taken a week after a fall shows a fine crack across a small bone at the base of the thumb, with no displacement. Which bone is shown, and why is the injury followed closely? The answer is the scaphoid. Its blood enters near the far end, so a break across the middle can starve the piece nearest the thumb.

Question 11388. Which carpal bone is broken most often, and why does the fragment nearest the thumb heal so poorly? The answer is the scaphoid, because its blood enters near the far end and travels backward along the bone. A break across its waist cuts off the supply to the piece nearest the thumb, and that piece may die.

Question 11389. A constructed illustration: an older person breaks the neck of the femur, and months later the head of the bone crumbles. Why did the head lose its blood supply? The answer is that the vessels to the head run along the neck, and a break there tears them. The head keeps only the small artery in the ligament to its pit.

Question 11390. After a break of the surgical neck of the humerus, which nerve is most at risk: the axillary nerve, the radial nerve, the median nerve, or the ulnar nerve? The answer is the axillary nerve, which winds around the back of the surgical neck against the bone. It supplies the deltoid, so the shoulder becomes flat.

Question 11391. A constructed illustration: a small child is lifted by one hand, and the arm then hangs with the palm turned down. Which structure has slipped, and why does it slip so easily at this age? The answer is the head of the radius, pulled partly out of the ring of ligament that holds it. In a small child that ring is loose.

Question 11392. How does the common fibular nerve relate to the neck of the fibula, and why does that relation matter? The answer is that the nerve wraps around the lateral side of the fibular neck just beneath the skin. A blow there or a tight cast can injure it, and the foot then drops.

Question 11393. A constructed illustration: a middle-aged person feels a sudden snap at the back of the ankle while pushing off and cannot rise on the toes. Which structure has ruptured? The answer is the Achilles tendon. Squeezing the calf no longer moves the foot, and the gap can often be felt above the heel.

Question 11394. Why does the Achilles tendon rupture most often in the zone a few centimeters above its insertion: the tendon is thinnest and least supplied with blood there, the tendon is widest there, the bone is weakest there, or the skin is thinnest there? The answer is that the tendon is thinnest and least supplied there.

Question 11395. A described radiograph of the hand shows a break across the neck of the fifth metacarpal, with the head of the bone tilted toward the palm. Which injury is shown, and why does the head tilt? The answer is a boxer's fracture. The pull of the small muscles of the hand tilts the head forward, and the knuckle is lost.

Question 11396. A constructed illustration: after a seizure, a person holds the arm against the chest and cannot lift it, with numbness over the outer side of the shoulder. Which joint has dislocated, and which nerve is injured? The answer is the shoulder joint, dislocated backward, with injury to the axillary nerve. Backward dislocation follows a strong muscular pull.

Question 11397. A described specimen shows a small ligament running from the pit in the head of the femur to the floor of the socket, carrying an artery with it. What is this ligament called? The answer is the ligament of the head of the femur. It is weak, and its artery supplies only a small part of the head.

Question 11398. A constructed illustration: a rider is thrown and the knee is forced into a bent position, and the knee can no longer be straightened. What has happened inside the joint? The answer is a torn meniscus whose flap has turned into the joint. The piece is caught between the femur and the tibia, blocking extension.

Question 11399. Why does a break of the shaft of the tibia often injure the vessel that passes between the two bones of the leg: the bones are bound by a membrane and the vessel lies against it, the vessel runs inside the bone, the muscles press on it, or the skin is tight? The answer is that the bones are bound by a membrane and the vessel lies against it.

Question 11400. Which statement explains why the medial meniscus is torn more often than the lateral one: it is attached to the medial collateral ligament and moves less, it is larger, it has a better blood supply, or it lies deeper in the joint? The answer is that it is attached to the medial collateral ligament and moves less, so it cannot slide away.

To summarize, structure and function answer one another throughout this chapter: a hollow shaft is light for its strength, cancellous plates follow the lines of stress, hyaline cartilage gives a nearly frictionless surface, and fibrocartilage takes compression where a pad must also resist tearing. The shape of a joint sets its range of movement, from the shallow glenoid that trades stability for mobility to the deep acetabulum that does the opposite, while the labrum and the menisci deepen a socket that would otherwise be too flat. The arrangement of muscle fibers decides whether a muscle is built for force or for distance, so a pennate muscle pulls harder and a parallel muscle shortens further, and the levers of a limb favor speed over force. The same reasoning explains injury, because the anterior cruciate ligament, the medial meniscus, the supraspinatus tendon, the scaphoid, the fibular neck and the watershed of the Achilles tendon are each weak for a reason that can be named.
