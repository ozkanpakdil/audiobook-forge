# Chapter 104 — Early Embryonic Development

The first three weeks after fertilization turn a single cell into a three-layered disc from which every tissue of the body will be built. This chapter follows that sequence: fertilization and cleavage, the morula and the blastocyst, implantation, the two-layered embryonic disc with its amniotic cavity and yolk sac, gastrulation, the notochord, the neural tube and the somites, and then the three germ layers with the structures each one gives rise to. It holds one hundred questions, and every question is answered in the same paragraph, immediately, so that nothing has to be carried in mind. The names here are few, but they explain almost every later chapter of anatomy. This is educational material, and it supports rather than replaces an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 10301. In the female reproductive tract, where does a sperm normally meet the oocyte? The answer is the ampulla of the uterine tube. That is the wide, curved segment of the tube that arches over the ovary. The narrow part of the tube next to the uterus is not the usual site of fertilization, and an embryo that implants there is abnormally placed.

Question 10302. Which covering does a sperm penetrate first as it reaches the oocyte: the corona radiata, the zona pellucida, the cell membrane of the oocyte, or the cortical granules? The answer is the corona radiata. It is the loose collar of follicle cells that still clings to the oocyte after ovulation. The zona pellucida, a thick coat, lies beneath it and is crossed next.

Question 10303. Which change must a sperm undergo in the female reproductive tract before it can fertilize an oocyte: capacitation, implantation, compaction, or gastrulation? The answer is capacitation. During capacitation the membrane of the sperm is remodeled and its acrosome becomes able to release the enzymes that digest a path through the coverings of the oocyte. The other three events belong to the embryo after fertilization.

Question 10304. A described section of a uterine tube shows one cell with two small round nuclei lying side by side inside a single membrane, surrounded by a ring of follicle cells. What has just formed? The answer is a fertilized oocyte, or zygote, whose two pronuclei have not yet fused.

Question 10305. Immediately outside the cell membrane of the oocyte lies which transparent coat, and what is its part in fertilization? The answer is the zona pellucida. It is a thick layer that lies between the oocyte and the corona radiata. A sperm must cross it, and once one sperm has entered, the coat changes so that no second sperm can pass.

Question 10306. After one sperm has entered the oocyte, what prevents a second sperm from entering: the cortical reaction, the acrosome reaction, capacitation, or compaction? The answer is the cortical reaction. The cortical granules release their contents into the space beneath the zona pellucida and alter it so that it can no longer be penetrated. This change is also called the zona reaction.

Question 10307. What name is given to the cell formed when the male and female pronuclei fuse, and how many chromosomes does it hold? The answer is the zygote, which holds forty-six chromosomes, twenty-three from each parent. It is the first cell of the new individual.

Question 10308. A constructed illustration: a pregnancy has implanted in the wall of the uterine tube rather than in the cavity of the uterus. Which part of the tube is the most common site for such an implantation? The answer is the ampulla. It is the widest part of the tube and the usual place of fertilization. Management belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 10309. True or false: during cleavage the number of cells increases while the total mass of the conceptus stays much the same. The answer is true. The cells produced are called blastomeres, and each division makes them smaller without adding bulk. The zona pellucida holds them together, so the whole structure stays about the size of the original zygote.

Question 10310. After several cleavages the conceptus becomes a solid ball of about sixteen cells with no cavity inside. Which of these names this stage: the morula, the blastocyst, the zygote, or the gastrula? The answer is the morula, which forms about four days after fertilization. Its name comes from the word for a mulberry, which it resembles.

Question 10311. As the morula hollows out, it becomes a sphere with an outer shell of flattened cells and a small cluster of larger cells at one pole. Name the two cell populations. The answer is the trophoblast outside and the embryoblast within. The trophoblast forms the embryonic part of the placenta, and the embryoblast forms the embryo itself.

Question 10312. A described specimen shows a hollow sphere in the cavity of the uterus, with a fluid-filled space inside, a thin outer wall, and a compact mass of cells pushed to one side. Which stage is shown? The answer is the blastocyst. The outer wall is the trophoblast, the inner mass is the embryoblast that will build the embryo, and the cavity is the blastocele.

Question 10313. Which of these pairs belongs together: the trophoblast with the embryo, the embryoblast with the embryo, the blastocele with the placenta, or the zona pellucida with the amniotic cavity? The answer is the embryoblast with the embryo. The inner cell mass gives rise to all three germ layers and therefore to the embryo.

Question 10314. What is the name of the fluid-filled cavity inside the blastocyst, and what does it allow the inner cell mass to do? The answer is the blastocele. Its fluid separates the embryoblast from the trophoblast and presses the inner cell mass to one side.

Question 10315. Name, in one spoken sentence, the two cell populations of the blastocyst and the structure each one forms. The answer is the trophoblast, which forms the embryonic part of the placenta, and the embryoblast, which forms the embryo. They lie side by side at one pole of the blastocyst.

Question 10316. On which day after fertilization does the blastocyst normally begin to attach to the lining of the uterus: the second day, the sixth day, the fourteenth day, or the twenty-first day? The answer is the sixth day. The trophoblast attaches to the uterine lining and begins to invade it.

Question 10317. Which of the two populations of the blastocyst lies directly against the uterine lining during implantation, and which lies deeper? The answer is the trophoblast against the lining, with the embryoblast lying deeper, beside the blastocele. The trophoblast is the outer shell, so it meets the uterine wall first.

Question 10318. A constructed illustration: about ten days after fertilization a woman notices a small amount of bleeding, and the pregnancy continues normally. Which process explains the bleeding? The answer is the implantation of the blastocyst into the uterine lining. As the trophoblast burrows into the lining, small vessels at the surface are opened.

Question 10319. Which layer of the trophoblast secretes human chorionic gonadotropin, the hormone that maintains the corpus luteum in early pregnancy? The answer is the syncytiotrophoblast. It is the outer layer, formed when the cells of the cytotrophoblast fuse and lose their separate boundaries. Its hormone keeps the corpus luteum secreting progesterone until the placenta takes over.

Question 10320. By the eighth day the embryoblast has become a flat disc of two layers. Which of these is the upper layer, the one that faces the amniotic cavity: the epiblast, the hypoblast, the trophoblast, or the syncytiotrophoblast? The answer is the epiblast. It is the thicker layer of tall cells next to the amniotic cavity.

Question 10321. A described section of an implanted embryo shows a slit-like cavity above a layer of tall cells, and below that layer a thin sheet of flat cells bounding another cavity. What is the slit above the tall cells? The answer is the amniotic cavity. It begins as a small space within the epiblast and then enlarges.

Question 10322. Which cavity appears first in the implanted embryo as a slit within the epiblast, and what fluid does it later contain? The answer is the amniotic cavity. It is roofed by cells that become the amnion and floored by the epiblast. It later fills with amniotic fluid, which cushions the fetus and keeps it at a steady temperature.

Question 10323. The cavity below the hypoblast is lined by a membrane that is not made of hypoblast cells. What is this cavity called: the amniotic cavity, the primary yolk sac, the chorionic cavity, or the blastocele? The answer is the primary yolk sac. It is lined by the exocoelomic membrane, which lies inside the hypoblast.

Question 10324. Which layer of the two-layered embryonic disc lies immediately above the primary yolk sac and immediately below the epiblast? The answer is the hypoblast. It is a thin sheet of small cells, and its lower surface faces the yolk sac. The epiblast lies above it and faces the amniotic cavity, so the disc has an upper and a lower layer.

Question 10325. True or false: the amnion is the outermost membrane around the conceptus and the chorion lies just inside it. The answer is false. The chorion is the outer membrane, formed from the trophoblast and the extraembryonic mesoderm. The amnion is the thin membrane that encloses only the amniotic cavity, and it lies inside the chorion.

Question 10326. Which of these pairs belongs together: the epiblast with the lining of the yolk sac, the hypoblast with the lining of the yolk sac, the epiblast with the amnion, or the hypoblast with the embryo? The answer is the hypoblast with the lining of the yolk sac. Its cells spread to form the membrane of the primary yolk sac.

Question 10327. As the embryo grows, a cavity appears outside the amnion and the yolk sac but inside the trophoblast. Name the mesodermal layer whose splitting creates this cavity, and name the cavity. The answer is the extraembryonic mesoderm, and the cavity is the chorionic cavity.

Question 10328. The embryo is attached to the trophoblast by a narrow band of mesoderm that will carry the umbilical vessels. Which of these names it: the connecting stalk, the yolk stalk, the notochord, or the primitive streak? The answer is the connecting stalk, which becomes the core of the umbilical cord. It begins short and lies at the caudal end of the embryo.

Question 10329. A described specimen shows finger-like projections growing from the outer surface of the chorion into the maternal tissue, each with a core of mesoderm and a covering of trophoblast. What are these projections, and what do some of them carry? The answer is the chorionic villi. Some carry fetal capillaries and become the exchange surface of the placenta, and those over the decidua basalis grow most.

Question 10330. A constructed illustration: an early pregnancy shows a greatly enlarged uterus filled with small cysts and no recognizable embryo, and the tissue contains chromosomes of paternal origin only. Which developmental abnormality is described? The answer is a complete hydatidiform mole. An empty oocyte is fertilized and the paternal chromosomes duplicate, so trophoblast grows while no embryo forms. Management belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 10331. Gastrulation begins about fifteen days after fertilization with the appearance of a linear thickening in the epiblast. What is this thickening called? The answer is the primitive streak. It appears at the caudal end of the embryonic disc and marks the start of the third week.

Question 10332. At the cranial end of the primitive streak lies a rounded thickening that organizes the cells around it and gives rise to the notochord. What is it called: the primitive node, the cloacal membrane, the oropharyngeal membrane, or the connecting stalk? The answer is the primitive node. It lies at the cranial tip of the streak and its cells form the notochordal process.

Question 10333. The primitive streak defines one end of the embryo. Which end of the embryonic disc does it mark, and which end lies opposite it? The answer is the caudal end, so the site of the future head lies opposite, at the cranial end. The streak is therefore a landmark of the long axis of the body.

Question 10334. Name, in a single sentence, the two layers that cells of the epiblast form after they have passed through the primitive streak. The answer is the intraembryonic mesoderm and the endoderm. The first cells to pass through displace the hypoblast and become the endoderm. Later cells spread between the epiblast and the endoderm as the intraembryonic mesoderm, and the epiblast remains as the ectoderm.

Question 10335. Which solid rod of cells forms from the primitive node, lies in the midline beneath the neural tube, and defines the axis of the embryo? The answer is the notochord. It grows toward the head from the node as the notochordal process and later becomes a solid rod.

Question 10336. The notochord largely disappears in the adult, but part of it persists inside the intervertebral discs. Which part: the annulus fibrosus, the nucleus pulposus, the anterior longitudinal ligament, or the vertebral body? The answer is the nucleus pulposus, the soft center of each disc. The rest of the notochord regresses as the vertebral bodies form around it.

Question 10337. A described cross section of an early embryo shows a flat plate of thickened ectoderm above a solid midline rod, with mesoderm on each side. Which structure is the thickened plate, and what will it become? The answer is the neural plate, which will form the brain and the spinal cord. The rod beneath it is the notochord.

Question 10338. A constructed illustration: a newborn has a mass at the lower end of the spine that contains skin, hair, cartilage and glandular tissue from more than one germ layer. From which structure does such a mass most often arise? The answer is a persistent remnant of the primitive streak, which forms a sacrococcygeal teratoma. Treatment belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 10339. A described set of sections cuts an early embryo across its long axis at three levels: through the rounded end of the primitive streak, through the middle of the streak, and through its caudal tip. Which section shows the notochord? The answer is the one through the rounded end, which is the primitive node. The notochord grows toward the head from the node, so it lies at the cranial end of the streak.

Question 10340. Which pair of membranes of the early embryo consists of ectoderm and endoderm only, with no mesoderm between the layers: the oropharyngeal and cloacal membranes, the amnion and chorion, the zona pellucida and the corona radiata, or the pleuropericardial and pleuroperitoneal membranes? The answer is the oropharyngeal and cloacal membranes. They later break down to open the mouth and the anal canal, one at each end of the gut tube.

Question 10341. About eighteen days after fertilization, the ectoderm above the notochord thickens into a slipper-shaped plate. What is this plate called? The answer is the neural plate. It is the first sign of the nervous system. Its edges rise as the neural folds, and the groove between them becomes the neural groove.

Question 10342. Cells that break away from the tips of the neural folds migrate widely through the embryo. What are they called: neural crest cells, somite cells, epiblast cells, or hypoblast cells? The answer is neural crest cells. They arise at the crests of the neural folds as the folds fuse. They then migrate and form nerves, pigment cells, and much of the facial skeleton.

Question 10343. A described series of sections through the back of an embryo shows a groove that deepens and then closes into a tube that separates from the surface ectoderm. Which structure has formed, and from which germ layer? The answer is the neural tube, formed from the ectoderm. The cranial opening closes about day twenty-five and the caudal opening about day twenty-seven.

Question 10344. A constructed illustration: a newborn has a defect in the lower back through which the spinal cord and its membranes protrude, and the opening lies over the lumbar region. Which process failed? The answer is the closure of the caudal part of the neural tube, which leaves spina bifida. Failure at the cranial end instead leaves the brain exposed. Care belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 10345. Beneath the neural tube, blocks of paraxial mesoderm separate into paired segments at a regular rate. What are these segments called, and how many pairs form in all? The answer is the somites, of which about forty-two to forty-four pairs form. They appear from about day twenty at a rate of about three pairs each day.

Question 10346. Which structure lies immediately lateral to the neural tube and immediately medial to the intermediate mesoderm in the early embryo? The answer is the paraxial mesoderm, which segments into the somites. It lies on each side of the neural tube along the back.

Question 10347. A cavity forms within the lateral plate mesoderm and splits it into two layers. Which of these names it: the intraembryonic coelom, the chorionic cavity, the amniotic cavity, or the blastocele? The answer is the intraembryonic coelom. It becomes the pericardial, pleural and peritoneal cavities. The somatic layer of the lateral plate lies against the body wall and the splanchnic layer against the gut.

Question 10348. Which of these pairs belongs together: the paraxial mesoderm with the somites, the intermediate mesoderm with the somites, the lateral plate mesoderm with the notochord, or the paraxial mesoderm with the kidneys? The answer is the paraxial mesoderm with the somites. The intermediate mesoderm gives the kidneys and the gonads, and the lateral plate gives the serous membranes and the muscle of the gut wall.

Question 10349. A described series of lateral sections shows the flat embryonic disc folding along its sides, so that its edges curl toward the midline and the amnion wraps around it. What does this folding achieve? The answer is that the flat disc becomes a cylindrical body with a gut tube inside. The yolk sac is pinched into a narrow stalk, the vitelline duct.

Question 10350. A described set of sagittal sections shows a block of mesoderm that first lies cranial to the developing heart and later lies caudal to it, as the head folds forward and the heart descends. What is this block, and what does it become? The answer is the septum transversum, which gives most of the diaphragm. Its change of position is a result of folding rather than movement of the block itself.

Question 10351. Which of the three germ layers gives rise to the epidermis of the skin, together with the hair, the nails and the sweat glands? The answer is the ectoderm. Its surface part forms the outer layer of the skin and the structures that grow from it. The dermis beneath comes from the mesoderm, so the skin has two layers of different origin.

Question 10352. Which germ layer forms the epidermis: the ectoderm, the mesoderm, the endoderm, or the neural crest alone? The answer is the ectoderm. The surface ectoderm stays outside and becomes the epidermis and its appendages. The neural crest contributes pigment cells to the skin but not the epidermis itself, and the dermis comes from the mesoderm.

Question 10353. A described section of developing skin shows a single outer layer of cells resting on a thicker layer of loose cells with scattered vessels. Which germ layer formed the outer layer, and which formed the layer beneath? The answer is that the outer layer, the epidermis, comes from the ectoderm, and the thicker layer beneath, the dermis, comes from the mesoderm.

Question 10354. The neural tube becomes the central nervous system. Which of these does it form: the brain and the spinal cord, the peripheral nerves and their ganglia, the epidermis, or the notochord? The answer is the brain and the spinal cord. The cranial part of the tube becomes the brain and the caudal part the spinal cord.

Question 10355. Pigment-producing cells that lie in the base of the epidermis and in the hair follicle migrate there during development. From which structure do they come? The answer is the neural crest. These cells are the melanocytes, and they travel from the neural folds into the skin.

Question 10356. A constructed illustration: a newborn does not pass meconium, and a segment of the colon is narrow and lacks the nerve cells of its wall. Which population of cells failed to reach that segment? The answer is the neural crest cells that form the enteric ganglia. They migrate along the gut, and a segment they do not reach cannot relax its muscle. Care belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 10357. At which site do neural crest cells first appear: between the neural tube and the surface ectoderm, deep to the notochord, inside the somites, or in the lining of the yolk sac? The answer is between the neural tube and the surface ectoderm. They arise at the crest of each neural fold, where the fold meets the surface layer.

Question 10358. Which of these pairs belongs together: the neural crest with the adrenal cortex, the neural crest with the adrenal medulla, the mesoderm with the adrenal medulla, or the endoderm with the adrenal medulla? The answer is the neural crest with the adrenal medulla. Its cells invade the cortical mass and become the medullary cells.

Question 10359. True or false: the adrenal cortex develops from neural crest cells that migrate into the abdomen. The answer is false. The cortex comes from the mesoderm of the posterior abdominal wall, and only the medulla comes from the neural crest. The two parts differ in origin, in function and in the hormones they produce.

Question 10360. Which of these gives rise to the cells of the adrenal medulla, which release epinephrine and norepinephrine: the ectoderm through the neural crest, the mesoderm, the endoderm, or the trophoblast? The answer is the ectoderm through the neural crest. Those cells migrate into the mesodermal cortical mass and settle in its center.

Question 10361. Which structure of the eye is formed from the surface ectoderm as it thickens opposite the optic vesicle and then sinks inward as a small vesicle? The answer is the lens. The optic vesicle induces the ectoderm above it to form the lens placode. The lens then loses its connection with the surface and becomes transparent.

Question 10362. A described section of a developing eye shows a rounded mass of cells lying just inside the surface layer, with the optic cup behind it and a thin stalk still joining it to the surface. Which structure is the rounded mass, and from which germ layer does it come? The answer is the lens, which comes from the surface ectoderm. The stalk that joins it disappears as the vesicle closes off.

Question 10363. The anterior part of the pituitary gland develops from a pouch of ectoderm that grows up from the roof of the mouth. Which of these is that pouch: the pouch of Rathke, a pharyngeal pouch, the lens placode, or the otic vesicle? The answer is the pouch of Rathke. The posterior part of the pituitary comes down from the neural ectoderm of the brain, so the two parts differ in origin.

Question 10364. Name, in one spoken sentence, three structures that arise from the ectoderm. The answer is the epidermis, the brain and the spinal cord, and the lens of the eye. The surface ectoderm gives the epidermis and its appendages, while the neural ectoderm gives the brain, the spinal cord and the retina.

Question 10365. Which of the three germ layers gives rise to the great majority of the muscles of the body, including the muscles of the limbs and the trunk? The answer is the mesoderm. Its paraxial part forms the somites, whose myotomes give the skeletal muscles.

Question 10366. The ventromedial part of a somite breaks up and migrates around the neural tube and the notochord. Which of these does it form: the vertebrae and the ribs, the skin of the back, the muscles of the back, or the dermis? The answer is the vertebrae and the ribs. This part of the somite is the sclerotome.

Question 10367. Within a somite, which subdivision lies immediately deep to the dermatome and immediately outside the sclerotome? The answer is the myotome, which forms the skeletal muscle of that segment. The dermatome lies most dorsally and gives the dermis. The sclerotome lies medial and ventral and gives the vertebrae and the ribs.

Question 10368. A described cross section of the back of an embryo shows, on each side of the neural tube, a block of mesoderm divided into a dorsal plate, a middle mass and a loose medial group. Name the three parts and the layer each one gives. The answer is the dermatome, the myotome and the sclerotome.

Question 10369. A constructed illustration: a newborn has severe difficulty breathing, and loops of intestine are found inside the chest on the left, having passed through an opening in the back part of the diaphragm. Which developmental closure failed? The answer is the pleuroperitoneal membrane, which normally completes the diaphragm. Failure leaves a passage between the abdomen and the chest. Care belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 10370. The dermatome of a somite spreads out beneath the epidermis and forms which layer: the dermis of the skin, the epidermis, the lining of the gut, or the notochord? The answer is the dermis of the skin. It gives the connective tissue of the dermis over the back and the body wall.

Question 10371. Which part of the mesoderm lies between the paraxial mesoderm and the lateral plate, and gives rise to the kidneys, the ureters and the gonads? The answer is the intermediate mesoderm. It forms a longitudinal ridge on each side of the embryo. Its duct systems become the urinary and genital passages.

Question 10372. The ureter develops as an outgrowth of a duct that belongs to which part of the mesoderm: the intermediate mesoderm, the paraxial mesoderm, the lateral plate mesoderm, or the endoderm? The answer is the intermediate mesoderm. The outgrowth is the ureteric bud, which grows from the mesonephric duct.

Question 10373. In the developing abdomen, which duct lies immediately lateral to the mesonephric ridge and gives off the bud from which the ureter and the collecting system form? The answer is the mesonephric duct. The ureteric bud grows from its lower end and penetrates the metanephric mesenchyme.

Question 10374. Which of these pairs belongs together: the paraxial mesoderm with the kidneys, the intermediate mesoderm with the kidneys, the lateral plate mesoderm with the vertebrae, or the paraxial mesoderm with the gut lining? The answer is the intermediate mesoderm with the kidneys. The paraxial mesoderm gives the somites with their vertebrae and muscles, and the lateral plate gives the serous membranes and the gut wall.

Question 10375. Which germ layer gives rise to the heart tube, the blood vessels and the blood cells? The answer is the mesoderm, chiefly its lateral plate part. The splanchnic layer of the lateral plate forms the heart and the vessels, and the blood cells arise from the same layer.

Question 10376. A described cross section of the body wall of an embryo shows the lateral plate mesoderm split into two layers, with a cavity between them. One layer lies against the ectoderm and the other against the endoderm. Name the two layers. The answer is the somatic layer against the ectoderm and the splanchnic layer against the endoderm.

Question 10377. Blood cells and the cells that line the smallest vessels arise from which germ layer: the mesoderm, the ectoderm, the endoderm, or the trophoblast? The answer is the mesoderm. Clusters of mesodermal cells in the wall of the yolk sac and in the embryo form the first blood cells and vessels.

Question 10378. A constructed illustration: a child has a small pouch on the wall of the ileum, opposite the attachment of the mesentery, and the pouch contains tissue like that of the stomach. Which embryonic structure has persisted? The answer is the vitelline duct, the stalk that once joined the midgut to the yolk sac. Its remains form a Meckel diverticulum. Care belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 10379. True or false: the epithelium that lines the gut and its glands comes from the endoderm, while the muscle and the connective tissue of the gut wall come from the mesoderm. The answer is true. The endoderm gives the lining and the glands, and the splanchnic mesoderm gives the smooth muscle, the connective tissue and the outer serous coat.

Question 10380. The liver and the pancreas develop as buds from the lining of the gut. Which germ layer gives the cells of these buds: the endoderm, the mesoderm, the ectoderm, or the neural crest? The answer is the endoderm. The buds grow from the foregut into the mesoderm around it.

Question 10381. The lining of the air passages and the alveoli develops as a bud from the foregut, while the cartilage and the muscle of the airways come from the layer around it. Which germ layer gives the lining? The answer is the endoderm. The lung bud grows into the splanchnic mesoderm, which forms the walls of the airways.

Question 10382. A described section of the foregut shows a diverticulum growing from its ventral wall into a mass of mesoderm, with a second smaller bud beside it. Which structures do the two buds form? The answer is the liver from the larger bud and the pancreas from the smaller one, and both come from the endoderm.

Question 10383. Which germ layer lies immediately deep to the intraembryonic mesoderm and forms the lining of the gut tube and of the yolk sac? The answer is the endoderm. It is the innermost of the three layers. It closes on itself as the embryo folds, so the lining of the yolk sac and the lining of the gut remain continuous with each other.

Question 10384. The thymus and the inferior parathyroid glands develop from which structures: the third and fourth pharyngeal pouches, the first and second pharyngeal arches, the neural crest, or the mesonephric duct? The answer is the third and fourth pharyngeal pouches, which are endodermal. The thymus and the inferior parathyroid come from the third pair of pouches, and the superior parathyroid from the fourth.

Question 10385. The thyroid gland contains two cell populations of different origin. Which cells produce calcitonin and come from the neural crest? The answer is the parafollicular cells, also called the C cells. They migrate into the thyroid from the neural crest. The follicular cells that make thyroid hormone come from the endoderm of the floor of the pharynx.

Question 10386. A constructed illustration: a child has a small firm swelling in the midline of the neck that moves upward when the tongue is pushed forward. Which developmental path explains it? The answer is the path the thyroid took when it descended from the floor of the pharynx into the neck. A remnant of the thyroglossal duct forms such a cyst. Care belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 10387. The middle ear cavity and the tube that connects it to the pharynx develop from which structure: the first pharyngeal pouch, the second pharyngeal pouch, the third pharyngeal arch, or the otic vesicle? The answer is the first pharyngeal pouch. It is an endodermal pocket that grows outward from the pharynx.

Question 10388. True or false: the notochord persists in the adult as the nucleus pulposus of the intervertebral discs. The answer is true. The vertebral bodies form around the notochord, which regresses everywhere except within the discs. There its remnant becomes the soft center of each disc.

Question 10389. Which germ layer forms the epithelium that lines the urinary bladder: the endoderm, the mesoderm, the ectoderm, or the neural crest? The answer is the endoderm. The bladder develops from the urogenital sinus, which is an endodermal cavity. The smooth muscle of the bladder wall comes from the mesoderm around it.

Question 10390. A described vessel has an inner lining of flattened cells and a wall of smooth muscle. The muscle comes from the mesoderm. Which germ layer gives the lining: the mesoderm, the endoderm, the ectoderm, or the neural crest? The answer is the mesoderm, by way of cells called angioblasts. The lining of a vessel is not endodermal, even though it faces a lumen, and that is a distinction students often miss.

Question 10391. A described section of the neck of an embryo shows a series of rounded ridges on each side, each containing a core of mesoderm, covered outside by ectoderm and lined inside by endoderm, with pockets between them. What are the ridges and the pockets? The answer is the pharyngeal arches, with the pharyngeal pouches inside and the pharyngeal clefts outside. Each arch carries its own nerve, artery and cartilage bar.

Question 10392. A constructed illustration: a newborn coughs and chokes at the first feeding, and a tube passed from the mouth does not reach the stomach. Which developmental event failed? The answer is the separation of the foregut into the esophagus and the trachea. The ridge that divides them did not complete, leaving a blind upper esophagus, often joined to the airway. Care belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 10393. In the developing neck, which layer of tissue lies immediately deep to the ectoderm of a pharyngeal cleft and immediately outside the endoderm of the corresponding pharyngeal pouch? The answer is the mesoderm of the pharyngeal arch. It fills the core of the arch between those two layers. The arch gives the muscles, the cartilage and the arteries of that segment.

Question 10394. A described developing tooth has a layer of cells that secrete dentin next to the enamel. Which cells are they: odontoblasts from the neural crest, ameloblasts from the neural crest, osteoblasts from the mesoderm, or fibroblasts from the endoderm? The answer is odontoblasts from the neural crest. The enamel is made by ameloblasts of the ectoderm, so dentin and enamel have different origins.

Question 10395. A described section through a young embryo shows three layers one above another: an outer layer of columnar cells, a middle layer of loose cells, and an inner layer of flat cells bounding a cavity. Name the three germ layers from outside inward. The answer is the ectoderm, the mesoderm and the endoderm. The cavity below the inner layer becomes the gut tube.

Question 10396. The trunk of the developing heart is divided into the aorta and the pulmonary trunk by a spiral septum. From which structure do the cells of that septum come? The answer is the neural crest. Cells that migrated from the neural folds enter the outflow tract and form the aorticopulmonary septum. Failure of this contribution leaves the two great vessels connected.

Question 10397. The apex of the urinary bladder is joined to the umbilicus by a fibrous cord in the adult. From which embryonic structure does that cord, and the bladder apex with it, derive: the allantois, the vitelline duct, the mesonephric duct, or the notochord? The answer is the allantois, an endodermal outgrowth of the hindgut. Its remnant becomes the median umbilical ligament, and the vitelline duct lies higher, along the ileum.

Question 10398. The connective tissue of the face and the bones of the facial skeleton come from a source different from that of the rest of the skeleton. Which of these is that source: the neural crest, the paraxial mesoderm, the lateral plate mesoderm, or the endoderm? The answer is the neural crest, whose cells form the ectomesenchyme of the head and neck. The rest of the skeleton comes from the mesoderm.

Question 10399. A described section of the developing intestine shows an inner lining of columnar cells, a ring of smooth muscle around it, and a thin outer covering. Name the germ layer of the lining and of the muscle. The answer is that the lining comes from the endoderm and the muscle and outer covering from the splanchnic mesoderm. The layers keep those relations in the adult gut.

Question 10400. A described organ has an inner lining of endodermal cells, a coat of smooth muscle from the splanchnic mesoderm, and nerve cells in its wall that came from the neural crest. Which organ is described: the small intestine, the kidney, the heart, or the thyroid gland? The answer is the small intestine, which receives all three contributions. The kidney has no endodermal lining and the heart no endodermal wall.

To summarize, the first three weeks turn a zygote into a three-layered disc. Cleavage produces the morula and then the blastocyst, whose trophoblast implants and whose embryoblast forms the embryo. The two-layered disc of epiblast and hypoblast is followed by gastrulation, when epiblast cells pass through the primitive streak to form the endoderm and the intraembryonic mesoderm, while the notochord grows forward from the primitive node and the neural plate rolls into the neural tube above it. The ectoderm gives the epidermis, the nervous system and the neural crest with its wide migrations; the mesoderm gives the somites with their vertebrae, muscles and dermis, the kidneys through the intermediate mesoderm, and the heart, the vessels and the blood through the lateral plate; and the endoderm gives the lining and the glands of the gut, the liver, the pancreas, the lungs and the bladder. Structures that cross the usual boundaries, such as the adrenal medulla and the facial skeleton from the neural crest, are the ones most often confused, and naming the germ layer of a structure aloud is the surest way to remember it.
