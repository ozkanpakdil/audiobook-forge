# Chapter 41 — Veins, Part Two

This chapter completes the tour of the venous system and then descends to the smallest vessels of all. It follows the veins of the upper limb and the lower limb from the fingers and the toes back to the great veins of the root of the neck and the groin, and it then examines the microcirculation, where arterioles, capillaries and venules exchange fluid, gases and cells with the tissues. It holds one hundred questions, numbered from question four thousand and one, and every one gives its answer immediately and then explains why that answer is right, so nothing has to be carried in mind. Superficial and deep veins are described together with the valves that keep blood moving toward the heart, and the capillary bed is described with its sphincters, its exchanges and its lymphatic partner. This is educational material, and it supports rather than replaces an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 4001. Which superficial vein begins at the lateral end of the dorsal venous network of the hand and climbs along the lateral border of the arm? The answer is the cephalic vein. Its name comes from the same root as the word head, because it climbs toward the shoulder and the neck. The basilic vein begins at the medial end of that network.

Question 4002. Which of these veins begins at the medial end of the dorsal venous network of the hand: the cephalic vein, the basilic vein, the median cubital vein, or the accessory cephalic vein? The answer is the basilic vein. It runs up the little finger side of the limb, while the cephalic vein runs up the thumb side.

Question 4003. What is the name of the vein that runs obliquely across the front of the elbow and joins the cephalic vein to the basilic vein? The answer is the median cubital vein. It lies in the subcutaneous tissue of the cubital fossa and is the vein most often used when blood is taken. The bicipital aponeurosis lies deep to it.

Question 4004. In the groove between the deltoid muscle and the pectoralis major, which vein ascends toward the shoulder? The answer is the cephalic vein. The deltoid lies lateral to that groove and the pectoralis major lies medial to it. The vein then pierces the clavipectoral fascia to join the axillary vein.

Question 4005. The basilic vein and the paired veins that accompany the brachial artery unite to form which vessel: the subclavian vein, the axillary vein, the cephalic vein, or the brachiocephalic vein? The answer is the axillary vein. The union takes place at the lower border of the teres major muscle. The cephalic vein joins the axillary vein separately, higher up.

Question 4006. A constructed illustration: a cannula placed in a vein on the lateral side of the forearm is left in place for several days, and the vein becomes tender, red and cord-like. Which vein is inflamed? The answer is the cephalic vein. It runs in the subcutaneous tissue, where it is easily cannulated and easily irritated. Management belongs to the treating clinician.

Question 4007. A described venogram of an arm shows one channel running up the lateral border of the limb, another running up the medial border, and a diagonal link between them at the elbow. Which veins are shown? The answer is the cephalic vein laterally and the basilic vein medially, with the median cubital vein as the link.

Question 4008. True or false: the cephalic vein ends in the axillary vein rather than in the basilic vein. The answer is true. The vein crosses the front of the shoulder between the deltoid and the pectoralis major and then pierces the clavipectoral fascia to join the axillary vein.

Question 4009. What name is given to the two veins that accompany the brachial artery through the arm? The answer is the venae comitantes, which means the accompanying veins. Each artery of the arm and the forearm is flanked by two such veins, one on either side. They communicate across the artery at intervals.

Question 4010. Which of these pairs belongs together: the cephalic vein with the lateral side, the basilic vein with the lateral side, the median cubital vein with the back of the wrist, or the dorsal venous network with the palm? The answer is the cephalic vein with the lateral side. The basilic vein belongs to the medial side, and the dorsal venous network lies on the back of the hand.

Question 4011. A small vein sometimes runs beside the cephalic vein on the lateral side of the forearm. Which of these names is given to it: the median cubital vein, the accessory cephalic vein, the median antebrachial vein, or the basilic vein? The answer is the accessory cephalic vein, a normal variant. The median antebrachial vein, when it exists, runs up the middle of the forearm.

Question 4012. The cephalic vein in the lower forearm lies close to which nerve, the one that supplies skin over the lateral side of the forearm? The answer is the lateral cutaneous nerve of the forearm. It is the continuation of the musculocutaneous nerve below the elbow. Because the two run together, a cut down onto the vein can injure the nerve.

Question 4013. Relative to the deep fascia, where do the cephalic vein and the basilic vein lie, and where do the brachial veins lie? The answer is that the cephalic and the basilic veins lie superficial to the deep fascia, and the brachial veins lie deep to it. The basilic vein pierces the fascia in the middle of the arm.

Question 4014. Name the two superficial veins that drain the dorsal venous network of the hand. The answer is the cephalic vein and the basilic vein. The cephalic vein leaves the network at its lateral end and the basilic vein at its medial end. A question of this kind is answered with the two names alone.

Question 4015. At which landmark is the axillary vein formed, and from which vessels? The answer is at the lower border of the teres major, from the union of the basilic vein with the venae comitantes of the brachial artery. It then runs upward and medially, in front of the axillary artery, and ends at the outer border of the first rib.

Question 4016. The deep veins of the upper limb differ from the superficial veins in which way: they are paired beside their artery, they have no valves, they lie outside the deep fascia, or they drain only the skin? The answer is that they are paired beside their artery. They do have valves, and they lie beneath the deep fascia.

Question 4017. A constructed illustration: after unusual effort with the arm held overhead, a person develops a swollen, dusky arm, and the deep veins of the axilla no longer fill on a described venogram. Which vein is obstructed, and why does the arm swell? The answer is the axillary vein. Obstruction raises the pressure behind it, so fluid leaves the capillaries and collects in the tissues.

Question 4018. A described dissection of the front of the elbow shows a vein crossing obliquely over a shining fibrous band, with the brachial artery and the median nerve deep to that band. Which vein has been exposed, and what separates it from the artery? The answer is the median cubital vein, separated from the artery by the bicipital aponeurosis.

Question 4019. A constructed illustration: a person with kidney failure needs repeated access to the bloodstream, and a surgeon connects the cephalic vein to the radial artery at the wrist. Why is the cephalic vein chosen, and why does it enlarge? The answer is that it is superficial, long and easily needled, and it enlarges because arterial pressure now distends it.

Question 4020. Which structure separates the subclavian vein from the subclavian artery at the root of the neck: the first rib, the scalenus anterior muscle, the clavicle, or the sternocleidomastoid muscle? The answer is the scalenus anterior. The vein lies in front of that muscle and the artery behind it, so the vein is the more superficial of the two.

Question 4021. The subclavian vein passes in front of which muscle on its way to join the internal jugular vein? The answer is the scalenus anterior. That muscle separates the vein in front from the subclavian artery and the brachial plexus behind. The two veins unite behind the sternoclavicular joint.

Question 4022. Which two veins unite behind the sternoclavicular joint, and what vessel do they form? The answer is the internal jugular vein and the subclavian vein, which form the brachiocephalic vein. The right brachiocephalic vein is short and nearly vertical, while the left one is longer and crosses the midline. The two unite to form the superior vena cava.

Question 4023. True or false: the basilic vein pierces the deep fascia in the middle of the arm and continues upward as a deep vessel. The answer is true. It leaves the subcutaneous tissue and runs beside the brachial artery, where it joins the venae comitantes of that artery. The cephalic vein stays superficial until it reaches the shoulder.

Question 4024. The cephalic vein is sometimes harvested to bypass a narrowed coronary artery. Which of these features explains why it is suitable: it is long and superficial, it is valveless, it lies between the muscles of the arm, or it drains into the superior vena cava? The answer is that it is long and superficial. Its valves must be dealt with during the operation.

Question 4025. Which of these pairs belongs together: the basilic vein with the medial side of the arm, the cephalic vein with the medial side, the median cubital vein with the back of the hand, or the venae comitantes with the skin? The answer is the basilic vein with the medial side of the arm. The cephalic vein belongs to the lateral side, and the venae comitantes lie deep beside the arteries.

Question 4026. Which superficial vein of the lower limb begins at the medial end of the dorsal venous arch of the foot and ascends in front of the medial malleolus? The answer is the great saphenous vein. It is the longest vein in the body and runs up the medial side of the leg and the thigh to end in the femoral vein.

Question 4027. The small saphenous vein ascends behind the lateral malleolus and ends in which vessel: the femoral vein, the popliteal vein, the great saphenous vein, or the external iliac vein? The answer is the popliteal vein. It enters the popliteal fossa and pierces the deep fascia to join that vein. The great saphenous vein ends in the femoral vein instead.

Question 4028. In which vein does the great saphenous vein end, and through which opening does it pass? The answer is the femoral vein, through the saphenous opening in the deep fascia of the thigh. Just before it ends, the vein receives the superficial epigastric, the superficial circumflex iliac and the superficial external pudendal veins.

Question 4029. Which vein passes behind the lateral malleolus: the great saphenous vein, the small saphenous vein, the popliteal vein, or the femoral vein? The answer is the small saphenous vein. It runs up the back of the calf beside the tendo calcaneus and then between the two heads of the gastrocnemius. The great saphenous vein runs in front of the medial malleolus instead.

Question 4030. A described dissection of the back of the knee shows a vein in the midline of the popliteal fossa joining a deeper vessel between the heads of the gastrocnemius. Which vein is it, and what does it join? The answer is the small saphenous vein joining the popliteal vein. The sural nerve runs beside it.

Question 4031. A constructed illustration: a person who stands for long hours develops swollen, twisted veins on the medial side of the leg, and the skin above the ankle becomes pigmented. Which superficial vein is affected, and why does standing make it worse? The answer is the great saphenous vein. Standing raises the pressure in the superficial veins, and failing valves allow blood to pool downward.

Question 4032. In the popliteal fossa, which of the two great vessels lies nearer the skin: the popliteal artery or the popliteal vein? The answer is the popliteal vein. It lies superficial, or anterior, to the artery, which is the deeper of the two. The tibial nerve lies most superficial of all, just beneath the deep fascia.

Question 4033. True or false: the deep veins of the leg are paired and accompany the arteries of the leg. The answer is true. Each of the anterior tibial, posterior tibial and peroneal arteries is flanked by two companion veins. The popliteal vein is formed where those companions unite.

Question 4034. Which veins unite to form the popliteal vein? The answer is the venae comitantes of the anterior tibial artery and the venae comitantes of the posterior tibial artery, together with the peroneal veins. They unite at the lower border of the popliteus muscle. The popliteal vein then ascends through the popliteal fossa.

Question 4035. Where does the femoral vein lie relative to the femoral artery as the two ascend from the opening in adductor magnus to the groin: posterolateral at the opening and medial in the groin, medial at the opening and posterolateral in the groin, anterior throughout, or lateral throughout? The answer is posterolateral at the opening and medial in the groin. The vein crosses behind the artery as it ascends.

Question 4036. Which of these pairs belongs together: the great saphenous vein with the medial side, the small saphenous vein with the medial side, the popliteal vein with the front of the ankle, or the femoral vein with the back of the calf? The answer is the great saphenous vein with the medial side. The small saphenous vein belongs to the back of the calf and the popliteal vein lies behind the knee.

Question 4037. Name the two superficial veins of the lower limb. The answer is the great saphenous vein and the small saphenous vein. The great saphenous vein runs up the medial side to the femoral vein, and the small saphenous vein runs up the back of the calf to the popliteal vein.

Question 4038. In the femoral triangle the femoral nerve, the femoral artery, the femoral vein and the femoral canal lie in a row from lateral to medial. Which structure lies immediately medial to the femoral vein? The answer is the femoral canal, the small compartment that carries lymph vessels and a lymph node.

Question 4039. Deep vein thrombosis of the leg most often begins in which site: the superficial veins of the thigh, the deep veins and muscular sinuses of the calf, the great saphenous vein at the ankle, or the dorsal veins of the foot? The answer is the deep veins and muscular sinuses of the calf. A clot there can travel to the lungs.

Question 4040. A constructed illustration: a person who has been immobile for a long period develops a swollen, tender calf, and a described ultrasound scan shows that one deep vein does not compress. Which veins of the calf are the usual site? The answer is the posterior tibial and peroneal veins and the muscular sinuses of the soleus. Obstruction raises the pressure behind them, so fluid collects in the ankle.

Question 4041. A described venogram of a leg shows contrast filling two narrow channels on each side of the tibia, a channel beside the fibula, and a single wider vessel behind the knee. Which vessels are shown? The answer is the paired venae comitantes of the tibial arteries, the peroneal veins and the popliteal vein. The deep veins of the leg are doubled until they unite behind the knee.

Question 4042. What is the direction of blood flow through the perforating veins that connect the superficial veins of the leg to the deep veins? The answer is from the superficial veins into the deep veins. Their valves open inward and close against any backward flow into the subcutaneous tissue. When those valves fail, the superficial veins become varicose.

Question 4043. When the great saphenous vein is removed from the leg, which structure is most at risk: the saphenous nerve, the sural nerve, the common peroneal nerve, or the tibial nerve? The answer is the saphenous nerve. It runs beside the vein in the subcutaneous tissue, so numbness follows the medial side of the leg and the foot.

Question 4044. The great saphenous vein passes in front of which bony prominence at the ankle, and which nerve accompanies it there? The answer is the medial malleolus, accompanied by the saphenous nerve. It crosses the lower end of the tibia on the medial side of the limb. The small saphenous vein passes behind the lateral malleolus instead.

Question 4045. A constructed illustration: a person with long-standing varicose veins develops a shallow, slow-healing sore on the skin just above the medial malleolus. Why does this site break down? The answer is that the superficial system is at fault and the skin just above the ankle carries the greatest column of venous pressure. Fluid and pigment collect in the skin, which then fails to heal.

Question 4046. A described section through the calf shows wide venous channels lying within the muscle belly of the soleus and opening into the deep veins beside the tibia. What are these channels? The answer is the muscular sinuses of the soleal veins. They hold a large volume of blood during immobility and are a favored site for a clot to form.

Question 4047. The femoral vein becomes which vessel as it passes beneath the inguinal ligament: the external iliac vein, the internal iliac vein, the great saphenous vein, or the popliteal vein? The answer is the external iliac vein. The change of name takes place beneath the inguinal ligament, where the vein lies medial to the femoral artery.

Question 4048. True or false: the great saphenous vein ends in the popliteal vein. The answer is false. The great saphenous vein ends in the femoral vein at the saphenous opening in the groin. It is the small saphenous vein that ends in the popliteal vein behind the knee.

Question 4049. From which venous structure of the foot do the great saphenous vein and the small saphenous vein arise? The answer is the dorsal venous arch on the back of the foot. The great saphenous vein leaves at the medial end of the arch and the small saphenous vein at the lateral end. The arch itself is fed by the dorsal digital veins of the toes.

Question 4050. Which of these pairs belongs together: the femoral vein with the thigh, the popliteal vein with the ankle, the peroneal vein with the groin, or the dorsal venous arch with the knee? The answer is the femoral vein with the thigh. The popliteal vein lies behind the knee, the peroneal vein beside the fibula, and the dorsal venous arch on the back of the foot.

Question 4051. Which type of capillary has pores in its endothelial cells that are closed by a thin diaphragm and is found in the wall of the intestine? The answer is the fenestrated capillary. The pores let fluid and small molecules cross quickly. Continuous capillaries, by contrast, have no pores.

Question 4052. Which of these is a feature of the wall of a capillary: a single layer of endothelial cells, three coats of muscle, valves along its length, or elastic lamellae? The answer is a single layer of endothelial cells. The capillary wall is the thinnest in the body so that diffusion distances stay short. Smooth muscle, valves and elastic tissue belong to larger vessels.

Question 4053. What is the name of the smallest vessel of the body, a tube of endothelium across whose wall gases and nutrients pass? The answer is the capillary. Its wall is one cell thick and rests on a thin basement membrane. Blood enters it from an arteriole and leaves through a venule.

Question 4054. A described electron micrograph shows a narrow tube whose wall is a single flattened endothelial cell resting on a basement membrane, with dense material sealing the junctions between neighboring cells. Which type of vessel is shown? The answer is a continuous capillary, the kind found in muscle, skin and the nervous system.

Question 4055. Which vessel lies immediately downstream of a capillary bed and is the site where exchange with the tissue fluid continues? The answer is the postcapillary venule. It is wider than the capillary, its wall is thin, and its junctions are looser. White blood cells leave the blood through its wall.

Question 4056. Which vessel carries a ring of smooth muscle at the origin of each capillary and so controls the flow into the capillary bed: the metarteriole, the postcapillary venule, the collecting venule, or the lymphatic capillary? The answer is the metarteriole, which gives off capillaries along its length. The smooth muscle rings are the precapillary sphincters.

Question 4057. A constructed illustration: a person stands without moving for several hours, and by evening the ankles are swollen and the shoes feel tight. Which part of the microcirculation is responsible, and why? The answer is the capillary and the venule, where the column of blood raises the pressure and pushes fluid into the tissues.

Question 4058. True or false: a capillary contains smooth muscle in its wall. The answer is false. The capillary wall is made of endothelial cells and their basement membrane, with no muscle at all. Smooth muscle appears in the arterioles and the metarterioles just before the capillary bed.

Question 4059. What name is given to the short vessel that leaves a terminal arteriole, runs across a capillary bed, gives off capillaries along its course and then becomes a venule? The answer is the metarteriole. It has smooth muscle along its length and is the thoroughfare channel of the bed. Rings of muscle at the capillary origins are the precapillary sphincters.

Question 4060. Which type of capillary is found in the liver, has gaps between its endothelial cells and a discontinuous basement membrane, and allows plasma proteins to leave the blood: the continuous capillary, the fenestrated capillary, the sinusoidal capillary, or the lymphatic capillary? The answer is the sinusoidal capillary. Its gaps are wide enough for proteins and even cells to pass.

Question 4061. A described light micrograph of the kidney shows a tuft of capillaries inside a rounded capsule, with a vessel entering and another leaving at the same pole. Which capillary network is shown? The answer is the glomerulus, whose capillaries are fenestrated and leaky. They are the most permeable capillaries in the body.

Question 4062. Name the three types of capillary found in the body. The answer is the continuous capillary, the fenestrated capillary and the sinusoidal capillary. The continuous type is the commonest and is sealed by tight junctions. The sinusoidal type is the leakiest and lies in the liver, the spleen and the bone marrow.

Question 4063. Which cell lies on the outside of a capillary, shares its basement membrane with the endothelial cell, and can contract to alter the width of the vessel? The answer is the pericyte. It is most numerous on the capillaries of the brain and the retina. Its contraction helps to regulate local flow and permeability.

Question 4064. Which force draws fluid back into a capillary from the tissue fluid: the blood pressure inside the capillary, the osmotic pressure of the plasma proteins, the pressure of the tissue fluid, or the pull of the lymphatic vessels? The answer is the osmotic pressure of the plasma proteins. Albumin holds water in the plasma, and its effect is the colloid osmotic pressure.

Question 4065. Which vessels lie immediately beside a capillary bed and return the small excess of tissue fluid to the bloodstream? The answer is the lymphatic capillaries. They begin blindly in the tissue, and the fluid they collect is called lymph. The lymph then passes through larger vessels and returns to the great veins of the neck.

Question 4066. A constructed illustration: a person with a failing left ventricle develops breathlessness, and a described chest radiograph shows fluid in the air spaces of both lungs. Why does the fluid collect in the lung? The answer is that the failing ventricle raises the pressure in the pulmonary capillaries, so fluid leaves them faster than the lymphatics can return it.

Question 4067. White blood cells leave the bloodstream at which point: the capillary, because its wall has gaps; the postcapillary venule, because its wall is thin and its junctions are loose; the arteriole, because it has smooth muscle; or the metarteriole, because it is wide? The answer is the postcapillary venule. Its junctions are the loosest in the microcirculation, so a white cell can squeeze between the cells.

Question 4068. A described section of the skin of a fingertip shows a coiled vessel with a thick muscular wall connecting a small artery directly to a small vein. What is this structure, and what does it do? The answer is an arteriovenous anastomosis, or glomus body. It opens in the cold to conserve heat and closes when the body must lose heat.

Question 4069. What name is given to a direct connection between a small artery and a small vein that bypasses the capillary bed? The answer is an arteriovenous anastomosis. Such shunts are numerous in the skin of the fingers, the toes, the nose and the ears. They are also called glomus bodies where they are coiled and thick-walled.

Question 4070. When a tissue works harder, which of these changes occurs in its microcirculation: the arterioles narrow, the precapillary sphincters open, the venules constrict, or the capillaries are withdrawn? The answer is that the precapillary sphincters open. Local waste products such as carbon dioxide and adenosine relax the smooth muscle.

Question 4071. Which of these pairs belongs together: a continuous capillary with the brain, a fenestrated capillary with the bone marrow, a sinusoidal capillary with skeletal muscle, or a lymphatic capillary with an arteriole? The answer is a continuous capillary with the brain. Fenestrated capillaries lie in the intestine and the endocrine glands, and sinusoidal capillaries in the liver, the spleen and the bone marrow.

Question 4072. What name is given to the small vessels that supply the wall of a large artery or vein from outside? The answer is the vasa vasorum, which means the vessels of the vessels. They ramify in the outer coat and the outer part of the middle coat. The inner part of the wall is nourished from the blood in the lumen.

Question 4073. In the wall of a capillary, which layer lies immediately outside the endothelial cell and separates it from the surrounding tissue? The answer is the basement membrane. Pericytes lie outside that membrane and share it with the endothelial cell. A capillary has no middle coat and no outer coat.

Question 4074. Which of these is a feature of a lymphatic capillary but not of a blood capillary: it is closed at one end, it carries red blood cells, it has a thick muscular wall, or it opens directly into a venule? The answer is that it is closed at one end. Lymphatic capillaries begin blindly in the tissue, and their endothelial cells overlap so that fluid can enter.

Question 4075. A described electron micrograph of a capillary in the brain shows endothelial cells joined by continuous tight junctions, no pores, and a thick basement membrane wrapped by processes of supporting cells. Why does this vessel exclude so many substances? The answer is that the tight junctions seal the spaces between the cells, so materials must cross the cells themselves.

Question 4076. A described section of the wall of the small intestine shows a finger-like projection with a single blind-ended vessel running up its center amid a crowd of tall cells. Which vessel is shown, and what does it carry? The answer is a lacteal, a lymphatic capillary of the intestinal villus. It carries the milky fluid of absorbed fat.

Question 4077. A constructed illustration: after an infection blocks the lymphatic vessels of a limb, the limb swells and the skin thickens, and pressing it leaves a dent that persists. Why does the fluid accumulate if the blood capillaries are normal? The answer is that the capillaries still filter fluid as they always did, but the lymphatics can no longer return it.

Question 4078. In the liver, which feature explains why plasma proteins can leave the blood: continuous endothelium with tight junctions, sinusoidal capillaries with gaps, fenestrated capillaries with diaphragms, or arteriovenous anastomoses? The answer is sinusoidal capillaries. Their gaps let large molecules and even cells pass, and the blood they carry comes from the portal vein.

Question 4079. What is the name of the mechanism by which the vessels of the renal medulla keep the tissue fluid concentrated while preserving the blood that flows through it? The answer is countercurrent exchange in the vasa recta. The descending and ascending limbs of those vessels run side by side and exchange salt and water without washing out the gradient.

Question 4080. Blood does not flow steadily through every capillary. Which of these describes the periodic opening and closing of the arterioles and sphincters: vasomotion, vasoconstriction, anastomosis, or filtration? The answer is vasomotion. The alternation of flow and stasis is a normal feature of the microcirculation.

Question 4081. A described light micrograph of the thin sheet of tissue between two loops of the intestine shows a network of branching channels of different widths, with a vessel entering at one corner and leaving at another. What is the network? The answer is a capillary bed, fed by an arteriole and drained by a venule.

Question 4082. What name is given to the ring of smooth muscle at the point where a capillary leaves a metarteriole? The answer is the precapillary sphincter. When it contracts, no blood enters that capillary, and when it relaxes, blood flows again. The sphincters respond to the local needs of the tissue rather than to a nerve.

Question 4083. Which of these capillary networks is the most permeable: the capillaries of skeletal muscle, the capillaries of the brain, the capillaries of the glomerulus, or the capillaries of the lung? The answer is the capillaries of the glomerulus. They are fenestrated, and the pressure inside them is high. The capillaries of the brain are the least permeable of the four.

Question 4084. Which vessel lies immediately upstream of the capillary bed in the mesentery and is the last vessel of the bed to contain smooth muscle? The answer is the metarteriole, fed in turn by the terminal arteriole. Downstream of the capillaries lies the postcapillary venule. Blood can cross the bed through the thoroughfare channel.

Question 4085. A constructed illustration: a person with long-standing diabetes develops leaking capillaries in the retina and protein in the urine, and a described biopsy shows that the basement membrane of the capillaries is thicker than normal. Why do these two organs suffer together? The answer is that both contain capillary networks under high pressure that depend on a delicate basement membrane.

Question 4086. A described cross-section of a large vein shows a thin wall with an inner lining, a modest layer of muscle, and an outer coat carrying small vessels and nerves. What are those small vessels called, and what do they supply? The answer is the vasa vasorum, and they supply the outer part of the wall. The inner part is nourished from the lumen.

Question 4087. In which organs are fenestrated capillaries with diaphragms especially numerous, and what do they allow? The answer is the endocrine glands and the intestine, where they allow rapid movement of fluid and small molecules across the capillary wall. The diaphragm across each pore is thinner than the endothelial cell itself.

Question 4088. How do oxygen and carbon dioxide cross the wall of a capillary: by active transport through the endothelial cell, by diffusion through the endothelial cell and its junctions, by filtration through pores, or by being carried by white blood cells? The answer is by diffusion. Both gases are lipid soluble and pass easily through the endothelial cell membrane.

Question 4089. Which change explains fluid leaving a capillary at its arterial end and returning at its venous end: the blood pressure falls along it while the osmotic pressure stays the same, the osmotic pressure falls, the tissue fluid pressure rises, or the wall thickens? The answer is that the blood pressure falls along the capillary while the colloid osmotic pressure stays the same. Near the venule the osmotic pull brings it back.

Question 4090. Which arrangement maintains the high pressure in the glomerular capillaries: a wide vessel entering and a narrow one leaving, a narrow vessel entering and a wide one leaving, equal widths on both sides, or a valve at the outlet? The answer is a wide vessel entering and a narrow one leaving. That narrow outlet holds blood back, so the pressure stays high.

Question 4091. A described cast of a capillary bed, made by injecting a hardening material into the vessels and dissolving the tissue, shows a mesh of fine tubes with a wider inlet and outlet. What does the cast reveal? The answer is the density of the network and the narrowness of its tubes. The cast cannot show flow, which only the living tissue displays.

Question 4092. A described microscopic section of the skin of a fingertip shows a coiled vessel with a thick muscular wall connecting a small artery to a small vein, while nearby capillaries lie empty. What is the structure, and why are the capillaries empty? The answer is an arteriovenous anastomosis, and the capillaries are empty because blood is shunted across it.

Question 4093. Which feature of the capillaries of the brain accounts for the blood-brain barrier: tight junctions between the endothelial cells, fenestrae closed by diaphragms, a discontinuous basement membrane, or wide intercellular clefts? The answer is tight junctions between the endothelial cells. They seal the spaces between neighboring cells, so substances must cross the cells themselves.

Question 4094. Which structure prevents blood from flowing backward in a vein: a valve, a sphincter, an elastic lamina, or a pericyte? The answer is a valve, a pair of cusps of thin connective tissue covered by endothelium. Valves are numerous in the veins of the limbs, where blood must climb against gravity. Failure of these valves allows blood to pool and the vein to dilate.

Question 4095. In the intestine, which vessel lies immediately beside the lymphatic capillary at the center of a villus and takes up the sugars and amino acids of a meal? The answer is the blood capillary of the villus. It drains into the portal vein and carries the absorbed nutrients to the liver. The lacteal beside it carries fat instead.

Question 4096. What happens in the microcirculation of the leg when the calf muscles contract during walking: capillary pressure in the foot rises, venous blood is pumped toward the heart, the precapillary sphincters close, or lymph stops flowing? The answer is that venous blood is pumped toward the heart. The fall in venous pressure keeps the ankle from swelling.

Question 4097. Which of these is not a function of the microcirculation: the exchange of gases between blood and tissue, the delivery of nutrients, the removal of waste products, or the production of red blood cells? The answer is the production of red blood cells, which takes place in the bone marrow. The other three are functions of the capillary bed.

Question 4098. A described electron micrograph of a capillary in the kidney shows an endothelial cell pierced by pores that are open rather than closed by a diaphragm, resting on a basement membrane. Which capillary is shown? The answer is a glomerular capillary, and water and small solutes pass through the open pores. Blood cells and large proteins are held back.

Question 4099. Which cells line the sinusoids of the liver and remove aged red blood cells and bacteria from the blood that flows past them? The answer is the Kupffer cells. They belong to the phagocytic family and rest on the endothelial lining of the sinusoid. Because the sinusoid has gaps, they lie in close contact with the plasma.

Question 4100. Which sequence describes the functional unit of the microcirculation: artery, capillary, vein; terminal arteriole, metarteriole with precapillary sphincters, capillaries, thoroughfare channel and postcapillary venule; arteriole, venule, lymphatic; or artery, arteriole, vein? The answer is the terminal arteriole, the metarteriole with its precapillary sphincters, the capillaries, the thoroughfare channel and the postcapillary venule. This unit can send blood through the capillaries or across the bed.

To summarize, the superficial veins of the upper limb begin in the dorsal venous network of the hand, with the cephalic vein on the lateral side and the basilic vein on the medial side, linked across the front of the elbow by the median cubital vein, while the deep veins travel as paired venae comitantes beside the arteries. The cephalic vein ends in the axillary vein after crossing the shoulder in the deltopectoral groove, and the basilic vein becomes deep in the middle of the arm. In the lower limb the great saphenous vein runs up the medial side from the dorsal venous arch to the femoral vein, and the small saphenous vein runs up the back of the calf to the popliteal vein, while the deep veins of the leg are paired and the perforating veins carry blood inward. The microcirculation begins with the terminal arteriole and its metarteriole, whose precapillary sphincters decide how much blood enters a bed of continuous, fenestrated or sinusoidal capillaries, and it ends in the postcapillary venule, the leakiest vessel of the system, where fluid, cells and lymph are exchanged. Filtration at the arterial end and reabsorption at the venous end depend on the balance between the blood pressure in the capillary and the osmotic pull of the plasma proteins, and the lymphatic capillaries return whatever is left.
