# Chapter 56 — Spinal Nerves

This chapter follows the spinal nerves from the cord to the tissues they serve, then turns to the autonomic nervous system that travels with them. It covers the lumbar plexus and the sacral plexus, then the sympathetic and the parasympathetic divisions. It holds one hundred questions, and each one states its answer immediately and then explains why it is right, so nothing is held in mind until the end. The plexuses are described by their roots and branches, and the two divisions by where their fibers begin, synapse and end. This is educational material, and it supports rather than replaces an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 5501. Which four spinal nerve roots give rise to the lumbar plexus? The answer is the first, second, third and fourth lumbar roots. These ventral rami join one another within the psoas major muscle. The fourth root also sends a branch down to help form the sacral plexus.

Question 5502. Which nerve supplies the quadriceps femoris: the obturator nerve, the femoral nerve, the sciatic nerve, or the inferior gluteal nerve? The answer is the femoral nerve. It arises from the second, third and fourth lumbar roots and enters the thigh behind the inguinal ligament. The obturator nerve supplies the adductors instead.

Question 5503. In the femoral triangle, on which side of the femoral artery does the femoral nerve lie? The answer is lateral to the artery. The femoral vein lies medial to the artery, so the three read from lateral to medial as nerve, artery and vein. The nerve lies outside the femoral sheath.

Question 5504. Which nerve of the lumbar plexus supplies most of the adductor muscles of the thigh? The answer is the obturator nerve. It is formed from the second, third and fourth lumbar roots and leaves the pelvis through the obturator canal. It supplies the adductor longus, the adductor brevis and the adductor magnus.

Question 5505. Which nerve runs through the inguinal canal and supplies skin over the upper medial part of the thigh? The answer is the ilioinguinal nerve. It is a branch of the first lumbar root and enters the canal beside the spermatic cord. It supplies the upper medial thigh and the root of the penis or the labium majus.

Question 5506. Which nerve is at risk where it passes under the lateral end of the inguinal ligament: the genitofemoral nerve, the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve, the iliohypogastric nerve, or the nerve to the psoas major? The answer is the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve. It is a purely sensory branch of the second and third lumbar roots. Compression there causes numbness and burning over the outer thigh.

Question 5507. A constructed illustration: a person describes burning and numbness over the outer surface of one thigh, with normal strength and normal reflexes, after wearing a tight belt for a long day. Which nerve is involved? The answer is the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve. It carries sensation from the skin of the lateral thigh and has no motor fibers.

Question 5508. A described dissection of the posterior abdominal wall shows a thick muscle beside the lumbar vertebrae, with a network of nerves threading through its substance. Which muscle is shown, and which plexus lies inside it? The answer is the psoas major, carrying the lumbar plexus. Its branches emerge from its lateral border and its anterior surface.

Question 5509. Name two branches of the lumbar plexus that supply the anterior abdominal wall, spoken as a list. The answer is the iliohypogastric nerve and the ilioinguinal nerve. Both arise from the first lumbar root, and the iliohypogastric nerve runs above the inguinal canal. The subcostal nerve above them is the twelfth thoracic nerve and is not part of the plexus.

Question 5510. Which roots form the lumbosacral trunk, and where does the trunk pass on its way to the pelvis? The answer is the fourth and fifth lumbar roots, descending across the ala of the ilium. The trunk carries the whole of the fifth lumbar root and part of the fourth. It passes in front of the sacroiliac joint.

Question 5511. Which nerve accompanies the great saphenous vein down the medial side of the leg: the sural nerve, the saphenous nerve, the tibial nerve, or the common fibular nerve? The answer is the saphenous nerve. It is the longest sensory branch of the femoral nerve and supplies skin as far as the medial border of the foot. It carries no motor fibers.

Question 5512. Which branch of the lumbar plexus divides into a femoral branch and a genital branch? The answer is the genitofemoral nerve, from the first and second lumbar roots. It pierces the psoas major and runs down on its anterior surface. The genital branch passes through the inguinal canal to the skin of the scrotum or the labium majus.

Question 5513. A constructed illustration: after a deep wound just below the middle of the inguinal ligament, the knee cannot be extended and the knee jerk is absent. Which nerve has been divided? The answer is the femoral nerve. It supplies the quadriceps femoris, which extends the knee, and it carries the reflex arc of the knee jerk.

Question 5514. True or false: the obturator nerve leaves the pelvis through the obturator foramen to reach the medial compartment of the thigh. The answer is true. It passes through the obturator canal at the top of the foramen, with the obturator artery and vein. It then divides into an anterior and a posterior branch.

Question 5515. A described cross section through the upper thigh shows three compartments separated by fibrous septa, with a large nerve lying in the anterior compartment beside the vessels. Which nerve is shown? The answer is the femoral nerve. It lies lateral to the femoral artery in the anterior compartment. The sciatic nerve lies in the posterior compartment instead.

Question 5516. Which of these pairs belongs together: the femoral nerve with the adductor muscles, the obturator nerve with the adductor muscles, the sciatic nerve with the quadriceps, or the ilioinguinal nerve with the diaphragm? The answer is the obturator nerve with the adductor muscles. The femoral nerve supplies the front of the thigh, and the sciatic nerve supplies the back of it.

Question 5517. Which nerve lies below the twelfth rib and supplies the muscles of the anterior abdominal wall without belonging to the lumbar plexus? The answer is the subcostal nerve, the anterior ramus of the twelfth thoracic spinal nerve. It runs forward between the transversus abdominis and the internal oblique and carries sensation from the lower part of the anterior abdominal wall.

Question 5518. Which structure lies immediately lateral to the femoral sheath as it passes behind the inguinal ligament? The answer is the femoral nerve. The sheath itself contains the femoral artery, the femoral vein and the femoral canal. The nerve lies outside it, lateral to the artery.

Question 5519. Which muscle of the gluteal region is supplied by a branch of the lumbar plexus rather than by the sacral plexus: the obturator internus, the gluteus medius, the piriformis, or the obturator externus? The answer is the obturator externus, which receives a branch of the obturator nerve. The obturator internus, the gluteus medius and the piriformis come from the sacral plexus.

Question 5520. In the posterior abdominal wall, the lumbar plexus lies within which part of the psoas major, and in which direction do its branches leave the muscle? The answer is within the posterior part of the muscle, with branches emerging from its lateral border and its anterior surface. The plexus lies in front of the transverse processes of the lumbar vertebrae.

Question 5521. Which nerve supplies skin over the lower part of the anterior abdominal wall and over the upper part of the buttock: the iliohypogastric nerve, the genitofemoral nerve, the obturator nerve, or the femoral nerve? The answer is the iliohypogastric nerve. It arises from the first lumbar root and runs above the inguinal canal.

Question 5522. A described dissection of the posterior abdominal wall shows a nerve crossing the front of the quadratus lumborum and passing behind the lower pole of the kidney. Which nerve fits this description? The answer is the subcostal nerve. It lies behind the kidney on both sides and in front of the quadratus lumborum. The iliohypogastric nerve lies lower and does not reach the kidney.

Question 5523. Which branch of the lumbar plexus supplies the quadratus lumborum muscle, and from which roots does it arise? The answer is the nerve to the quadratus lumborum, from the twelfth thoracic and the first four lumbar roots. It lies on the anterior surface of the muscle, deep to the kidney. The psoas major receives separate branches from the second and third lumbar roots.

Question 5524. A constructed illustration: after a fracture of the superior pubic ramus, a person cannot bring the thigh inward against resistance and the inner side of the thigh feels numb. Which nerve has been injured in the obturator canal? The answer is the obturator nerve. It supplies the adductor group of the thigh and the skin over the inner thigh. The femoral nerve is spared because it lies lateral in the thigh.

Question 5525. Which of these pairs belongs together: the femoral nerve with the second, third and fourth lumbar roots, the obturator nerve with the first and second sacral roots, the sciatic nerve with the first and second lumbar roots, or the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve with the fourth lumbar root alone? The answer is the femoral nerve with the second, third and fourth lumbar roots.

Question 5526. Which spinal nerve roots form the sciatic nerve? The answer is the fourth and fifth lumbar roots together with the first, second and third sacral roots. The fibers pass out of the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen below the piriformis. The nerve is the thickest in the body.

Question 5527. Which two nerves are the terminal divisions of the sciatic nerve: the tibial and the common fibular, the femoral and the obturator, the superior and inferior gluteal, or the pudendal and the posterior femoral cutaneous? The answer is the tibial and the common fibular nerves. They separate in the lower part of the thigh or in the popliteal fossa. The tibial nerve continues down the back of the leg.

Question 5528. A constructed illustration: a blow to the outer side of the knee just below the head of the fibula leaves the foot hanging, and the person cannot lift the toes. Which nerve is injured? The answer is the common fibular nerve. It winds around the neck of the fibula, where it lies just beneath the skin. It supplies the muscles that lift the foot and the toes.

Question 5529. Which nerve passes through the greater sciatic foramen above the piriformis: the superior gluteal nerve, the inferior gluteal nerve, the sciatic nerve, or the posterior femoral cutaneous nerve? The answer is the superior gluteal nerve. It arises from the fourth and fifth lumbar roots and the first sacral root. The other three leave the pelvis below the piriformis.

Question 5530. Which bony landmark lies immediately medial to the sciatic nerve as it crosses the gluteal region: the greater trochanter, the ischial tuberosity, the anterior superior iliac spine, or the pubic symphysis? The answer is the ischial tuberosity, which lies inferomedial to the nerve when the hip is extended. The greater trochanter lies lateral to it. A hard seat presses the nerve between the two.

Question 5531. A described dissection of the gluteal region shows a large nerve emerging from under the lower border of a pear-shaped muscle, with a smaller nerve above that muscle. Which muscle is shown, and which two nerves are described? The answer is the piriformis, with the sciatic nerve below it and the superior gluteal nerve above it. The inferior gluteal nerve also emerges below the piriformis.

Question 5532. Which nerve supplies the gluteus maximus: the superior gluteal nerve, the inferior gluteal nerve, the sciatic nerve, or the pudendal nerve? The answer is the inferior gluteal nerve. It arises from the fifth lumbar root and the first and second sacral roots. The superior gluteal nerve supplies the gluteus medius and the gluteus minimus instead.

Question 5533. Which nerve leaves the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen, hooks around the sacrospinous ligament, and re-enters through the lesser sciatic foramen? The answer is the pudendal nerve. It arises from the second, third and fourth sacral roots and lies medial to the internal pudendal artery. It supplies the muscles and the skin of the perineum.

Question 5534. Which muscle of the gluteal region is supplied by a nerve that passes through the lesser sciatic foramen: the gluteus medius, the obturator internus, the piriformis, or the gluteus maximus? The answer is the obturator internus. Its nerve leaves the pelvis with the pudendal nerve, hooks around the ischial spine, and enters the lesser sciatic foramen. The muscle lies on the side wall of the pelvis.

Question 5535. True or false: the pudendal nerve lies medial to the internal pudendal artery as both pass around the ischial spine. The answer is true. The nerve lies on the medial side of the artery at that point. Both then run forward into the pudendal canal beside the ischium.

Question 5536. Which nerve carries sensation from the skin of the lateral border of the foot: the saphenous nerve, the sural nerve, the deep fibular nerve, or the medial plantar nerve? The answer is the sural nerve. It is formed in the back of the leg from branches of the tibial nerve and the common fibular nerve. It runs down beside the small saphenous vein.

Question 5537. A constructed illustration: a person cannot stand on the toes of one foot and the ankle jerk is lost, while sensation is diminished over the sole. Which nerve is affected, and where does it run? The answer is the tibial nerve, which passes behind the medial malleolus in the tarsal tunnel. It supplies the calf muscles that point the foot down and the small muscles of the sole.

Question 5538. A described cross section of the popliteal fossa shows a large nerve lying most superficially, with a vein behind it and an artery deepest against the bone. Which nerve is the superficial one? The answer is the tibial nerve. The popliteal vein lies behind it, and the popliteal artery lies deepest. The nerve continues down the back of the leg.

Question 5539. Which nerve supplies the perineal muscles and the skin of the scrotum or the labia majora: the posterior femoral cutaneous nerve, the pudendal nerve, the sciatic nerve, or the superior gluteal nerve? The answer is the pudendal nerve. Its perineal branches supply the superficial perineal muscles and the skin of the urogenital triangle. The posterior femoral cutaneous nerve supplies skin only, and that of the thigh.

Question 5540. Which nerve supplies sensation to the skin of the back of the thigh and the upper part of the calf? The answer is the posterior femoral cutaneous nerve. It arises from the first, second and third sacral roots and leaves the pelvis below the piriformis. It runs down the back of the thigh just deep to the fascia.

Question 5541. Which of these structures passes through the greater sciatic foramen below the piriformis and then supplies the muscles of the back of the thigh: the superior gluteal nerve, the sciatic nerve, the pudendal nerve, or the nerve to the obturator internus? The answer is the sciatic nerve. It descends between the greater trochanter and the ischial tuberosity. The pudendal nerve also passes below the piriformis but turns back into the perineum.

Question 5542. A described specimen of the lower end of the spinal cord shows a tapering tip at the level of the first or second lumbar vertebra, with a bundle of nerve roots descending below it. What is the tip called, and what is the bundle called? The answer is the conus medullaris, with the cauda equina below it. The roots of the lower lumbar and sacral nerves descend to their own foramina.

Question 5543. Which single finding best separates a lesion of the fifth lumbar root from a lesion of the common fibular nerve at the neck of the fibula: weakness of the hip abductors, weakness of the toe extensors, weakness of the ankle dorsiflexors, or numbness over the dorsum of the foot? The answer is weakness of the hip abductors, which the root lesion causes and the peripheral lesion spares. Both cause a foot drop.

Question 5544. Which sacral roots carry the parasympathetic fibers that leave the spinal cord for the pelvic organs, and by what name are those fibers known below the plexus? The answer is the second, third and fourth sacral roots, whose fibers form the pelvic splanchnic nerves. They arise from the lateral part of the gray matter of the sacral cord. They travel to ganglia near the pelvic organs.

Question 5545. A described magnetic resonance imaging scan of the lower back shows a bundle of nerve roots filling the lower part of the spinal canal, with the cord ending well above it. At which level does the cord end, and what is the name of the bundle? The answer is the first or second lumbar vertebra, with the cauda equina below. A disc that presses this bundle produces pain that follows the root.

Question 5546. On which muscle does the sacral plexus lie, and which nerve emerges above that muscle? The answer is the piriformis, with the superior gluteal nerve emerging above it. The sacral plexus spreads out on the front of the piriformis. The sciatic nerve leaves the pelvis below it.

Question 5547. True or false: the sciatic nerve usually leaves the pelvis as a single trunk below the piriformis. The answer is true. In a common variant the nerve is divided, with the common fibular part passing through the piriformis and the tibial part passing below it. That variant matters when the muscle is cut.

Question 5548. Which nerve supplies the gluteus medius and the gluteus minimus, and from which roots does it arise: the inferior gluteal nerve from the fifth lumbar root, the superior gluteal nerve from the fourth and fifth lumbar and first sacral roots, the sciatic nerve from the fourth lumbar root downward, or the pudendal nerve from the sacral roots? The answer is the superior gluteal nerve.

Question 5549. A described specimen shows the pelvis from above, with one flat band running from the sacrum to the ischial spine and another from the sacrum to the ischial tuberosity. Which ligaments are shown, and which nerve hooks around the first? The answer is the sacrospinous and sacrotuberous ligaments, with the pudendal nerve hooking around the sacrospinous ligament.

Question 5550. Which of these sacral plexus branches is entirely motor to the gluteal muscles and carries no fibers to the skin: the posterior femoral cutaneous nerve, the inferior gluteal nerve, the pudendal nerve, or the sciatic nerve? The answer is the inferior gluteal nerve. It supplies only the gluteus maximus. The posterior femoral cutaneous nerve is entirely sensory, and the others carry both kinds of fiber.

Question 5551. From which segments of the spinal cord does the sympathetic division receive its fibers? The answer is the first thoracic to the second lumbar segments. Its preganglionic fibers leave in the anterior roots of those spinal nerves. They join the sympathetic trunk through the white rami communicantes.

Question 5552. Where does the sympathetic trunk lie in the thorax: beside the vertebral bodies on each side, inside the spinal canal, in front of the sternum, or within the lung? The answer is beside the vertebral bodies on each side, just behind the parietal pleura. Each trunk runs from the neck to the coccyx. Its ganglia are joined to the spinal nerves by rami communicantes.

Question 5553. Which structure covers the thoracic sympathetic trunk from the front: the parietal pleura, the peritoneum, the pericardium, or the deep fascia of the back? The answer is the parietal pleura, which lies between the trunk and the inside of the chest wall. The trunk itself lies against the heads of the ribs and the sides of the vertebral bodies.

Question 5554. Which sympathetic ganglion is formed where the lower cervical ganglion and the first thoracic ganglion fuse, and where does it lie? The answer is the stellate ganglion, also called the cervicothoracic ganglion, lying in front of the neck of the first rib. It gives fibers to the upper limb and to the heart. Injury to it produces a drooping eyelid and a small pupil.

Question 5555. Which of these statements about the rami communicantes is correct: the white rami carry myelinated fibers from the spinal nerve to the trunk, the gray rami carry myelinated fibers back to the spinal nerve, both rami occur at every spinal nerve, or the gray rami carry no nerve fibers at all? The answer is that the white rami carry myelinated fibers to the trunk. The gray rami carry unmyelinated fibers back, and occur at every spinal nerve.

Question 5556. A described section of the thoracic spinal cord shows a small column of cells in the lateral part of the gray matter between the first thoracic and the second lumbar segments. What is that column, and where do its fibers leave the cord? The answer is the intermediolateral cell column, whose fibers leave in the anterior roots. They pass through the white rami communicantes to the sympathetic trunk.

Question 5557. A constructed illustration: a small tumor at the top of one lung presses on the sympathetic chain on that side, and the pupil is small, the eyelid droops, and the face does not sweat. What is the name of this described group of signs? The answer is Horner syndrome, from interruption of the sympathetic supply to the head.

Question 5558. True or false: the preganglionic fibers of the sympathetic division are myelinated and appear white in a fresh specimen. The answer is true. They leave the spinal cord in the anterior roots and run in the white rami communicantes. The postganglionic fibers are unmyelinated and appear gray.

Question 5559. Which nerves carry sympathetic fibers from the thoracic trunk down to the abdominal ganglia, and from which segments does the greater one arise? The answer is the splanchnic nerves, and the greater splanchnic nerve arises from the fifth to the ninth thoracic segments. The lesser splanchnic nerve comes from the tenth and eleventh segments. They pierce the diaphragm and end on the celiac ganglion.

Question 5560. Which large prevertebral ganglion lies at the level of the first lumbar vertebra, around the origin of the celiac trunk: the celiac ganglion, the superior mesenteric ganglion, the inferior mesenteric ganglion, or the aorticorenal ganglion? The answer is the celiac ganglion, which lies on each side of the celiac trunk. The superior mesenteric ganglion lies lower.

Question 5561. Which prevertebral ganglion receives the splanchnic nerves that supply the midgut: the celiac ganglion, the superior mesenteric ganglion, the inferior mesenteric ganglion, or the renal ganglion? The answer is the superior mesenteric ganglion, which lies at the origin of the superior mesenteric artery. The celiac ganglion serves the foregut, and the inferior mesenteric ganglion serves the hindgut.

Question 5562. Which gland receives its nerve supply directly from preganglionic sympathetic fibers, with no synapse in a ganglion? The answer is the adrenal medulla, which is stimulated by fibers traveling in the splanchnic nerves. Its cells act as modified postganglionic neurons. They release epinephrine and norepinephrine into the blood.

Question 5563. A described dissection of the head shows a chain of ganglia beside the vertebral column, with fibers following the arteries into the skull and other fibers reaching the skin of the face and the scalp. Which fibers reach the sweat glands of the face? The answer is the sympathetic fibers, which travel with the arteries and then with branches of the trigeminal nerve. They are unusual in being cholinergic.

Question 5564. How many pairs of sympathetic ganglia are commonly described in the cervical region: two, three, four, or five? The answer is three pairs, named the superior, the middle and the inferior cervical ganglia. The inferior one usually fuses with the first thoracic ganglion. The superior cervical ganglion is the largest of the three.

Question 5565. In the neck, in which plane does the sympathetic trunk lie relative to the carotid sheath, and what separates the two? The answer is that the trunk lies behind the sheath, separated from it by the prevertebral fascia and the longus colli muscle. The trunk lies in front of the transverse processes of the cervical vertebrae. The vagus nerve lies inside the sheath.

Question 5566. Which of these structures receives postganglionic sympathetic fibers through a gray ramus: the sweat glands of the foot, the adrenal medulla, the heart, or the pupil? The answer is the sweat glands of the foot, whose fibers follow the spinal nerves of the leg. The adrenal medulla is supplied by preganglionic fibers, and the heart and the pupil by fibers that travel with arteries or with nerves.

Question 5567. A constructed illustration: after an operation in the upper chest, one hand is warm, dry and flushed while the other is normal. Which fibers have been interrupted on that side? The answer is the sympathetic fibers to that limb, which normally narrow the vessels and stimulate the sweat glands. Loss of them leaves the vessels dilated and the skin dry.

Question 5568. Which of these pairs belongs together: the greater splanchnic nerve with the celiac ganglion, the greater splanchnic nerve with the inferior mesenteric ganglion, the lesser splanchnic nerve with the head and neck, or the sacral splanchnic nerves with the upper limb? The answer is the greater splanchnic nerve with the celiac ganglion. The lesser splanchnic nerve ends on the aorticorenal ganglion.

Question 5569. Which sympathetic ganglion lies at the level of the sixth cervical vertebra, near the inferior thyroid artery, and sends fibers to the heart? The answer is the middle cervical ganglion. It gives the middle cardiac nerve. The superior cervical ganglion lies higher, at the level of the second and third cervical vertebrae.

Question 5570. Which effect belongs to stimulation of the sympathetic fibers to the heart: slowing of the heart rate, an increase in the heart rate and in the force of contraction, constriction of the bronchial muscle, or secretion of digestive enzymes? The answer is an increase in the heart rate and in the force of contraction. The parasympathetic fibers slow the heart instead.

Question 5571. A described stained section shows a ganglion beside the vertebral column joined to the spinal nerve by two short roots, one pale and one dark. Which root carries myelinated fibers into the ganglion? The answer is the white ramus communicans, which carries preganglionic fibers into the trunk. The gray ramus carries unmyelinated fibers back to the spinal nerve.

Question 5572. Which explanation best accounts for the fact that sympathetic fibers reach the skin of the face although no sympathetic fibers leave the spinal cord above the first thoracic segment? The answer is that preganglionic fibers ascend in the sympathetic trunk to the superior cervical ganglion, where they synapse. Their postganglionic fibers then follow the arteries and the branches of the trigeminal nerve to the skin.

Question 5573. Which of these pairs belongs together: the superior cervical ganglion with the dilator of the pupil, the stellate ganglion with the muscles of the foot, the celiac ganglion with the sweat glands of the hand, or the inferior mesenteric ganglion with the heart? The answer is the superior cervical ganglion with the dilator of the pupil, whose fibers run with the internal carotid artery.

Question 5574. Which of these actions is produced by the sympathetic division: narrowing of the pupil, widening of the pupil, slowing of the heart, or increased movement of the gut? The answer is widening of the pupil, produced by the dilator muscle of the iris. The parasympathetic fibers narrow the pupil and slow the heart, and they increase the activity of the gut.

Question 5575. A described specimen shows a chain of ganglia on the front of the sacrum, ending in a single small ganglion at the tip of the coccyx. What is that ending called, and how do the two chains arrive there? The answer is the ganglion impar, where the two sympathetic trunks join in the midline. The trunks lie in front of the sacrum, medial to the sacral foramina.

Question 5576. From which two regions of the central nervous system do the parasympathetic fibers arise? The answer is the brain, through four cranial nerves, and the sacral part of the spinal cord. The cranial outflow leaves with the third, seventh, ninth and tenth cranial nerves. The sacral outflow leaves with the second, third and fourth sacral nerves.

Question 5577. Which four cranial nerves carry parasympathetic fibers: the first, second, third and fourth, the third, seventh, ninth and tenth, the fifth, sixth, seventh and eighth, or the ninth, tenth, eleventh and twelfth? The answer is the third, the seventh, the ninth and the tenth cranial nerves. They supply the pupil, the lacrimal and salivary glands, and the organs of the chest and the abdomen.

Question 5578. In the neck, between which two structures does the vagus nerve descend: between the internal carotid artery and the internal jugular vein, between the trachea and the esophagus, between the sternocleidomastoid and the trapezius, or between the two bellies of the digastric? The answer is between the internal carotid artery and the internal jugular vein, inside the carotid sheath.

Question 5579. Which ganglion lies in the orbit and contains the parasympathetic fibers that constrict the pupil? The answer is the ciliary ganglion, which lies behind the eye near the optic nerve. Its fibers arrive with the oculomotor nerve and leave in the short ciliary nerves. They supply the sphincter of the pupil and the ciliary muscle.

Question 5580. Which ganglion supplies the lacrimal gland with parasympathetic fibers: the ciliary ganglion, the pterygopalatine ganglion, the otic ganglion, or the submandibular ganglion? The answer is the pterygopalatine ganglion, which lies in the pterygopalatine fossa. Its fibers travel with the zygomatic nerve and then with the lacrimal nerve. The otic ganglion supplies the parotid gland.

Question 5581. Which ganglion hangs from the lingual nerve above the deep part of the submandibular gland? The answer is the submandibular ganglion. Its parasympathetic fibers arrive with the chorda tympani and the lingual nerve. They supply the submandibular gland and the sublingual gland.

Question 5582. A described dissection of the head shows a small ganglion just below the foramen ovale, hanging from a nerve that supplies the muscles of chewing. Which ganglion is shown, and which gland does it supply? The answer is the otic ganglion, which supplies the parotid gland. Its preganglionic fibers come from the ninth cranial nerve and synapse in the ganglion.

Question 5583. A constructed illustration: a person has a drooping eyelid and a pupil that is fixed and wide, and the eye turns outward at rest. Which cranial nerve is affected, and which parasympathetic pathway is lost? The answer is the third cranial nerve, with loss of the fibers that constrict the pupil. The same nerve supplies most of the muscles that move the eye.

Question 5584. Which fibers of the third cranial nerve synapse in the ciliary ganglion: the motor fibers to the medial rectus, the parasympathetic fibers to the sphincter of the pupil, the sensory fibers of the cornea, or the sympathetic fibers to the dilator of the pupil? The answer is the parasympathetic fibers to the sphincter of the pupil. The motor fibers pass through the ganglion without synapsing.

Question 5585. Which parasympathetic nerve supplies the heart, the lungs and most of the gut as far as the left colic flexure? The answer is the vagus nerve, the tenth cranial nerve. It descends through the neck and the thorax and passes through the diaphragm. Its fibers reach the organs through the plexuses around them.

Question 5586. Which parts of the large intestine receive parasympathetic fibers from the pelvic splanchnic nerves rather than from the vagus nerve? The answer is the descending colon, the sigmoid colon and the rectum. They lie beyond the left colic flexure, where the vagal supply ends. The splanchnic nerves reach them through the inferior hypogastric plexus.

Question 5587. From which spinal segments do the pelvic splanchnic nerves arise: the first and second lumbar, the second, third and fourth sacral, the fifth lumbar and first sacral, or the eleventh and twelfth thoracic? The answer is the second, third and fourth sacral segments. Their fibers leave through the anterior roots of those spinal nerves. They form the sacral part of the parasympathetic outflow.

Question 5588. True or false: parasympathetic preganglionic fibers are usually long and synapse in ganglia close to or inside the organ they supply. The answer is true. The ganglia of the parasympathetic division lie near or within the target organ, so the postganglionic fibers are short. In the sympathetic division the arrangement is the reverse.

Question 5589. A constructed illustration: after an injury to the sacral part of the spinal cord, the bladder no longer empties and a catheter is needed. Which fibers have been lost, and what is their normal action on the bladder? The answer is the pelvic splanchnic fibers, which normally make the detrusor muscle contract. Their loss leaves the bladder unable to empty on its own.

Question 5590. Which of these statements about the bladder belongs with the pelvic splanchnic nerves: they contract the detrusor and relax the internal sphincter, they relax the detrusor, they contract the external sphincter, or they have no action on the bladder? The answer is that they contract the detrusor and relax the internal sphincter, so the bladder empties. Sympathetic fibers close the internal sphincter instead.

Question 5591. A described section of the sacral spinal cord shows preganglionic cells in the lateral gray matter at the second, third and fourth sacral segments, with fibers leaving in the anterior roots. Where do those fibers go after they leave the sacral foramina? The answer is to the pelvic splanchnic nerves, which pass to the pelvic plexuses and synapse near the organs.

Question 5592. Which parasympathetic fibers constrict the pupil, and which constrict the bronchial muscle? The answer is the oculomotor fibers for the pupil and the vagus fibers for the bronchi. Both belong to the cranial outflow. The oculomotor fibers synapse in the ciliary ganglion, and the vagal fibers synapse in small ganglia in the wall of the airway.

Question 5593. Name two ganglia of the head in which parasympathetic fibers of the seventh cranial nerve synapse, spoken as a list. The answer is the pterygopalatine ganglion and the submandibular ganglion. Fibers reach the first through the greater petrosal nerve and the second through the chorda tympani. The ciliary ganglion belongs to the third cranial nerve instead.

Question 5594. Which nerve carries the fibers that increase the movement of the gut, and where do those fibers synapse? The answer is the vagus nerve, whose fibers synapse in ganglia lying within the wall of the gut. From there, short postganglionic fibers spread through the enteric plexuses. The sympathetic fibers reduce the movement of the gut instead.

Question 5595. Which secretion depends on parasympathetic fibers that travel with the ninth cranial nerve: saliva from the parotid gland, tears from the lacrimal gland, saliva from the submandibular gland, or acid from the stomach? The answer is saliva from the parotid gland. The ninth nerve carries the preganglionic fibers to the otic ganglion, and their fibers reach the gland with the auriculotemporal nerve.

Question 5596. A constructed illustration: a person has difficulty swallowing and a hoarse voice, and the soft palate droops on one side. Which cranial nerve is affected, and what does that nerve carry? The answer is the tenth cranial nerve, the vagus, which carries parasympathetic fibers to the throat and the chest. The drooping palate and the hoarse voice follow loss of the muscles it supplies.

Question 5597. Name two organs whose parasympathetic supply comes from the sacral outflow rather than from the vagus nerve, spoken as a list. The answer is the bladder and the rectum. The pelvic splanchnic nerves reach both through the pelvic plexuses. The descending colon and the sigmoid colon are supplied in the same way.

Question 5598. A described dissection shows two nerves running down the neck on either side of the trachea, one inside the carotid sheath and one behind it. Which of the two slows the heart? The answer is the one inside the sheath, the vagus nerve. The other is the sympathetic trunk, whose fibers make the heart beat faster.

Question 5599. Which of these pairs belongs together: the pterygopalatine ganglion with the parotid gland, the otic ganglion with the parotid gland, the ciliary ganglion with the submandibular gland, or the submandibular ganglion with the lacrimal gland? The answer is the otic ganglion with the parotid gland. The pterygopalatine ganglion supplies the lacrimal gland, and the submandibular one supplies the salivary glands.

Question 5600. Which statement describes the enteric nervous system correctly: it is a network of neurons in the gut wall that can act on its own, it is part of the sympathetic trunk, it lies in the spinal cord, or it carries fibers to the salivary glands? The answer is that the network in the wall of the gut can act on its own. The vagus and the sympathetic nerves modify its activity but do not replace it.

To summarize, the lumbar plexus is formed by the first four lumbar roots within the psoas major, and its chief branches are the femoral, obturator and lateral femoral cutaneous nerves. The sacral plexus is formed by the lumbosacral trunk and the first three sacral roots on the piriformis, and it gives the sciatic, gluteal, pudendal and posterior femoral cutaneous nerves, with the sciatic nerve dividing into the tibial and common fibular nerves. The sympathetic division arises from the first thoracic to the second lumbar segments and prepares the body for exertion, while the parasympathetic division arises from the third, seventh, ninth and tenth cranial nerves and from the second, third and fourth sacral segments and restores the body at rest. Holding the roots, the relations and the targets together is what makes these nerves usable, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.
