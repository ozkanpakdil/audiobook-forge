# Chapter 111 — Neurological Anatomy

Neurological anatomy is the map a clinician needs before any examination of the nervous system can mean anything, and this chapter walks that map from the spinal cord upward. It covers the membranes around the brain and the cord, their blood supply, the plexuses of the limbs, the twelve cranial nerves, the tracts and nuclei of the brainstem, and the injuries that bring each of them to the bedside. It holds one hundred questions, and each one gives its answer immediately and then explains why, so nothing has to be held in mind. This is educational material, and it supports rather than replaces an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 11001. Which membrane of the meninges lies directly against the brain, following it into every sulcus? The answer is the pia mater. It is the innermost of the three and is too thin to be peeled from the cortex. The dura and the arachnoid lie outside it, with cerebrospinal fluid between the arachnoid and the pia.

Question 11002. The space between the arachnoid and the pia is filled with cerebrospinal fluid. Which of these names it: the epidural space, the subdural space, the subarachnoid space, or the extradural space? The answer is the subarachnoid space. It surrounds the whole brain and cord and carries the arteries into the sulci. The subdural space is only potential, and the epidural lies outside the dura.

Question 11003. A described magnetic resonance image of the lower spine shows the tapered end of the cord with a thin strand continuing down toward the sacrum. Which strand is shown? The answer is the filum terminale, a thread of pia mater that anchors the cord to the coccyx. The cord itself ends at the conus medullaris.

Question 11004. How many pairs of spinal nerves leave the cord: twenty-eight, thirty-one, thirty-three, or thirty-six? The answer is thirty-one. There are eight cervical pairs, twelve thoracic, five lumbar, five sacral and one coccygeal. Each pair leaves the vertebral canal through its own intervertebral foramen, just above or below its own vertebra.

Question 11005. Within the vertebral canal, which structure lies immediately anterior to the dura of the cord: the ligamentum flavum, the posterior longitudinal ligament, the lamina, or the pedicle? The answer is the posterior longitudinal ligament. It runs down the back of the vertebral bodies and discs, and the dura rests against it. The ligamentum flavum lies behind the cord.

Question 11006. A constructed illustration: a needle enters the lower back at the third lumbar spine to obtain cerebrospinal fluid. Which space does it enter, and why is that level chosen? The answer is the subarachnoid space. The cord ends about the first or second lumbar vertebra, so below that only descending roots can be touched. The needle passes between the laminae.

Question 11007. Which single artery runs down the anterior midline of the cord and supplies its anterior two thirds? The answer is the anterior spinal artery, formed from branches of the two vertebral arteries. It lies in the anterior median fissure. The posterior columns and horns are supplied by the two posterior spinal arteries.

Question 11008. The upper cervical cord gains an extra supply from which artery, which ascends through the foramen magnum beside the cord: the vertebral, the basilar, the occipital, or the posterior cerebral? The answer is the vertebral artery. It gives the anterior spinal artery and small branches along the nerve roots. Lower down the cord depends on those radicular arteries.

Question 11009. True or false: in the adult the lower end of the cord lies at the first or second lumbar vertebra. The answer is true. The cord and the vertebral column grow at different rates, so the cord ends well above the bottom of the dural sac. Below it only the roots of the cauda equina descend.

Question 11010. A described specimen of the lower dural sac shows a spray of nerve roots descending around a thin central strand, like a horse's tail. What is that collection called, and why does it descend? The answer is the cauda equina. Those roots travel down to reach the foramina below the end of the cord. The thin central strand is the filum terminale.

Question 11011. The phrenic nerve, the motor nerve of the diaphragm, arises chiefly from which roots: the second, third and fourth cervical, the fourth and fifth cervical, the first and second thoracic, or the fifth and sixth cervical? The answer is the second, third and fourth cervical. They form the cervical plexus and give the phrenic nerve. Irritation of the diaphragm can be felt at the shoulder.

Question 11012. Which large vein lies immediately anterior to the anterior scalene muscle, while the brachial plexus and the subclavian artery pass behind it? The answer is the subclavian vein. It crosses the first rib in front of the muscle and joins the internal jugular vein behind the clavicle. In the lower neck the vein lies in front of the artery.

Question 11013. A constructed illustration: after a fall on the shoulder, an arm hangs with the elbow straight and the palm turned backward. Which roots of the brachial plexus are injured? The answer is the fifth and sixth cervical roots, which form the upper trunk. The shoulder muscles and the flexors of the elbow are paralyzed, and the forearm stays pronated.

Question 11014. In the axilla the brachial plexus is rearranged into cords named for their relation to which artery: the axillary, the subclavian, the brachial, or the profunda brachii? The answer is the axillary artery. The lateral, posterior and medial cords surround it before dividing into the terminal nerves. They travel with it in the axillary sheath.

Question 11015. Which nerve of the upper limb arises from the posterior cord and winds through the spiral groove on the back of the humerus? The answer is the radial nerve. That groove lies against the shaft, so a fracture of the middle of the humerus can injure it. It supplies the extensors of the arm and forearm and the skin over the back of the hand.

Question 11016. A described magnetic resonance image of the neck base shows nerve roots emerging between two muscles and joining into three trunks. Which structures are shown? The answer is the roots and trunks of the brachial plexus, between the anterior and middle scalene muscles. The fifth and sixth cervical roots form the upper trunk, the seventh runs alone, and the eighth cervical with the first thoracic forms the lower trunk.

Question 11017. Which of these muscles is supplied by the median nerve: the flexor carpi ulnaris, the abductor pollicis brevis, the first dorsal interosseous, or the extensor digitorum? The answer is the abductor pollicis brevis, a thenar muscle. The median nerve reaches it by a recurrent branch that turns back around the flexor retinaculum. The flexor carpi ulnaris and the interossei are supplied by the ulnar nerve.

Question 11018. Which of these pairs belongs together: the ulnar nerve with the groove behind the medial epicondyle, the radial nerve with the medial epicondyle, the median nerve with the spiral groove, or the axillary nerve with the cubital tunnel? The answer is the ulnar nerve with the groove behind the medial epicondyle. Striking it sends a shock down the little finger. The median nerve passes through the carpal tunnel.

Question 11019. Name two nerves of the upper limb that arise from the medial cord of the brachial plexus, spoken as a list. The answer is the ulnar nerve and the medial cutaneous nerve of the forearm. The medial cord also gives the medial pectoral nerve and a root to the median nerve. The ulnar nerve is the largest of its branches.

Question 11020. Which nerve supplies the deltoid and the skin over the lower part of the shoulder, and is stretched when the shoulder dislocates? The answer is the axillary nerve. It arises from the posterior cord and winds around the surgical neck of the humerus. It also supplies the teres minor.

Question 11021. Which nerve is most often injured when a fracture crosses the middle of the shaft of the humerus: the axillary, the radial, the median, or the ulnar nerve? The answer is the radial nerve. It lies in the spiral groove against the bone, so the fracture and the callus that follows can trap it. The wrist drops because the extensors are paralyzed.

Question 11022. Which nerve arises from the lumbar plexus, descends beside the psoas muscle, and supplies the front of the thigh? The answer is the femoral nerve. It leaves the abdomen under the inguinal ligament, lateral to the femoral artery. It supplies the quadriceps and the skin of the front of the thigh and the medial side of the leg.

Question 11023. A constructed illustration: after a blow to the outer side of the knee, a person cannot lift the foot and the toes drag. Which nerve is injured in its described position? The answer is the common fibular nerve, also called the peroneal nerve. It winds around the neck of the fibula under the skin, where it is easily compressed. It supplies the muscles that lift the foot.

Question 11024. A described magnetic resonance image of the pelvis shows a thick nerve leaving the greater sciatic foramen below the piriformis muscle. Which nerve is shown, and what does it supply? The answer is the sciatic nerve, the largest nerve in the body. It runs down the back of the thigh, supplies the hamstrings and then divides into the tibial and common fibular nerves.

Question 11025. Within the femoral triangle, which structure lies immediately lateral to the femoral artery: the femoral vein, the femoral nerve, the great saphenous vein, or the adductor longus? The answer is the femoral nerve. From medial to lateral the order is vein, artery, nerve, so the pulse is felt just medial to the nerve. The vein lies medial to the artery.

Question 11026. Which nerve supplies the muscles of the anterior compartment of the leg, which lift the foot: the tibial, the deep fibular, the superficial fibular, or the sural nerve? The answer is the deep fibular nerve. It also supplies the small muscles on the top of the foot and the skin between the first two toes. The superficial fibular nerve turns the foot outward.

Question 11027. True or false: the obturator nerve supplies the muscles of the medial compartment of the thigh. The answer is true. It arises from the second, third and fourth lumbar roots and leaves the pelvis through the obturator foramen. It supplies the adductors and a small area of skin on the inner thigh.

Question 11028. Which nerve runs down the medial side of the leg beside the great saphenous vein and supplies the skin there? The answer is the saphenous nerve, the longest cutaneous branch of the femoral nerve. It accompanies the vein below the knee. It carries sensation from the medial side of the leg and the instep.

Question 11029. In the popliteal fossa, which of these lies most superficial, nearest the skin: the popliteal artery, the popliteal vein, the tibial nerve, or the common fibular nerve? The answer is the tibial nerve. From the surface inward the order is nerve, vein, then artery, which lies deepest against the bone. That order matters when a swelling presses on them.

Question 11030. A described magnetic resonance image of the ankle shows a nerve passing behind the medial malleolus in a tunnel with tendons and vessels. Which nerve is shown, and what does pressure there cause? The answer is the tibial nerve, in the tarsal tunnel roofed by the flexor retinaculum. Pressure causes numbness and burning of the sole and weakness of the small muscles of the foot.

Question 11031. A constructed illustration: after a deep cut behind the knee, a person cannot rise on tiptoe. Which nerve and which muscle group are involved? The answer is the tibial nerve and the calf muscles, the gastrocnemius and the soleus. They plantar flex the ankle and are the chief muscles of standing on tiptoe. The ankle jerk also depends on that nerve.

Question 11032. The ankle reflex depends chiefly on which root: the fourth lumbar, the fifth lumbar, the first sacral, or the second sacral? The answer is the first sacral root. Tapping the Achilles tendon stretches the calf muscles, and the reflex arc runs through that root. The knee jerk depends chiefly on the third and fourth lumbar roots.

Question 11033. Within the carotid sheath in the neck, which structure lies immediately posterior to the internal carotid artery? The answer is the vagus nerve, the tenth cranial nerve. The internal jugular vein lies lateral to the artery, and the vagus lies behind, between the two. The sheath holds the artery medially, the vein laterally and the nerve behind.

Question 11034. Which cranial nerve carries parasympathetic fibers to the heart, the lungs and the gut, and by which ordinal is it named? The answer is the tenth cranial nerve, the vagus. It leaves the skull through the jugular foramen and descends in the carotid sheath. Its branches reach the chest and the abdomen, where they slow the heart and drive the gut.

Question 11035. Which of these cranial nerves carries no motor fibers at all: the optic, the oculomotor, the trigeminal, or the hypoglossal? The answer is the optic nerve, the second cranial nerve, which is purely sensory. The oculomotor and hypoglossal are almost purely motor, and the trigeminal carries both sensation and the motor supply to the muscles of chewing.

Question 11036. Which of these pairs belongs together: the third cranial nerve with the superior oblique, the fourth cranial nerve with the superior oblique, the sixth with the medial rectus, or the twelfth with the soft palate? The answer is the fourth cranial nerve with the superior oblique. That muscle turns the eye down and in, so its palsy tilts the head. The sixth nerve supplies the lateral rectus.

Question 11037. A described cross section of the midbrain shows a nucleus in the floor of the cerebral aqueduct and a nerve emerging between the two cerebral peduncles. Which cranial nerve is shown? The answer is the third cranial nerve, the oculomotor. Its nucleus lies in the midbrain in front of the aqueduct. Its fibers pass through the red nucleus and leave between the peduncles.

Question 11038. Which nerve leaves the brainstem between the pons and the medulla, medial to the facial nerve: the fifth, the sixth, the eighth, or the twelfth? The answer is the sixth cranial nerve, the abducens. It runs forward through the subarachnoid space and the cavernous sinus to the lateral rectus. That long course makes it the first nerve to suffer when pressure rises inside the skull.

Question 11039. Name two of the cranial nerves that carry parasympathetic fibers to the head, spoken as a list. The answer is the third cranial nerve, the oculomotor, and the seventh, the facial. The oculomotor fibers constrict the pupil, and the facial fibers reach the tear glands and the salivary glands below the jaw. The ninth and tenth nerves carry the same kind of fibers further down.

Question 11040. Which cranial nerve is tested by asking a person to push out the tongue, and by which ordinal is it named? The answer is the twelfth cranial nerve, the hypoglossal, which leaves the skull through its own canal beside the foramen magnum. A palsy of one side makes the tongue deviate toward the weak side.

Question 11041. Which cranial nerve supplies the muscles of facial expression: the fifth, the seventh, the ninth, or the twelfth? The answer is the seventh cranial nerve, the facial. It leaves the skull through the internal acoustic meatus and then the stylomastoid foramen. Its palsy drops the corner of the mouth and prevents the eye from closing.

Question 11042. A described section of the internal acoustic meatus shows two nerves side by side, one toward the cochlea and one toward the vestibule. Which cranial nerve is shown, and what are its two parts? The answer is the eighth cranial nerve, with a cochlear part for hearing and a vestibular part for balance. The facial nerve travels with them into the same canal.

Question 11043. Which cranial nerve is the great sensory nerve of the face, and how many divisions does it have? The answer is the fifth cranial nerve, the trigeminal, which has three. The ophthalmic division supplies the forehead and the eye, the maxillary the cheek and the upper teeth, and the mandibular the lower jaw and the tongue.

Question 11044. Which division of the trigeminal nerve supplies the skin of the lower lip and the chin: the ophthalmic, the maxillary, the mandibular, or the facial? The answer is the mandibular division. It leaves the skull through the foramen ovale and also supplies the muscles of chewing. The facial nerve supplies the muscles of expression, not the skin.

Question 11045. A constructed illustration: after a blow to the cheek, the skin over the upper jaw and the upper lip is numb while the face still moves. Which nerve is injured? The answer is the maxillary division of the fifth cranial nerve. It leaves the skull through the foramen rotundum and crosses the back of the cheekbone. Because the facial nerve is intact, the face still moves.

Question 11046. Which vessel lies immediately anterior to the pons, separating it from the clivus? The answer is the basilar artery, formed where the two vertebral arteries join at the lower border of the pons. It runs upward in the midline and divides into the two posterior cerebral arteries.

Question 11047. A nucleus in the medulla sends its fibers out to the muscles of the tongue. Which cranial nerve does it belong to: the ninth, the tenth, the eleventh, or the twelfth? The answer is the twelfth cranial nerve, the hypoglossal. Its nucleus lies near the midline in the lower medulla, and its fibers leave between the pyramid and the olive.

Question 11048. Which cranial nerve runs up through the foramen magnum and supplies the sternocleidomastoid and the trapezius? The answer is the eleventh cranial nerve, the accessory. Its fibers arise in the upper cervical cord and ascend into the skull before turning back out. That ascent explains why it leaves the skull again through the jugular foramen.

Question 11049. True or false: the seventh cranial nerve carries taste from the anterior two thirds of the tongue. The answer is true. Those fibers travel with the lingual nerve and then join the facial nerve through the chorda tympani. The posterior third sends its taste fibers with the ninth cranial nerve instead.

Question 11050. A described magnetic resonance image of the base of the skull shows a large opening through which the medulla and its membranes pass, with a nerve beside the cord. Which opening and which nerve are shown? The answer is the foramen magnum, with the eleventh cranial nerve beside the cord. The vertebral arteries pass through it as well.

Question 11051. A constructed illustration: a small infarct in the medial medulla leaves the tongue weak on one side and the limbs weak on the other, with loss of position sense there too. Which pattern is illustrated: lateral medullary syndrome, medial medullary syndrome, a half section of the cord, or a central cord cavity? The answer is medial medullary syndrome. The twelfth nerve, the pyramid and the medial lemniscus are damaged together.

Question 11052. Which vessel runs in the midline in front of the pons, formed where the two vertebral arteries join? The answer is the basilar artery. It lies in the groove on the front of the pons and gives the pontine branches, the labyrinthine artery and the superior cerebellar arteries. At the upper border of the pons it divides into the two posterior cerebral arteries.

Question 11053. A constructed illustration: a slowly growing mass between the cerebellum and the pons causes deafness and ringing in one ear, and later numbness of the face on that side. Which nerve is pressed first? The answer is the eighth cranial nerve, at the cerebellopontine angle near the internal acoustic meatus. The facial nerve lies beside it and is pressed later.

Question 11054. Which of these forms the medial wall of the lateral ventricle: the septum pellucidum, the corpus callosum, the thalamus, or the head of the caudate nucleus? The answer is the septum pellucidum, a thin double membrane between the two lateral ventricles. The corpus callosum forms the roof, the thalamus part of the floor, and the caudate nucleus the lateral wall.

Question 11055. What is the name of the great band of white matter that joins the two cerebral hemispheres beneath the longitudinal fissure? The answer is the corpus callosum. Its fibers cross the midline and connect matching areas of the two hemispheres. Its middle part is the body, its front end the genu, and its back end the splenium.

Question 11056. A described coronal section of the brain shows a fan of white matter converging between the basal ganglia and the thalamus. Which structure is shown, and what happens when it is destroyed? The answer is the internal capsule. Its fibers carry movement and sensation between the cortex and the lower centers, and because they are packed so tightly a small lesion can paralyze the opposite side.

Question 11057. A constructed illustration: a small bleed in the posterior limb of the internal capsule weakens the face, the arm and the leg on the opposite side. Which of these tracts has been interrupted: the spinothalamic tract, the corticospinal tract, the posterior column, or the spinocerebellar tract? The answer is the corticospinal tract. A lesion the size of a pea can cause a dense paralysis.

Question 11058. Which of these pairs belongs together: the precentral gyrus with the motor cortex, the postcentral gyrus with the motor cortex, the superior temporal gyrus with vision, or the occipital lobe with hearing? The answer is the precentral gyrus with the motor cortex. It lies in front of the central sulcus and sends out the corticospinal fibers. The occipital lobe serves vision.

Question 11059. Name two structures that lie within the posterior cranial fossa, spoken as a list. The answer is the cerebellum and the brainstem, meaning the pons and the medulla. The fossa is the lowest and largest of the three, roofed by the tentorium cerebelli. The internal acoustic meatus and the jugular foramen open into it.

Question 11060. Which part of the brainstem lies between the pons above and the spinal cord below and holds the centers for the heart and for breathing? The answer is the medulla oblongata. It contains the nuclei of several cranial nerves and the crossing of the corticospinal fibers. The fourth ventricle lies behind it.

Question 11061. The gag reflex is tested by touching the back of the throat. Which cranial nerve carries its sensory limb: the seventh, the ninth, the eleventh, or the twelfth? The answer is the ninth cranial nerve, the glossopharyngeal. It carries sensation from the back of the throat and the posterior third of the tongue. The tenth nerve lifts the palate.

Question 11062. Which cranial nerve supplies the parotid gland with its secretomotor fibers, and through which ganglion do they pass? The answer is the ninth cranial nerve, the glossopharyngeal, through the otic ganglion. The fibers leave the skull through the jugular foramen and reach the ganglion below the foramen ovale. From there they travel with a branch of the fifth nerve.

Question 11063. A constructed illustration: a person has a hoarse voice, the soft palate droops on one side, and the gag reflex is lost there. Which nerve is damaged, and through which opening does it leave the skull? The answer is the tenth cranial nerve, the vagus, through the jugular foramen. It supplies the muscles of the palate, the pharynx and the larynx.

Question 11064. A described magnetic resonance image of the brain shows a narrow slit of fluid in the midline between the two thalami, roofed by a thin membrane with a fringe of vessels. Which space is shown? The answer is the third ventricle. It opens in front into the lateral ventricles and narrows behind into the cerebral aqueduct.

Question 11065. Which structure lies immediately behind the brainstem and below the occipital lobes, separated from the cerebrum by the tentorium cerebelli? The answer is the cerebellum, which fills the posterior cranial fossa behind the pons and the medulla. Its two hemispheres are joined in the midline by the vermis.

Question 11066. Which of these forms the roof of the fourth ventricle: the cerebellum, the pons, the medulla, or the thalamus? The answer is the cerebellum. The floor is formed by the pons and the medulla, and the roof by the cerebellar vermis and thin sheets of tissue. The ventricle narrows above into the cerebral aqueduct and opens below into the subarachnoid space.

Question 11067. True or false: most fibers of the corticospinal tract cross to the opposite side in the lower medulla. The answer is true. The crossing is visible as the decussation of the pyramids on the front of the medulla. After crossing, the fibers descend in the lateral column of the cord to the lower motor neurons.

Question 11068. Which ascending tract lies in the posterior column of the cord and carries vibration and position sense from the leg? The answer is the fasciculus gracilis, which ends in the nucleus gracilis of the medulla. It lies on the medial side of the posterior column, next to the midline. The fibers from the arm lie lateral to it in the fasciculus cuneatus.

Question 11069. A lesion divides one half of the spinal cord. Pain and temperature are lost on the opposite side and position sense on the same side. What is that pattern called: Brown-Sequard syndrome, syringomyelia, tabes dorsalis, or a central cord lesion? The answer is Brown-Sequard syndrome, the result of a half section of the cord. The spinothalamic fibers have already crossed, while the posterior column fibers have not.

Question 11070. A described transverse section of the spinal cord shows a cavity in the center of the gray matter, with loss of pain and temperature in both arms and preserved position sense. Which condition is illustrated? The answer is syringomyelia. The cavity begins near the center of the cord and destroys the crossing spinothalamic fibers as they pass. The sensory loss therefore appears on both sides.

Question 11071. A constructed illustration: after a blow to the front of the head, a person can no longer smell. Which nerve and which part of the skull are involved? The answer is the first cranial nerve, the olfactory, and the cribriform plate of the ethmoid bone. Its fine fibers pass through the holes of that thin plate. Leakage of cerebrospinal fluid from the nose may follow.

Question 11072. Which cranial nerve passes through the optic canal with the ophthalmic artery: the second, the third, the fourth, or the sixth? The answer is the second cranial nerve, the optic nerve, which carries sight from the retina to the optic chiasm. The third, fourth and sixth nerves reach the eye muscles through the superior orbital fissure instead.

Question 11073. Within the cavernous sinus, which cranial nerve lies immediately lateral to the internal carotid artery? The answer is the sixth cranial nerve, the abducens, running forward in the sinus beside the artery. The third and fourth nerves and the first two divisions of the fifth lie in the wall of the sinus.

Question 11074. Which cranial nerve supplies the lateral rectus muscle, which turns the eye outward? The answer is the sixth cranial nerve, the abducens, which has the longest intracranial course of any cranial nerve. Because it turns the eye outward, a palsy leaves the eye turned inward by the unopposed medial rectus.

Question 11075. Which cranial nerve passes through the superior orbital fissure and supplies the superior oblique: the third, the fourth, the sixth, or the first division of the fifth? The answer is the fourth cranial nerve, the trochlear, the only one to leave the back of the brainstem. Its muscle turns the eye down and in, so its palsy makes the person tilt the head.

Question 11076. Which of these pairs belongs together: the jugular foramen with the ninth, tenth and eleventh nerves, the foramen rotundum with the mandibular division, the internal acoustic meatus with the twelfth nerve, or the hypoglossal canal with the eighth? The answer is the jugular foramen with the ninth, tenth and eleventh nerves. The mandibular division leaves through the foramen ovale.

Question 11077. A described magnetic resonance image of the base of the skull shows a nerve passing through a small canal of its own in the occipital bone beside the foramen magnum. Which nerve is shown? The answer is the twelfth cranial nerve, the hypoglossal, in the hypoglossal canal. It supplies the muscles of the tongue, and its course is short and direct.

Question 11078. Which cranial nerve carries taste from the posterior third of the tongue: the seventh, the ninth, the tenth, or the twelfth? The answer is the ninth cranial nerve, the glossopharyngeal, whose fibers reach the tongue by way of the lingual branch. The seventh nerve carries taste from the anterior two thirds instead.

Question 11079. A described section of the brain is cut from front to back exactly through the midline and shows the corpus callosum along its whole length. In which plane is it cut, and how much of the brain does it show? The answer is the median plane, a sagittal section through the middle. It shows the medial surface of both hemispheres, with the corpus callosum between them.

Question 11080. Which plexus is formed by the anterior rami of the first four cervical nerves, and which nerve descends from it to the diaphragm? The answer is the cervical plexus, from which the phrenic nerve descends. The plexus also gives the small nerves that supply the skin of the neck and the back of the head.

Question 11081. Which plexus is formed by the anterior rami of the lower four cervical nerves and the first thoracic: the cervical, the brachial, the lumbar, or the sacral? The answer is the brachial plexus, which passes between the scalene muscles and then through the axilla. Its five roots join into three trunks, six divisions, three cords and five terminal nerves.

Question 11082. A described dissection of the neck shows a nerve lying on the front of the scalene muscle and descending to enter the chest between the subclavian vein and the subclavian artery. Which nerve is shown, and which muscle does it supply? The answer is the phrenic nerve, which supplies the diaphragm. It carries fibers from the third, fourth and fifth cervical roots.

Question 11083. Which nerve emerges from the medial border of the psoas muscle and leaves the pelvis through the obturator foramen? The answer is the obturator nerve, from the second, third and fourth lumbar roots. It supplies the adductor muscles of the thigh and the skin over the inner side of the thigh.

Question 11084. Which roots form the upper trunk of the brachial plexus: the fifth and sixth cervical, the eighth cervical and first thoracic, the seventh cervical alone, or the fourth cervical? The answer is the fifth and sixth cervical roots, which join between the scalene muscles and divide into two. The eighth cervical and first thoracic roots form the lower trunk.

Question 11085. A constructed illustration: a person wakes with a face that droops on one side, cannot close the eye and cannot wrinkle the forehead, while the arms and legs move normally. Which nerve is affected? The answer is the seventh cranial nerve, the facial. The whole side is weak, including the forehead, which shows that the nerve itself is at fault.

Question 11086. Which structure lies immediately deep to the parotid gland and divides it into a superficial and a deep part? The answer is the facial nerve, the seventh cranial nerve, which enters the gland from behind and breaks into its branches within it. The parotid duct crosses the masseter and opens beside the upper molar teeth.

Question 11087. A constructed illustration: a person cannot lift the shoulder and cannot turn the head against resistance, while the arms and legs are strong. Which of these nerves is damaged: the ninth cranial nerve, the tenth, the eleventh, or the twelfth? The answer is the eleventh cranial nerve, the accessory, at the jugular foramen. Its fibers supply the sternocleidomastoid and the trapezius.

Question 11088. Which cranial nerve supplies the muscle that lifts the upper eyelid, and where is its nucleus? The answer is the third cranial nerve, the oculomotor, whose nucleus lies in the midbrain in front of the cerebral aqueduct. A palsy of the nerve drops the eyelid and turns the eye outward and down.

Question 11089. True or false: the nerve to the stapedius muscle and the chorda tympani are branches of the fifth cranial nerve. The answer is false. Both belong to the seventh cranial nerve, the facial, given off inside the bony canal as it runs to the stylomastoid foramen. The chorda tympani carries taste from the anterior two thirds of the tongue.

Question 11090. A described cross section of the spinal cord through the cervical enlargement shows a broad anterior horn. Which cells does that horn hold, and what do their fibers supply? The answer is the lower motor neurons, whose fibers supply the muscles of the arm. The horn is wide here because so many muscles of the hand and forearm are represented.

Question 11091. Which of these tracts carries the impulses that produce voluntary movement: the spinothalamic, the corticospinal, the posterior column, or the spinocerebellar? The answer is the corticospinal tract, which begins in the motor cortex and ends on the lower motor neurons of the cord. The posterior column carries vibration and position sense.

Question 11092. Which structure lies immediately lateral to the internal capsule: the thalamus, the lentiform nucleus, the caudate nucleus, or the corpus callosum? The answer is the lentiform nucleus, made of the putamen and the globus pallidus. The thalamus lies on the medial side, and the capsule is the narrow gate for nearly all fibers to and from the cortex.

Question 11093. A constructed illustration: blood from a burst swelling at the base of the brain spreads through the fluid around the brain and the cord. Which space holds it, and where does that fluid normally return to the blood? The answer is the subarachnoid space, with absorption into the dural venous sinuses through the arachnoid granulations. Blood there irritates the meninges and stiffens the neck.

Question 11094. Which artery supplies most of the lateral surface of the cerebral hemisphere: the anterior cerebral, the middle cerebral, the posterior cerebral, or the basilar? The answer is the middle cerebral artery, which runs in the lateral sulcus and sends branches over the insula and the convexity. The anterior cerebral artery supplies the medial surface.

Question 11095. Which artery arises from the vertebral artery and supplies the lateral part of the medulla and the lower surface of the cerebellum? The answer is the posterior inferior cerebellar artery, which winds around the upper medulla. Blockage of it produces a lateral medullary syndrome with loss of pain and temperature on the opposite side of the body.

Question 11096. A described computed tomography scan of the head shows a small patch of low density in the posterior limb of the internal capsule. Which tract has been damaged, and what will the person be unable to do? The answer is the corticospinal tract, and the opposite side of the body cannot be moved. The fibers are packed so closely that a tiny lesion causes a large weakness.

Question 11097. Which of these connects the third ventricle to the fourth: the cerebral aqueduct, the interventricular foramen, the central canal, or the median aperture? The answer is the cerebral aqueduct, a narrow channel through the midbrain. The interventricular foramen joins each lateral ventricle to the third, and the central canal lies in the cord.

Question 11098. Which of these pairs belongs together: the middle cerebral artery with the lateral surface, the anterior cerebral artery with the occipital lobe, the posterior cerebral artery with the frontal lobe, or the basilar artery with the medial surface? The answer is the middle cerebral artery with the lateral surface. The anterior cerebral artery supplies the medial surface, and the posterior cerebral artery the occipital lobe.

Question 11099. A described section of the brainstem is cut at right angles to its long axis and shows the cerebral aqueduct surrounded by a thick cap of gray matter, with two large peduncles in front. Which part is shown, and in which plane? The answer is the midbrain, cut in a transverse plane. The two peduncles carry the corticospinal fibers down toward the pons.

Question 11100. Which swelling on the posterior root of a spinal nerve holds the cell bodies of the sensory neurons that enter the cord? The answer is the posterior root ganglion, also called the dorsal root ganglion. Each spinal nerve has one, lying in the intervertebral foramen. The anterior root beside it carries the fibers that leave the cord.

To summarize, the brain and the cord are wrapped in the dura mater, the arachnoid mater and the pia mater, and the cerebrospinal fluid that cushions them lies in the subarachnoid space and returns to the blood through the arachnoid granulations. The spinal cord ends near the first or second lumbar vertebra, so the roots below it form the cauda equina, and its blood comes from the anterior spinal artery in front and the two posterior spinal arteries behind. The plexuses of the limbs are built from named roots, and each nerve has a place where it is most exposed, from the spiral groove of the humerus for the radial nerve to the neck of the fibula for the common fibular nerve. The twelve cranial nerves are named by ordinal, and each carries its own mixture of fibers and leaves through its own opening in the skull. Knowing where each tract crosses, where each nerve lies, and which artery supplies which surface is what lets an examination finding be read back to a structure.
