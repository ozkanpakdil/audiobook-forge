# Chapter 47 — Thymus

Every account of the body's defenses begins with a small organ that sits in the middle of the chest and with the network of vessels that carries lymph through the whole body. This chapter covers the thymus, its development, its structure and its relations in the mediastinum, and then the great lymphatic territories by which lymph from every region reaches the venous system. It holds one hundred questions, and each one gives its answer immediately after the question and then explains why that answer is right, so nothing has to be held in mind until the end. The thymus belongs to the lymphoid organs, and the drainage territories described here are the map a clinician uses to understand where a tumor may travel. This is educational material, and it supports rather than replaces an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 4601. In which part of the mediastinum does the thymus lie: the posterior mediastinum, the anterior part of the superior mediastinum, the middle mediastinum, or the root of the neck? The answer is the anterior part of the superior mediastinum. It occupies the space immediately behind the sternum and in front of the great vessels and the pericardium. In the child it fills that space, and with age it shrinks and is replaced by fat.

Question 4602. Which of these structures lies immediately anterior to the thymus: the sternum, the trachea, the aortic arch, or the pericardium? The answer is the sternum. The gland lies against the back of the manubrium and the upper part of the body of the sternum. The trachea and the aortic arch lie behind it.

Question 4603. A described radiograph of a young child shows a soft-tissue shadow filling the upper part of the anterior mediastinum, with a wavy lower border where the ribs press into it. Which organ is shown? The answer is the thymus. It is relatively large in early life and may widen the upper mediastinal shadow. The wavy border is produced by the ribs indenting the soft gland.

Question 4604. From which pharyngeal pouch does the thymus develop? The answer is the third pharyngeal pouch. Paired buds grow from that pouch on each side and later fuse in the midline. They migrate downward into the anterior mediastinum, which is why thymic tissue sometimes remains in the neck.

Question 4605. Which gland develops from the same pharyngeal pouch as the thymus: the thyroid gland, the superior parathyroid gland, the inferior parathyroid gland, or the ultimobranchial body? The answer is the inferior parathyroid gland. It and the thymus both arise from the third pharyngeal pouch and travel down together. The superior parathyroid gland comes from the fourth pouch.

Question 4606. Which great vessels lie immediately posterior to the thymus? The answer is the left brachiocephalic vein and the arch of the aorta. The vein crosses the midline in front of the branches of the arch, and the thymus lies in front of both. The pericardium and the heart lie below its lower pole.

Question 4607. A constructed illustration: a young adult has a mass in the anterior mediastinum and drooping eyelids with weakness that worsens through the day. Which organ is the likely source of the mass? The answer is the thymus. Tumors of the thymus are associated with myasthenia gravis, a disorder of the junction between nerve and muscle. Management of that disorder belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 4608. True or false: the thymus reaches its greatest relative size in adult life. The answer is false. The gland is largest in relation to body weight around puberty, and after that it gradually involutes. In the older adult it is a thin fatty remnant in the anterior mediastinum.

Question 4609. Which of these is the whorled structure found in the medulla of the thymus: the lymphoid follicle, the thymic corpuscle, the germinal center, or the medullary cord? The answer is the thymic corpuscle, also called the Hassall corpuscle. It is a coiled mass of flattened epithelial reticular cells. The corpuscles become more numerous with age and are the surest sign that a section comes from the medulla.

Question 4610. Which cells form the thymic corpuscles: the flattened epithelial reticular cells of the medulla, the epithelial reticular cells that seal the cortical capillaries, the lymphocytes of the cortex, or the macrophages of the cortex? The answer is the flattened epithelial reticular cells of the medulla. They pile up into the whorls of the corpuscle. The cells that seal the cortical capillaries stay in the cortex and never form whorls.

Question 4611. Which two zones make up each lobule of the thymus? The answer is the cortex and the medulla. The cortex is the dark outer zone, packed with developing lymphocytes. The medulla is the pale center, where the lymphocytes that survive selection are released.

Question 4612. A described stained section of the thymus shows a deeply stained outer zone crowded with small cells and a pale center containing whorled structures. Which zone is the dark outer one? The answer is the cortex. Its color comes from the dense packing of developing lymphocytes. The pale center with the whorls is the medulla.

Question 4613. A described transverse section of the superior mediastinum is taken at the level of the fourth thoracic vertebra. Which structure lies between the back of the sternum and the left brachiocephalic vein? The answer is the thymus. The vein crosses the midline behind it, and the trachea lies further back still. This slice therefore shows the gland in its anterior position.

Question 4614. Which membrane lies immediately lateral to the thymus on each side, separating it from the lung? The answer is the mediastinal pleura. The phrenic nerve runs downward on that membrane just lateral to the gland. The lung therefore does not touch the thymus directly.

Question 4615. The arterial supply of the thymus comes mainly from branches of which artery: the internal thoracic artery, the pulmonary trunk, the descending thoracic aorta, or the axillary artery? The answer is the internal thoracic artery. Small branches also reach it from the inferior thyroid artery in the neck. Its veins follow the arteries and drain into neighboring veins.

Question 4616. Into which vein do the veins of the thymus drain: the right brachiocephalic, the left brachiocephalic, the superior vena cava, or the azygos vein? The answer is the left brachiocephalic vein, joined by the internal thoracic and inferior thyroid veins. The gland sits directly in front of that large vein. This is why an enlarged thymus can press on it.

Question 4617. Name two secondary lymphoid organs that a mature T lymphocyte leaving the thymus goes on to populate. The answer is a lymph node and the spleen. Lymphocytes that have been selected in the thymus leave by the blood and settle in these organs. There they gather in the regions where they meet the antigens they were selected to recognize.

Question 4618. A constructed illustration: an infant has repeated infections and almost no T lymphocytes in the blood, and imaging shows no thymic shadow. Which part of the embryo failed to develop? The answer is the third pharyngeal pouch. Failure of that pouch leaves the infant without a thymus and without the inferior parathyroid glands. The pattern is a recognized congenital one rather than a disease of the gland itself.

Question 4619. Which cells of the thymus arrive from the bone marrow, and which form its framework? The answer is that the lymphocytes arrive from the bone marrow, while the epithelial framework is endodermal. The epithelial reticular cells come from the third pharyngeal pouch. The two populations meet in the gland and cooperate in the selection of lymphocytes.

Question 4620. In which part of the thymus is the blood-thymus barrier found: the medulla, the cortex, the capsule, or the hilum? The answer is the cortex. The barrier keeps developing lymphocytes in the cortex away from circulating antigens. The medulla is deliberately leaky, so that lymphocytes there can meet antigens.

Question 4621. Which of these forms part of the blood-thymus barrier: fenestrated capillaries, continuous capillaries joined by tight junctions, lymphatic sinuses, or wide venous channels? The answer is continuous capillaries joined by tight junctions. Around them lie a basal lamina, a layer of epithelial reticular cells and macrophages. Together they keep the cortical lymphocytes sealed off from the blood.

Question 4622. True or false: the cortex of each thymic lobule is completely walled off from its neighbors by connective tissue. The answer is false. The septa separate the cortices, but the medulla is continuous from one lobule to the next. A section that seems to show many separate lobules is therefore joined in the middle.

Question 4623. A described section of the thymus shows the lymphoid tissue cut into blocks by bands of connective tissue running inward from the surface, each band carrying small vessels. What are those bands? The answer is the septa, also called trabeculae. They carry blood vessels into the gland. Between them the lymphoid tissue is arranged in lobules, each with its own cortex and a shared medulla.

Question 4624. A described specimen shows a soft, bilobed organ with a finely lobulated surface, taken from the upper chest of a child. Which organ is it? The answer is the thymus. The two lobes are joined by connective tissue across the midline. The lobulated surface reflects the septa that divide the cortex.

Question 4625. Which structure lies immediately below the lower pole of the thymus in the chest? The answer is the fibrous pericardium, over the heart. The gland rests on it and does not usually reach the diaphragm. The pericardium therefore separates the thymus from the heart.

Question 4626. A constructed illustration: a person with a large mass in the anterior mediastinum has swelling of the face and neck. Which vein, lying directly behind the thymus, is being compressed? The answer is the left brachiocephalic vein. Blood from the head and the left arm returns through it to the heart. A mass in front of it can narrow it and dam the flow.

Question 4627. Which statement about the thymus in adult life is correct: it grows steadily larger, it is replaced by fat, it becomes a lymph node, or it moves into the abdomen? The answer is that it is replaced by fat. The lymphoid tissue shrinks from puberty onward and the spaces fill with adipose tissue. The remnant stays in the anterior mediastinum.

Question 4628. What name is given to the cortical cells that wrap themselves around clusters of developing lymphocytes? The answer is thymic nurse cells. They are a specialized form of cortical epithelial reticular cell. They enclose the lymphocytes and give them a protected place in which to be tested.

Question 4629. Name two structures that develop from the third pharyngeal pouch. The answer is the thymus and the inferior parathyroid gland. Both begin in the neck and descend to their adult positions. That shared journey explains why parathyroid tissue is sometimes found inside the thymus.

Question 4630. Which of these pairs belongs together: the cortex with positive selection, the medulla with positive selection, the capsule with lymphocyte maturation, or the septa with the thymic corpuscles? The answer is the cortex with positive selection. Lymphocytes that can recognize the body's own marking molecules are kept alive there. Negative selection, which removes the self-reactive ones, happens in the medulla.

Question 4631. The upper poles of the thymus reach into the neck. Deep to which pair of muscles do they lie? The answer is the sternohyoid and sternothyroid muscles. They lie in the midline of the lower neck, behind those strap muscles. Thymic tissue can therefore present as a neck mass in a child.

Question 4632. Which nerve supplies the thymus with its parasympathetic fibers: the phrenic nerve, the vagus nerve, the sympathetic trunk, or the glossopharyngeal nerve? The answer is the vagus nerve, the tenth cranial nerve. Sympathetic fibers reach it from the sympathetic trunk. These nerves follow the vessels into the gland and have little to do with its immune work.

Question 4633. Which statement about the lymphatic vessels of the thymus is correct: it has both afferent and efferent vessels, it has efferent vessels only, it has afferent vessels only, or it has none? The answer is that it has efferent vessels only. No lymphatic vessel carries lymph into the thymus. The lymphocytes that populate it arrive through the blood.

Question 4634. A described low-power section of the thymus shows several dark lobules whose pale centers join across the septa. Which part of the gland is continuous between the lobules? The answer is the medulla. The septa cut the cortex into separate caps but stop short of the medulla. The lymphocytes that are ready to leave therefore travel through one continuous central channel.

Question 4635. A constructed illustration: a thymic tumor is removed and the pathologist reports that the tumor cells form whorls and carry epithelial markers. From which cells has the tumor arisen? The answer is the thymic epithelial reticular cells. Tumors of the thymus are tumors of its epithelium, not of its lymphocytes. Decisions about treatment belong to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 4636. Down to about which level does the adult thymus reach: the second costal cartilage, the fourth costal cartilage, the eighth thoracic vertebra, or the diaphragm? The answer is the fourth costal cartilage. Below that level the heart and its pericardium fill the anterior mediastinum. In the child the gland often reaches a little lower.

Question 4637. Which structure lies immediately behind the upper part of the thymus in the neck: the trachea, the esophagus, the sternum, or the internal jugular vein? The answer is the trachea. The gland lies in front of the windpipe in the midline. The esophagus lies behind the trachea and is therefore not the immediate posterior relation.

Question 4638. Which of these pairs belongs together: the flattened epithelial reticular cells with the blood-thymus barrier, the thymic nurse cells with the medulla, the thymic corpuscle with the cortex, or the lymphocytes of the medulla with the secretion of antibodies? The answer is the flattened epithelial reticular cells with the blood-thymus barrier. They seal the cortical capillaries and the surface of the lobule. The corpuscles belong to the medulla and the nurse cells to the cortex.

Question 4639. True or false: the thymus is a primary lymphoid organ. The answer is true. A primary lymphoid organ is one in which lymphocytes are produced and selected, and the thymus shares that role with the bone marrow. Lymph nodes, the spleen and the tonsils are secondary organs, in which mature lymphocytes meet antigen.

Question 4640. Which of these is a primary lymphoid organ: a lymph node, the spleen, the thymus, or a tonsil? The answer is the thymus. Lymphocytes are made and selected within it. The other three are secondary lymphoid organs, in which mature lymphocytes respond to antigen.

Question 4641. In the superior mediastinum, which structure lies between the thymus in front and the trachea behind? The answer is the left brachiocephalic vein. It crosses from left to right in front of the branches of the aortic arch. The chain from front to back is therefore thymus, vein, trachea.

Question 4642. A constructed illustration: an infant has a soft lump in the lower neck that moves when the infant swallows, and no other abnormality. Which tissue, left behind during its descent, may explain it? The answer is thymic tissue. The gland begins in the neck and travels down to the chest, and fragments may remain along the path. Such a remnant is a normal variation of descent.

Question 4643. A described radiograph of an older adult shows only a thin soft-tissue shadow in the anterior mediastinum where a child has a broad glandular shadow. What has happened to the thymus? The answer is that it has involuted and been replaced by fat. The fatty remnant casts almost no shadow. The change is a normal part of aging and not disease.

Question 4644. Which cells replace the lymphoid tissue of the thymus as the gland involutes? The answer is fat cells. They fill the spaces left as the lymphocytes and epithelial cells decrease in number. The remnant is therefore a fatty body in the anterior mediastinum rather than a lymphoid organ.

Question 4645. Which process removes lymphocytes that would attack the body's own tissues: positive selection in the cortex, negative selection in the medulla, phagocytosis in the blood, or selection in a lymph node? The answer is negative selection in the medulla. Lymphocytes that react strongly against the body's own molecules are made to die there. Positive selection in the cortex does the opposite, keeping those that can recognize the body's marking molecules.

Question 4646. Which feature distinguishes the medulla of the thymus from its cortex: fewer lymphocytes and the presence of thymic corpuscles, a denser packing of lymphocytes, a high rate of cell division, or the blood-thymus barrier? The answer is fewer lymphocytes and the presence of thymic corpuscles. The medulla is paler because its cells are less crowded. The blood-thymus barrier belongs to the cortex.

Question 4647. A described specimen of the thymus from an older adult shows a thin rim of lymphoid tissue around a core of fat. Which two changes does the specimen show? The answer is involution of the lymphoid tissue and its replacement by fat. Both are the ordinary changes of age. The lobular pattern may still be faintly visible in the fatty remnant.

Question 4648. Which statement about the thymus in the newborn is correct: it is very small and hard to see, it is relatively large and may widen the upper mediastinum on a radiograph, it lies in the posterior mediastinum, or it contains no lymphocytes? The answer is that it is relatively large and may widen the upper mediastinum. Its shadow can be mistaken for disease in a child.

Question 4649. A described radiograph of an infant shows a large soft-tissue mass in the upper anterior mediastinum that narrows the air column of the trachea from in front. Which organ is responsible, and from which direction is it pressing? The answer is the thymus, pressing on the trachea from in front. Its size varies widely in infancy, so the finding must be read with the whole picture.

Question 4650. Which statement about positive selection in the thymus is correct: it happens in the cortex and keeps lymphocytes that recognize the body's own molecules, it happens in the medulla and destroys self-reactive cells, it happens in the blood after the cells leave, or it removes lymphocytes that cannot divide? The answer is that it happens in the cortex and keeps lymphocytes able to recognize the body's own molecules. Those that cannot are left to die.

Question 4651. At which structure does the thoracic duct begin? The answer is the cisterna chyli. It is a sac in front of the bodies of the first and second lumbar vertebrae. All the lymph from below the diaphragm reaches it before entering the chest.

Question 4652. Which vessel carries lymph from the left side of the head and neck, the left upper limb, and the whole body below the diaphragm: the right lymphatic duct, the thoracic duct, the left jugular trunk alone, or the azygos vein? The answer is the thoracic duct. The right lymphatic duct takes only the right side of the head, neck and chest and the right upper limb.

Question 4653. A described dissection of the root of the neck shows a thin-walled duct arching upward and laterally to open at the junction of the left internal jugular and left subclavian veins. Which duct is shown? The answer is the thoracic duct. Its arch lies behind the left common carotid artery. It opens into the left venous angle, where the two veins meet.

Question 4654. Which regions of the body are drained by the right lymphatic duct? The answer is the right side of the head and neck, the right upper limb and the right side of the chest. It is a short trunk, only about one or two centimeters long. It ends at the right venous angle.

Question 4655. At about which level does the thoracic duct cross from the right side of the mediastinum to the left: the first, the fourth or fifth, the eighth, or the twelfth thoracic vertebra? The answer is the fourth or fifth thoracic vertebra. It begins on the right of the vertebral column and crosses behind the esophagus. Above that level it continues upward on the left.

Question 4656. In the posterior mediastinum, between which two vessels does the thoracic duct ascend? The answer is the descending aorta on its left and the azygos vein on its right. It lies in front of the vertebral bodies and behind the esophagus. This narrow bed protects the duct but makes it easy to injure.

Question 4657. A constructed illustration: after an operation in the back of the chest, milky fluid collects in the pleural cavity. Which channel has most likely been injured, and what is the fluid? The answer is the thoracic duct, and the fluid is chyle. Chyle is lymph that has taken up fat from the small intestine. Its milky look is the clue to the injury.

Question 4658. True or false: the cisterna chyli receives the intestinal trunk as well as the two lumbar trunks. The answer is true. The intestinal trunk brings the fatty lymph from the gut. The lumbar trunks bring lymph from the lower limbs, the pelvis and the abdominal wall.

Question 4659. What is the name of the milky lymph that drains from the small intestine, and why is it milky? The answer is chyle, and it is milky because it carries absorbed fat. The fat leaves the gut in tiny particles that give the lymph its white color. The lymph vessels of the villi that collect it are called lacteals.

Question 4660. At which venous junction does the thoracic duct end: the right venous angle, the left venous angle, the superior vena cava, or the azygos vein? The answer is the left venous angle, where the left internal jugular and the left subclavian veins meet. The duct sometimes ends in one of those veins instead. From there the lymph joins the blood.

Question 4661. Which group of lymph nodes receives the lymph from the small intestine before it enters the intestinal trunk? The answer is the superior mesenteric nodes. They lie in the mesentery along the superior mesenteric vessels. From them the lymph passes to the cisterna chyli.

Question 4662. A described section of a lymph node shows a fibrous capsule, a dark outer zone with rounded masses of cells, a paler center, and a hilum. In which zone do the rounded masses lie? The answer is the cortex. The rounded masses are the lymphoid follicles, and they enlarge when the node is active. The medulla, nearer the hilum, is the paler center.

Question 4663. A described cross section of the posterior mediastinum shows the esophagus in the midline, the thoracic duct behind it, and two large vessels on either side. Which vessel lies immediately to the left of the duct? The answer is the descending aorta. The azygos vein lies to its right. That pairing gives the duct its standard address in the posterior mediastinum.

Question 4664. Which of these regions does the superficial inguinal group of nodes drain: the skin of the lower limb, the testis, the kidney, or the uterus? The answer is the skin of the lower limb, apart from a strip on the back of the calf and the lateral border of the foot. Those areas drain first to the popliteal nodes.

Question 4665. In the femoral canal, which structure lies immediately medial to the femoral vein? The answer is the deep inguinal lymph node, sometimes called the node of Cloquet. It is the highest of the deep inguinal nodes. Lymph from the deep tissues of the leg reaches it before passing to the external iliac nodes.

Question 4666. To which group of lymph nodes does the testis drain, and why? The answer is the para-aortic, or lumbar, nodes. The testis develops on the posterior abdominal wall and takes its lymph drainage with it. Lymph from the testis therefore never passes through the inguinal nodes, although the skin of the scrotum does.

Question 4667. A constructed illustration: a young man has a painless swelling of one testis, and imaging shows enlarged nodes deep in the upper abdomen. Which node group is involved? The answer is the para-aortic nodes, beside the abdominal aorta. They lie at the level where the testis began its descent. This pattern of spread follows the embryology of the gland.

Question 4668. Which of these structures drains to the para-aortic nodes rather than to the superficial inguinal nodes: the scrotum, the ovary, the skin of the perineum, or the skin of the lower abdominal wall? The answer is the ovary. Like the testis, it develops high on the posterior abdominal wall. The other three drain to the superficial inguinal nodes.

Question 4669. To which group of lymph nodes does most of the breast drain: the parasternal, the axillary, the deep cervical, or the inguinal nodes? The answer is the axillary group. About three quarters of the lymph from the gland passes there, and the pectoral nodes are often the first to receive it. The remaining lymph leaves by other routes.

Question 4670. Which part of the breast drains to the parasternal nodes along the internal thoracic vessels: the lateral part, the medial part, the lower part, or the skin of the nipple? The answer is the medial part. Its vessels pass through the chest wall beside the internal thoracic artery. Lymph from the lower part of the gland may reach nodes in the abdomen through the sheath of the rectus muscle.

Question 4671. Which of these pairs belongs together: the tip of the tongue with the submental nodes, the tip of the tongue with the occipital nodes, the upper lip with the deep cervical nodes only, or the scalp of the back of the head with the parotid nodes? The answer is the tip of the tongue with the submental nodes. The back of the scalp drains to the occipital nodes instead.

Question 4672. What is the name of the left supraclavicular lymph node that may receive lymph from the abdominal organs? The answer is Virchow's node. It lies just above the left clavicle at the lower end of the thoracic duct. Lymph from the stomach and other abdominal organs can reach it, which is why it is felt for in the neck.

Question 4673. A constructed illustration: a person with a tumor of the stomach has a hard, enlarged node above the left clavicle. Through which channel did the tumor cells reach the neck? The answer is the thoracic duct. Lymph from the gut passes up the duct and can lodge in the node at its termination. The finding is a described illustration of a known pattern and not a diagnosis.

Question 4674. A described lymphangiogram of the leg shows vessels running up the medial side of the calf and the thigh and filling nodes in the groin. Which group is filling? The answer is the superficial inguinal nodes. They receive the superficial vessels of the lower limb. The deep vessels of the leg pass first to the popliteal nodes.

Question 4675. Which part of the anal canal drains to the superficial inguinal nodes: the part above the pectinate line, the part below it, the whole canal, or none of it? The answer is the part below the pectinate line. It develops from the outer ectodermal layer, like the skin. The part above the line drains to the internal iliac and para-aortic nodes.

Question 4676. In the root of the neck, which structure lies immediately in front of the arch of the thoracic duct? The answer is the left common carotid artery, with the internal jugular vein beside it. The duct passes behind them before it opens into the venous angle. The carotid sheath and its contents therefore lie in front of the duct at that level.

Question 4677. Which trunks join to form the cisterna chyli? The answer is the two lumbar trunks and the intestinal trunk. The lumbar trunks drain the lower limbs, the pelvis and the abdominal wall. The intestinal trunk drains the gut and its glands.

Question 4678. Which group of nodes lies at the hilum of each lung and receives the lymph from the lung substance? The answer is the bronchopulmonary, or hilar, nodes. Lymph then passes to the tracheobronchial nodes around the lower trachea. From there it enters the bronchomediastinal trunk on each side.

Question 4679. A described dissection of the upper limb after removal of the axillary nodes shows pitting swelling of the arm. Which channels have been divided? The answer is the lymphatic vessels that carried lymph from the arm to the axillary nodes. With those nodes gone, tissue fluid collects in the limb. The swelling is an illustration of how completely the arm depends on that route.

Question 4680. A constructed illustration: a woman has a hard lump in the upper outer part of the breast and a hard node in the armpit. Which group of axillary nodes is most likely involved? The answer is the pectoral, or anterior, group, which lies along the lower border of the pectoralis minor. About three quarters of the lymph from the breast passes to the axillary nodes. Assessment and treatment belong to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 4681. Which of these pairs belongs together: the popliteal nodes with the lateral border of the foot, the popliteal nodes with the medial side of the thigh, the inguinal nodes with the brain, or the deep cervical nodes with the foot? The answer is the popliteal nodes with the lateral border of the foot. Lymph from that strip travels up the short saphenous route to the popliteal fossa. The medial side of the thigh drains to the inguinal nodes.

Question 4682. Along which vein do the deep cervical lymph nodes lie in the neck? The answer is the internal jugular vein. One of them, the jugulodigastric node, lies below the posterior belly of the digastric muscle. It receives the lymph from the palatine tonsil.

Question 4683. At which venous junction does the right lymphatic duct end? The answer is the right venous angle, where the right internal jugular and right subclavian veins meet. It is a short vessel, about one centimeter long. It may open as several separate small vessels instead of one trunk.

Question 4684. True or false: the thoracic duct carries lymph from both lower limbs. The answer is true. All the lymph from below the diaphragm gathers at the cisterna chyli and passes up the duct. The right lymphatic duct carries no lymph from the limbs below the diaphragm.

Question 4685. Name the three trunks that join the thoracic duct near its termination. The answer is the left jugular trunk, the left subclavian trunk and the left bronchomediastinal trunk. They bring lymph from the left side of the head and neck, the left upper limb and the left side of the chest. The duct ends at the left venous angle just after receiving them.

Question 4686. A described section of the wall of the small intestine shows a blind-ended vessel in the center of each villus. What is that vessel, and what does it carry? The answer is a lacteal, a lymphatic capillary. It carries the fat that has been absorbed by the villus. The fat gives the lymph its milky appearance.

Question 4687. Which structure lies immediately behind the cisterna chyli? The answer is the bodies of the first and second lumbar vertebrae. The sac lies in front of them and behind the aorta. It is the lowest part of the thoracic duct.

Question 4688. What name is given to the lymph nodes that lie around the abdominal aorta and receive lymph from the kidneys and the gonads? The answer is the para-aortic, or lumbar, nodes. They also receive the lymph from the common iliac nodes below. Their efferent vessels form the lumbar trunks.

Question 4689. To which groups of nodes does the cervix of the uterus drain? The answer is the internal and external iliac nodes, with the sacral nodes and the nodes in the parametrium. The upper part of the body of the uterus drains instead to the para-aortic nodes along the ovarian vessels. That difference follows the different origins of the two parts.

Question 4690. A constructed illustration: after a difficult operation in the upper abdomen, a milky fluid collects in both pleural cavities and the person becomes short of breath. Which single channel is the likely source, and which two cavities are involved? The answer is the thoracic duct and the two pleural cavities. Its contents are chyle, the fatty lymph from the gut. Injury high in the chest or neck lets the chyle escape.

Question 4691. A described section of a lymph node shows vessels entering its convex surface and leaving at its hilum. Which vessels enter, and which leave? The answer is that afferent vessels enter the convex surface and one or two efferent vessels leave at the hilum. The flow is one way, from the subcapsular sinus through the cortex to the medulla. A node therefore filters lymph rather than merely storing it.

Question 4692. Which statement about the lymphatics of the diaphragm is correct: the two surfaces are sealed off from one another, the two surfaces communicate through small openings, the diaphragm has no lymphatics, or its lymph drains only to the groin? The answer is that the two surfaces communicate through small openings. Fluid in the abdomen can therefore pass into the pleural lymphatics. That route explains how abdominal fluid can be joined by fluid around the lung.

Question 4693. By which vessels does the thymus drain its lymph, and to which nodes does that lymph pass? The answer is its efferent lymphatic vessels, which pass to the anterior mediastinal, brachiocephalic and tracheobronchial nodes. The thymus has no afferent lymphatics. Its lymphocytes reach it through the blood.

Question 4694. Which groups of nodes receive the lymph from the thyroid gland? The answer is the prelaryngeal, pretracheal and paratracheal nodes, and then the deep cervical nodes. The gland lies against the front and sides of the trachea. This is why a swelling of the gland may be felt to move when the person swallows.

Question 4695. In the superior mediastinum, which structure lies immediately to the left of the thoracic duct? The answer is the left mediastinal pleura. The duct lies against it as it ascends behind the left subclavian artery. Its right side faces the esophagus.

Question 4696. A described lymphangiogram of the arm shows a chain of vessels running up the medial side and a group of nodes in the armpit. Which group of nodes has filled? The answer is the lateral group of axillary nodes, which lies along the axillary vein. The superficial vessels of the arm follow the basilic vein to it. Vessels following the cephalic vein pass instead to the apical nodes.

Question 4697. Which statement about the drainage of the tongue is correct: the tip drains to the submental nodes and the lateral margin to the submandibular nodes, the whole tongue drains to the inguinal nodes, the tip drains to the deep cervical nodes only, or the tongue has no lymphatics? The answer is that the tip drains to the submental nodes and the lateral margin to the submandibular nodes. The midline of the tip drains to both sides.

Question 4698. Which of these pairs belongs together: the kidney with the para-aortic nodes, the kidney with the superficial inguinal nodes, the bladder with the popliteal nodes, or the ovary with the deep cervical nodes? The answer is the kidney with the para-aortic nodes. It lies against the posterior abdominal wall, and its lymph follows its vessels. The bladder drains to the internal and external iliac nodes.

Question 4699. What is the name of the lymph nodes at the hilum of the spleen, and where does their lymph go next? The answer is the splenic, or hilar, nodes, and their lymph passes to the celiac nodes. The spleen lies in the left upper part of the abdomen, behind the stomach. Its lymph joins the intestinal trunk and so reaches the cisterna chyli.

Question 4700. Which of these statements correctly pairs a structure with its draining lymph nodes: the anal margin with the internal iliac nodes, the cervix with the superficial inguinal nodes, the glans of the penis with the deep inguinal nodes, or the kidney with the superficial inguinal nodes? The answer is the glans of the penis with the deep inguinal nodes. The skin of the shaft drains to the superficial inguinal nodes instead.

To summarize, the thymus is a primary lymphoid organ of the anterior superior mediastinum, developed from the third pharyngeal pouch, lying behind the sternum and in front of the left brachiocephalic vein, the aortic arch and the pericardium, and reaching down to about the fourth costal cartilage. Each lobule has a cortex, where lymphocytes are selected positively and sealed off from antigen by the blood-thymus barrier, and a continuous medulla, where negative selection removes self-reactive cells and where the thymic corpuscles lie, and the gland reaches its greatest relative size around puberty before it is replaced by fat. It has efferent lymphatics only, and its lymphocytes arrive by blood. The lymphatic territories then carry lymph to two venous angles: the thoracic duct drains everything below the diaphragm and the left side of the head, neck, chest and upper limb, while the right lymphatic duct drains the right side of the head and neck, the right upper limb and the right side of the chest. That map explains the familiar patterns, the para-aortic nodes for the testis, the ovary and the kidney, the inguinal nodes for the skin below the umbilicus and the perineum, the axillary nodes for most of the breast, and the node at the end of the duct above the left clavicle.
