# Chapter 26 — Head and Face

This chapter works through the muscles that move the lower jaw and the muscles that move the eye, two small groups whose actions are easy to confuse by ear. It begins with the four classic muscles of mastication, the masseter, the temporalis and the two pterygoid muscles, along with the smaller muscles that help to open the mouth, and it then turns to the six muscles that move the eyeball and to the levator of the upper eyelid. It holds one hundred questions, and every question is answered immediately after it is asked, with the reason given in the same paragraph, so that nothing has to be held in mind until the end. The order runs from the muscles of mastication to the muscles of the orbit. This is educational material, and it supports rather than replaces an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 2501. Which muscle of mastication forms the thick pad over the side of the jaw, arises from the zygomatic arch, and stands out when the teeth are clenched? The answer is the masseter. It inserts on the outer surface of the ramus and on the angle of the mandible. Its fibers run downward and slightly backward, so they lift the lower jaw against the upper one.

Question 2502. Which muscle of mastication is a broad fan in the temple whose tendon passes deep to the zygomatic arch and grips the coronoid process of the mandible? The answer is the temporalis. Its anterior fibers lift the jaw and its posterior fibers pull it backward. The deep temporal nerves enter it from the floor of the temporal fossa.

Question 2503. Which muscle of mastication arises from the medial surface of the lateral pterygoid plate and inserts on the inner surface of the ramus of the mandible? The answer is the medial pterygoid. It faces the masseter across the ramus, and the two together form a sling that lifts the lower jaw. It also helps to pull the jaw forward and to grind from side to side.

Question 2504. Which muscle of mastication has two heads that arise from the greater wing of the sphenoid and from the outer surface of the lateral pterygoid plate? The answer is the lateral pterygoid. Its fibers run backward and slightly upward to the neck of the mandible and to the disc of the temporomandibular joint. It is the only muscle of the group that pulls the jaw forward.

Question 2505. Which nerve supplies the muscles of mastication: the facial nerve, the mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve, the maxillary division of the trigeminal nerve, or the hypoglossal nerve? The answer is the mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve. The facial nerve moves the muscles of facial expression, the maxillary division is sensory, and the hypoglossal nerve moves the tongue.

Question 2506. Which group of four muscles is taught as the muscles of mastication: the masseter, the temporalis and the two pterygoid muscles, the four suprahyoid muscles, the four muscles of the soft palate, or the muscles of facial expression? The answer is the masseter, the temporalis and the two pterygoid muscles. The suprahyoid muscles help to open the jaw, but they are not counted in that group.

Question 2507. Which duct crosses the outer surface of the masseter, turns inward through the cheek, and opens beside the upper second molar tooth? The answer is the parotid duct. It leaves the parotid gland in front of the ear and runs forward over the masseter. The facial artery also crosses the lower border of the mandible near the anterior edge of the same muscle.

Question 2508. When the two lateral pterygoid muscles contract together, which movement of the mandible do they produce: elevation, depression with protrusion, retraction, or tightening of the lips? The answer is depression with protrusion. They are the only muscles of the group that can pull the lower jaw forward. The digastric and the other suprahyoid muscles assist with the opening.

Question 2509. A constructed illustration: a fall onto the chin drives the head of the mandible backward, and the jaw deviates toward the injured side when the mouth opens. Which muscle of mastication is most likely to be involved? The answer is the lateral pterygoid. Its fibers insert on the neck of the mandible and on the joint disc, both of which are disturbed by the displaced condyle.

Question 2510. A described dissection of the cheek shows a flat muscle running from the zygomatic arch to the angle of the jaw, with a slender duct lying across it and a vessel crossing its lower border. Which muscle is shown? The answer is the masseter, crossed by the parotid duct. The vessel at its lower border is the facial artery.

Question 2511. Which muscle of mastication inserts on the coronoid process and the anterior border of the ramus of the mandible, and is reached only by passing deep to the zygomatic arch? The answer is the temporalis. Because its tendon lies behind the arch, the arch shelters it from a direct blow. Its posterior fibers are the main retractors of the lower jaw.

Question 2512. Which part of which muscle pulls the mandible backward after it has been pushed forward: the superficial fibers of the masseter, the posterior fibers of the temporalis, the medial pterygoid, or the lateral pterygoid? The answer is the posterior fibers of the temporalis. Their pull is almost horizontal, while the anterior fibers of the same muscle pull the jaw upward.

Question 2513. True or false: every muscle of mastication is supplied by the mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve. The answer is true. The masseter, the temporalis and both pterygoid muscles receive branches of that division. The muscles that open the mouth, such as the digastric, are supplied by other nerves and are counted separately.

Question 2514. Which two nerves descend between the medial pterygoid muscle and the ramus of the mandible, and how do they lie relative to each other? The answer is the lingual nerve and the inferior alveolar nerve, with the lingual nerve in front. Both are branches of the mandibular division. The inferior alveolar nerve then enters the mandibular foramen.

Question 2515. Which statement about the origin of the two pterygoid muscles is correct: the lateral pterygoid from the medial surface of the plate, the medial pterygoid from its lateral surface, the lateral pterygoid from that lateral surface, or both from the medial surface? The answer is that the lateral pterygoid arises from the lateral surface. The medial pterygoid takes the medial surface of the same plate.

Question 2516. Name two muscles that lift the mandible, spoken as a short list. The answer is the masseter and the temporalis. The medial pterygoid is a third elevator and works with the masseter as a sling around the ramus. All three close the mouth, while opening is produced mainly by the suprahyoid muscles and by gravity.

Question 2517. A described radiograph of the jaw shows a slender process of bone rising in front of the neck of the mandible and a fan of muscle shadow above it filling the temple. Which process and which muscle are shown? The answer is the coronoid process, with the temporalis attached to it. The condylar process lies behind it.

Question 2518. A constructed illustration: after a deep cut in the cheek, a person cannot push the lower jaw forward, though the mouth still opens and closes. Which muscle is most likely to be weakened? The answer is the lateral pterygoid. It is the only muscle that protrudes the mandible. Elevation and depression depend on the other muscles of the group and on the suprahyoid muscles.

Question 2519. Which muscle lies immediately deep to the ramus of the mandible and runs almost vertically downward and forward from the base of the skull to the angle of the jaw? The answer is the medial pterygoid. The masseter lies on the outer side of the same bone. Together they form a sling that suspends the angle of the mandible.

Question 2520. Which muscle of mastication lies immediately deep to the zygomatic arch and fills the temporal fossa? The answer is the temporalis. The arch crosses over its tendon, and the deep temporal vessels and nerves lie between the muscle and the bone of the fossa. Its fan shape lets different parts of it pull the jaw in different directions.

Question 2521. Which muscle of mastication is the most powerful elevator of the lower jaw and is wrapped in a strong fascia that gives it a firm purchase? The answer is the masseter. Its fibers arise from the zygomatic arch and insert on the ramus and the angle of the mandible. In a person who grinds the teeth, it becomes visibly thickened.

Question 2522. Which structure passes between the two pterygoid muscles as it runs from the infratemporal fossa toward the pterygopalatine fossa: the maxillary artery, the facial artery, the inferior alveolar nerve, or the parotid duct? The answer is the maxillary artery. It crosses the region deep to the lateral pterygoid and then passes between the two pterygoid muscles.

Question 2523. Which pair belongs together: the masseter with elevation of the mandible, the lateral pterygoid with retraction, the medial pterygoid with depression, or the temporalis with protrusion? The answer is the masseter with elevation of the mandible. The temporalis also elevates the jaw, while the lateral pterygoid protrudes it and the medial pterygoid helps to grind.

Question 2524. The mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve supplies the muscles of mastication. Which further loss belongs to a lesion of that division: drooping of the corner of the mouth, numbness of the lower lip and chin, weakness of the tongue, or loss of smell? The answer is numbness of the lower lip and chin. The other three belong to other nerves.

Question 2525. The movements of the mandible take place at the temporomandibular joint, where a disc divides the joint into two parts. In which plane does the sliding movement of protrusion occur, and about which axis does the hinge movement of elevation occur? The answer is the transverse plane for sliding forward, and a transverse axis for the hinge.

Question 2526. Which muscle of mastication is the only one whose fibers run upward and backward from the base of the skull to the neck of the mandible? The answer is the lateral pterygoid. All the other elevators run downward from the skull to the jaw. That upward direction explains why its contraction pulls the mandible forward and downward.

Question 2527. A constructed illustration: a person cannot open the mouth more than a finger's width, and the jaw deviates to one side. The trouble lies in front of the ear, where a small muscle holds the disc of the joint forward. Which muscle is implicated? The answer is the lateral pterygoid. Its upper head attaches to the disc and to the capsule.

Question 2528. A described cross section through the head at the level of the upper molar teeth shows a vertical plate of bone, a muscle on each side of it, and a duct opening into the cheek. Which two muscles grip the plate? The answer is the masseter outside the ramus and the medial pterygoid inside it, with the parotid duct opening beside the tooth.

Question 2529. Which of these muscles does not belong to the group called the muscles of mastication: the masseter, the temporalis, the digastric, or the medial pterygoid? The answer is the digastric. It lies above the hyoid bone and helps to open the mouth. It is an accessory muscle of opening rather than one of the four muscles of mastication.

Question 2530. Which structure lies immediately medial to the temporomandibular joint and separates the joint from the middle ear, and which ligament runs from the spine of the sphenoid to the lingula of the mandible? The answer is the tympanic plate of the temporal bone, and the sphenomandibular ligament. The ligament lies medial to the joint and behind the lateral pterygoid.

Question 2531. Which muscle of mastication arises partly from the greater wing of the sphenoid and partly from the lateral surface of the lateral pterygoid plate? The answer is the lateral pterygoid. The upper head is the smaller of the two. Both heads converge on the neck of the mandible and on the disc of the temporomandibular joint.

Question 2532. Which statement about the blood supply of the muscles of mastication is correct: all are supplied by the facial artery, all are supplied by branches of the maxillary artery, the masseter alone is supplied by the lingual artery, or the pterygoid muscles are supplied by the ophthalmic artery? The answer is that all are supplied by branches of the maxillary artery.

Question 2533. True or false: the lateral pterygoid muscle takes part both in opening the mouth and in moving the jaw from side to side. The answer is true. Its fibers pull the head of the mandible forward and downward on the side that contracts. Alternate contraction of the two muscles produces the side to side movement used in grinding.

Question 2534. A constructed illustration: after a blow to the side of the head, the mouth cannot be opened and the jaw deviates toward the injured side. Which muscle is most likely trapped by the fractured arch above it? The answer is the temporalis. Its tendon passes deep to the zygomatic arch on its way into the temple. A depressed fracture of the arch can block that tendon.

Question 2535. When the muscles of mastication on one side are paralyzed, the jaw deviates toward the paralyzed side as the mouth opens. Which muscle acting on the sound side explains that deviation: the masseter, the temporalis, the lateral pterygoid, or the medial pterygoid? The answer is the lateral pterygoid. It pushes the sound condyle forward, so the chin swings away from it.

Question 2536. Which muscle of mastication arises from the medial surface of a plate of the sphenoid rather than from the base of the skull or from the zygomatic arch? The answer is the medial pterygoid. Its main head comes from the medial surface of the lateral pterygoid plate, with a small slip from the palatine bone. It inserts on the inner surface of the ramus.

Question 2537. Which pair belongs together: the masseter with the temporal fossa, the temporalis with the zygomatic arch, the medial pterygoid with the inner surface of the ramus, or the lateral pterygoid with the coronoid process? The answer is the medial pterygoid with the inner surface of the ramus. The masseter and the lateral pterygoid take origin from the arch and the sphenoid respectively.

Question 2538. Which nerve lies immediately deep to the lateral pterygoid muscle as both descend toward the mandibular foramen: the lingual nerve, the inferior alveolar nerve, the buccal nerve, or the chorda tympani? The answer is the inferior alveolar nerve. It passes down behind the lateral pterygoid and enters the mandibular foramen. The lingual nerve lies in front of it, nearer the tongue.

Question 2539. A described specimen shows the mandible removed, with a fan-shaped muscle above it attached to a coronoid process and a muscle strap below and behind attached to the angle. Which muscles are shown, and which of them lies inside the ramus? The answer is the temporalis above, and the medial pterygoid inside the ramus, with the masseter outside.

Question 2540. Which muscle of mastication lies immediately lateral to the lateral pterygoid plate and immediately deep to the tendon of the temporalis? The answer is the lateral pterygoid. Its fibers run backward from the plate to the neck of the mandible. The maxillary artery lies deep to it before it passes between the two pterygoid muscles.

Question 2541. Which muscle of mastication is responsible for the visible fullness in front of the ear in a person who clenches the teeth hard? The answer is the masseter. Its upper fibers arise from the lower border of the zygomatic arch. The parotid gland lies behind it and over its posterior part.

Question 2542. Which movement of the mandible is produced by the medial pterygoid when it contracts together with the lateral pterygoid of the opposite side: straight elevation, side to side grinding, wide opening, or retraction? The answer is side to side grinding. The two muscles pull the jaw in opposite directions, so the mandible swings across.

Question 2543. A described section through the infratemporal fossa shows a muscle running downward and forward to the angle of the jaw and a nerve descending just behind its lower border. Which muscle and which nerve are shown? The answer is the medial pterygoid, with the inferior alveolar nerve descending behind it toward the mandibular foramen.

Question 2544. Which of these statements about the temporalis is correct: it arises from the infratemporal crest, it inserts on the neck of the mandible, it is the widest elevator of the jaw, or it is supplied by the facial nerve? The answer is that it is the widest elevator of the jaw. Its fan covers the whole temporal fossa, and its anterior fibers lift the mandible.

Question 2545. True or false: the masseter and the medial pterygoid approach the mandible from opposite sides and together form a sling. The answer is true. The masseter lies on the outer surface of the ramus and the medial pterygoid on the inner surface. Both pull the angle upward and slightly forward, so together they close the mouth.

Question 2546. Which muscle of mastication arises partly from the pyramidal process of the palatine bone in addition to the lateral pterygoid plate? The answer is the medial pterygoid. That small slip broadens its origin at the back of the palate. It inserts on the rough surface on the inner side of the angle of the mandible, facing the masseter across the bone.

Question 2547. A described dissection shows the mandible divided at the midline and reflected, revealing four muscles attached to it, two on the outer surface and two on the inner. Which muscle lies nearest the front on the inner surface? The answer is the temporalis, inserting on the coronoid process, with the medial pterygoid behind it near the angle. The masseter and the lateral pterygoid lie outside.

Question 2548. A constructed illustration: after an injury in front of the ear, a person can still clench the teeth, but food collects between the cheek and the gums on that side. Which nerve is most likely damaged, and why is the bite preserved? The answer is the facial nerve, which supplies the buccinator. The muscles of mastication take the trigeminal nerve, so the bite is spared.

Question 2549. A described section through the head behind the cheek shows a thick muscle strap running from the base of the skull downward and forward to the angle of the mandible, with a nerve lying between it and the ramus. Which muscle and which nerve are shown? The answer is the medial pterygoid, and the inferior alveolar nerve, which descends between it and the bone.

Question 2550. Which pair belongs together: the masseter with depression of the mandible, the lateral pterygoid with protrusion, the medial pterygoid with retraction, or the temporalis with outward movement of the jaw? The answer is the lateral pterygoid with protrusion. No other muscle of the group pulls the lower jaw forward. Retraction is the work of the posterior fibers of the temporalis.

Question 2551. Which muscle arises from the common tendinous ring at the apex of the orbit and runs straight forward to the medial side of the eyeball? The answer is the medial rectus. It turns the eye inward toward the nose. It is the strongest adductor among the six muscles that move the eyeball.

Question 2552. Which extraocular muscle runs straight forward from the tendinous ring to the lateral side of the eyeball and turns the eye outward? The answer is the lateral rectus. It is supplied by the abducens nerve, the sixth cranial nerve, which enters its inner surface. Its action is pure abduction, with no twisting of the eye.

Question 2553. Which muscle of the orbit arises from the frontal bone above and medial to the opening of the orbit, passes through a fibrous loop, and inserts on the upper surface of the eyeball behind its equator? The answer is the superior oblique. The loop it passes through is the trochlea. It is supplied by the trochlear nerve.

Question 2554. How many extraocular muscles move each eyeball: four, five, six, or seven? The answer is six. Four of them are rectus muscles and two are oblique muscles. The levator of the upper eyelid is closely associated with them but is usually counted separately from the six.

Question 2555. Which nerve supplies the lateral rectus muscle: the oculomotor nerve, the trochlear nerve, the abducens nerve, or the optic nerve? The answer is the abducens nerve, the sixth cranial nerve. The oculomotor nerve supplies four of the muscles that move the eye, and the trochlear nerve supplies the superior oblique.

Question 2556. A described dissection of the orbit from above shows a flat muscle running straight forward from the apex and a second muscle whose tendon turns through a loop on the medial wall before running backward and outward. Which two muscles are shown? The answer is the superior rectus and the superior oblique, whose tendon passes through the trochlea.

Question 2557. Which extraocular muscle lies immediately below the levator of the upper eyelid and runs forward just above the eyeball? The answer is the superior rectus. The two travel together from the tendinous ring, and the same nerve branch supplies both of them. The superior rectus is the muscle that lifts the eye when the eye looks outward.

Question 2558. Which extraocular muscle arises from the floor of the orbit just lateral to the groove for the tear sac and runs upward and outward over the eyeball? The answer is the inferior oblique. It is the only extraocular muscle that arises from the front of the orbit. It turns the eye upward and outward when the eye looks up.

Question 2559. Which muscle turns the eye downward when the eye is turned inward toward the nose: the inferior rectus, the superior oblique, the inferior oblique, or the lateral rectus? The answer is the superior oblique. Its tendon passes through the trochlea, so its pull is directed backward and outward, and it depresses the adducted eye.

Question 2560. A constructed illustration: a person sees double when looking to the right, and the right eye cannot turn outward. The right eye rests turned inward. Which muscle is weak, and which nerve supplies it? The answer is the right lateral rectus, supplied by the abducens nerve.

Question 2561. A described magnetic resonance imaging scan of the orbit in cross section shows a small muscle lying against the medial wall of the orbit and a larger one against its floor, with the optic nerve between them. Which muscles are shown? The answer is the medial rectus along the medial wall and the inferior rectus along the floor.

Question 2562. Which muscle of the orbit lifts the upper eyelid: the superior rectus, the levator of the upper eyelid, the superior oblique, or the orbicularis oculi? The answer is the levator of the upper eyelid. It arises above the tendinous ring and inserts into the skin of the lid and the upper tarsal plate. The orbicularis oculi closes the lid instead.

Question 2563. Name the two extraocular muscles that are not supplied by the oculomotor nerve, spoken as a list with their nerves. The answer is the lateral rectus, supplied by the abducens nerve, and the superior oblique, supplied by the trochlear nerve. All the other muscles of the orbit, including the levator of the upper eyelid, take the oculomotor nerve.

Question 2564. Which statement about the common tendinous ring is correct: it sits at the apex of the orbit, it surrounds the optic canal alone, it gives origin to all six extraocular muscles, or it lies at the front of the orbit? The answer is that it sits at the apex of the orbit. Four rectus muscles and the superior oblique arise from it, while the inferior oblique arises from the floor.

Question 2565. Which muscle lies immediately lateral to the optic nerve as it enters the back of the eye, and which muscle lies immediately above that nerve? The answer is the lateral rectus beside it and the superior rectus above it. The optic nerve enters the eye slightly medial to the center of the posterior pole.

Question 2566. Which muscle arises from the orbital floor beside the tear sac, passes outward and upward over the lower part of the eyeball, and runs almost at right angles to the other oblique muscle? The answer is the inferior oblique, which turns the eye upward and outward.

Question 2567. Why does the superior oblique muscle depress the eye when the eye is turned inward but not when the eye is turned outward? The answer is that its line of pull passes through the trochlea on the medial wall. The tendon then runs backward and outward, so the direction of its pull depends on where the eye is looking.

Question 2568. True or false: the four rectus muscles all arise from the common tendinous ring at the apex of the orbit. The answer is true. The superior oblique also arises there, close to the ring. The inferior oblique is the exception, because it arises from the floor of the orbit near its front.

Question 2569. A described cross section through the middle of the orbit, cut from side to side, shows four flat muscles arranged around the eyeball with the optic nerve in the middle and the levator of the lid above the topmost one. Which four muscles are these? The answer is the superior, medial, inferior and lateral rectus muscles.

Question 2570. Which extraocular muscle turns the eye straight inward: the medial rectus, the lateral rectus, the superior oblique, or the inferior oblique? The answer is the medial rectus. It runs straight from the tendinous ring to the inner side of the eyeball. The two oblique muscles twist the eye and lift or lower it rather than turning it inward.

Question 2571. Which nerve enters the orbit through the superior orbital fissure inside the common tendinous ring and then runs forward on the inner surface of the lateral rectus? The answer is the abducens nerve. It lies between the two heads of that muscle for part of its course. Its long path makes it the cranial nerve most often affected by raised pressure inside the skull.

Question 2572. A constructed illustration: a person has a drooping eyelid, the eye rests turned down and out, and the pupil is wide and does not narrow to light. Which nerve is affected, and which muscles explain the position of the eye? The answer is the oculomotor nerve, the third cranial nerve. The lateral rectus and the superior oblique are left unopposed.

Question 2573. Which statement about the origins of the oblique and rectus muscles is correct: the inferior oblique arises from the orbital floor and the medial rectus from the medial wall, the superior oblique arises from the orbital floor, both oblique muscles arise from the medial wall, or both rectus muscles arise from the orbital floor? The answer is that the inferior oblique arises from the floor and the medial rectus from the medial wall.

Question 2574. Which pair belongs together: the lateral rectus with the trochlear nerve, the superior oblique with the abducens nerve, the medial rectus with the oculomotor nerve, or the levator of the lid with the facial nerve? The answer is the medial rectus with the oculomotor nerve. The lateral rectus takes the abducens nerve and the superior oblique takes the trochlear nerve.

Question 2575. Which extraocular muscle runs from the tendinous ring straight forward to the top of the eyeball, below the levator of the upper eyelid? The answer is the superior rectus. It turns the eye upward, and because its line of pull is slightly oblique it also turns the eye inward and twists its upper pole toward the nose.

Question 2576. Which movement does the inferior rectus produce when the eye is turned outward: elevation, depression, inward twisting, or outward twisting? The answer is depression. When the eye is turned outward, the line of pull of the inferior rectus lies in the vertical plane, so its main action is to lower the eye. The superior oblique is the inward twister.

Question 2577. A described dissection of the orbit from the lateral side shows a muscle running forward above the eyeball and, below it, a tendon that turns through a loop on the medial wall before fanning out over the top and back of the globe. Which two muscles are shown? The answer is the superior rectus above and the superior oblique, whose tendon passes through the trochlea.

Question 2578. Which structures pass through the common tendinous ring in addition to the extraocular muscles that arise from it? The answer is the optic nerve, the ophthalmic artery, and the nasociliary nerve, with the abducens nerve entering through the ring as well. The frontal, lacrimal and trochlear nerves lie outside the ring.

Question 2579. Which statement about the insertion of the rectus muscles is correct: they insert into the sclera in front of the junction with the cornea, they insert into the sclera a short distance behind that junction, they insert into the cornea itself, or they insert into the sheath of the optic nerve? The answer is that they insert into the sclera a short distance behind the junction with the cornea.

Question 2580. Which extraocular muscle inserts on the upper surface of the eyeball behind the equator, after its tendon has passed through a pulley on the medial wall of the orbit? The answer is the superior oblique. Its tendon approaches the eye from the medial side and turns backward and outward to reach the sclera.

Question 2581. A constructed illustration: when a person looks to the right, the left eye moves inward poorly, while the right eye turns outward with a fine flickering movement. The fault lies not in the muscles but in the bundle that links the two nerve nuclei in the brainstem. Which muscle is weak, and on which side? The answer is the left medial rectus.

Question 2582. Which muscle is the only one of the six that arises from the front of the orbit rather than from the apex or from the upper medial wall: the superior oblique, the inferior oblique, the medial rectus, or the superior rectus? The answer is the inferior oblique. It springs from the orbital floor just lateral to the groove for the tear sac and passes upward and outward.

Question 2583. A described computed tomography scan through the orbit shows a thick band of muscle lying against the medial wall and a second band against the lateral wall, with the eyeball between them. Which muscles are shown? The answer is the medial rectus against the nasal wall and the lateral rectus against the outer wall.

Question 2584. Which pair of muscles shares the same nerve supply because they arise close together and act together: the superior rectus and the levator of the upper eyelid, the medial rectus and the lateral rectus, the two oblique muscles, or the inferior rectus and the superior oblique? The answer is the superior rectus and the levator of the upper eyelid.

Question 2585. Name the two muscles that lift the eyeball and the two that lower it, spoken as a short list. The answer is the superior rectus and the inferior oblique for lifting, and the inferior rectus and the superior oblique for lowering. The two oblique muscles work best when the eye is turned inward.

Question 2586. A thin sheet of smooth muscle lies just deep to the levator of the upper eyelid and helps to hold the lid raised. Is it supplied by the same nerve as the levator, and why does that matter? The answer is no. The levator takes the oculomotor nerve, while the smooth sheet receives sympathetic fibers, so the two can fail separately.

Question 2587. Which muscle of the orbit has a tendon that becomes broad and flat after it passes through a pulley, so that its pull is spread over the upper and lateral surfaces of the eyeball? The answer is the superior oblique. The broad tendon lets it twist and lower the eye.

Question 2588. Which statement about the action of the superior rectus is correct: it elevates the eye in every position of gaze, it elevates the eye best when the eye is turned outward, it depresses the eye when the eye is turned inward, or it turns the eye outward? The answer is that it elevates the eye best when the eye is turned outward.

Question 2589. True or false: the inferior oblique muscle turns the eye upward only when the eye is turned inward. The answer is true in practice. Its line of pull is oblique, so its lifting action is greatest when the eye is adducted toward the nose. It twists the upper pole away from the nose in every position of gaze.

Question 2590. A described MRI scan of the orbit taken from the front shows the optic nerve in the center, four muscles arranged around it like the spokes of a wheel, and a fifth muscle cut obliquely in the upper medial corner. Which muscle is the fifth one? The answer is the superior oblique, whose tendon turns through the trochlea in the upper medial part of the orbit.

Question 2591. A person cannot turn the eye downward and inward, though the eye moves well in every other direction. Which muscle is weak, and why is it the muscle that fails in that movement? The answer is the superior oblique. It is the main depressor of the adducted eye, because its tendon turns through the trochlea and pulls the eye backward and outward.

Question 2592. A constructed illustration: after a blow to the upper medial corner of the orbit, the eye cannot be turned downward when it looks toward the nose. Which muscle is injured, and through which structure does its tendon pass at that site? The answer is the superior oblique, whose tendon passes through the trochlea on the frontal bone.

Question 2593. Which pair belongs together: the inferior oblique with lifting the adducted eye, the superior oblique with lifting the adducted eye, the inferior rectus with lifting the abducted eye, or the medial rectus with twisting the eye? The answer is the inferior oblique with lifting the adducted eye. The superior oblique lowers the adducted eye, and the inferior rectus lowers the abducted eye.

Question 2594. Which muscle turns the upper pole of the eye away from the nose, that is, twists the eye outward: the superior oblique, the inferior oblique, the superior rectus, or the medial rectus? The answer is the inferior oblique. The inferior rectus also twists the eye outward, while the superior oblique and the superior rectus twist it the other way.

Question 2595. Which extraocular muscle is supplied by the trochlear nerve, the fourth cranial nerve? The answer is the superior oblique. It is the only muscle in the body supplied by that nerve. The nerve enters the orbit above the tendinous ring and runs forward on the upper surface of the muscle.

Question 2596. Why does the superior rectus turn the eye inward as well as upward, while the medial rectus turns it inward alone? The answer is that the orbit runs forward and outward, so the line of pull of the superior rectus lies at an angle to the axis of the eye. The medial rectus lies in the horizontal plane.

Question 2597. A described dissection of the orbit from above shows the levator of the upper eyelid lifted away, revealing a muscle running forward to the top of the globe and a nerve entering its lower surface from the medial side. Which muscle and which nerve are shown? The answer is the superior rectus, supplied by the superior division of the oculomotor nerve.

Question 2598. Which single extraocular muscle both twists the eye and lifts it when the eye is turned inward: the superior rectus, the inferior rectus, the superior oblique, or the inferior oblique? The answer is the inferior oblique. Its oblique line of pull makes it a lifter and an outward twister of the adducted eye, while the superior oblique lowers the adducted eye.

Question 2599. A constructed illustration: a person with a weak superior oblique on one side tilts the head toward the opposite shoulder to avoid double vision. Which action of the weak muscle does that posture spare, and why does it relieve the double vision? The answer is the twisting action that keeps the upper pole of the eye turned toward the nose. Tilting the head the other way would call on the weak muscle.

Question 2600. A described MRI scan of the orbits taken from the front shows one eyeball resting turned slightly outward while the other looks straight ahead, and the muscle along the lateral wall of the affected orbit appears thin. Which muscle is at fault, and which nerve supplies it? The answer is the lateral rectus, supplied by the abducens nerve.

To summarize, the muscles of mastication are the masseter, the temporalis and the two pterygoid muscles, all supplied by the mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve, and they lift, protrude and grind the lower jaw. The masseter lies outside the ramus of the mandible and the medial pterygoid inside it, so they form a sling, while the lateral pterygoid alone pulls the jaw forward and the posterior fibers of the temporalis pull it back. The six muscles that move the eyeball are the four rectus muscles and the two oblique muscles, all supplied by the oculomotor nerve except the lateral rectus, which takes the abducens nerve, and the superior oblique, which takes the trochlear nerve. The oblique muscles act most strongly when the eye is turned inward, and the rectus muscles act best when the eye looks along their line of pull. Naming the attachment, the nerve and the direction of pull together is what fixes each muscle in memory.
