# Chapter 16 — Pelvic Girdle

This chapter covers the pelvic girdle and the two bones of the lower limb that meet it, the femur and the patella. It begins with the acetabulum, the socket that receives the head of the femur, and then takes the obturator foramen below it and the sacroiliac region behind, where the spine joins the hip bone. From there it turns to the femur, from its head and neck to its condyles, and it ends at the patella, the sesamoid bone that rides in front of the knee. It holds one hundred questions, and each one gives its answer immediately after the question and then explains why that answer is right, so nothing has to be held in mind until the end. All measurements and relations are the ordinary textbook ones, and variation between healthy people is normal. This is educational material, and it supports rather than replaces an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 1501. Which three bones of the hip bone meet to form the acetabulum, the socket that receives the head of the femur? The answer is the ilium, the ischium and the pubis. In the child the three are separated by a Y-shaped cartilage that meets in the floor of the socket. In the adult they are fused into a single hip bone.

Question 1502. Of the three bones that meet in the acetabulum, which contributes the smallest part: the ilium, the ischium or the pubis? The answer is the pubis. The ilium forms the upper part of the socket and the ischium the lower and posterior part, each contributing about two fifths. The pubis adds only the front fifth, so its share is the smallest.

Question 1503. Which large opening lies immediately anteroinferior to the acetabulum in the hip bone? The answer is the obturator foramen. It lies below and in front of the socket and is closed by a membrane except at its upper margin. Because the two structures are neighbors, a break in the pubis or the ischium often reaches the rim of the socket.

Question 1504. A described dried hip bone shows a cup-shaped socket whose smooth articular surface is shaped like a horseshoe, open at its lower margin. Name the socket and the shape of that surface. The answer is the acetabulum, with its lunate surface. The horseshoe curves around a central depression that carries no cartilage, and its two ends are joined below by a ligament.

Question 1505. Which part of the acetabulum carries no articular cartilage: the lunate surface, the fossa, the rim or the transverse ligament? The answer is the fossa. It is the central depression of the socket, and it holds fat and the ligament of the head of the femur. The lunate surface around it is the part that bears the load.

Question 1506. What is the name of the fibrocartilaginous rim attached to the margin of the acetabulum, which deepens the socket? The answer is the acetabular labrum. It bridges the notch at the lower margin and grips the head of the femur around its equator. A tear of this rim can let the head shift within the socket.

Question 1507. A constructed illustration: a person is struck on the front of a bent knee, and the head of the femur is driven inward against the socket. Which part of the hip bone gives way? The answer is the quadrilateral plate, the flat sheet of bone that forms the medial wall of the acetabulum. A break that reaches it is described as a fracture of the anterior column.

Question 1508. The ligament of the head of the femur carries a small artery into the socket. From which artery does that vessel usually arise? The answer is the obturator artery. It sends a branch through the acetabular notch and along the ligament. In the adult it feeds only a small part of the head.

Question 1509. Which direction does the open face of the acetabulum point: straight laterally, anterolaterally and slightly inferiorly, posterosuperiorly or straight medially? The answer is anterolaterally and slightly inferiorly. The socket looks forward and outward as well as a little downward, which lets the femur swing forward and out to the side.

Question 1510. True or false: in the child the ilium, the ischium and the pubis are separated within the acetabulum by a Y-shaped cartilage. The answer is true. The three bones meet in the floor of the socket at the triradiate cartilage. It closes around puberty, after which the socket is a single piece of bone.

Question 1511. The sockets of the two hip bones face away from each other. Describe the direction of each opening and say how the two directions compare. The answer is that each opens laterally, forward and slightly downward, so the two openings point in opposite directions. The plane of one socket is the mirror image of the other.

Question 1512. A described computed tomography section through the pelvis shows a dense curved line of bone roofing the socket, and its slope is measured. Which part of the acetabulum is being assessed? The answer is the subchondral roof of the socket, often called the sourcil. Its slope shows how well the socket covers the head. A steep, short roof is the mark of a shallow socket.

Question 1513. Which smooth ridge runs forward along the inner surface of the ilium from the sacroiliac joint to the pubic tubercle? The answer is the arcuate line, which continues across the hip bone as the iliopectineal line. It marks the pelvic brim and divides the wide false pelvis above from the narrow true pelvis below. The pelvic organs lie beneath it.

Question 1514. A constructed illustration: a described socket fracture divides the bone into a front part running from the iliac crest to the pubis and a back part running from the greater sciatic notch to the ischial tuberosity. What are these two parts called? The answer is the anterior column and the posterior column. Each carries one wall of the socket, so a fracture of both leaves the socket free to displace.

Question 1515. In a child, which artery provides most of the blood reaching the growing head of the femur: the obturator artery, the medial circumflex femoral artery, the lateral circumflex femoral artery or the inferior gluteal artery? The answer is the medial circumflex femoral artery. Its branches climb the neck inside the capsule and enter the head at the growth plate.

Question 1516. Which spine of the ilium lies just above the acetabulum and gives origin to the straight head of the rectus femoris: the anterior superior iliac spine, the anterior inferior iliac spine, the posterior superior iliac spine or the ischial spine? The answer is the anterior inferior iliac spine. It sits on the front of the ilium immediately above the socket.

Question 1517. A described specimen of a hip bone shows a broad flat blade above and a deep cup below, with a large opening in front of the cup. Name the cup. The answer is the acetabulum. It is the socket that receives the head of the femur, and the opening in front of it is the obturator foramen.

Question 1518. What band bridges the gap at the lower margin of the acetabulum and completes the rim of the socket? The answer is the transverse acetabular ligament. It spans the notch and turns it into a small opening. Vessels and a small nerve branch pass through it into the socket.

Question 1519. A constructed illustration: a described radiograph of an infant hip shows a socket that is shallow and steeply roofed, with the head sitting high and to the outer side. Which structure has failed to deepen the socket? The answer is the acetabular labrum. When the socket does not grow around the head, the head drifts upward and the labrum may be folded into the joint.

Question 1520. Which statement about the artery of the ligament of the head of the femur is correct: it is the main supply, it comes from the obturator artery, it comes from the sciatic nerve or it comes from the femoral artery? The answer is that it comes from the obturator artery. It feeds only a small part of the head, because the vessels that climb the neck carry most of the blood.

Question 1521. Which opening in the skeleton is the largest of all, lying below and in front of the acetabulum? The answer is the obturator foramen. It is bounded by the pubis above and in front and by the ischium below and behind, and it is closed by a strong membrane. Only a small canal at its upper margin stays open.

Question 1522. Which two bones form the obturator foramen: the ilium and the ischium, the pubis and the ischium, the ilium and the pubis, or the sacrum and the ischium? The answer is the pubis and the ischium. The two rami of the pubis bound it in front and above, and the ramus of the ischium bounds it behind and below. The ilium takes no part in it.

Question 1523. True or false: the obturator canal lies at the upper margin of the obturator foramen and transmits the obturator nerve and vessels. The answer is true. The membrane closes the rest of the foramen, and only this small gap is left. Through it pass the nerve, the artery and the vein that share the name of the foramen.

Question 1524. Which muscle arises from the pelvic surface of the obturator membrane and the bone around it? The answer is the obturator internus. It covers the inner surface of the membrane and leaves the pelvis through the lesser sciatic foramen. Its tendon turns a right angle around the ischial spine before it reaches the femur.

Question 1525. Which muscle arises from the outer surface of the obturator membrane: the obturator internus, the obturator externus, the piriformis or the quadratus femoris? The answer is the obturator externus. It covers the outer surface of the membrane and the bone around it. Its tendon runs up behind the neck of the femur and ends in the trochanteric fossa.

Question 1526. True or false: the obturator membrane closes the obturator foramen completely, leaving no gap. The answer is false. The membrane leaves a small opening at the upper margin, the obturator canal. The obturator nerve and vessels pass through that canal, and nothing passes through the membrane itself.

Question 1527. A constructed illustration: a slender and older person describes pain running down the inner side of the thigh, and a loop of bowel has slipped through a narrow gap at the front of the pelvis. Which gap is it? The answer is the obturator canal. The hernia presses on the obturator nerve, which explains the pain down the inner thigh.

Question 1528. A described dried pelvis shows an oval gap below and in front of each socket. In which sex is that gap smaller and more triangular in shape? The answer is the female. The female obturator foramen tends to be smaller and more triangular, while in the male it is larger and more oval. This is one of several differences used to tell a male skeleton from a female one.

Question 1529. The obturator nerve enters the thigh through the obturator canal. How do its two branches lie in relation to the adductor brevis? The answer is that one branch lies in front of the muscle and the other behind it. The anterior branch supplies the adductor longus and the gracilis, while the posterior branch supplies the adductor magnus.

Question 1530. The tendon of which muscle passes through the lesser sciatic foramen: the obturator internus, the obturator externus, the piriformis or the gluteus medius? The answer is the obturator internus. It leaves the pelvis through that foramen, bounded above by the sacrospinous ligament and below by the sacrotuberous ligament.

Question 1531. A constructed illustration: at a described operation at the groin, an artery is found crossing the lacunar ligament from above. Which artery is it? The answer is an abnormal obturator artery. It has arisen from the inferior epigastric artery instead of the internal iliac and runs where the lacunar ligament is divided.

Question 1532. Name, spoken as a sentence, two structures that pass through the obturator canal. The answer is the obturator nerve and the obturator artery. The obturator vein runs with them. All three leave the pelvis at the upper margin of the obturator foramen. That canal is the only part of the foramen that stays open.

Question 1533. Which groove runs obliquely across the inferior surface of the superior pubic ramus and forms the roof of the obturator canal? The answer is the obturator groove. The canal lies between this groove and the upper edge of the obturator membrane, and its floor is the membrane itself. The nerve and vessels run along it as they leave the pelvis.

Question 1534. Relative to the acetabulum, in which direction does the obturator foramen lie within the hip bone? The answer is anteroinferior to the socket, so it sits below and in front of it. The socket occupies the middle of the outer surface of the bone, and the foramen opens onto the lower part of the same surface.

Question 1535. Which structure does not pass through the obturator canal: the obturator nerve, the obturator artery, the obturator vein or the sciatic nerve? The answer is the sciatic nerve. It leaves the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen instead. The canal is too narrow and too far forward to carry a nerve of that size.

Question 1536. Which joint joins the ear-shaped auricular surface of the ilium to the ear-shaped surface of the sacrum? The answer is the sacroiliac joint. The two surfaces are covered with cartilage and interlock, and strong ligaments bind them together behind. Part of the joint is synovial and part is a fibrous union.

Question 1537. Which ligament is the strongest of those binding the sacroiliac joint: the anterior sacroiliac, the interosseous sacroiliac, the sacrospinous or the iliolumbar? The answer is the interosseous sacroiliac ligament. It fills the deep space between the iliac tuberosity and the sacrum and is the main restraint against movement. The anterior ligament is a thin sheet by comparison.

Question 1538. Which two ligaments convert the greater and lesser sciatic notches of the hip bone into the greater and lesser sciatic foramina? The answer is the sacrotuberous ligament and the sacrospinous ligament. The sacrospinous crosses the lesser notch and the sacrotuberous the greater. Together they close the open notches into two firm passages.

Question 1539. A described computed tomography scan of the pelvis shows both sacroiliac joints with irregular margins, erosions and bridges of new bone, and the two sides look alike. Which joints are shown, and what does the pattern suggest? The answer is the sacroiliac joints, in a pattern that suggests an inflammatory arthritis of the spine rather than simple wear. The changes begin at the lower end of the joint and spread upward.

Question 1540. Which structure leaves the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen below the piriformis muscle: the superior gluteal nerve, the sciatic nerve, the obturator nerve or the pudendal artery? The answer is the sciatic nerve. It is the largest nerve in the body and descends behind the hip joint into the thigh. The superior gluteal nerve leaves above the piriformis.

Question 1541. Which ligament runs from the transverse process of the fifth lumbar vertebra to the back of the iliac crest? The answer is the iliolumbar ligament. It anchors the lowest mobile vertebra to the hip bone and helps to steady the lumbosacral junction. A second band sometimes runs from the fourth lumbar vertebra, which is a normal variant.

Question 1542. When the sacrum tilts forward so that its promontory moves downward and forward, what is the movement called: nutation, counternutation, inversion or abduction? The answer is nutation. It narrows the pelvic inlet slightly and widens the outlet, and it is checked mainly by the sacrotuberous ligament. The opposite movement, counternutation, tilts the promontory backward.

Question 1543. The pudendal nerve leaves the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen and then returns through the lesser sciatic foramen. Around which structure does it hook as it turns back? The answer is the sacrospinous ligament. The nerve and the internal pudendal vessels pass between the sacrospinous and sacrotuberous ligaments. The turn carries them to the canal beside the ischial spine.

Question 1544. A described dried sacrum shows a gap at its lower end where the bony roof is missing, with two small horns on either side of the gap. What is the gap called, and what do the horns mark? The answer is the sacral hiatus, and the horns are the sacral cornua. The gap is where the laminae of the fifth sacral vertebra failed to fuse.

Question 1545. A constructed illustration: a young adult describes pain in the buttock and lower back that is worst in the early morning, and a described radiograph shows both sacroiliac joints narrowed and irregular. Which joints are affected? The answer is the sacroiliac joints, and the changes usually begin at the lower end and spread upward. The pattern belongs to an inflammatory arthritis, and its management belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 1546. True or false: the sacroiliac joint allows only a slight gliding movement, restrained by very strong posterior ligaments. The answer is true. The interlocking surfaces and the interosseous ligament limit movement to a few degrees. What movement there is helps the pelvis to absorb the weight of the trunk above it.

Question 1547. Which structures bound the greater sciatic foramen: the greater sciatic notch and the sacrotuberous ligament, the lesser sciatic notch and the sacrospinous ligament, the obturator membrane and the ischial spine, or the pubic ramus and the inguinal ligament? The answer is the greater sciatic notch and the sacrotuberous ligament. The sacrospinous ligament divides the space into the greater foramen above and the lesser below.

Question 1548. Which groove on the hip bone lies in front of the auricular surface and is deeper and more common in the female skeleton? The answer is the preauricular sulcus. It lies between the auricular surface and the greater sciatic notch. Because it is more marked in women, it is one of the features used when the sex of a skeleton is estimated.

Question 1549. When a person stands on one leg, which structures resist the tendency of the ilium to rotate and of the sacrum to tilt forward: the anterior sacroiliac ligament alone, the interosseous and posterior sacroiliac ligaments, the inguinal ligament, or the obturator membrane? The answer is the interosseous and posterior sacroiliac ligaments, helped by the sacrotuberous and sacrospinous ligaments.

Question 1550. A described skeleton shows a triangular bone wedged between the two hip bones, with its broad base above and its tip below. Name the bone and say what joins its base. The answer is the sacrum, whose base sits above and joins the fifth lumbar vertebra. Its tip points downward to the coccyx, and its ear-shaped surfaces form the sacroiliac joints.

Question 1551. Which is the longest and strongest bone in the body, running from the hip to the knee? The answer is the femur. It carries the weight of the trunk and passes it on to the leg below. Its strength comes from its thick shaft and from the way its bony plates are arranged to resist the lines of force.

Question 1552. What is the small depression on the head of the femur called, and what occupies it: the fovea capitis, the trochanteric fossa, the intercondylar fossa or the linea aspera? The answer is the fovea capitis, which holds the ligament of the head of the femur. It lies just below and behind the center of the head, and the artery of the ligament runs through it.

Question 1553. Which statement about the angle between the neck and the shaft of the adult femur is correct: it measures about 125 degrees, about 90 degrees, about 60 degrees, or about 30 degrees? The answer is about 125 degrees. The angle is larger in the newborn and decreases as weight bearing begins. A value well below the usual one is called coxa vara.

Question 1554. A described specimen shows a long bone with a rounded head on a narrow neck, two prominences at its upper end, and a shaft that curves gently forward. Name the bone. The answer is the femur. The two prominences are the greater and the lesser trochanters. The head fits into the socket of the hip bone.

Question 1555. Which ridge runs across the front of the upper femur between the two trochanters? The answer is the intertrochanteric line. It marks the anterior boundary between the neck and the shaft and gives attachment to the iliofemoral ligament. Behind, the two trochanters are joined instead by the intertrochanteric crest.

Question 1556. Which structure lies on the posterior surface of the upper femur between the trochanters: the intertrochanteric line, the intertrochanteric crest, the spiral line or the gluteal tuberosity? The answer is the intertrochanteric crest. It carries a rounded bump called the quadrate tubercle, where the obturator externus inserts. The line lies on the front of the bone.

Question 1557. A constructed illustration: an older adult falls and breaks the neck of the femur within the capsule. Which artery must be torn for the head to lose its blood supply? The answer is the medial circumflex femoral artery. Its branches climb the neck inside the capsule, so a break within the capsule can divide them and starve the head.

Question 1558. On which surface of the upper femur does the lesser trochanter lie, and which muscle inserts into it? The answer is the posteromedial surface, and the muscle is the iliopsoas. Its tendon crosses the front of the hip joint and turns backward to reach the trochanter. The greater trochanter, by contrast, lies on the lateral side.

Question 1559. Which of these pairs belongs together: the greater trochanter with the gluteus medius, the lesser trochanter with the gluteus minimus, the linea aspera with the iliopsoas, or the adductor tubercle with the piriformis? The answer is the greater trochanter with the gluteus medius. The lesser trochanter receives the iliopsoas, the linea aspera the adductor group, and the adductor tubercle the adductor magnus.

Question 1560. Above the linea aspera, the posterior ridge of the femur splits into which lines: the spiral line and the gluteal tuberosity, the intertrochanteric line and crest, the arcuate line and the pectineal line, or the supracondylar lines? The answer is the spiral line and the gluteal tuberosity. Below, the ridge splits again into the two supracondylar lines.

Question 1561. Which vessel enters the shaft of the femur through a small canal on its posterior surface near the linea aspera? The answer is the nutrient artery of the femur. It usually arises from a perforating branch of the deep artery of the thigh. It travels within the medullary cavity toward the upper end of the bone.

Question 1562. A described radiograph of the hip shows that the smooth arc running along the lower border of the femoral neck no longer continues into the upper border of the obturator foramen. What is that arc called, and what does its break mean? The answer is the line of Shenton, and its break marks a displaced fracture of the neck or a dislocation of the hip. The upper end has moved upward.

Question 1563. Which of these pairs belongs together: the fovea capitis with the ligament of the head of the femur, the trochanteric fossa with the gluteus medius, the intertrochanteric line with the posterior capsule, or the adductor tubercle with the popliteus? The answer is the fovea capitis with the ligament of the head. The trochanteric fossa receives the obturator externus, and the adductor tubercle receives the adductor magnus.

Question 1564. At what level on the surface of the body does the tip of the greater trochanter lie? The answer is at the level of the pubic tubercle, about one hand's breadth below the iliac crest. It can be felt on the outer side of the hip when the thigh is rotated. In a displaced hip fracture the trochanter rises above its usual level.

Question 1565. Which muscle inserts into the trochanteric fossa on the medial side of the greater trochanter: the obturator internus, the obturator externus, the piriformis or the gluteus minimus? The answer is the obturator externus. Its tendon runs upward and backward behind the neck of the femur to reach the fossa. The other three end on the medial surface of the greater trochanter.

Question 1566. Which smooth triangular area lies on the back of the lower femur between the two supracondylar lines? The answer is the popliteal surface. The popliteal vessels rest against it as they cross the back of the knee. Its upper part is continuous with the linea aspera above.

Question 1567. Which of the two femoral condyles is longer and reaches farther downward: the lateral, the medial, neither, or are they equal? The answer is the medial condyle. It extends farther distally because the shaft of the femur slopes inward, and this keeps the joint line of the knee nearly horizontal when a person stands upright.

Question 1568. A described radiograph of an older adult shows a break across the neck of the femur, and the break lies within the capsule. Why is this break more likely to endanger the head than one farther out? The answer is that the vessels feeding the head run inside the capsule along the neck. A break within the capsule can tear them.

Question 1569. In a described adolescent hip, the growth plate of the femur has slipped so that the neck turns outward while the head stays in the socket. Which part has remained in place: the head, the neck, the greater trochanter or the shaft? The answer is the head, which stays in the acetabulum while the neck and shaft rotate outward. Weakness of the growth plate during rapid growth explains the slip.

Question 1570. A described coronal section of the upper femur shows a thick plate of bone running up the medial side of the neck toward the head. What is this plate called, and what does it do? The answer is the calcar femorale, a strong buttress that supports the medial part of the neck and helps to carry the weight of the body onto the head.

Question 1571. Which opening in the tendon of the adductor magnus transmits the femoral artery and vein from the thigh to the back of the knee? The answer is the adductor hiatus. Once the vessels have passed through it they are called the popliteal artery and vein. The tendon forms the opening just above the adductor tubercle.

Question 1572. Which prominence of the upper femur can be felt with the fingers on the outer side of the hip: the greater trochanter, the lesser trochanter, the adductor tubercle or the ischial tuberosity? The answer is the greater trochanter. It lies just under the skin on the lateral side of the hip. The lesser trochanter lies deep on the back of the bone and cannot be felt.

Question 1573. At birth the head and neck of the femur are turned forward more than they are in the adult. What is this twist called, and what happens to it with growth? The answer is torsion, and it decreases as the child walks and the limb bears weight. An excess of it left in the adult turns the toes inward.

Question 1574. A described radiograph of the shaft of the femur shows a thin dark line running obliquely through the cortex toward the upper end of the bone, with smooth edges on both sides. Is this a fracture? The answer is that it is a nutrient canal, not a break. The canal runs away from the knee and has smooth, dense margins. A fracture line has jagged edges instead.

Question 1575. Which of these pairs belongs together: the patellar surface of the femur with the patella, the intercondylar fossa with the patellar ligament, the medial epicondyle with the lateral collateral ligament, or the lateral condyle with the adductor tubercle? The answer is the patellar surface with the patella. The intercondylar fossa holds the cruciate ligaments, and the adductor tubercle sits on the medial epicondyle.

Question 1576. Which muscle tendon lies immediately in front of the hip joint and separates the joint from the femoral vessels? The answer is the iliopsoas tendon. It crosses the front of the capsule on its way to the lesser trochanter. A bursa lies between the tendon and the joint, and it is the largest bursa in the body.

Question 1577. Which surface of the lower femur articulates with the patella: the popliteal surface, the patellar surface, the intercondylar fossa or the linea aspera? The answer is the patellar surface. It lies on the front of the lower end between the two condyles. Its lateral part is broader and reaches higher than its medial part.

Question 1578. Which roughened ridge on the posterior surface of the femur, below and behind the greater trochanter, receives the insertion of the gluteus maximus? The answer is the gluteal tuberosity. It forms the upper end of the lateral lip of the linea aspera. When it is very prominent it makes a third trochanter, which is a normal variant.

Question 1579. Which statement about the intercondylar fossa of the femur is correct: it gives attachment to the cruciate ligaments, it receives the patella, it gives attachment to the adductor magnus, or it lodges the popliteal artery? The answer is that it gives attachment to the cruciate ligaments. The anterior cruciate ligament attaches to the medial surface of the lateral condyle.

Question 1580. What is the name of the small rounded prominence on the medial epicondyle of the femur, where the adductor magnus is inserted? The answer is the adductor tubercle. It caps the upper part of the medial epicondyle and can be felt just above the knee on the inner side. The medial collateral ligament attaches below it.

Question 1581. Which is the largest sesamoid bone in the body, lying in the tendon of the quadriceps femoris? The answer is the patella. It is a triangular bone that develops within the tendon in front of the knee. Because it lies inside the tendon, it has no periosteum of its own.

Question 1582. Within the tendon of which muscle does the patella develop: the quadriceps femoris, the biceps femoris, the gastrocnemius or the sartorius? The answer is the quadriceps femoris. The patella is a sesamoid bone formed inside that tendon. The tendon continues below the bone as the patellar ligament.

Question 1583. Which structure attaches to the apex of the patella, and which attaches to its base? The answer is that the patellar ligament attaches to the apex and the quadriceps tendon to the base. The ligament joins the apex to the tibial tuberosity, so the pull of the quadriceps passes through the bone to the shin. The base is the broader upper border.

Question 1584. Which bone is small and triangular, wider above than below, and carries the thickest articular cartilage in the body: the patella, the pisiform, the calcaneus or the talus? The answer is the patella. Its smooth back faces the femur and its rough front lies just under the skin, and the whole bone sits inside the quadriceps tendon.

Question 1585. Which facet of the patella lies along its medial border and touches the medial femoral condyle only when the knee is deeply bent? The answer is the odd facet. It is a narrow strip on the extreme medial edge of the articular surface. In full extension it does not touch the femur at all.

Question 1586. Which of the two main facets of the patella is the larger: the medial, the lateral, neither, or are they equal? The answer is the lateral facet. It is broader and more concave than the medial one and bears most of the load against the lateral femoral condyle. The medial facet is smaller and flatter.

Question 1587. A constructed illustration: a young person turns sharply on a bent knee, and the patella is described as having moved to the outer side of the joint. Which ligament is the main restraint that has been overcome? The answer is the medial patellofemoral ligament, which runs from the medial epicondyle of the femur to the medial border of the patella.

Question 1588. True or false: the articular cartilage of the patella is thicker than the cartilage anywhere else in the body. The answer is true. The cartilage on the back of the patella is the thickest in the body, which is why it withstands the great pressures of the kneecap against the femur. Its thickness also helps to spread that force.

Question 1589. Which of these pairs belongs together: the medial patellofemoral ligament with the medial epicondyle of the femur, the patellar ligament with the base of the patella, the quadriceps tendon with the apex of the patella, or the lateral retinaculum with the medial border? The answer is the medial patellofemoral ligament with the medial epicondyle.

Question 1590. Two constructed illustrations describe breaks of the patella. In the first, a direct blow leaves the bone broken into several pieces. In the second, a sudden contraction of the quadriceps tears the bone across. Which break is transverse, and why? The answer is the second, because the pull of the muscle separates the two halves. A direct blow crushes the bone instead.

Question 1591. Which ridge divides the back of the patella into its two main facets? The answer is the vertical ridge. It runs from the base to the apex and separates the larger lateral facet from the smaller medial one. In a living knee it fits into the groove between the femoral condyles.

Question 1592. When the knee is straight, how much of the patella rests against the femur: all of its back, only its lowest part, its upper part, or none of it? The answer is only its lowest part. In full extension the bone rides above the groove and rests against a pad of fat, so contact is limited to its lower margin.

Question 1593. A described radiograph of the knee shows a small separate piece of bone at the upper outer corner of the patella, with smooth dense edges, and there is no step in the rest of the bone. Is this a break? The answer is that it is a bipartite patella, a normal variant in which one ossification center has not fused. Its smooth, dense margins separate it from a fresh fracture.

Question 1594. Which of these pairs belongs together: the ligament of the head of the femur with the fovea capitis, the transverse acetabular ligament with the acetabular fossa, the interosseous sacroiliac ligament with the auricular surface, or the medial patellofemoral ligament with the lateral epicondyle? The answer is the ligament of the head with the fovea capitis.

Question 1595. Name, spoken as a sentence, one structure attached to the base of the patella and one attached to its apex. The answer is the quadriceps tendon at the base and the patellar ligament at the apex. The tendon pulls from above, and the ligament carries that pull down to the tibial tuberosity.

Question 1596. A described specimen of the knee region shows a small triangular bone whose pointed lower end is joined to a bony prominence below by a thick band. Name the bone, the prominence and the band. The answer is the patella, the tibial tuberosity and the patellar ligament. The band is the continuation of the quadriceps tendon.

Question 1597. A constructed illustration: a person falls onto the front of the knee, and afterwards there is a gap between the broken pieces of the patella and the knee cannot be straightened. Why does the gap open? The answer is that the quadriceps tendon above and the patellar ligament below pull the pieces apart, and the retinacula at the sides are torn with them.

Question 1598. Which structures attach the sides of the patella to the femur and the tibia: the cruciate ligaments, the retinacula, the menisci or the coronary ligaments? The answer is the retinacula. The medial and lateral retinacula are expansions of the vasti that blend with the sides of the patella. They help to keep the bone centered in the groove.

Question 1599. Name, spoken as a sentence, the two bones that meet at the knee joint and carry the weight of the body through it. The answer is the femur and the tibia. The femur rests its condyles on the flat upper surface of the tibia. The patella lies in front of them within the quadriceps tendon.

Question 1600. A described lateral radiograph of the knee shows the patella lying well above the groove of the femur, so that only its lowest margin overlaps the front of the bone. What is this position called, and why does it matter? The answer is a high-riding patella. The bone enters the groove later than usual as the knee bends, so it is less firmly seated.

To summarize, the acetabulum is formed by the ilium, the ischium and the pubis, and its horseshoe-shaped lunate surface is the only part that bears the load of the head of the femur. The obturator foramen lies anteroinferior to the socket and is closed by a membrane except for the canal that carries the obturator nerve and vessels. Behind, the sacroiliac joint is bound by the interosseous ligament, the sacrotuberous and sacrospinous ligaments turn the sciatic notches into foramina, and nutation of the sacrum is checked by the ligaments behind the joint. The femur is the longest bone in the body, its head carries the fovea capitis, its neck meets the shaft at about 125 degrees, and the medial circumflex femoral artery supplies the head through vessels that climb the neck. The patella is the largest sesamoid bone, it lies in the quadriceps tendon, and its lateral facet is the larger of the two.
