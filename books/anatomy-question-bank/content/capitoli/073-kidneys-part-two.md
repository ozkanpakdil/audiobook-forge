# Chapter 73 — Kidneys, Part Two

This chapter continues the study of the kidney and then follows the stream of urine out of it. It begins with the renal pelvis, the calyces and the hilum, the door through which the vessels and the collecting system leave the organ, and then follows each ureter from the kidney down to the bladder. It holds one hundred questions, and each one gives its answer immediately after the question and then explains why that answer is right. Nothing has to be held in mind until an answer key, because the answer arrives in the same paragraph. This is educational material, and it supports rather than replaces an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 7201. Which structure collects urine from the major calyces and funnels it downward into the ureter: a renal pyramid, the renal pelvis, a renal column, or the renal capsule? The answer is the renal pelvis. It is the expanded upper end of the collecting system, and it lies inside the renal sinus. At its lower end it narrows and continues without a break as the ureter.

Question 7202. What is the name of the cavity inside the kidney that contains the pelvis, the calyces, the blood vessels and a pad of fat? The answer is the renal sinus. It opens onto the medial border of the kidney through the hilum, so the collecting system can leave the organ there. Its fat surrounds the other contents and separates them from one another.

Question 7203. What name is given to the vertical slit on the medial border of the kidney through which the vessels, the nerves and the pelvis pass? The answer is the renal hilum. It is the doorway into the renal sinus rather than a simple hole, and it transmits the renal vein, the renal artery and the renal pelvis in that order from front to back.

Question 7204. Within the hilum, which of the three main structures lies most anterior and which lies most posterior? The answer is the renal vein in front and the renal pelvis behind, with the renal artery between them. That order is worth memorizing, because a surgeon who approaches the pedicle from the front meets the vein first and the pelvis last.

Question 7205. A described coronal section of a kidney shows several cup-shaped chambers, each one wrapped around the tip of a medullary pyramid. What are those chambers, and what do they receive? The answer is the minor calyces, and each one receives urine from the papilla it cups. Several minor calyces then join to form a major calyx.

Question 7206. What is the name of the pointed tip of a renal pyramid, the part that projects into a minor calyx? The answer is the renal papilla. Its surface is pierced by many small openings, the area cribrosa, through which the collecting ducts deliver urine. From the calyx the urine has no way back into the medulla.

Question 7207. How many major calyces does one kidney usually have: one, two or three, five, or eight? The answer is usually two or three. A superior and an inferior major calyx are the rule, and a middle one is often present between them. They unite at the medial part of the sinus to form the renal pelvis.

Question 7208. A constructed illustration: a young person has intermittent aching pain in one loin, and a study shows a dilated renal pelvis with a normal ureter below it. Which structure is most likely crossing and obstructing the pelviureteric junction? The answer is an accessory renal artery running to the lower pole. Such an artery is a common variant, and it crosses the junction exactly where the pelvis narrows into the ureter.

Question 7209. True or false: at the hilum the renal vein lies in front of the renal artery. The answer is true. The vein is the most anterior of the three main structures, the artery lies in the middle, and the pelvis lies at the back. That is also the order in which a surgeon meets them.

Question 7210. Which structure leaves the kidney at the lower part of the hilum: the renal vein, the renal artery, the renal pelvis, or the renal capsule? The answer is the renal pelvis. It tapers at the lower end of the hilum and then continues as the ureter. The vein and the artery enter and leave higher up in the hilum.

Question 7211. What is the funnel-shaped part of the collecting system that lies inside the renal sinus and receives the major calyces? The answer is the renal pelvis. It is the last chamber within the kidney, and below it the tube continues as the ureter. Its shape varies, and both a broad and a narrow pelvis are normal.

Question 7212. Which division of the renal artery passes behind the renal pelvis, and why does that relation matter? The answer is the posterior division, which gives the posterior segmental artery. It curves behind the pelvis on its way to the posterior surface of the kidney. The pelvis therefore has to be moved aside before that artery can be seen.

Question 7213. A described specimen shows one calyx cupped over two separate papillae instead of one. What is that calyx called, and where is it usually found? The answer is a compound calyx, and it is most often found at the upper and the lower pole. There the pyramids fuse, so two or three papillae open into a single chamber.

Question 7214. What is the name of the band of cortical tissue that runs between two renal pyramids and carries vessels inward? The answer is a renal column. It is cortex that has been pushed between the pyramids, and it is continuous with the cortex at the surface. The vessels within it supply the neighboring medulla.

Question 7215. Which large vessel does the left renal vein cross on its way to the inferior vena cava: the aorta, the portal vein, the left gonadal vein, or the inferior mesenteric vein? The answer is the aorta. The left renal vein is longer than the right one because it has to cross the midline. The right renal vein is short and crosses nothing.

Question 7216. Which vein from the left side of the body joins the left renal vein instead of draining directly into the inferior vena cava? The answer is the left gonadal vein. The left suprarenal vein usually joins the left renal vein as well. On the right side both of those veins drain into the inferior vena cava on their own.

Question 7217. Name, as a list of two, the veins that most often join the left renal vein. The answer is the left suprarenal vein and the left gonadal vein. The left inferior phrenic vein often joins it too. One consequence is that the left renal vein carries blood not only from the kidney but also from the adrenal gland and the gonad.

Question 7218. Which statement about the two renal arteries is correct: the right artery is longer and passes behind the inferior vena cava, the left artery is longer and passes behind the cava, the two are always the same length, or the right artery arises from the cava? The answer is that the right artery is longer and passes behind the inferior vena cava.

Question 7219. A described contrast study of a kidney shows a branching tree of chambers filling from the papillae outward. Through which parts of that tree does the contrast pass, in order? The answer is the minor calyces, then the major calyces, then the renal pelvis. From the pelvis it runs on into the ureter. The whole tree lies inside the renal sinus.

Question 7220. A constructed illustration: a person has blood in the urine after a minor knock, and a study suggests that the left renal vein is compressed between the aorta and the artery to the small intestine. Which vein is involved, and what is its normal course? The answer is the left renal vein, which normally crosses in front of the aorta below that artery.

Question 7221. Which of these pairs belongs together: a minor calyx with a renal papilla, a major calyx with the ureter, the renal pelvis with the renal capsule, or a renal column with the bladder? The answer is a minor calyx with a renal papilla. The papilla projects into the calyx, and it is the pelvis, not the major calyx, that is continuous with the ureter.

Question 7222. Where may a normal renal pelvis lie: always inside the renal sinus, always outside the kidney, either inside the sinus or partly outside the hilum, or inside the peritoneal cavity? The answer is that it may lie either inside the sinus or partly outside the hilum. Both appearances are normal variants. A pelvis that projects outside the kidney is not in itself a disease.

Question 7223. What is the name of the opening on the medial border of the kidney that leads into the space holding the calyces? The answer is the hilum. It is a vertical slit rather than a round hole, and its edges are formed by the medial border of the organ. Behind it lies the renal sinus.

Question 7224. Inside the renal sinus, does the pelvis lie in front of or behind the branches of the renal vessels? The answer is behind them. The artery and its branches lie in front of the pelvis, and the veins lie in front of the artery. The pelvis therefore occupies the back of the sinus.

Question 7225. True or false: the substance of the kidney can be cut without pain, while stretching its fibrous coat is painful. The answer is true. The parenchyma itself carries no pain fibers, but the capsule and the pelvis do. That is why obstruction and swelling cause pain while a needle passed into the kidney does not.

Question 7226. A constructed illustration: a person has a dull, constant ache in one flank that never comes in waves, and a study shows a swollen kidney with a normal collecting system. Which covering of the kidney is being stretched? The answer is the fibrous capsule. It is richly supplied with pain fibers, and slow swelling stretches it, so the pain is steady rather than colicky.

Question 7227. A described frontal section of a kidney shows a fan of triangular stripes whose bases face the surface and whose tips point inward. What are those stripes, and where do their tips end? The answer is the renal pyramids, and their tips are the papillae, which open into the minor calyces. Cortex fills the spaces between the pyramids.

Question 7228. Into which structure does the renal pelvis empty: the bladder, the renal vein, the ureter, or the renal capsule? The answer is the ureter. The point at which the two meet is the pelviureteric junction, and it lies at the lower end of the hilum. From there the tube runs downward on the posterior abdominal wall.

Question 7229. At which vertebra does each renal artery arise from the abdominal aorta? The answer is the second lumbar vertebra. The two arteries leave the aorta just below the superior mesenteric artery, and the right one then passes behind the inferior vena cava. Their level is a useful landmark for the whole kidney.

Question 7230. Which way does the renal hilum face in a person standing upright, and how does the upper pole compare with the lower pole? The answer is that the hilum faces anteromedially and the upper pole lies more medial than the lower pole. The long axis of the kidney therefore slopes downward and laterally. That tilt follows the lateral border of the psoas major.

Question 7231. Why does blocking one segmental artery of a kidney damage a wedge of tissue rather than the whole organ: because the segments share one artery, because the segmental arteries are end arteries, because the artery supplies both kidneys, or because the capsule takes over the supply? The answer is that the segmental arteries are end arteries, so only the segment they supply is lost.

Question 7232. Which artery, when it is present as an accessory vessel, commonly crosses the pelviureteric junction on its way to the lower pole of the kidney? The answer is an accessory lower polar renal artery. It arises from the aorta and reaches the lower pole below the hilum. It is one recognized cause of intermittent obstruction at the junction.

Question 7233. Name, in order from front to back, the three main structures that pass through the renal hilum. The answer is the renal vein, the renal artery and the renal pelvis. Lymphatic vessels and autonomic nerves travel with them, and the fat of the sinus fills the spaces between them. The same order holds inside the sinus.

Question 7234. A constructed illustration: a person has recurrent pain in one flank after drinking a large volume of fluid, and a study shows a normal ureter with a dilated pelvis and calyces above it. Which junction is failing, and what is the ordinary anatomy of that junction? The answer is the pelviureteric junction, where the funnel-shaped pelvis narrows into the ureter at the lower end of the hilum.

Question 7235. A described study shows a kidney whose calyces and pelvis are wider than usual and whose medulla is thinned outward. What has happened to the collecting system, and where does the cause lie? The answer is that the system is dilated, and the cause lies below it, at the pelviureteric junction or in the ureter. The parenchyma thins because the pressure behind the obstruction is passed back to it.

Question 7236. Which part of a renal pyramid projects into a minor calyx: the base, the apex, the column, or the capsule? The answer is the apex, which is also called the papilla. The base of the pyramid faces outward toward the cortex, and the cortex wraps around it. The apex points inward toward the sinus.

Question 7237. What fills the renal sinus around the pelvis, the calyces and the vessels, and becomes more abundant with age? The answer is fat. It cushions the collecting system and the vessels, and in older people it is plentiful enough to widen the apparent outline of the sinus on a scan. Fat is one of the normal contents of that space.

Question 7238. Which organ rests against the hilum of the left kidney, and which vessels run near it there? The answer is the tail of the pancreas, with the splenic vessels. The hilum of the left kidney therefore faces the pancreas, whereas the hilum of the right kidney faces the descending part of the duodenum. Both of those organs lie in front of the kidney.

Question 7239. To which group of lymph nodes does lymph from a kidney drain first: the inguinal nodes, the para-aortic nodes near the origin of the renal artery, the iliac nodes, or the nodes at the hilum of the lung? The answer is the para-aortic nodes, at about the level of the second lumbar vertebra. Lymph from the capsule drains to the same group.

Question 7240. True or false: the left kidney usually lies a little higher than the right. The answer is true. The right kidney is pushed downward by the large right lobe of the liver, which occupies the space above it. The difference is small, and it is a normal finding rather than a sign of disease.

Question 7241. A described cross section of the abdomen at the second lumbar vertebra shows the two kidneys, and in front of the left one a vein running across the midline. Which vein is it, and what does it cross? The answer is the left renal vein, which crosses in front of the aorta to reach the inferior vena cava. The right renal vein is short and crosses nothing.

Question 7242. How many minor calyces does one kidney usually contain: about 3, about 7 to 13, about 20, or exactly 30? The answer is about 7 to 13. Each minor calyx is cupped over one or more papillae, and they join in groups to form two or three major calyces.

Question 7243. What is the name of the collecting chamber formed where the major calyces unite, before the ureter begins? The answer is the renal pelvis. It lies within the renal sinus, and it leaves the kidney at the lower end of the hilum. Its wall is thin and can be seen clearly when it is filled with contrast.

Question 7244. Which of these pairs belongs together: the anterior division of the renal artery with the apical, upper, middle and lower segmental arteries, the posterior division with all four of those arteries, the renal vein with the segmental arteries, or the renal pelvis with the segmental arteries? The answer is the anterior division with those four segmental arteries. The posterior division gives the single posterior segmental artery.

Question 7245. A constructed illustration: a stone has lodged where the renal pelvis becomes the ureter, and the person has severe waves of pain in one flank. Which junction holds the stone, and where is that junction? The answer is the pelviureteric junction, at the lower end of the renal hilum, where the funnel of the pelvis narrows into the ureter. It is the first narrow point of the tube.

Question 7246. Why can a surgeon remove one segment of a kidney without destroying the rest of the organ: because all the segments share one artery, because each segment has its own end artery, because the capsule supplies the cortex, or because the pelvis feeds the medulla? The answer is that each segment has its own end artery. The plane between the anterior and the posterior territories has very few vessels.

Question 7247. A described dissection of the kidney shows the medial border opened, with a vein lying in front, an artery behind it, and a funnel-shaped tube behind both. Which structures are these, in that order? The answer is the renal vein, the renal artery and the renal pelvis, in the hilum from front to back.

Question 7248. Which muscle lies immediately behind the renal pelvis and the upper ureter as they leave the kidney? The answer is the psoas major. Both the pelvis and the ureter rest against its anterior surface, and the medial edge of that muscle marks their line of descent through the abdomen.

Question 7249. Which statement about the renal pelvis is correct: it is formed by the union of the major calyces, it is formed by the union of the minor calyces, it lies outside the renal fascia, or it drains into the renal vein? The answer is that it is formed by the union of the major calyces. It carries urine away, and it never carries blood.

Question 7250. A described coronal section of the abdomen passes through both kidneys and shows the pelvis of each one lying below and behind the vessels. In which plane was the kidney cut, and why does the pelvis appear behind the vessels? The answer is a coronal plane along the long axis of the kidney. The pelvis lies behind the vessels because it occupies the back of the renal sinus.

Question 7251. What name is given to each of the two muscular tubes that carry urine from a kidney to the bladder? The answer is the ureter. Each one begins at the pelviureteric junction and ends at the wall of the bladder, and each is about 25 to 30 centimeters long.

Question 7252. Which of these describes the course of a ureter: from the bladder up to the kidney, from the renal pelvis downward to the bladder, from the kidney to the urethra, or from the kidney to the colon? The answer is from the renal pelvis downward to the bladder. Urine travels in that direction only, helped by waves of contraction in the wall of the tube.

Question 7253. A described contrast study outlines a thin tube running down the posterior abdominal wall and crossing a muscle on each side. Which muscle does it cross, and on which surface does the tube lie? The answer is the psoas major, and the ureter lies against its anterior surface, behind the peritoneum. The tube follows the line of that muscle all the way to the pelvis.

Question 7254. In front of which vessel does each ureter cross the pelvic brim: the abdominal aorta, the common iliac artery at its bifurcation, the external iliac vein, or the inferior vena cava? The answer is the common iliac artery at its bifurcation. That crossing lies close to the sacroiliac joint, and it is one of the points at which the ureter is narrow.

Question 7255. Which vessels cross in front of the abdominal ureter on their way to the gonad? The answer is the gonadal vessels, the testicular artery and vein or the ovarian artery and vein. They cross the ureter obliquely, so the ureter lies behind them. That crossing is a useful landmark when the tube is being traced downward.

Question 7256. A described study shows both ureters outlined, each one lying against a triangular muscle behind the peritoneum and then turning into the pelvis. Where does each tube change direction, and what does it cross at that point? The answer is at the pelvic brim, where it crosses the common iliac artery at its bifurcation.

Question 7257. A constructed illustration: a person has severe waves of pain that begin in one loin and travel down to the groin, and a study shows a small stone at the pelvic brim. Which structure holds the stone, and why does the pain travel downward? The answer is the ureter, whose pain is referred along the segments that supply it, from the upper lumbar region toward the groin.

Question 7258. Apart from the gonadal vessels, which vessels cross the right ureter in front? The answer is the right colic and ileocolic vessels. On the left side the corresponding crossing is made by the left colic vessels. The ureter itself passes behind all of them, and it also passes behind the descending part of the duodenum on the right.

Question 7259. In the female pelvis, which artery crosses the ureter and lies above it: the internal pudendal artery, the uterine artery, the obturator artery, or the superior gluteal artery? The answer is the uterine artery. It runs medially toward the uterus and crosses over the ureter, which passes beneath it on its way to the bladder.

Question 7260. True or false: the ureter lies behind the peritoneum along its whole abdominal course. The answer is true. It is a retroperitoneal structure, so it stays outside the peritoneal cavity as it runs down to the pelvic brim. It keeps that position in the pelvis as well.

Question 7261. At which point is the lumen of the ureter narrowest: at the pelviureteric junction, where it crosses the pelvic brim, where it passes through the wall of the bladder, or halfway down the abdomen? The answer is where it passes through the wall of the bladder. Stones lodge most often there, and also at the two narrow points above it.

Question 7262. What is the name of the short part of the ureter that runs obliquely through the wall of the bladder? The answer is the intramural part. It is about 2 centimeters long, and its oblique course is what keeps urine from running back up the tube. It is the narrowest part of the whole ureter.

Question 7263. Inside the pelvis, does the ureter lie in front of or behind the internal iliac artery, and where does it turn toward the bladder? The answer is that it lies in front of the internal iliac artery. It runs down the lateral wall of the pelvis and turns forward and medially near the ischial spine to reach the bladder.

Question 7264. A constructed illustration: during an operation to remove the uterus, the surgeon must identify a tube running close to the cervix. Which structure is it, and what is its relation to the uterine artery there? The answer is the ureter, which passes beneath the uterine artery close to the lateral part of the cervix.

Question 7265. A described dissection of the female pelvis shows an artery running medially toward the uterus and a tube passing below it on its way to the bladder. Which two structures are shown, and which one lies higher? The answer is the uterine artery and the ureter, with the artery lying higher. The tube passes beneath the artery and then runs forward to the bladder.

Question 7266. In the male pelvis, which structure crosses the ureter from its lateral to its medial side: the ductus deferens, the obturator nerve, the middle rectal artery, or the inferior epigastric artery? The answer is the ductus deferens. It crosses above the ureter, and the ureter then passes beneath it before it enters the bladder.

Question 7267. What is the name of the outermost coat of the ureter, the layer that carries its blood vessels and allows the tube to be freed at operation? The answer is the adventitia. Inside it lie the muscular coat and the mucous membrane, and the vessels run along the tube within the adventitia.

Question 7268. Which of these pairs belongs together: the abdominal ureter with the psoas major, the pelvic ureter with the diaphragm, the pelviureteric junction with the bladder, or the intramural ureter with the kidney? The answer is the abdominal ureter with the psoas major. The pelvic ureter runs against the lateral wall of the pelvis instead.

Question 7269. Name, in order from above downward, the three points at which the ureter is narrow. The answer is the pelviureteric junction, the crossing of the pelvic brim, and the point at which the tube enters the wall of the bladder. These are the places where a stone is most likely to stop.

Question 7270. By what means is urine carried along the ureter: by gravity alone, by waves of contraction of its muscular wall, by suction from the bladder, or by the movement of the diaphragm? The answer is waves of contraction of its muscular wall. Gravity helps, but the tube itself propels the urine, and it does so whether the person is standing or lying down.

Question 7271. True or false: the wave of contraction that carries urine down the ureter continues even after every nerve to the tube has been cut. The answer is true. The muscle of the ureter starts its own contraction, so the tube can still propel urine after it has been separated from its nerve supply.

Question 7272. What is the name of the ridge raised inside the bladder by the oblique course of the ureter through its wall? The answer is the ureteric fold. It runs from the ureteric orifice toward the internal urethral orifice at the neck of the bladder. It is one of the landmarks of the inside of the bladder.

Question 7273. Which nerves carry the pain of a stone in the ureter: fibers from the upper abdomen, sympathetic fibers from the lower thoracic and upper lumbar segments, the vagus nerve, or the phrenic nerve? The answer is the sympathetic fibers from the lower thoracic and upper lumbar segments, with the sacral parasympathetic fibers. Pain is therefore felt in the loin and the groin rather than deep in the chest.

Question 7274. Which part of the gut lies in front of the right ureter near the hilum of the kidney? The answer is the descending part of the duodenum. The ureter passes behind it on the right side. Further down, the right ureter also lies behind the root of the mesentery and the cecum.

Question 7275. A described plain radiograph of the abdomen shows two long soft-tissue shadows running from the region of the kidneys downward and medially toward the pelvis. Which structures are they, and what line do they follow? The answer is the ureters, and their line follows the lateral edge of the psoas major on each side.

Question 7276. A constructed illustration: a person has pain in one flank that comes in waves and spreads to the groin, and the urine contains blood. Which structure is most likely partly blocked, and how does its pain behave? The answer is the ureter. The pain is colicky rather than steady, and it does not settle with rest or with a change of position.

Question 7277. Which statement about the blood supply of the ureter is correct: it comes from a single artery, it comes from several arteries that join along its length, it comes only from the renal artery, or it has no supply of its own? The answer is that it comes from several arteries that join along its length. Branches of the renal, gonadal, aortic, iliac and vesical arteries all reach it.

Question 7278. Which artery of the pelvis sends a branch that climbs the ureter and helps to keep its lower part alive after the vessels above have been divided? The answer is the uterine artery in the female, with the corresponding vesical branches in the male. Those branches run upward in the adventitia and join the ones from above.

Question 7279. A described section through the base of the bladder shows a tube running obliquely through the muscular wall for about 2 centimeters before it opens inside. What does that arrangement achieve? The answer is that it makes a valve. When the bladder contracts and the pressure inside it rises, the oblique passage is squeezed shut, so urine cannot run back up the tube.

Question 7280. A constructed illustration: a child has an infection of the urinary tract, and a study shows urine running back from the bladder into one ureter. Which part of the ureter is failing to close, and what is its usual length? The answer is the intramural part, whose oblique course is normally about 2 centimeters long. A short intramural segment is a recognized cause of such backflow.

Question 7281. Which statement about the opening of the ureter is correct: it lies in the trigone of the bladder, it lies in the urethra, it lies at the apex of the bladder, or it lies in the rectum? The answer is that it lies in the trigone. The two ureteric orifices and the internal urethral orifice mark the corners of that smooth triangular area.

Question 7282. A described view of the inside of the bladder shows a smooth triangular area with two slit-like openings above and a third opening below. What is that area, and what forms its upper corners? The answer is the trigone, and its upper corners are the two ureteric orifices. The lower corner is the internal urethral orifice.

Question 7283. Which of these pairs belongs together: the three narrow points of the ureter with the sites where stones lodge, the three narrow points with the sites of urine formation, the trigone with the kidney, or the renal pelvis with the urethra? The answer is the three narrow points with the sites where stones lodge. They are the pelviureteric junction, the pelvic brim and the bladder wall.

Question 7284. In the male pelvis, which structures lie close to the lower ureter as it approaches the bladder? The answer is the ductus deferens, which crosses above it, and the seminal vesicle, which lies near it. The ureter then runs forward and pierces the wall of the bladder.

Question 7285. Where does each ureter open: into the renal pelvis, into the bladder, into the urethra, or into the colon? The answer is into the bladder. It opens at the ureteric orifice on the trigone, having passed obliquely through the muscular wall of the bladder. Nothing but urine passes through it.

Question 7286. What is the name of the junction at which the renal pelvis becomes the ureter? The answer is the pelviureteric junction. It lies at the lower end of the renal hilum, and it is the first of the three narrow points of the ureter. Its narrowing is part of normal anatomy.

Question 7287. A described contrast study shows a column of urine that stops abruptly in the lower part of one ureter, with a small rounded defect at the point where it stops. What does the defect represent, and why did it stop there? The answer is a stone lodged at one of the narrow points. The narrowest point of the tube is where it passes through the wall of the bladder.

Question 7288. What happens to the intramural part of the ureter when the bladder fills and contracts: it opens wider, it is compressed and closes, it is pulled out of the bladder wall, or it grows longer? The answer is that it is compressed and closes. The pressure of the full bladder squeezes the oblique passage shut, and that is what stops urine from running back up the tube.

Question 7289. A constructed illustration: a person has pain in one loin that comes in waves, and a study shows a stone at the pelviureteric junction. Which structure is obstructed, and what lies immediately above the stone? The answer is the ureter at its first narrow point, with the renal pelvis immediately above it.

Question 7290. How long is the part of the ureter that runs through the wall of the bladder: about half a centimeter, about 2 centimeters, about 6 centimeters, or about 12 centimeters? The answer is about 2 centimeters. That oblique passage acts as a valve, and it is the narrowest part of the whole tube.

Question 7291. In the female pelvis, which structure lies immediately in front of the lower ureter as the ureter approaches the bladder? The answer is the lateral part of the vagina, with the cervix above and medial to the tube. The ureter passes above the lateral vaginal fornix, and that relation is why it is at risk during operations on the pelvis.

Question 7292. To which region is pain from the lower end of the ureter usually referred: the back of the neck, the groin and the external genitalia on the same side, the sole of the foot, or the top of the shoulder? The answer is the groin and the external genitalia on the same side. Pain from the upper ureter is felt in the loin instead.

Question 7293. What name is given to the opening of the ureter into the bladder? The answer is the ureteric orifice. There are two of them, one on each side of the trigone, and each is a slit rather than a round hole. Urine enters the bladder through them.

Question 7294. Why is the pain of a stone in the ureter described as colic rather than as a steady ache: because the stone cuts the wall, because the muscular wall contracts in waves against the obstruction, because the peritoneum becomes inflamed, or because the kidney itself is painless? The answer is that the muscular wall contracts in waves against the obstruction. Each wave brings a fresh surge of pain.

Question 7295. A described study of a doubled collecting system on one side shows two ureters, one opening low and medially and dilated above, the other opening higher and laterally and showing backflow. Which ureter drains the upper part of the kidney? The answer is the one that opens low and medially. In a complete duplication the upper part drains through the lower opening.

Question 7296. Which of these pairs belongs together: a ureter that opens low and medially with the upper part of a duplicated kidney, one that opens low and medially with the lower part, one that opens high and laterally with the upper part, or both with the same part? The answer is the low and medial opening with the upper part of the kidney. The two buds cross as they descend.

Question 7297. Why does one kidney have two ureters in a complete duplication: because the metanephric tissue divides late, because the ureteric bud divides before it reaches the metanephric tissue, because the wall of the bladder grows too far upward, or because the renal pelvis lies outside the hilum? The answer is that the ureteric bud divides before it reaches the metanephric tissue, so two buds grow into it.

Question 7298. During an operation on the back of the abdomen, how is the ureter usually identified: by its pulsation, by its adhesion to the peritoneum, by its yellow color, or by the fact that it lies inside the peritoneal cavity? The answer is by its adhesion to the peritoneum. As the peritoneum is peeled back, the ureter comes with it, and its fine network of vessels marks it out.

Question 7299. Why is the ureter supplied by branches of many different arteries: because it has no anastomoses in its wall, because it develops from the mesonephric duct and takes branches from the vessels it passes, because it develops from the peritoneum, or because no other artery reaches it? The answer is that it develops from the mesonephric duct and takes branches from the vessels it passes. Those branches join along its length.

Question 7300. What is the name of the tube that continues the renal pelvis downward and opens into the bladder? The answer is the ureter. It is about 25 to 30 centimeters long, it lies behind the peritoneum, and it is narrowest where it passes through the wall of the bladder.

To summarize, the renal pelvis is formed by the union of two or three major calyces, fed by minor calyces cupped over the renal papillae, and the whole collecting system lies within the fat-filled renal sinus that opens at the hilum. At the hilum the renal vein lies most anterior, the renal artery in the middle and the renal pelvis most posterior, and the left renal vein crosses in front of the aorta and usually receives the left suprarenal and left gonadal veins. Each ureter runs from the pelviureteric junction down the posterior abdominal wall on the psoas major, behind the peritoneum, crosses the pelvic brim in front of the common iliac artery at its bifurcation, and enters the bladder obliquely for about 2 centimeters. The three narrow points are the pelviureteric junction, the pelvic brim and the bladder wall, and they are the places where stones lodge. The intramural part acts as a valve, the uterine artery crosses the tube in the female and the ductus deferens in the male, and the muscle of the tube drives urine onward by its own waves.
