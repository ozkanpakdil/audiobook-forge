# Chapter 57 — Autonomic Nervous System

This chapter covers the autonomic nervous system and the meninges with the cerebrospinal fluid they enclose. The first half follows the two divisions of the autonomic nervous system from their origins in the spinal cord and the brainstem to the ganglia where they relay, and it includes the enteric nervous system of the gut wall. The second half describes the meninges, the dural folds and venous sinuses, and the cerebrospinal fluid. It holds one hundred questions, and each one gives its answer at once and then explains why that answer is right, so nothing has to be held in mind. This is educational material, and it supports rather than replaces an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 5601. Which division of the autonomic nervous system arises from the spinal cord segments in the thoracolumbar region? The answer is the sympathetic division. Its preganglionic fibers leave the spinal cord between the first thoracic and the second lumbar segments. The parasympathetic division arises instead from the brainstem and from the sacral segments.

Question 5602. Which division of the autonomic nervous system is described as the craniosacral outflow? The answer is the parasympathetic division. Its fibers leave through four cranial nerves and through the second, third and fourth sacral segments. The sympathetic division arises from the thoracolumbar segments instead.

Question 5603. Which cranial nerve carries the parasympathetic fibers that reach the parotid gland: the third, the seventh, the ninth, or the tenth? The answer is the ninth cranial nerve, the glossopharyngeal. Its fibers travel by way of the lesser petrosal nerve to the otic ganglion.

Question 5604. Which small parasympathetic ganglion lies in the orbit, lateral to the optic nerve, and relays the fibers that constrict the pupil? The answer is the ciliary ganglion. It receives preganglionic fibers from the third cranial nerve and sends short ciliary nerves to the eyeball. A sympathetic root also passes through it without relaying.

Question 5605. What name is given to the fused lower cervical and first thoracic sympathetic ganglion, which lies in front of the neck of the first rib? The answer is the stellate ganglion. It is formed where the inferior cervical ganglion joins the first thoracic ganglion. Its fibers follow the vessels of the arm.

Question 5606. A constructed illustration: a described lesion interrupts the sympathetic fibers on one side of the neck. Which signs would be expected on that side of the face? The answer is a small pupil, a drooping upper eyelid, a sunken-looking eye and absent sweating. The fibers that dilate the pupil and lift the eyelid travel with the sympathetic chain, and the same pathway carries the fibers to the sweat glands.

Question 5607. Which of these structures receives postganglionic sympathetic fibers that release acetylcholine rather than norepinephrine: the sweat glands, the arrector pili muscles, the arterioles of the skin, or the dilator muscle of the pupil? The answer is the sweat glands. They are the standard exception to the rule that sympathetic endings are adrenergic.

Question 5608. True or false: every postganglionic sympathetic fiber releases norepinephrine at its ending. The answer is false. The fibers that supply the sweat glands release acetylcholine instead. That single exception is worth remembering, because it is the one place where a sympathetic ending behaves like a parasympathetic one. All preganglionic fibers, sympathetic and parasympathetic alike, release acetylcholine.

Question 5609. A described cross section of the spinal cord shows a small column of nerve cells in the lateral part of the gray matter, between the second thoracic and the second lumbar segments. What does that column contain? The answer is the cell bodies of the preganglionic sympathetic neurons. Their fibers leave in the ventral roots and reach the sympathetic trunk. No such column is present below the second lumbar segment.

Question 5610. Along the sympathetic trunk, at which levels do white rami communicantes connect the trunk to the spinal nerves? The answer is at every level from the first thoracic to the second lumbar segment. White rami carry the myelinated preganglionic fibers into the trunk. Above and below that range the trunk connects to the spinal nerves only by gray rami.

Question 5611. Which statement about a sympathetic preganglionic fiber is correct: it is myelinated and short, it is unmyelinated and long, it is myelinated and long, or it is unmyelinated and short? The answer is that it is myelinated and short. The postganglionic fiber that continues from the ganglion is unmyelinated and long.

Question 5612. A described section of the adrenal gland shows a central mass of pale cells arranged in cords around wide venous channels. What are those cells? The answer is the chromaffin cells of the adrenal medulla. They are modified postganglionic sympathetic neurons that pour epinephrine and norepinephrine into the blood. They are supplied directly by preganglionic fibers, with no ganglion in between.

Question 5613. The greater splanchnic nerve is formed by preganglionic fibers from which spinal cord segments: the first to the fourth thoracic, the fifth to the ninth thoracic, the tenth and eleventh thoracic, or the first and second lumbar? The answer is the fifth to the ninth thoracic segments. The lesser splanchnic nerve comes from the tenth and eleventh, and the least splanchnic nerve from the twelfth.

Question 5614. Which large prevertebral ganglion lies around the origin of the celiac trunk, in front of the aorta at the level of the twelfth thoracic vertebra? The answer is the celiac ganglion. It receives the greater and the lesser splanchnic nerves. Its branches follow the arteries to the stomach, the liver, the spleen, the pancreas and the small intestine.

Question 5615. Name the three prevertebral ganglia of the abdomen, spoken as a list. The answer is the celiac ganglion, the superior mesenteric ganglion and the inferior mesenteric ganglion. Each lies around the artery of the same name, in front of the aorta. They relay the splanchnic nerves on their way to the gut.

Question 5616. Which of these pairs belongs together: the third cranial nerve with the otic ganglion, the seventh with the ciliary ganglion, the ninth with the ciliary ganglion, or the tenth with the ganglia in the wall of the gut? The answer is the tenth cranial nerve with the ganglia in the wall of the gut. The third nerve relays in the ciliary ganglion and the ninth in the otic ganglion.

Question 5617. Postganglionic fibers from which ganglion dilate the pupil: the ciliary, the pterygopalatine, the otic, or the superior cervical? The answer is the superior cervical ganglion. Its fibers run with the internal carotid artery and then along the ophthalmic artery to the eye. The ciliary ganglion does the opposite and constricts the pupil.

Question 5618. What is the name of the paired chain of ganglia that runs down each side of the vertebral column from the base of the skull to the coccyx? The answer is the sympathetic trunk, also called the sympathetic chain. It carries about twenty-two ganglia on each side, joined by longitudinal nerve fibers.

Question 5619. In the neck, what relation does the sympathetic trunk bear to the carotid sheath? The answer is that the trunk lies posterior to the sheath. It lies in front of the prevertebral muscles. The internal carotid artery and the internal jugular vein lie in front of it, inside the sheath.

Question 5620. A constructed illustration: a described mass at the apex of one lung presses on the sympathetic chain in the neck. Which signs would be expected on the same side of the face? The answer is a small pupil, a drooping eyelid, a sunken-looking eye and absence of sweating.

Question 5621. A described dissection of the neck shows three small swellings along the sympathetic trunk, one at the base of the skull, one midway, and one near the first rib. What are they? The answer is the superior, middle and inferior cervical ganglia. The inferior one often fuses with the first thoracic ganglion to form the stellate ganglion.

Question 5622. Which of these effects is produced by the parasympathetic division: dilation of the pupil, an increase in heart rate, a copious flow of watery saliva, or dilation of the bronchioles? The answer is a copious flow of watery saliva. The other three are sympathetic effects. Parasympathetic activity generally prepares the body for rest and digestion.

Question 5623. Which of these effects is produced by the sympathetic division: constriction of the pupil, slowing of the heart, dilation of the pupil, or increased movement of the gut? The answer is dilation of the pupil. Sympathetic activity also raises the heart rate and reduces the movement of the gut.

Question 5624. Which cranial nerve supplies parasympathetic fibers to the heart, the lungs and most of the gut, and is the longest of the cranial nerves? The answer is the tenth cranial nerve, the vagus. It leaves the skull through the jugular foramen and descends in the carotid sheath. It carries about four fifths of all parasympathetic outflow.

Question 5625. Within the carotid sheath, what is the position of the vagus nerve relative to the common carotid artery and the internal jugular vein? The answer is that the nerve lies posterior to both and between them. The artery lies medial and the vein lateral. The sympathetic trunk lies behind the sheath altogether.

Question 5626. As the stomach rotates during development, the two vagus nerves come to lie one in front of the esophagus and one behind it. Which one ends as the anterior vagal trunk? The answer is the left vagus nerve. Rotation carries it to the front, and it continues as the anterior trunk. The right vagus becomes the posterior trunk.

Question 5627. Which statement about the vagal trunks is correct: the left vagus forms the posterior trunk, the right vagus forms the anterior trunk, the left vagus forms the anterior trunk, or both trunks come from the right vagus? The answer is that the left vagus forms the anterior trunk. The stomach rotates during development and carries the left nerve to the front.

Question 5628. What name is given to the parasympathetic nerves that leave the second, third and fourth sacral segments and run to the pelvic organs? The answer is the pelvic splanchnic nerves. They carry preganglionic fibers to ganglia lying close to or inside the organs they supply. They reach the descending colon, the rectum, the bladder and the reproductive organs.

Question 5629. The distal part of the large intestine receives its parasympathetic supply from which nerves, and where do they arise? The answer is the pelvic splanchnic nerves, from the second, third and fourth sacral segments. The vagus nerve reaches the gut only as far as the distal part of the transverse colon.

Question 5630. A constructed illustration: a described injury destroys the sacral parasympathetic supply to the bladder. Which function is lost? The answer is the contraction of the detrusor muscle that empties the bladder. The sympathetic fibers relax the detrusor and close the internal sphincter instead. The external sphincter is skeletal muscle and is supplied by the pudendal nerve.

Question 5631. Which nerves carry the parasympathetic fibers that contract the detrusor muscle of the bladder: the vagus, the pelvic splanchnic nerves, the greater splanchnic nerve, or the hypogastric nerves? The answer is the pelvic splanchnic nerves. They arise from the second, third and fourth sacral segments. The hypogastric nerves are sympathetic and relax the detrusor.

Question 5632. True or false: the sympathetic fibers to the bladder contract the detrusor muscle and empty it. The answer is false. Sympathetic fibers relax the detrusor and tighten the internal urethral sphincter, which helps to store urine. Emptying is the work of the parasympathetic pelvic splanchnic nerves.

Question 5633. Which of these pairs belongs together: a dry mouth with the sympathetic division, a dry mouth with the parasympathetic division, a full bladder with the parasympathetic division, or a full bladder with the sympathetic division? The answer is a full bladder with the sympathetic division. Sympathetic activity relaxes the detrusor and closes the internal sphincter.

Question 5634. What is the name of the network of nerve cells and fibers embedded in the wall of the gut, which can act without instructions from the brain? The answer is the enteric nervous system. It is often counted as a third division of the autonomic nervous system.

Question 5635. The myenteric plexus of the gut wall lies between which two layers: the mucosa and the submucosa, the two layers of the muscularis externa, the serosa and the muscularis externa, or the mucosa and the serosa? The answer is between the two layers of the muscularis externa. It lies between the circular and the longitudinal muscle. The submucosal plexus lies deeper, between the circular muscle and the mucosa.

Question 5636. A described section of the small intestine shows a fine network of nerve cells lying in the loose tissue just outside the mucosa, separate from the larger plexus between the muscle layers. Which plexus is it? The answer is the submucosal plexus, also called the plexus of Meissner. It controls the local secretion and the flow of blood in the gut wall.

Question 5637. Which cells of the gut wall act as the pacemakers that set the rhythm of its movement: the enteric neurons of the myenteric plexus, the interstitial cells of Cajal, the smooth muscle cells of the mucosa, or the goblet cells of the epithelium? The answer is the interstitial cells of Cajal. They lie among the smooth muscle cells and generate slow rhythmic waves. The enteric neurons modify the rhythm rather than set it.

Question 5638. A constructed illustration: a described newborn fails to pass meconium, and a biopsy of the rectum shows no ganglion cells in the myenteric plexus. What has gone wrong? The answer is that the enteric neurons failed to reach that segment during development. These cells are derived from the neural crest and migrate down the gut. The segment without ganglia stays tightly contracted.

Question 5639. Which statement about the enteric nervous system is correct: it works only when the vagus nerve is active, it can organize peristalsis on its own, it contains no sensory neurons, or it is found only in the stomach? The answer is that it can organize peristalsis on its own. Its reflex circuits are complete within the gut wall.

Question 5640. A described dissection of the thorax shows two nerves descending on the surface of the esophagus and joining in a network around it. Which nerves are shown, and what do they become below the diaphragm? The answer is the right and the left vagus nerves, which form the esophageal plexus. Below the diaphragm they become the posterior and the anterior vagal trunks.

Question 5641. Within the wall of the gut, what is the position of the submucosal plexus relative to the circular muscle and the mucosa? The answer is that it lies between the circular muscle and the mucosa. The myenteric plexus lies outside the circular muscle instead, between it and the longitudinal layer.

Question 5642. Name the two main nerve plexuses of the enteric nervous system, spoken as a pair. The answer is the myenteric plexus and the submucosal plexus. The myenteric plexus lies between the two muscle layers and controls movement. The submucosal plexus lies nearer the lumen and controls secretion and blood flow.

Question 5643. Which nerve carries the parasympathetic fibers to the stomach, the small intestine and the proximal part of the large intestine? The answer is the vagus nerve, the tenth cranial nerve. It reaches the gut by way of the esophageal plexus and the vagal trunks. The distal large intestine is supplied by the pelvic splanchnic nerves instead.

Question 5644. A constructed illustration: a described interruption of the blood supply to the heart causes pain felt in the left arm and the upper chest. Which fibers carry that pain, and why does it point to the arm? The answer is the visceral afferent fibers that run with the sympathetic nerves from the upper thoracic segments. The skin of the arm is supplied by the same segments, so the brain misreads the source.

Question 5645. Why can a single sympathetic preganglionic fiber produce a widespread response: because each fiber is very long, because one fiber branches to synapse on many postganglionic neurons, because the postganglionic fibers are myelinated, or because the ganglia lie inside the organs? The answer is that one fiber branches to synapse on many postganglionic neurons. The adrenal medulla adds to the effect by releasing its hormones into the blood.

Question 5646. The sympathetic fibers that reach the sweat glands of the face and the dilator muscle of the pupil relay in which ganglion: the ciliary, the pterygopalatine, the superior cervical, or the stellate? The answer is the superior cervical ganglion. Its postganglionic fibers follow the carotid arteries and their branches to the head and the face.

Question 5647. A described dissection of the sympathetic trunk shows a delicate gray strand running from the trunk back to a spinal nerve at a level below the second lumbar segment. What does that strand carry? The answer is unmyelinated postganglionic sympathetic fibers on their way to the skin and the blood vessels. Because it carries no myelin it looks gray.

Question 5648. True or false: some sympathetic fibers leave the spinal cord and pass through the sympathetic trunk without synapsing, ending in a prevertebral ganglion or in the adrenal medulla. The answer is true. They relay in the celiac, the superior mesenteric and the inferior mesenteric ganglia, or in the adrenal medulla directly.

Question 5649. Which structure is an exception to the rule that the autonomic motor pathway uses two neurons in series: the ciliary ganglion, the sweat gland, the adrenal medulla, or the detrusor muscle? The answer is the adrenal medulla. Its cells are modified postganglionic neurons that are stimulated directly by preganglionic fibers and release their product into the blood. Every other autonomic effector is reached through a ganglion.

Question 5650. A described specimen shows the wall of the colon with a narrow segment in which no nerve cells can be found in either plexus, while the bowel above the segment is wide and thick. What does the specimen show? The answer is an aganglionic segment of the kind seen when the enteric neurons fail to migrate. The narrow segment cannot relax, so the bowel above it dilates.

Question 5651. Which meninx is the outermost and toughest, and how many layers does it have inside the skull? The answer is the dura mater, which has two layers inside the skull. The outer layer is the periosteum of the bone and the inner layer is the meningeal layer. In the spinal canal the dura has only the meningeal layer.

Question 5652. Which of the three meninges is the innermost, follows every sulcus, and carries the blood vessels that supply the surface of the brain? The answer is the pia mater. It is applied directly to the surface of the brain and the spinal cord. The arachnoid lies outside it, separated from it by the subarachnoid space.

Question 5653. Through which structures does cerebrospinal fluid pass from the subarachnoid space into the venous blood: the choroid plexuses, the arachnoid granulations, the openings of the fourth ventricle, or the interventricular foramina? The answer is the arachnoid granulations. They project into the superior sagittal sinus and its lateral lacunae.

Question 5654. A described dissection of the skull cap shows a sickle-shaped sheet of fibrous tissue standing in the midline between the two cerebral hemispheres, with a vein running in its upper edge. Which fold is it, and which sinus lies in that edge? The answer is the falx cerebri, with the superior sagittal sinus in its upper margin. Its lower free edge carries the inferior sagittal sinus.

Question 5655. Which dural fold forms a tent above the cerebellum and separates it from the occipital lobes of the cerebrum? The answer is the tentorium cerebelli. Its free front edge bounds the tentorial notch, through which the midbrain passes. Its attached outer edge encloses the transverse sinus.

Question 5656. A constructed illustration: a described blow to the side of the head tears an artery that runs in a groove on the inner surface of the skull, and blood collects between the bone and the dura. Which artery is torn? The answer is the middle meningeal artery. It lies outside the dura and is torn where the skull is thin at the pterion. The collection is an extradural hematoma.

Question 5657. The artery that grooves the inner surface of the skull and lies between the dura and the bone is a branch of which vessel: the maxillary artery, the superficial temporal artery, the internal carotid artery, or the vertebral artery? The answer is the maxillary artery. Its middle meningeal branch enters the skull through the foramen spinosum.

Question 5658. True or false: in the spinal canal the dura mater is separated from the bone by a real space filled with fat and a venous plexus. The answer is true. Inside the skull the same layer is stuck to the bone, so the cranial extradural space is only a potential one. Blood or pus can collect in the spinal space.

Question 5659. A described computed tomography scan of the head shows a crescent-shaped collection of blood hugging the inner surface of the skull over one hemisphere and crossing the suture lines. Where is the blood, and which vessels have bled? The answer is the subdural space, from bridging veins that run to the superior sagittal sinus. A lens-shaped collection instead points to an artery outside the dura.

Question 5660. The bridging veins that bleed in a subdural hematoma run between which two structures? The answer is between the surface of the cerebral hemispheres and the superior sagittal sinus. They cross the subdural space on their way to the sinus. Stretching of these thin-walled veins is what tears them.

Question 5661. The falx cerebri is attached at the front to which bony crest, and at the back to which ridge: the crista galli and the internal occipital protuberance, the sella turcica and the foramen magnum, the cribriform plate and the mastoid process, or the petrous ridge and the frontal crest? The answer is the crista galli and the internal occipital protuberance. It stands in the longitudinal fissure between the hemispheres.

Question 5662. What is the name of the tent-shaped fold of dura that roofs the posterior cranial fossa and separates the cerebrum from the cerebellum? The answer is the tentorium cerebelli. Its free edge bounds the tentorial notch. The falx cerebelli hangs below it in the midline behind the cerebellum.

Question 5663. Which part of the brain passes through the tentorial notch, and which nerves lie beside it: the midbrain, with the third and fourth cranial nerves; the pons with the fifth and sixth, the medulla with the ninth and tenth, or the thalamus with the first and second? The answer is the midbrain, with the third and the fourth cranial nerves. The notch is the gap in the free edge of the tentorium.

Question 5664. A described section through the base of the skull shows a small horizontal sheet of dura roofing a bony pit, with a stalk passing through a hole in its middle. What is the sheet, and what lies immediately below it? The answer is the diaphragma sellae, with the pituitary gland in the hypophyseal fossa beneath it. The stalk that pierces it is the infundibulum.

Question 5665. Which venous channel runs in the upper margin of the falx cerebri, from the front of the skull to the internal occipital protuberance? The answer is the superior sagittal sinus. It begins at the foramen cecum and ends at the confluence of the sinuses. Cerebrospinal fluid enters it through the arachnoid granulations.

Question 5666. A constructed illustration: a described infection in the face spreads along a vein into the venous channel beside the body of the sphenoid bone. Which cranial nerve, lying free within that channel, is likely to be affected first? The answer is the sixth cranial nerve, which controls the lateral rectus muscle. It lies free within the sinus, while the other nerves lie in its wall.

Question 5667. Which of these structures lies within the cavernous sinus rather than in its lateral wall: the internal carotid artery, the ophthalmic division of the trigeminal nerve, the third cranial nerve, or the fourth cranial nerve? The answer is the internal carotid artery, together with the sixth cranial nerve. The third and the fourth cranial nerves and both divisions of the trigeminal nerve lie in the lateral wall.

Question 5668. Which of these pairs belongs together: the falx cerebri with the pituitary gland, the diaphragma sellae with the pituitary gland, the tentorium cerebelli with the olfactory tract, or the falx cerebelli with the midbrain? The answer is the diaphragma sellae with the pituitary gland. It forms the roof of the hypophyseal fossa.

Question 5669. A described specimen opens the superior sagittal sinus and shows small tufted bodies projecting into its lumen from the dura beside it. What are they, and what do they do? The answer is the arachnoid granulations, which return cerebrospinal fluid to the venous blood. They push the arachnoid through the wall of the sinus.

Question 5670. What is the name of the meeting point of the sinuses at the internal occipital protuberance, where the superior sagittal, the straight and both transverse sinuses come together? The answer is the confluence of the sinuses. The superior sagittal sinus usually drains into the right transverse sinus. The straight sinus usually drains into the left.

Question 5671. The straight sinus runs along the line where which two dural folds meet, and which two channels join to form it? The answer is where the falx cerebri meets the tentorium cerebelli, formed by the inferior sagittal sinus and the great cerebral vein. It ends at the confluence of the sinuses.

Question 5672. Which of these pairs belongs together: the sigmoid sinus with the internal jugular vein, the transverse sinus with the external jugular vein, the superior sagittal sinus with the facial vein, or the cavernous sinus with the subclavian vein? The answer is the sigmoid sinus with the internal jugular vein. The sigmoid sinus leaves the skull through the jugular foramen and becomes the internal jugular vein.

Question 5673. The choroid plexus is found in all of the following except which one: the lateral ventricles, the third ventricle, the fourth ventricle, or the cerebral aqueduct? The answer is the cerebral aqueduct, which has no choroid plexus. It is a narrow channel with no room for one. The plexus is present in the other three cavities.

Question 5674. Through which opening does cerebrospinal fluid pass from a lateral ventricle into the third ventricle? The answer is the interventricular foramen, also called the foramen of Monro. It lies between the anterior end of the thalamus and the fornix. Each lateral ventricle has one.

Question 5675. Which of these sequences correctly traces the flow of cerebrospinal fluid: lateral ventricle, third ventricle, cerebral aqueduct, fourth ventricle; lateral ventricle, fourth ventricle, third ventricle, cerebral aqueduct; third ventricle, lateral ventricle, fourth ventricle, cerebral aqueduct; or fourth ventricle, cerebral aqueduct, third ventricle, lateral ventricle? The answer is the first, from the lateral ventricle to the third, then through the aqueduct to the fourth. From there it reaches the subarachnoid space.

Question 5676. A described coronal section of the brain shows two slit-like cavities in the hemispheres, one on each side of a midline cavity, with a thin layer of tissue and vessels in the roof of each. What are the cavities, and what is the tissue? The answer is the lateral ventricles, with the choroid plexus in their roofs. The midline cavity is the third ventricle.

Question 5677. A constructed illustration: a described blockage in the narrow channel between the third and the fourth ventricle causes the two lateral ventricles and the third to swell while the fourth stays normal in size. What is the blockage, and what is the condition? The answer is a block of the cerebral aqueduct, which causes a noncommunicating hydrocephalus. The fluid cannot reach the subarachnoid space.

Question 5678. In which situation does the whole ventricular system dilate while the pathways between the ventricles stay open: when the aqueduct is blocked, when the arachnoid granulations fail to absorb the fluid, when an opening of the fourth ventricle is blocked, or when one lateral ventricle is blocked? The answer is when the arachnoid granulations fail to absorb the fluid. The fluid still moves, so all the ventricles enlarge.

Question 5679. What is the name of the narrow channel that connects the third ventricle to the fourth, running through the midbrain? The answer is the cerebral aqueduct. It lies dorsal to the tegmentum of the midbrain. A blockage here stops the flow of cerebrospinal fluid and dilates the ventricles above it.

Question 5680. Cerebrospinal fluid leaves the fourth ventricle through which openings, and into which space does it pass? The answer is the median aperture and the two lateral apertures, into the subarachnoid space. The median aperture opens into the cisterna magna behind the medulla. From there the fluid circulates around the brain and the spinal cord.

Question 5681. Name the three openings through which cerebrospinal fluid leaves the fourth ventricle, spoken as a list. The answer is the median aperture and the two lateral apertures. The median aperture lies in the roof behind, and the lateral apertures lie at the sides. Fluid passing through them enters the cisterna magna and the subarachnoid space.

Question 5682. About how much cerebrospinal fluid does an adult hold at any moment, and how much is produced each day: about 15 milliliters, replaced once a day; about 150 milliliters, replaced several times a day; about 500 milliliters, replaced once a day; or about 1,500 milliliters, replaced once a week? The answer is about 150 milliliters, replaced several times a day. The choroid plexuses produce about 500 milliliters a day.

Question 5683. True or false: the choroid plexus is present in all four ventricles of the brain. The answer is false. It is present in both lateral ventricles, in the third ventricle and in the fourth ventricle, but not in the cerebral aqueduct. The aqueduct is a narrow channel without room for a plexus.

Question 5684. What is the name of the space between the arachnoid and the pia mater, which contains cerebrospinal fluid and the larger blood vessels of the brain surface? The answer is the subarachnoid space. It is widened in places into cisterns, such as the cisterna magna behind the medulla.

Question 5685. A constructed illustration: a described sudden severe headache is followed by blood in the cerebrospinal fluid and by neck stiffness, with no blood outside the dura. Where has the bleeding occurred? The answer is the subarachnoid space, usually from a small aneurysm on one of the arteries of the circle of Willis. The blood mixes with the cerebrospinal fluid. Blood outside the dura would collect in the extradural space instead.

Question 5686. Why is a lumbar puncture placed between the third and the fourth lumbar spines: because the spinal cord ends above that level, because the dura ends there, because the largest cistern lies there, or because the filum terminale lies there? The answer is that the spinal cord ends above that level. In the adult the cord ends at about the first lumbar vertebra. Only the cauda equina lies below it.

Question 5687. Which fold of pia mater runs out laterally between the dorsal and the ventral roots of the spinal nerves and anchors the spinal cord to the dura? The answer is the denticulate ligament. There are about twenty-one pairs of these triangular teeth. They lie in the subarachnoid space along the whole length of the cord.

Question 5688. A described magnetic resonance image of the lower spine shows a tapering cord that ends at about the first lumbar vertebra, with a bundle of fine nerve roots below it and a thin thread continuing downward. What does the image show? The answer is the conus medullaris, the cauda equina and the filum terminale. The thread of pia anchors the cord to the coccyx.

Question 5689. The filum terminale is a continuation of which meningeal layer, and where does it attach: the dura, attaching to the coccyx; the arachnoid, attaching to the sacrum; the pia, attaching to the coccyx; or the pia, attaching to the tentorium? The answer is the pia, attaching to the coccyx. It descends from the tip of the conus medullaris, pierces the dura at the second sacral segment, and becomes the coccygeal ligament.

Question 5690. What is the name of the wide part of the subarachnoid space below the end of the spinal cord, which contains the cauda equina and cerebrospinal fluid? The answer is the lumbar cistern. It lies between the second lumbar vertebra and the second sacral segment.

Question 5691. Compared with blood plasma, how does normal cerebrospinal fluid differ: it contains more protein and less glucose, it contains about two thirds as much glucose and much less protein, it contains no glucose at all, or it contains the same amount of protein? The answer is that it holds about two thirds as much glucose and much less protein. It is clear and colorless. Its low protein content distinguishes it from blood.

Question 5692. Which of these pairs belongs together: the dura mater with the layer that follows every sulcus, the arachnoid with the layer that follows every sulcus, the pia mater with the layer that follows every sulcus, or the pia mater with the outermost layer? The answer is the pia mater with the layer that follows every sulcus. The arachnoid does not dip into the sulci. The dura is the thick outer covering.

Question 5693. A described computed tomography scan of an infant shows a very large head, two greatly enlarged fluid-filled spaces in the hemispheres, and a thin rim of brain tissue around them. What is shown? The answer is hydrocephalus, with dilated lateral ventricles and compressed brain. In an infant the sutures are open, so the skull enlarges instead of the pressure rising.

Question 5694. A constructed illustration: a described patient has a stiff neck, a headache and pain when the hip is flexed with the knee straight, and the cerebrospinal fluid contains many white cells. Which tissue is inflamed? The answer is the meninges, inflamed in meningitis. The stiffness and the pain on stretching are signs of meningeal irritation.

Question 5695. Into which channel does most of the cerebrospinal fluid pass when it is absorbed: the superior sagittal sinus, the internal jugular vein, the straight sinus, or the cavernous sinus? The answer is the superior sagittal sinus. The arachnoid granulations project into it and into its lateral lacunae.

Question 5696. The subarachnoid space extends forward around which cranial nerve as far as the eyeball, and what does raised pressure inside it produce? The answer is the optic nerve, and raised pressure produces swelling of the optic disc. The fluid-filled sheath surrounds the nerve behind the eye.

Question 5697. A described specimen of the brain shows a sickle-shaped fold standing in the midline of the cerebrum and a tent-shaped fold beneath the occipital lobes, with a vein running along the line where the two folds meet. Which vein is it? The answer is the straight sinus. It runs where the falx cerebri joins the tentorium cerebelli.

Question 5698. At which level does the dura mater of the spinal cord end, and what is the name of the ligament that continues below that point? The answer is the second sacral segment, with the coccygeal ligament below it. The dura is prolonged over the filum terminale as a slender covering.

Question 5699. Which of these is a direct effect of the cerebrospinal fluid on the brain: it lowers the effective weight of the brain from about 1,400 grams, it supplies the neurons with oxygen, it produces the impulses of the cortex, or it carries venous blood away? The answer is that it lowers the effective weight of the brain to about 50 grams. The brain floats instead of resting on the skull.

Question 5700. A described radiograph of the skull shows a thin area where four bones meet at the side of the vault, above the ear. What is that area called, and why does it matter? The answer is the pterion, where the frontal, the parietal, the temporal and the sphenoid bones meet. The middle meningeal artery runs beneath it. A blow there can tear the artery and cause bleeding outside the dura.

To summarize, the autonomic nervous system works through two neurons in series, a myelinated preganglionic fiber that leaves the central nervous system and an unmyelinated postganglionic fiber that reaches the organ, and the sympathetic division is the thoracolumbar outflow while the parasympathetic division is the craniosacral outflow. The sympathetic trunk, its cervical ganglia, the splanchnic nerves and the prevertebral ganglia distribute the sympathetic fibers, the vagus nerve carries about four fifths of the parasympathetic outflow, and the pelvic splanchnic nerves supply the pelvic organs, with the adrenal medulla, the sweat glands and the enteric plexuses as the exceptions and additions worth remembering. The meninges are the dura, the arachnoid and the pia, and the dural folds, the venous sinuses and the subarachnoid cisterns organize the spaces they enclose. The cerebrospinal fluid is made by the choroid plexus in the ventricles, flows through the interventricular foramen, the third ventricle, the cerebral aqueduct and the fourth ventricle into the subarachnoid space, and is returned to the superior sagittal sinus by the arachnoid granulations.
