# Chapter 106 — Cardiovascular Development

This chapter follows the embryo from the first beating of the heart tube to the finished organs, and it holds one hundred questions on how the body is built. It begins with the heart and the great vessels, moves to the nervous system, then to the bones and the muscles, and closes with the abnormalities that appear when a step goes wrong. Each question is answered immediately after it is asked, and the reason follows the answer. This is educational material, and it supports rather than replaces an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 10501. Which chamber of the heart is formed mainly by the cells of the first heart field? The answer is the left ventricle. The first heart field lies in the splanchnic mesoderm and folds into the primary heart tube. The second heart field adds the right ventricle and much of the outflow tract.

Question 10502. Which segment of the heart tube lies between the primitive atrium and the bulbus cordis: the sinus venosus, the primitive ventricle, the truncus arteriosus, or the aortic sac? The answer is the primitive ventricle. It follows the primitive atrium in the series, and the bulbus cordis follows it and becomes most of the right ventricle.

Question 10503. The single heart tube is formed when two vessels in the pericardial cavity fuse in the midline. What are they called? The answer is the endocardial tubes. They arise from the cardiogenic mesoderm on each side and are brought together as the embryo folds. Their fusion begins at the cranial end.

Question 10504. Which of these provides the muscular wall of the heart tube: the endocardium, the cardiac jelly, the splanchnic mesoderm around the tube, or the proepicardial organ? The answer is the splanchnic mesoderm, whose cells become the myocardium. The endocardium lines the lumen and the cardiac jelly lies between the two.

Question 10505. The dilated venous end of the heart tube, later absorbed into the wall of the right atrium, is called what? The answer is the sinus venosus. Its right horn is taken into the right atrium and its left horn becomes the coronary sinus. Blood from the yolk sac, the placenta and the body wall reaches the heart through it.

Question 10506. In which direction does the heart tube normally bend, and what is that bending called? The answer is that it bends to the right, a movement called dextral looping. That bend places the future right ventricle on the right and the future left ventricle on the left. A bend to the left is a rare reversal.

Question 10507. A described section of an early embryo shows a straight tube in the pericardial cavity, wide at one end and narrow at the other, with the narrow end opening into arched vessels. Which segment lies at the narrow end? The answer is the truncus arteriosus, which continues into the aortic sac and the aortic arches. The wide venous end begins with the sinus venosus.

Question 10508. The bulbus cordis lies between which two segments of the heart tube, and which chamber does most of it become? The answer is that it lies between the primitive ventricle and the truncus arteriosus, and most of it becomes the right ventricle. Its proximal part joins the right ventricle.

Question 10509. Which structure divides the truncus arteriosus into the aorta and the pulmonary trunk: the septum primum, the aorticopulmonary septum, the endocardial cushions, or the muscular ventricular septum? The answer is the aorticopulmonary septum, which grows through the truncus in a spiral. Cells from the neural crest take part in it, and its spiral course makes the two great vessels cross.

Question 10510. True or false: the heart is the first organ to begin working in the embryo. The answer is true. It begins to beat during the fourth week, while most other organs are still faint outlines. Blood moves through the embryo before the kidneys, the lungs or the gut take shape.

Question 10511. The opening in the atrial septum that lets blood pass from the right atrium to the left before birth is called what? The answer is the foramen ovale, bounded by the free edge of the septum secundum. The flap of the septum primum lies over it on the left and closes it after birth, leaving the fossa ovalis.

Question 10512. Which part of the interventricular septum forms from the fusion of the endocardial cushions with the aorticopulmonary septum: the muscular part, the membranous part, the inlet part, or the trabecular part? The answer is the membranous part. The muscular part grows upward from the floor of the ventricle, and a defect is left below the aortic valve when the membranous part fails to close.

Question 10513. A described cast of the great vessels shows one wide trunk leaving the ventricles, with a spiral ridge inside dividing it into two channels. Which structure is described? The answer is the aorticopulmonary septum, whose two channels become the aorta and the pulmonary trunk. Its spiral course turns the pulmonary trunk in front of the aorta.

Question 10514. Which aortic arch artery lies immediately caudal to the fourth arch on each side, and what do its branches become? The answer is the sixth aortic arch, whose branches become the pulmonary arteries. On the left the sixth arch also forms the ductus arteriosus. The fifth arch is rudimentary and usually disappears.

Question 10515. A constructed illustration: a newborn has a continuous murmur and bounding pulses, and the short vessel joining the pulmonary trunk to the descending aorta has stayed open. Which structure is involved? The answer is the ductus arteriosus, which before birth carries blood from the pulmonary trunk into the aorta. It normally closes after birth and becomes the ligamentum arteriosum.

Question 10516. Which pair belongs together: the right horn of the sinus venosus with the smooth wall of the right atrium, the left horn with the ligamentum teres, the left umbilical vein with the ligamentum venosum, or the ductus venosus with the coronary sinus? The answer is the right horn with the smooth wall of the right atrium. The left horn becomes the coronary sinus, while the ductus venosus becomes the ligamentum venosum.

Question 10517. Which of these is formed from the right anterior cardinal vein together with the right common cardinal vein: the superior vena cava, the inferior vena cava, the azygos vein, or the left brachiocephalic vein? The answer is the superior vena cava. The left brachiocephalic vein comes from an anastomosis between the two anterior cardinal veins, and the azygos vein from the right posterior cardinal vein.

Question 10518. Which vessel carries oxygenated blood from the placenta to the fetal liver? The answer is the umbilical vein, in particular the left umbilical vein. It runs inside the umbilical cord and enters the liver, where most of its blood passes into the ductus venosus. The right umbilical vein disappears early.

Question 10519. A described dissection of a fetal heart shows a flap of thin tissue on the left side of the atrial septum that can be pushed aside with a probe. Which structure is shown? The answer is the valve of the foramen ovale, formed by the septum primum. It lets blood pass from the right atrium to the left, and after birth it seals the opening.

Question 10520. Which of these carries the most oxygenated blood in the fetus: the umbilical vein, an umbilical artery, the ductus arteriosus, or the superior vena cava? The answer is the umbilical vein, which returns from the placenta where the fetal blood has taken up oxygen. The umbilical arteries carry the least oxygenated blood back to the placenta.

Question 10521. The remnant of the ductus arteriosus lies immediately distal to which structure, and what is that remnant called? The answer is that it lies immediately distal to the left subclavian artery, at the end of the aortic arch, and the remnant is the ligamentum arteriosum. The left recurrent laryngeal nerve hooks around it.

Question 10522. A constructed illustration: a deeply cyanosed newborn is found to have the aorta arising from the right ventricle and the pulmonary trunk arising from the left. Which developmental error explains this? The answer is a failure of the aorticopulmonary septum to spiral. The septum still divides the truncus into two vessels, but it does not twist, so they stay parallel.

Question 10523. In tetralogy of Fallot the aorticopulmonary septum deviates in which direction: anteriorly, posteriorly, to the left, or to the right? The answer is anteriorly, which narrows the pulmonary outflow and leaves the aorta overriding the ventricular septal defect. The other two features are the septal defect itself and right ventricular hypertrophy.

Question 10524. Name the fetal vessel that carries oxygenated blood to the fetus and the vessels that carry it away. The answer is the umbilical vein, which brings oxygenated blood to the fetus, and the two umbilical arteries, which take it away. All three run in the umbilical cord, and the arteries arise from the internal iliac arteries.

Question 10525. A described cross section of a newborn heart shows a probe passing from the right atrium into the left atrium through an opening in the septum. Which opening is shown, and what closes it? The answer is the foramen ovale, closed by the flap of the septum primum. The rise in pressure in the left atrium presses that flap against the septum secundum, leaving the fossa ovalis.

Question 10526. Which of the three germ layers gives rise to the entire nervous system? The answer is the ectoderm. A thickened strip of it, the neural plate, lies above the notochord and folds into a groove and then a tube. The skin that covers the back comes from the same layer.

Question 10527. Which of these lists the three primary brain vesicles: the prosencephalon, the mesencephalon and the rhombencephalon; the telencephalon, the diencephalon and the myelencephalon; the cerebrum, the cerebellum and the brainstem; or the thalamus, the pons and the medulla? The answer is the prosencephalon, the mesencephalon and the rhombencephalon. The other names belong to the secondary vesicles or to adult structures.

Question 10528. Which cells arise at the crest of the neural folds, leave the nervous system and migrate widely through the embryo? The answer is the neural crest cells. They form the sensory and autonomic ganglia, the Schwann cells and the adrenal medulla, among other structures, and are sometimes called the fourth germ layer.

Question 10529. Which secondary brain vesicle gives rise to the medulla oblongata: the myelencephalon, the metencephalon, the diencephalon, or the telencephalon? The answer is the myelencephalon, the lowest part of the hindbrain. The metencephalon gives the pons and the cerebellum, and the diencephalon gives the thalamus and the hypothalamus.

Question 10530. A described transverse section of an early embryo shows a closed tube just beneath the surface ectoderm, with a solid rod of cells immediately below it. Which two structures are shown? The answer is the neural tube above and the notochord below. The tube will form the brain and the spinal cord, and the notochord later becomes part of the intervertebral disc.

Question 10531. The groove on the inner wall of the neural tube that separates the motor columns from the sensory columns is called what? The answer is the sulcus limitans. It runs along the length of the tube on each side. The basal plate lies below it and becomes motor, while the alar plate above it becomes sensory.

Question 10532. Which structure produces most of the cerebrospinal fluid: the ependymal lining of the central canal, the arachnoid granulations, the choroid plexus, or the pia mater? The answer is the choroid plexus, a fringe of vascular tissue covered by ependymal cells that hangs in each ventricle. The arachnoid granulations absorb the fluid back into the venous blood.

Question 10533. The pouch of oral ectoderm that grows upward toward the base of the brain and forms the anterior lobe of the pituitary gland is called what? The answer is Rathke pouch. It loses its connection with the roof of the mouth and comes to lie in the sella turcica, while the posterior lobe comes from the diencephalon.

Question 10534. In which region does the neural tube first close: the cervical region, the head, the lower back, or the tail? The answer is the cervical region. Closure begins there and runs in both directions, so the tube is briefly open at each end. The cranial opening closes a little before the caudal one.

Question 10535. A described section of the spinal cord of a fetus shows a narrow central canal lined by cells, with a rounded mass of gray matter on each side. Which plate forms the dorsal part of that gray matter? The answer is the alar plate, which lies above the sulcus limitans and receives the sensory fibers. The basal plate below gives the motor cells of the ventral horn.

Question 10536. Which structure lies immediately ventral to the neural tube, and what does it become in the adult? The answer is the notochord, which becomes the nucleus pulposus of the intervertebral discs. It runs beneath the tube and induces the overlying ectoderm to form the nervous system, and the vertebral bodies later form around it.

Question 10537. Which of these is a derivative of the neural crest: the Schwann cell, the oligodendrocyte, the astrocyte, or the ependymal cell? The answer is the Schwann cell, which wraps the axons of the peripheral nerves and forms their myelin sheath. The other three are support cells of the central nervous system and come from the neural tube.

Question 10538. True or false: the microglia of the brain are derived from the neural crest. The answer is false. The microglia come from mesodermal precursors that migrate into the nervous system from the yolk sac. They are the resident scavenger cells of the brain.

Question 10539. Which cells line the ventricles of the brain and the central canal of the spinal cord in the adult? The answer is the ependymal cells, a single layer that keeps the cerebrospinal fluid in contact with the nervous tissue. The choroid plexus is built from the same cells together with blood vessels.

Question 10540. The channel that connects the third ventricle with the fourth in the adult brain is derived from the cavity of which vesicle: the telencephalon, the diencephalon, the mesencephalon, or the myelencephalon? The answer is the mesencephalon, whose cavity narrows into the cerebral aqueduct as the walls thicken. The cavity of the telencephalon becomes the lateral ventricles.

Question 10541. A described section of a fetal brain shows a thin roof over a large cavity, with a fringe of vascular tissue hanging into it and thick walls of gray matter around it. Which cavity and which structure are shown? The answer is a lateral ventricle with its choroid plexus. The thin roof and the large cavity belong to the telencephalon, which forms the cerebral hemisphere.

Question 10542. The fourth ventricle lies between which two structures, and through which openings does it communicate with the space around the brain? The answer is that it lies between the cerebellum behind and the pons and medulla in front, and it communicates through the lateral apertures and the median aperture. The fluid leaves through them into the subarachnoid space.

Question 10543. Which of these is not derived from the neural crest: the adrenal medulla, the melanocytes of the skin, the pia mater and the arachnoid mater, or the cortex of the adrenal gland? The answer is the cortex of the adrenal gland, which comes from the intermediate mesoderm. The medulla of the same gland comes from the neural crest and behaves like a sympathetic ganglion.

Question 10544. A constructed illustration: a newborn has a thin sac over the lower back, and the sac contains nerve tissue. Which defect is this, and which opening failed to close? The answer is a myelomeningocele, from failure of the caudal neuropore. The vertebral arches are missing, so the sac and its nerves lie outside the canal.

Question 10545. Failure of the cranial neuropore to close leaves which defect: anencephaly, spina bifida, hydrocephalus, or a cleft palate? The answer is anencephaly. Without a closed tube the cranial vault fails to form and the exposed brain tissue degenerates. Spina bifida follows failure at the caudal end.

Question 10546. Which pair belongs together: the mesencephalon with the cerebral aqueduct, the myelencephalon with the cerebellum, the diencephalon with the medulla, or the telencephalon with the third ventricle? The answer is the mesencephalon with the cerebral aqueduct. The metencephalon gives the cerebellum, while the diencephalon encloses the third ventricle.

Question 10547. Name the two layers of the meninges that are derived from the neural crest. The answer is the pia mater and the arachnoid mater. Together they form the leptomeninges, the delicate coverings that lie against the brain and the spinal cord. The thick outer dura mater comes from the mesoderm.

Question 10548. Within the wall of the neural tube, does the alar plate lie above or below the sulcus limitans, and is it sensory or motor? The answer is that the alar plate lies above the sulcus limitans and is sensory, while the basal plate below it is motor. That dorsal sensory and ventral motor arrangement persists in the adult spinal cord.

Question 10549. A constructed illustration: an infant's head enlarges steadily, and the lateral ventricles are dilated while the channel between the third and the fourth ventricle is narrow. Which structure is narrowed? The answer is the cerebral aqueduct, part of the midbrain. Its narrowing blocks the flow of cerebrospinal fluid and dilates the ventricles above it.

Question 10550. A described section of a fetal brain shows an outpouching of the wall of the diencephalon that reaches the surface ectoderm and will form the retina and the optic nerve. Which structure is described? The answer is the optic vesicle, from the diencephalon. The surface ectoderm thickens into the lens and the stalk narrows into the optic nerve.

Question 10551. Which blocks of mesoderm lie on each side of the neural tube and give rise to the vertebrae and the skeletal muscles? The answer is the somites, which form in pairs from the paraxial mesoderm beginning near the head. About forty-four pairs are formed, and each one splits into three parts.

Question 10552. Which part of a somite gives rise to the vertebral column: the sclerotome, the myotome, the dermatome, or the somatopleure? The answer is the sclerotome, whose cells migrate around the notochord and the neural tube to enclose them. The myotome forms muscle and the dermatome forms the dermis of the back.

Question 10553. The thickened strip of ectoderm at the tip of a developing limb bud that directs its growth is called what? The answer is the apical ectodermal ridge. It lies at the outer margin of the bud and signals the mesoderm beneath it to keep growing. Removing it stops the limb from lengthening.

Question 10554. Which of these bones develops by intramembranous ossification, without a cartilage model: the parietal bone, the femur, the humerus, or a vertebra? The answer is the parietal bone, which forms directly in a sheet of mesenchyme. The other three all pass through a cartilage model first.

Question 10555. A described section through a limb bud shows a solid core of mesenchyme covered by ectoderm, with a thickened ridge of ectoderm along the outer edge. What is the ridge, and what does it do? The answer is the apical ectodermal ridge, which drives growth from the shoulder toward the fingers and keeps a zone of dividing cells alive beneath it.

Question 10556. True or false: the upper limb buds appear before the lower limb buds. The answer is true. The arms begin as small swellings on the body wall a few days before the legs do. Later the legs catch up in length, while the arms stay ahead in the finer movements of the fingers.

Question 10557. Which part of the somite gives the deep muscles of the back, and which nerves supply them? The answer is the epaxial part of the myotome, supplied by the dorsal rami of the spinal nerves. The hypaxial part forms the muscles of the body wall and the limbs and is supplied by the ventral rami.

Question 10558. Which part of a somite forms the dermis of the back: the sclerotome, the myotome, the dermatome, or the notochord? The answer is the dermatome, whose cells spread beneath the ectoderm and form the deep layer of the skin of the back. The notochord is not part of a somite and becomes the nucleus pulposus.

Question 10559. Which structure lies in the center of the intervertebral disc, and from which tissue does the ring around it develop? The answer is the nucleus pulposus, the remnant of the notochord, with the annulus fibrosus around it developing from the sclerotome. Together they allow movement between two vertebral bodies.

Question 10560. A described section of a developing long bone shows a cartilage shaft with a collar of bone around its middle and a cavity forming inside. What is the collar? The answer is the bony collar around the diaphysis, the first bone to appear. It forms beneath the perichondrium without a cartilage model, and the shaft inside is hollowed into the marrow cavity.

Question 10561. Where does the primary ossification center of a long bone appear: in the shaft, in the upper epiphysis, in the lower epiphysis, or in the epiphyseal plate? The answer is in the shaft, that is, in the diaphysis. Secondary centers appear later in the epiphyses at each end, and growth in length continues at the epiphyseal plate.

Question 10562. The band of cartilage that separates the shaft of a growing long bone from its end and allows the bone to lengthen is called what? The answer is the epiphyseal plate, which lies between the diaphysis and the epiphysis. Cartilage is replaced by bone on the shaft side, and when the plate closes, growth in length stops.

Question 10563. A constructed illustration: a newborn's foot is turned inward and downward, and the sole faces the other foot. Which deformity is this? The answer is clubfoot, also called talipes equinovarus. The heel is drawn up and the forefoot turns inward. The bones themselves are normal at first, and the deformity lies in the soft tissues around the ankle.

Question 10564. Which of these describes the rotation of the upper limb bud: it rotates laterally so the extensor muscles lie posterior, it rotates medially so the extensors lie anterior, it does not rotate, or it rotates so the thumb lies medial? The answer is that it rotates laterally, so the extensor muscles come to lie on the back of the arm. The lower limb rotates the other way.

Question 10565. Why is the diaphragm supplied by nerves from the upper cervical segments, although the muscle lies far below the neck? The answer is that the muscle develops in the neck from the septum transversum and then descends, carrying its nerve supply with it. That is why the phrenic nerve runs so long a course.

Question 10566. A described radiograph of the hand of a young child shows a gap of dark cartilage between the shaft of each bone and a separate rounded center at its end. What are those centers, and what will happen to them? The answer is the secondary ossification centers of the epiphyses. Each will grow until it fills the epiphysis and then fuses with the shaft, and the dark gap is the epiphyseal plate.

Question 10567. Which pair belongs together: the sclerotome with the vertebral body, the myotome with the dermis, the dermatome with the skeletal muscle, or the somite with the notochord? The answer is the sclerotome with the vertebral body. The myotome gives skeletal muscle and the dermatome the dermis of the back, while the notochord is a separate midline rod.

Question 10568. True or false: the sternum develops from two bars of mesoderm that fuse in the midline, and they join from above downward. The answer is true. The two sternal bars form in the front of the chest wall and meet first at the top, which is why a split in the sternum is found at the lower end.

Question 10569. How are the separate fingers and toes formed from the solid plate of the limb bud: by growth of new tissue between them, by cell death between them, by fusion of separate rays, or by a second wave of somite cells? The answer is by cell death in the tissue between them, a programmed breakdown of the mesenchyme between the digital rays. When that fails, the digits stay joined.

Question 10570. Name the three parts of a somite and say what each becomes. The answer is the sclerotome, which becomes the vertebral column and the ribs, the myotome, which becomes skeletal muscle, and the dermatome, which becomes the dermis of the back. All three come from the same block of paraxial mesoderm beside the neural tube.

Question 10571. A constructed illustration: a child has a short neck, a low hairline at the back and very limited turning of the head, and the cervical vertebrae are fused. Which anomaly is this? The answer is the Klippel-Feil anomaly, in which the cervical somites fail to separate so two or more vertebrae are joined. The number of fused segments varies.

Question 10572. The upward peg of bone on which the head turns, formed from the sclerotome of the first cervical segment, is called what? The answer is the dens of the axis, also called the odontoid process. It belongs developmentally to the first cervical vertebra but fuses with the second, and the first loses its own body and becomes a ring.

Question 10573. A described sagittal section of the vertebral column of a fetus shows a rod of tissue running through the middle of each vertebral body and swelling inside each disc. What is that rod, and what will remain of it? The answer is the notochord, whose remnant inside each disc is the nucleus pulposus. The vertebral bodies form around it and replace it almost completely.

Question 10574. A constructed illustration: a newborn girl has a hip that can be pushed out of its socket, and the socket itself is shallow. Which developmental problem is this, and which structure is too shallow? The answer is developmental dysplasia of the hip, with a shallow acetabulum. The socket forms from the ilium, the ischium and the pubis and deepens late in development.

Question 10575. An extra rib that arises from the seventh cervical vertebra and may press on the lower part of the brachial plexus develops from which structure: the costal process of a cervical sclerotome, the sternal bar, the dermatome, or the notochord? The answer is the costal process of a cervical sclerotome. Every vertebra has costal elements, and in the thorax they grow into ribs.

Question 10576. Which congenital heart defect is the most common of all, a hole in the septum between the two ventricles? The answer is the ventricular septal defect. It usually lies in the membranous part of the septum, just below the aortic valve. Many small defects close on their own.

Question 10577. Which of these is the most common cyanotic congenital heart defect: the ventricular septal defect, tetralogy of Fallot, coarctation of the aorta, or an atrial septal defect? The answer is tetralogy of Fallot, whose four features are pulmonary stenosis, a ventricular septal defect, an overriding aorta and right ventricular hypertrophy. The other three are acyanotic.

Question 10578. A described radiograph of a child's chest shows a small boot-shaped heart with a lifted apex and dark, underfilled lungs. Which congenital defect does this suggest? The answer is tetralogy of Fallot. The narrow pulmonary outflow leaves the lungs underfilled, and the enlarged right ventricle lifts the apex.

Question 10579. A newborn has a continuous murmur and bounding pulses, and the vessel that joined the pulmonary trunk to the descending aorta before birth has failed to close. Which vessel is it, and what should it have become? The answer is the ductus arteriosus, which should have become the ligamentum arteriosum. While open, it lets blood flow from the aorta into the pulmonary circulation.

Question 10580. A child has a defect in the upper part of the atrial septum near the fossa ovalis, and blood passes from the left atrium to the right. Which defect is this: an ostium secundum atrial septal defect, a ventricular septal defect, a patent ductus arteriosus, or tricuspid atresia? The answer is an ostium secundum atrial septal defect. The shunt runs from left to right because the left atrial pressure is higher after birth.

Question 10581. Which pair belongs together: a patent ductus arteriosus with the vessel joining the pulmonary trunk to the aorta, an atrial septal defect with the ventricular septum, a ventricular septal defect with the atrial septum, or coarctation with the pulmonary valve? The answer is a patent ductus arteriosus with the vessel joining the pulmonary trunk to the aorta. The two septal defects are named for the septum they lie in.

Question 10582. The fossa ovalis, the remnant of the fetal opening between the atria, lies in which part of the interatrial septum, and on which side does its flap lie? The answer is that it lies in the lower part of the septum, with the flap of the septum primum on its left side. The firmer septum secundum lies on the right.

Question 10583. In the adult form of coarctation of the aorta, where does the narrowing lie: just proximal to the ductus arteriosus, just distal to the ductus arteriosus, at the aortic root, or in the ascending aorta? The answer is just distal to the ligamentum arteriosum, the remnant of the ductus. The infantile form lies proximal to it, and the adult form produces collateral circulation and notching of the ribs.

Question 10584. The ductus arteriosus joins which two vessels, and where does its aortic end lie relative to the left subclavian artery? The answer is that it joins the pulmonary trunk to the aortic arch, and its aortic end lies just distal to the origin of the left subclavian artery. The left recurrent laryngeal nerve hooks around it there.

Question 10585. The congenital defect in which the two shelves of tissue that should meet in the midline of the roof of the mouth fail to fuse is called what? The answer is a cleft palate. The shelves grow from the maxillary processes and meet from front to back, and when they fail to meet the mouth and the nose stay connected.

Question 10586. In which anomaly is the tricuspid valve displaced downward into the right ventricle, so that part of the ventricle lies above the valve? The answer is the Ebstein anomaly. The displaced valve leaves the upper part of the right ventricle as part of the atrium, so the right atrium is large and the working right ventricle is small.

Question 10587. In transposition of the great arteries, what is the essential error: the aorta arises from the right ventricle and the pulmonary trunk from the left, both vessels arise from the right ventricle, the septum fails to close, or the pulmonary trunk is narrowed? The answer is that the aorta arises from the right ventricle and the pulmonary trunk from the left. The two circulations then run in parallel.

Question 10588. A described section of the heart of a newborn shows a very small left ventricle with a thick wall, a tiny aortic valve and a large right ventricle. Which anomaly is shown? The answer is a hypoplastic left heart. The left side cannot pump blood to the body, so the right ventricle must do it through the ductus arteriosus.

Question 10589. In a heart with tetralogy of Fallot, the aorta lies immediately above which defect, and what is that position called? The answer is that it lies immediately above the ventricular septal defect, a position called overriding. The aorticopulmonary septum has deviated, so the aorta sits over the hole and blood from both ventricles can enter it.

Question 10590. True or false: a cleft lip results from failure of the maxillary process to fuse with the medial nasal process. The answer is true. The two processes meet on each side of the upper lip and normally merge. When they do not, a gap is left, which may reach into the nose and the palate.

Question 10591. Which pair belongs together: anencephaly with failure of the cranial neuropore to close, spina bifida with failure of the cranial neuropore, a cleft palate with failure of the neural tube, or a patent ductus arteriosus with failure of the neural crest to migrate? The answer is anencephaly with failure of the cranial neuropore. Spina bifida belongs to the caudal opening and a cleft palate to the facial processes.

Question 10592. In which anomaly is the lower part of the cerebellum and the brainstem displaced downward through the foramen magnum? The answer is the Chiari malformation. The common form is associated with a myelomeningocele and with hydrocephalus, and the displaced tissue can block the flow of cerebrospinal fluid.

Question 10593. A child has a patch of hair and a dimple over the lower spine, with no sac, and a vertebral arch is missing beneath the skin. Which defect is this: spina bifida occulta, a myelomeningocele, an encephalocele, or a meningocele? The answer is spina bifida occulta. The arch is missing, but the cord and its coverings stay inside the canal.

Question 10594. A constructed illustration: a newborn has no cranial vault above the orbits, and the tissue at the top of the head is degenerated. Which defect has occurred, and at which end of the neural tube? The answer is anencephaly, from failure of the cranial neuropore to close during the fourth week. Without a closed tube the vault cannot form over the brain.

Question 10595. On a described chest radiograph of a child with coarctation of the aorta, small notches are seen along which border of which ribs? The answer is along the inferior border of the third to the eighth ribs. The enlarged intercostal arteries press on the bone from below and hollow it out.

Question 10596. A constructed illustration: a newborn with a large ventricular septal defect is breathless and feeds poorly, and a loud murmur is heard over the left side of the chest. Which structure carries the extra blood flow? The answer is the pulmonary trunk, since blood is shunted from the left ventricle into the lungs. The murmur comes from the turbulent flow rather than from the hole itself.

Question 10597. A double aortic arch forms a complete ring around which pair of structures: the trachea and the esophagus, the aorta and the pulmonary trunk, the two bronchi, or the two recurrent laryngeal nerves? The answer is the trachea and the esophagus. The two arches pass on either side of them and compress both, so the infant wheezes and feeds poorly.

Question 10598. A constructed illustration: a premature infant has a machinery murmur, and the vessel between the pulmonary trunk and the aorta has stayed open. Which structure is involved, and why does blood flow from the aorta into the lungs? The answer is the ductus arteriosus. After birth the aortic pressure is higher, so blood runs the other way and the lungs receive too much.

Question 10599. A described section of a heart shows a single large atrioventricular opening with a common valve, a defect in the lower part of the atrial septum and a defect in the upper part of the ventricular septum. Which anomaly is shown? The answer is a common atrioventricular canal. The endocardial cushions have failed to fuse, so the septa above and below stay incomplete.

Question 10600. A newborn is in respiratory distress, and loops of bowel are found in the left side of the chest. Which structure failed to close the opening through which they entered: the pleuroperitoneal membrane, the septum transversum, the dorsal mesentery, or the pleuropericardial membrane? The answer is the pleuroperitoneal membrane, which closes the pleuroperitoneal canal. When it fails, the abdominal contents herniate into the chest.

To summarize, the heart begins as two endocardial tubes that fuse into a single tube, which loops to the right and is then divided by the aorticopulmonary septum, the endocardial cushions and the parts of the interventricular septum. The aortic arches, the cardinal veins and the umbilical and vitelline veins leave behind the great vessels and their adult remnants, among them the ligamentum arteriosum, the ligamentum teres, the ligamentum venosum and the fossa ovalis. The nervous system comes from the neural plate, closes first in the cervical region and divides into five secondary vesicles, while the neural crest supplies the sensory and autonomic ganglia, the Schwann cells, the adrenal medulla, and the pia mater and arachnoid mater. The skeleton and the muscles come from the somites, whose sclerotome, myotome and dermatome become the vertebrae, the muscles and the skin of the back, while the limb bud grows under the apical ectodermal ridge. Congenital defects are the visible record of a step that did not finish, from the septal defects and tetralogy of Fallot to the neural tube defects, the cleft lip and palate, and the anomalies of the hip and the cervical spine.
