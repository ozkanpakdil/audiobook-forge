# Chapter 86 — Ear

The ear is two organs in one small space: a mechanical receiver that gathers sound, and a fluid-filled analyzer that turns it into nerve signals. This chapter follows the path of sound from the auricle, through the external acoustic meatus, across the tympanic membrane, along the three ossicles of the middle ear, and into the cochlea and the vestibular apparatus of the inner ear. It holds one hundred questions, and every question gives its answer immediately after it, with the reason, so nothing has to be held in mind until the end. The items follow the route the sound travels, beginning with the external ear, then the middle ear, the ossicles, the tympanic membrane and the inner ear. This is educational material, and it supports rather than replaces an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 8501. Which part of the ear is the visible elastic cartilage flap on the side of the head, and what tissue forms its lowest part? The answer is the auricle, also called the pinna, whose lobule is fat and fibrous tissue rather than cartilage. The auricle collects sound and funnels it into the ear canal.

Question 8502. How long is the external acoustic meatus in an adult: about 10 millimeters, about 25 millimeters, about 50 millimeters, or about 80 millimeters? The answer is about 25 millimeters. Most of that length lies within the temporal bone, and only the outer part is cartilaginous. In an infant the canal is shorter, which is one reason the small ear needs care.

Question 8503. A described otoscopic view shows the outer part of the ear canal lined by skin with fine hairs and yellow wax, and the deeper part lined by thin pale skin with no hairs. Which parts of the canal are shown? The answer is the cartilaginous part outside and the bony part deeper. The cartilaginous part carries the hairs and the wax glands, while the bony part has neither.

Question 8504. Which nerve supplies the skin of the anterior wall and the roof of the external acoustic meatus? The answer is the auriculotemporal nerve, a branch of the mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve. It also supplies the skin in front of the ear and over the temple. Pain from a boil in that part of the canal is therefore felt in front of the ear.

Question 8505. Which glands in the ear canal produce the wax that protects it, and in which part of the canal do they lie? The answer is the ceruminous glands, which are modified sweat glands lying in the cartilaginous part. Their secretion mixes with the oil of the sebaceous glands to form cerumen.

Question 8506. In which part of the ear canal are hairs, sebaceous glands and ceruminous glands found together: the bony part, the cartilaginous part, both parts equally, or neither part? The answer is the cartilaginous part. It is the outer third and is continuous with the skin of the auricle. The bony part is lined by skin so thin that it lies on the periosteum.

Question 8507. A constructed illustration: a swimmer has an itchy, painful ear canal that is tender when the auricle is pulled, and the canal is swollen and wet. Which condition does this describe, and which layer is inflamed? The answer is inflammation of the external ear, with the skin of the canal involved. Such illustrations show why the canal is a common site of trouble after water exposure. The care of it belongs to the treating clinician.

Question 8508. True or false: the external acoustic meatus is a straight tube that runs directly from the auricle to the tympanic membrane. The answer is false. The canal is shaped like a gentle S, curving first forward and then backward. That is why the auricle is pulled upward and backward during examination in an adult.

Question 8509. What name is given to the curved rim that forms the outer border of the auricle? The answer is the helix. It runs from the front of the ear around the top and down to the lobule. The ridge inside it, dividing the concha from the rest of the auricle, is the antihelix.

Question 8510. Which nerve supplies the skin of the back of the auricle and the lower part of the concha: the auriculotemporal nerve, the great auricular nerve, the lesser occipital nerve, or the facial nerve? The answer is the great auricular nerve, which comes from the cervical plexus. The lesser occipital nerve supplies the upper part of the back of the auricle. The auriculotemporal nerve serves the front.

Question 8511. Which nerve explains a cough when the skin of the deep canal is touched with a speculum: the facial nerve, the glossopharyngeal nerve, the auricular branch of the vagus nerve, or the mandibular division of the trigeminal? The answer is the auricular branch of the vagus nerve. It supplies the posterior wall of the canal and the concha. Because the vagus also supplies the throat, stimulating its auricular branch can provoke a cough.

Question 8512. A described illustration shows an infant whose ear canal runs almost horizontally and is shorter than an adult canal, with the tympanic membrane nearly flat. How should the auricle be drawn during examination, and why? The answer is that it is pulled downward and backward. The infant canal is short and nearly straight, so the membrane is easier to see from below.

Question 8513. In a standing person the external acoustic meatus is not directed straight sideways. In which two directions does it run as it leaves the auricle? The answer is forward and slightly downward. That direction protects the canal and helps it drain.

Question 8514. Which part of the auricle is composed of fat and fibrous tissue and contains no cartilage? The answer is the lobule, the soft lowest part of the ear. All the rest of the auricle is supported by a single piece of elastic cartilage.

Question 8515. Which nerve supplies the skin of the anterior wall of the external acoustic meatus and the temple in front of the ear? The answer is the auriculotemporal nerve. The great auricular nerve, the lesser occipital nerve and the auricular branch of the vagus supply the back of the auricle. The pattern explains why ear pain can be felt in the jaw or the temple.

Question 8516. Which of these pairs belongs together: the helix with the prominent flap in front of the canal, the tragus with the curved outer rim, the concha with the deep bowl beside the canal, or the lobule with the firm cartilage of the ear? The answer is the concha with the deep bowl beside the canal. The tragus is the flap in front of the canal, and the lobule has no cartilage.

Question 8517. Into which lymph nodes does the auricle drain: only the deep cervical nodes, the parotid nodes in front with the mastoid and superficial cervical nodes behind, the nodes below the jaw, or the nodes in the armpit? The answer is the parotid nodes in front with the mastoid and superficial cervical nodes behind. The pattern follows the nerve and blood supply of the region.

Question 8518. A described study compares the ear canal to a tube closed at one end by the tympanic membrane, which boosts sounds of particular frequencies. Why does the canal behave this way, and which range does it favor? The answer is that the canal acts as a resonator, and being about 25 millimeters long it amplifies sounds of roughly 2 to 4 kilohertz. That range carries much of the information in speech.

Question 8519. A described dissection exposes the bowl-shaped hollow of the auricle that lies just outside the opening of the ear canal. What is that hollow called? The answer is the concha. The canal begins at the depth of the concha, and the tragus partly covers its opening. The concha helps gather sound into the canal.

Question 8520. A described specimen shows the auricle forming from small swellings around the first pharyngeal groove. From which arches do these swellings arise, and what does that origin explain? The answer is the first and second pharyngeal arches, which give six small hillocks. Because the middle ear and the ossicles arise from the same arches, a child born with a malformed auricle may also have a malformed middle ear.

Question 8521. Which nerve supplies the skin of the posterior wall of the ear canal and the skin over the back of the auricle? The answer is the auricular branch of the vagus nerve, with a small contribution from the great auricular nerve. The vagus branch also supplies the concha. The facial nerve sends a small branch to the same region.

Question 8522. A constructed illustration: a newborn has a small, poorly formed auricle and no visible ear canal on one side. Which structures may share the developmental fault, and why? The answer is the middle ear and the ossicles, which form from the same first and second pharyngeal arches as the auricle. The inner ear usually escapes, because it develops from a separate patch of ectoderm.

Question 8523. In which part of which bone does the tympanic cavity lie? The answer is the petrous part of the temporal bone. It is a narrow air-filled space between the tympanic membrane outside and the inner ear inside. Its roof is a thin plate of bone beneath the middle cranial fossa.

Question 8524. Which of these names the part of the tympanic cavity that lies above the level of the tympanic membrane: the hypotympanum, the mesotympanum, the epitympanum, or the mastoid antrum? The answer is the epitympanum, also called the attic. The mesotympanum lies at the level of the membrane and the hypotympanum below it.

Question 8525. A described view of the medial wall of the middle ear shows a rounded bulge of bone, an opening above it closed by the footplate of a small bone, and a second opening below it closed by a membrane. What are these three features? The answer is the promontory, the oval window and the round window. The promontory covers the first turn of the cochlea.

Question 8526. Which structure forms the roof of the tympanic cavity and separates it from the middle cranial fossa: the tegmen tympani, the mastoid antrum, the pyramid, or the promontory? The answer is the tegmen tympani. It is a thin plate of bone, in places only a fraction of a millimeter thick. Infection can spread upward through it in rare cases.

Question 8527. Which tube connects the tympanic cavity with the nasopharynx, and what is its main function? The answer is the pharyngotympanic tube, also called the auditory tube. It equalizes the pressure on the two sides of the tympanic membrane.

Question 8528. How many walls has the tympanic cavity, and which one carries the oval and round windows: four walls with the windows on the roof, five with the windows on the floor, six with the windows on the medial wall, or three with the windows on the lateral wall? The answer is six walls, with the windows on the medial wall. That medial wall faces the inner ear.

Question 8529. Why is infection of the middle ear more common in infants than in adults? The answer is that the pharyngotympanic tube is shorter, wider and more horizontal in the infant. Secretions from the throat therefore reach the middle ear more easily.

Question 8530. Which large vein lies immediately below the floor of the tympanic cavity, and which nerve runs across that floor? The answer is the internal jugular vein below, with the tympanic branch of the glossopharyngeal nerve crossing the floor. The floor is a thin plate of bone over the vein. That nerve is one of the roots of the tympanic plexus.

Question 8531. Which is the smallest skeletal muscle in the body, which nerve supplies it, and what does it do? The answer is the stapedius, supplied by the facial nerve, and it pulls the stapes to damp the vibration of the ossicular chain. It lies in a small bony pyramid on the posterior wall of the middle ear.

Question 8532. Which nerve supplies the tensor tympani muscle: the facial nerve, the mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve, the glossopharyngeal nerve, or the vagus nerve? The answer is the mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve. The tensor tympani pulls the handle of the malleus inward and tenses the tympanic membrane. The stapedius, its partner, is supplied by the facial nerve.

Question 8533. A described dissection follows a fine nerve that leaves the facial nerve in its canal, crosses the medial surface of the malleus, passes between the malleus and the incus, and leaves the middle ear by a small fissure. Which nerve is it, and what does it carry? The answer is the chorda tympani, which carries taste from the front two thirds of the tongue. After leaving the middle ear it joins the lingual nerve.

Question 8534. Which opening in the posterior wall of the tympanic cavity leads backward and upward into the mastoid antrum? The answer is the aditus to the mastoid antrum. Through it the mucous membrane of the middle ear is continuous with the air cells of the mastoid process.

Question 8535. A constructed illustration: a child has a red bulging tympanic membrane, fever and tenderness over the bone behind the ear. Which two cavities are involved, and how do they connect? The answer is the tympanic cavity and the mastoid air cells, joined by the aditus to the mastoid antrum. The illustration shows how infection passes from the middle ear into the mastoid.

Question 8536. Which of these pairs belongs together: the roof of the middle ear with the jugular bulb, the floor with the tegmen tympani, the medial wall with the oval window, or the anterior wall with the mastoid air cells? The answer is the medial wall with the oval window. The roof lies under the tegmen tympani and the floor over the jugular bulb.

Question 8537. Which structure runs in the medial wall of the middle ear just above the oval window: the chorda tympani, the facial nerve in its bony canal, the internal carotid artery, or the pharyngotympanic tube? The answer is the facial nerve in its bony canal. It runs horizontally above the oval window and then turns downward in the posterior wall. The chorda tympani branches from it lower down.

Question 8538. Into which space does the pharyngotympanic tube open, and at what level does that opening lie? The answer is the nasopharynx, at the level of the back of the nasal cavity behind the soft palate. The opening lies just above the floor of the nasopharynx. Its cartilage and muscle help it open during swallowing.

Question 8539. True or false: the tympanic cavity is filled with air and is lined by mucous membrane that is continuous with the lining of the pharyngotympanic tube. The answer is true. The cavity is an air space, and its lining is a thin mucous membrane. That continuity is why infection can travel from the throat to the middle ear.

Question 8540. What is the small bony projection on the posterior wall of the tympanic cavity, and which muscle tendon emerges from its summit? The answer is the pyramidal eminence, and the tendon of the stapedius emerges from it. Its tendon runs forward to the neck of the stapes.

Question 8541. Name the two muscles of the middle ear and the nerve that supplies each one. The answer is the tensor tympani, supplied by the mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve, and the stapedius, supplied by the facial nerve. Both act as dampers when loud sound reaches the ear.

Question 8542. A constructed illustration: a person has a discharging ear for months and then develops weakness of the face on the same side. Which nerve is involved, and why does middle ear disease threaten it? The answer is the facial nerve, which runs in a bony canal in the medial and posterior walls of the middle ear. A thin bony roof over the canal lets disease reach the nerve.

Question 8543. A described surgical view shows a nerve running horizontally above the oval window and then turning sharply downward in the posterior wall. What is the name and the course of that nerve? The answer is the facial nerve. It enters the temporal bone by the internal acoustic meatus, runs in the medial wall above the oval window, and turns down to leave by the stylomastoid foramen.

Question 8544. Which wall of the tympanic cavity separates it from the internal carotid artery, and what opening does that wall carry: the roof, the anterior wall with the pharyngotympanic tube, the floor with the jugular bulb, or the posterior wall with the aditus? The answer is the anterior wall, which carries the opening of the pharyngotympanic tube. The carotid canal lies just in front of that thin wall.

Question 8545. Which special sense is carried by the chorda tympani, and from which part of the tongue does it come? The answer is taste, from the front two thirds of the tongue. The nerve crosses the middle ear and leaves to join the lingual nerve. It also carries secretomotor fibers to the salivary glands below the jaw.

Question 8546. Which ossicle of the middle ear is attached to the tympanic membrane, and by which of its parts? The answer is the malleus, attached by its handle, also called the manubrium. The handle is fixed to the fibrous layer of the membrane along its upper part.

Question 8547. Which ossicle lies between the malleus and the stapes in the chain? The answer is the incus. Its body lies in the attic and articulates with the head of the malleus. Its long process runs downward and ends in a small knob that meets the head of the stapes.

Question 8548. Which is the smallest bone in the human body, and where does its base sit? The answer is the stapes, whose base, or footplate, sits in the oval window. It is shaped like a stirrup, with a head, a neck and two crura. Its base is held in the window by a ring of ligament.

Question 8549. Which of these lists the parts of the malleus: head, neck, lateral process and handle; body, long crus and short crus; head, neck and two crura; or base, apex and spiral lamina? The answer is head, neck, lateral process and handle. The second list describes the incus and the third describes the stapes.

Question 8550. A described specimen shows three small bones in a chain from a membrane to a small window, joined end to end and suspended in an air space by fine ligaments. Which bones are shown, and what is their function? The answer is the malleus, the incus and the stapes. They conduct vibration from the tympanic membrane to the oval window and match the impedance of air to that of fluid.

Question 8551. Which part of the incus articulates with the head of the stapes: the body, the short crus, the long crus with its lenticular process, or the lateral process? The answer is the long crus with its lenticular process. The long process runs downward behind the posterior part of the membrane. Its tip becomes the lenticular process, which forms the incudostapedial joint.

Question 8552. From which pharyngeal arches do the ossicles develop: the malleus and the incus from the first arch with the stapes from the second, all three from the first arch, all three from the second, or the malleus from the second with the others from the first? The answer is the malleus and the incus from the first arch, and the stapes from the second. The first arch also forms the muscles of mastication.

Question 8553. What is the name of the flat base of the stapes, and in which opening does it sit? The answer is the footplate, which sits in the oval window of the medial wall of the middle ear. It is fixed to the margin of the window by the annular ligament.

Question 8554. A constructed illustration: a young adult has slowly worsening hearing in one ear, and testing shows that sound is conducted less well by air than by bone while the membrane looks normal. Where is the fault likely to lie? The answer is at the stapes and the oval window, where new bone can fix the footplate in place. The illustration is the classic picture of a conductive loss with a normal membrane.

Question 8555. The middle ear matches the impedance of air to that of the fluid of the inner ear. Which factor gives most of that gain: the areal ratio of the tympanic membrane to the oval window, the mass of the ossicles, the length of the ear canal, or the round window? The answer is the areal ratio, about 17 to 1. The lever action of the chain adds a gain of about 1.3, giving roughly 22 times in all.

Question 8556. To which part of which ossicle does the tensor tympani muscle attach, and in which direction does it pull? The answer is the handle of the malleus, which it pulls inward toward the medial wall. That movement tenses the tympanic membrane and reduces the amplitude of its vibration. The muscle lies in a bony canal above the pharyngotympanic tube.

Question 8557. A described operation shows a pearly mass in the attic of the middle ear, and the long process of the incus has been eaten away. Which condition is shown, and why is that particular ossicle the one usually lost? The answer is a cholesteatoma. Its expanding sac erodes the nearest bone, and the long process of the incus is thin and lies close to the attic.

Question 8558. True or false: the stapes is the smallest bone in the human body. The answer is true. It weighs only a few milligrams and is about 3 millimeters long. Its footplate is smaller than the tympanic membrane by a factor of about 17, and that difference is the basis of the middle ear's pressure gain.

Question 8559. Which of these pairs belongs together: the malleus with the stapes footplate, the incus with the lenticular process, the stapes with the handle, or the malleus with the two crura? The answer is the incus with the lenticular process. The handle belongs to the malleus, while the crura and the footplate belong to the stapes.

Question 8560. A constructed illustration: after a facial palsy on one side, ordinary sounds seem painfully loud in that ear, though hearing is otherwise unchanged. Which small muscle has stopped working, and why does its loss have this effect? The answer is the stapedius, which is supplied by the facial nerve. Without its damping, loud sound moves the ossicular chain more than it should.

Question 8561. Into which ossicle does the tendon of the stapedius insert: the handle of the malleus, the long crus of the incus, the neck of the stapes, or the footplate of the stapes? The answer is the neck of the stapes. The muscle lies in the pyramidal eminence and pulls the stapes backward.

Question 8562. Which type of joint unites the head of the malleus with the body of the incus: a hinge joint, a saddle joint, a ball and socket joint, or a suture? The answer is a saddle joint, which allows movement in two planes. The incudostapedial joint is a ball and socket joint and becomes bony in later life. Both joints are supported by fine ligaments.

Question 8563. What is the name of the small central depression on the outer surface of the tympanic membrane, where the tip of the handle of the malleus ends? The answer is the umbo. The cone of light seen at examination spreads forward and downward from the umbo.

Question 8564. How many layers has the pars tensa of the tympanic membrane: one, two, three, or four? The answer is three. The outer layer is skin, the middle layer is fibrous, and the inner layer is mucous membrane. The pars flaccida lacks the middle fibrous layer, which makes it the weaker part of the membrane.

Question 8565. A described otoscopic view shows a pearly gray, translucent membrane with a bright triangle of light in its lower front part and a faint line down its center. What are these features? The answer is the cone of light and the handle of the malleus. The cone of light is the reflection of the examining light. Loss of that triangle may mean the membrane is thickened or pushed outward.

Question 8566. Which part of the tympanic membrane lies above the lateral process of the malleus and lacks a fibrous middle layer: the pars tensa, the pars flaccida, the umbo, or the cone of light? The answer is the pars flaccida, also called the flaccid part. Because it is weak, it is a common site of retraction.

Question 8567. The upper, lax part of the tympanic membrane is known by the name of the anatomist who described it. What is that name? The answer is the membrane of Shrapnell, now usually called the pars flaccida. It lies above the lateral process of the malleus.

Question 8568. When the tympanic membrane is divided into four quadrants by two lines crossing at the umbo, which quadrant lies above and behind that point: the anterosuperior, the anteroinferior, the posterosuperior, or the posteroinferior? The answer is the posterosuperior quadrant. It lies behind the handle of the malleus and above the umbo. The incus and the stapes lie behind it in the middle ear.

Question 8569. A constructed illustration: a person near an explosion has sudden pain, ringing and reduced hearing, and the membrane shows a small tear with a little blood. What has happened, and which layer has given way? The answer is a rupture of the tympanic membrane from a sudden pressure wave, and all three layers have split. The membrane often heals by itself, and the care of it belongs to the treating clinician.

Question 8570. Which two nerves supply the outer surface of the tympanic membrane, and how does that supply explain referred ear pain? The answer is the auriculotemporal nerve and the auricular branch of the vagus nerve. Both also supply other regions, so irritation of the membrane can be felt in the jaw, the temple or the throat.

Question 8571. A described otoscopic view shows a dull, dark membrane drawn inward, with the handle of the malleus more horizontal than usual and the cone of light shortened or absent. What does this appearance mean? The answer is a retracted tympanic membrane, produced when the pressure in the middle ear falls below the pressure in the canal.

Question 8572. True or false: the tympanic membrane lies at right angles to the floor of the external acoustic meatus. The answer is false. It is tilted, so that its upper part leans outward and its lower part leans inward. The membrane therefore faces outward, forward and downward, which is why it is best seen from below and in front.

Question 8573. Which of these pairs belongs together: the anterosuperior quadrant with the region above and in front of the umbo, the posteroinferior quadrant with the region below and behind the umbo, the pars flaccida with the lower part of the membrane, or the umbo with the rim? The answer is the anterosuperior quadrant with the region above and in front of the umbo. The umbo is the central point, not the rim.

Question 8574. In which direction does the outer surface of the tympanic membrane face: straight outward, outward and backward, outward, forward and downward, or inward and upward? The answer is outward, forward and downward. That direction explains why the canal is examined from below and in front.

Question 8575. A clinician needs to open the tympanic membrane to drain fluid and chooses a site where the risk to the ossicles and the chorda tympani is lowest. Which quadrant is that: the anterosuperior, the posterosuperior, the posteroinferior, or the anteroinferior? The answer is the posteroinferior quadrant. It lies below and behind the umbo, away from the ossicles and the chorda tympani. The decision belongs to the treating clinician.

Question 8576. Which two folds of mucous membrane run from the tympanic membrane to the walls of the middle ear, bounding the pars flaccida above? The answer is the anterior and posterior mallear folds. Between them and above them lies the pars flaccida.

Question 8577. A described examination shows a small crusted hole in the upper lax part of the tympanic membrane, with a pearly mass behind it. Which part of the membrane has perforated, and what does the mass suggest? The answer is the pars flaccida, and the mass suggests a cholesteatoma. Perforations of that part are called attic perforations. They differ from the central perforations of the pars tensa.

Question 8578. Name the three layers of the tympanic membrane, from outside inward. The answer is skin, then fibrous tissue, then mucous membrane. The fibrous layer gives the pars tensa its strength and its tension and the membrane its pearly appearance. The pars flaccida lacks that middle layer.

Question 8579. Which structure of the inner ear is shaped like a snail shell and contains the organ of hearing? The answer is the cochlea. It makes about two and a half turns around a central bony pillar called the modiolus.

Question 8580. Which three parts make up the bony labyrinth: the cochlea, the vestibule and the semicircular canals; the cochlea, the malleus and the incus; the utricle, the saccule and the cochlear duct; or the oval window, the round window and the promontory? The answer is the cochlea, the vestibule and the three semicircular canals. The utricle and the saccule are parts of the membranous labyrinth inside the vestibule.

Question 8581. A described section across the cochlea shows three fluid-filled channels, a thin membrane bearing a ridge of cells, and a bony shelf projecting from the central pillar. Which channels are shown, and which one holds the sensory cells? The answer is the scala vestibuli, the scala media and the scala tympani. The sensory cells lie in the organ of Corti, on the basilar membrane within the scala media.

Question 8582. On which membrane does the organ of Corti rest, and between which two fluid spaces does that membrane separate? The answer is the basilar membrane, which separates the scala media above it from the scala tympani below. The sensory hair cells sit on it, and their hairs touch the tectorial membrane above.

Question 8583. What is the name of the fluid that fills the membranous labyrinth of the inner ear and is rich in potassium? The answer is endolymph. The unusual composition of endolymph is what allows the hair cells to work.

Question 8584. Which statement about the fluids of the inner ear is correct: perilymph is rich in potassium and endolymph in sodium, endolymph is rich in potassium while perilymph resembles extracellular fluid, both have the same composition, or perilymph lies inside the cochlear duct? The answer is that endolymph is rich in potassium and perilymph resembles extracellular fluid. The cochlear duct contains endolymph and the other two channels contain perilymph.

Question 8585. A constructed illustration: a person has attacks of spinning dizziness with ringing in one ear and fluctuating hearing loss, and the symptoms come in episodes. Which part of the inner ear is involved? The answer is the membranous labyrinth, where the endolymph is under increased pressure. The cochlear duct and the vestibular apparatus are both affected.

Question 8586. Which two inner ear organs carry a macula with tiny crystals of calcium carbonate, and what do those organs detect? The answer is the utricle and the saccule. Their maculae detect the position of the head and linear acceleration. The crystals, called otoconia, give the macula its weight, so it bends when the head tilts.

Question 8587. What is the name of the sensory ridge inside the swelling at the base of each semicircular canal, and what does it detect? The answer is the crista ampullaris, which detects rotation of the head, that is, angular acceleration. Each ridge sits in an ampulla and is covered by a gelatinous cupula.

Question 8588. How many semicircular canals are there in each inner ear, and how are they arranged: two, at right angles to each other; three, in three planes; four, in two pairs; or five, in a spiral? The answer is three, lying roughly in three planes at right angles to one another. They are named the anterior, the posterior and the lateral canals.

Question 8589. A described map of the cochlea shows that the basilar membrane is narrow and stiff near the oval window and wide and flexible near the apex. What does this arrangement explain? The answer is that high frequencies act near the base and low frequencies near the apex. The arrangement gives the cochlea a place code for pitch. Fibers from each region carry that information to the brain.

Question 8590. At the apex of the cochlea, which two channels are joined by a small opening, and why does that opening matter? The answer is the scala vestibuli and the scala tympani, joined by the helicotrema. Pressure applied at the oval window can therefore be released at the round window.

Question 8591. A constructed illustration: a person has one-sided hearing loss, ringing and unsteadiness, and tests show a problem of the eighth cranial nerve; later that side of the face becomes weak. Where does the lesion lie, and why are both nerves affected? The answer is the internal acoustic meatus, where the facial nerve and the vestibulocochlear nerve run together. A growth there compresses both.

Question 8592. True or false: the endolymph that fills the cochlear duct is rich in potassium, while the perilymph of the scala vestibuli and the scala tympani is rich in sodium. The answer is true. The difference in composition creates the electrical potential that the hair cells use. The stria vascularis on the outer wall of the cochlear duct secretes the endolymph.

Question 8593. Which of these pairs belongs together: the organ of Corti with balance, the crista ampullaris with hearing, the macula of the utricle with linear acceleration, or the helicotrema with taste? The answer is the macula of the utricle with linear acceleration. The organ of Corti serves hearing, and the crista ampullaris detects rotation.

Question 8594. Which opening in the medial wall of the middle ear is closed by a membrane and lies below the oval window? The answer is the round window, closed by the secondary tympanic membrane. It gives the perilymph somewhere to move when the stapes pushes on the oval window.

Question 8595. Which artery supplies the inner ear, and why does its blockage cause permanent deafness: the middle meningeal artery, the labyrinthine artery from the anterior inferior cerebellar artery, the superficial temporal artery, or the occipital artery? The answer is the labyrinthine artery, which enters the internal acoustic meatus. It is an end artery with almost no collateral supply.

Question 8596. A constructed illustration: a person gets a brief burst of spinning dizziness when rolling over in bed, hearing is normal, and the attacks last less than a minute. Which canal is usually involved, and what has moved into it? The answer is the posterior semicircular canal, into which loose otoconia have drifted. The crystals make that canal respond to a change of position it should ignore.

Question 8597. A described cross section of the internal acoustic meatus shows several bundles of nerve fibers and one small artery in a bony canal that runs toward the brain. Which nerves are they? The answer is the facial nerve, the cochlear nerve, the superior vestibular nerve and the inferior vestibular nerve, with the labyrinthine artery. The cochlear and vestibular nerves together form the vestibulocochlear nerve.

Question 8598. Within the medial wall of the middle ear, which of the two windows lies higher, and what is the functional consequence of that arrangement? The answer is the oval window, which lies above the round window. The stapes drives the upper window inward, and the lower window bulges outward in response.

Question 8599. Name the three semicircular canals of one inner ear. The answer is the anterior, the posterior and the lateral canals. The anterior canal of one ear lies in the same plane as the posterior canal of the other ear.

Question 8600. In an older adult, hearing for high frequencies is lost first while the tympanic membrane looks normal. Which structure is failing, and how does this differ from a conductive loss: the ossicles, the hair cells of the basal turn of the cochlea, the tympanic membrane, or the pharyngotympanic tube? The answer is the hair cells of the basal turn of the cochlea. A conductive loss instead affects the outer or the middle ear.

To summarize, the external ear gathers sound with the auricle and the external acoustic meatus, whose cartilaginous outer part carries hairs and wax glands while its bony inner part is lined by thin skin. The tympanic membrane, tilted and built of three layers, turns that sound into vibration, and the ossicles carry it across the middle ear, where the areal ratio of the membrane to the oval window and the lever action of the chain match the impedance of air to that of fluid. The stapedius and the tensor tympani damp the chain, and the facial nerve runs through the medial wall close to the oval window. The inner ear holds the cochlea, where the organ of Corti on the basilar membrane sorts sound by frequency, and the vestibule and the semicircular canals, where the maculae and the cristae detect position and rotation in endolymph.
