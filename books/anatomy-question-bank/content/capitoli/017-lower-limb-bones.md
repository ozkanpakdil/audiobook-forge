# Chapter 17 — Lower Limb Bones

This chapter covers the bones of the lower limb below the knee: the tibia, the fibula, the tarsal bones, the metatarsal bones and the phalanges. It follows the weight-bearing line from the knee down to the tips of the toes, naming each bone, its parts, and the structures attached to it. The chapter holds one hundred questions, and every question gives its answer immediately in the same paragraph and then explains why that answer is right, so nothing has to be held in mind until the end. The bones of the foot are small and easily confused, and every description here is written to be heard rather than seen. All measurements and relations are the ordinary textbook ones, and variation between healthy people is normal. This is educational material, and it supports rather than replaces an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 1601. Which bone of the leg is the thick medial bone that carries the weight of the body down to the ankle? The answer is the tibia. It is the second largest bone in the body, exceeded only by the femur. The tibia lies medially and passes the weight from the knee to the foot, while the fibula beside it carries almost none.

Question 1602. Which of these four bones takes part in the knee joint: the tibia, the fibula, the talus, or the calcaneus? The answer is the tibia. Its two upper surfaces, the medial condyle and the lateral condyle, articulate with the femur. The fibula lies beside it but never enters the knee joint, and the talus and calcaneus lie far below.

Question 1603. A described radiograph of the knee shows the upper end of a leg bone broadened into two flat surfaces, with a raised central spike between them and a stout forward projection below. Which bone is shown? The answer is the tibia. The spike is the intercondylar eminence, and the forward projection is the tibial tuberosity, where the patellar ligament attaches.

Question 1604. What is the name of the bony prominence felt on the inner side of the ankle? The answer is the medial malleolus. It is the downward projection of the lower end of the tibia. The lateral malleolus, felt on the outer side, belongs to the fibula, and the two together grip the talus and form the bony frame of the ankle.

Question 1605. Which bone lies immediately lateral to the shaft of the tibia and is joined to it along most of its length by a tough membrane? The answer is the fibula. The interosseous membrane stretches between the two bones. It separates the muscles of the front of the leg from those of the back and gives them a surface to arise from.

Question 1606. Four structures lie around the knee: a band continuing the quadriceps tendon, a band from the outer thigh, the biceps femoris tendon, and the ligament on the inner side. Which attaches to the tibial tuberosity? The answer is the band continuing the quadriceps tendon, the patellar ligament. The iliotibial band reaches the tibia further out, and the biceps femoris tendon crosses to the fibular head.

Question 1607. A constructed illustration: a player takes a blow to the shin, the skin splits, and the broken bone lies exposed. Why is this injury so easily open? The answer is that the anteromedial surface of the tibia lies directly beneath the skin. Only skin and a thin layer of tissue cover the bone there, so a break presses through the surface.

Question 1608. What is the name of the sharp ridge that a finger can follow down the front of the leg? The answer is the anterior border of the tibia. It is the most prominent of the three borders of the shaft. It lies just beneath the skin with no muscle over it, so it is the part bruised in a kick to the shin.

Question 1609. A described cross section of the leg shows a triangular bone whose three borders are named anterior, medial and lateral, and whose sharpest border lies nearest the skin. Which bone is shown, and which border is nearest the front? The answer is the tibia, with its anterior border nearest the skin. Its lateral border gives attachment to the interosseous membrane.

Question 1610. Which of these features belongs to the tibia rather than to the fibula: the soleal line, the malleolar fossa, the styloid process, or the subcutaneous lateral surface? The answer is the soleal line. It is the oblique ridge on the posterior surface of the tibial shaft from which part of the soleus muscle arises. The other three features all belong to the fibula.

Question 1611. Which bone rests upon the upper articular surfaces of the tibia and completes the knee joint from above? The answer is the femur. Its two condyles roll and glide upon the condyles of the tibia. The patella lies in front of the joint within the quadriceps tendon, and it is the largest sesamoid bone in the body.

Question 1612. Which surface of the tibial shaft lies beneath the skin along its whole length and gives attachment to no muscle? The answer is the medial surface. It lies between the anterior border and the medial border and is covered only by skin and a thin layer of tissue. That is why a swelling or a fracture there shows so plainly.

Question 1613. A described specimen shows one small opening on the posterior surface of the tibial shaft, just below the soleal line. From which vessel does the artery that enters it come: the anterior tibial artery, the posterior tibial artery, the fibular artery, or the popliteal artery? The answer is the posterior tibial artery, whose nutrient branch enters the bone and divides within the shaft.

Question 1614. A described specimen shows the tibia broken across its middle while the fibula stays whole and the leg has not shortened. Which structure most limits the displacement: the patellar ligament, the interosseous membrane, the crural fascia, or the calcaneal tendon? The answer is the interosseous membrane, which with the unbroken fibula ties the two bones together along their shafts.

Question 1615. True or false: the tibia lies medial to the fibula and bears the weight of the body. The answer is true. The tibia is the thicker of the two bones, and its upper end takes the femur at the knee. The fibula lies on the lateral side of the leg and carries almost none of the body weight.

Question 1616. A described radiograph of the ankle shows a broad square projection on the inner side of the joint and a longer projection that reaches lower on the outer side. Which bones carry these two projections? The answer is the tibia on the inner side and the fibula on the outer side. The medial malleolus is shorter and sits higher than the lateral malleolus.

Question 1617. Which muscle arises from the oblique ridge that runs down and medially across the posterior surface of the upper tibia? The answer is the soleus. That ridge is the soleal line. The soleus joins the gastrocnemius above it, and the two muscles share the calcaneal tendon, which pulls upon the heel.

Question 1618. Which bone articulates with the lower articular surface of the tibia: the calcaneus, the talus, the navicular, or the cuboid? The answer is the talus. Its rounded upper surface, the trochlea, receives the tibia from above and the fibula from the side. The calcaneus lies below the talus, and the navicular lies in front of it.

Question 1619. Name the two bones whose lower ends form the bony socket that holds the ankle, spoken as a pair. The answer is the tibia and the fibula. Their malleoli grip the talus on either side to make the ankle mortise. The tibia carries the weight, and the fibula completes the ring and gives the socket its outer wall.

Question 1620. A described section through an adult lower leg shows the tibial shaft turning slightly outward as it descends, so that the foot lies a little lateral to the knee. Which term names that twist, and about which axis does it occur? The answer is lateral torsion of the tibia, about the long axis of the bone, a normal feature of the adult limb.

Question 1621. Which long thin bone lies on the outer side of the leg from the knee to the ankle? The answer is the fibula. It is slender, carries no weight from the knee, and serves chiefly to give attachment to muscles and to steady the ankle. Its head can be felt just below and behind the outer side of the knee.

Question 1622. The lower end of the fibula forms which prominence: the medial malleolus, the lateral malleolus, the tibial tuberosity, or the sustentaculum tali? The answer is the lateral malleolus. It projects downward on the outer side of the ankle and is easily felt through the skin. The medial malleolus belongs to the tibia and the sustentaculum tali to the calcaneus.

Question 1623. Which nerve winds around the neck of the fibula just below its head, where it lies against the bone? The answer is the common fibular nerve. It is the most commonly injured nerve of the lower limb because it is so exposed against the bone. Its branches supply the muscles that lift the foot.

Question 1624. A constructed illustration: a blow lands on the outer side of the upper leg, the neck of the fibula breaks, and the foot afterwards drags and cannot be lifted. Which structure was injured, and why? The answer is the common fibular nerve, pressed against the bone at the neck. The weakness of the muscles beyond that point produces the foot drop.

Question 1625. A described radiograph shows a slender bone whose upper end is a small rounded knob with a short spike beside it and whose lower end is a long pointed process. Which bone is shown, and what are its two ends? The answer is the fibula, with its head above and its lateral malleolus below. The spike on the head is the styloid process.

Question 1626. What is the name of the small joint between the head of the fibula and the outer side of the tibia, just below the knee? The answer is the proximal tibiofibular joint. It is a synovial joint that permits only slight movement. Its partner, the distal tibiofibular joint, lies at the ankle and is a fibrous joint instead.

Question 1627. Since the fibula carries no weight from the knee, what is its chief role: to bear weight at the ankle, to give attachment to muscles, to articulate with the femur, or to form the inner wall of the ankle socket? The answer is to give attachment to muscles and to steady the ankle. Several muscles of the leg arise from its shaft.

Question 1628. True or false: the fibula takes part in the knee joint and carries part of the weight of the body. The answer is false. The fibula does not reach the knee joint, and it carries no weight at all. It serves for the attachment of muscles and for the stability of the ankle.

Question 1629. Which of these pairs belongs together: the fibular head with the biceps femoris tendon, the fibular head with the patellar ligament, the lateral malleolus with the deltoid ligament, or the fibular neck with the tibial nerve? The answer is the fibular head with the biceps femoris tendon. The lateral malleolus anchors the lateral ankle ligaments, and the common fibular nerve lies at the neck.

Question 1630. Which structure attaches to the styloid process at the back of the head of the fibula: the lateral collateral ligament of the knee, the calcaneal tendon, the plantar fascia, or the tibialis posterior tendon? The answer is the lateral collateral ligament of the knee, also called the fibular collateral ligament. It runs from the fibular head to the outer condyle of the femur.

Question 1631. On the ankle, which malleolus reaches lower, the one on the inner side or the one on the outer side? The answer is the lateral malleolus of the fibula on the outer side. It reaches lower and further back than the medial malleolus of the tibia. That deeper grip helps to keep the ankle from rolling outward.

Question 1632. A constructed illustration: an ankle turns inward, and the bone felt on the outer side is tender and moves as a separate piece. Which joint has been disrupted: the ankle, the distal tibiofibular joint, the subtalar joint, or the midtarsal joint? The answer is the distal tibiofibular joint, with a break of the lateral malleolus, so the two bones no longer hold the talus in one ring.

Question 1633. A described cross section through the middle of the leg shows two bones joined by a membrane, one triangular and thick, the other slender with four borders. Which is which? The answer is that the tibia is the thick triangular bone, lying medially. The membrane between them separates the muscles of the front from those of the back.

Question 1634. What is the name of the border of the fibula that gives attachment to the interosseous membrane? The answer is the interosseous border, also called its medial border, which faces the tibia along the shaft. The other borders are named anterior, posterior and lateral. The lateral surface between them gives origin to the fibularis longus and the fibularis brevis.

Question 1635. Which of these statements about the fibula is correct: it articulates with three bones at the ankle, it carries weight from the knee, it lies medial to the tibia, or its head receives the biceps femoris tendon? The answer is that its head receives the biceps femoris tendon. The fibula lies lateral and carries no weight, and its lower end joins only the ankle joint.

Question 1636. Name two muscles of the lateral compartment of the leg that arise from the shaft of the fibula, spoken as a pair. The answer is the fibularis longus and the fibularis brevis. Both arise from the lateral surface of the shaft. Their tendons pass behind the lateral malleolus and turn the sole of the foot outward.

Question 1637. Which of these structures binds the lower ends of the tibia and the fibula to each other at the ankle: the deltoid ligament, the interosseous tibiofibular ligament, the calcaneal tendon, or the plantar fascia? The answer is the interosseous tibiofibular ligament, the strongest part of the distal tibiofibular syndesmosis. That joint is fibrous, whereas the deltoid ligament binds the tibia to the talus.

Question 1638. A constructed illustration: a twisting injury breaks the fibula high near the knee, the ankle is badly displaced, and the inner side of the same ankle is tender. Which structures have failed? The answer is the inferior tibiofibular syndesmosis together with the medial ligament of the ankle. The ring around the talus has given way, and the break lies far above it.

Question 1639. A described specimen shows a bone the length of the lower leg that ends below in a rounded knob with a shallow pit on its inner surface and never reaches the knee joint. Which bone is it, and what is the pit? The answer is the fibula, and the pit is the malleolar fossa. The posterior talofibular ligament attaches within that fossa.

Question 1640. Which of these features is found on the fibula rather than on the tibia: the fibular notch, the malleolar fossa, the tibial tuberosity, or the intercondylar eminence? The answer is the malleolar fossa. It lies on the medial and posterior surface of the lateral malleolus. The fibular notch is the shallow cut on the lower tibia that receives the fibula.

Question 1641. Which tarsal bone sits between the bones of the leg above and the calcaneus below and takes part in the ankle joint? The answer is the talus. Its rounded upper surface fits into the socket made by the tibia and the fibula. Almost no muscle attaches to the talus, which is why so much of its surface is covered with cartilage.

Question 1642. Which bone of the foot is the largest, forms the heel, and touches the ground behind? The answer is the calcaneus. The calcaneal tendon attaches to its back, and it carries the whole body weight as the heel strikes the ground. The talus rests upon it and forms the subtalar joint above.

Question 1643. Which tarsal bone lies between the head of the talus behind and the three cuneiform bones in front: the cuboid, the navicular, the calcaneus, or the lateral cuneiform? The answer is the navicular. Its name means little boat, because its hollowed back fits the rounded head of the talus. It is one of the keystones of the arch of the foot.

Question 1644. A described radiograph of the foot taken from above shows seven small bones in two rows, with one bone set between the ankle bones behind and the row in front. Which bone is that, and what does its position achieve? The answer is the navicular, which carries the weight of the arch forward into the front of the foot.

Question 1645. The cuboid lies immediately in front of one bone and immediately behind two others. Name those bones. The answer is the calcaneus behind it and the fourth and fifth metatarsals in front. The cuboid also touches the lateral cuneiform and the navicular along its inner side.

Question 1646. How many cuneiform bones take part in forming the foot, and which of them is the largest: two with the lateral the largest, three with the medial the largest, three with the intermediate the largest, or two with the medial the largest? The answer is three cuneiform bones, of which the medial is the largest. The medial cuneiform receives the first metatarsal.

Question 1647. A constructed illustration: a person lands from a height on both feet and breaks the bone between the ankle and the foot across its narrow neck. Which bone is injured, and why is its blood supply a concern? The answer is the talus. Its blood reaches it through a small channel in the neck, so a break there can starve the body of the bone.

Question 1648. True or false: the calcaneus receives the weight of the body from the talus and passes it to the ground at the heel. The answer is true. The talus rests upon the calcaneus at the subtalar joint, and the weight then travels backward to the calcaneal tuberosity and forward into the front of the foot.

Question 1649. Which of these pairs belongs together: the sustentaculum tali with the talus, the sustentaculum tali with the navicular, the cuboid with the talus, or the navicular with the calcaneal tendon? The answer is the sustentaculum tali with the talus. It is a shelf of bone on the calcaneus that carries the head of the talus, while the cuboid never touches the talus.

Question 1650. Which structure runs in the groove on the under surface of the sustentaculum tali of the calcaneus: the flexor hallucis longus tendon, the tibialis anterior tendon, the plantar fascia, or the fibularis longus tendon? The answer is the tendon of the flexor hallucis longus, which passes forward beneath the shelf, whereas the fibularis longus tendon runs on the cuboid.

Question 1651. What is the name of the shelf of bone on the inner side of the calcaneus that projects beneath the head of the talus? The answer is the sustentaculum tali. It supports the talus and helps to hold up the arch of the foot. A groove on its under surface carries the tendon of the flexor hallucis longus.

Question 1652. A described section through the ankle shows a rounded bone held on both sides by bony walls, resting upon a broader bone below and touching a third bone in front. Name the bones described. The answer is the talus between the two malleoli, the calcaneus below it, and the navicular in front. The talus is the only foot bone that joins the leg.

Question 1653. Which tarsal bone lies directly beneath the talus and forms the joint through which the heel turns inward and outward? The answer is the calcaneus. That joint is the subtalar joint. It is not the ankle joint, which lies above the talus and moves the foot up and down.

Question 1654. A constructed illustration: a person falls from a ladder and lands upon the heel; the ankle is spared but the heel is broad and painful. Which bone is broken, and why is the ankle spared? The answer is the calcaneus. The force passes through the heel, so the talus is driven down into the calcaneus, and the ankle above receives little of it.

Question 1655. Which bone is the keystone of the medial longitudinal arch of the foot: the cuboid, the navicular, the lateral cuneiform, or the calcaneus? The answer is the navicular. It lies at the summit of the arch between the talus behind and the cuneiforms in front. The cuboid belongs to the outer arch instead.

Question 1656. A described section through the foot is cut across the middle of the tarsus so that the talus and the calcaneus appear side by side, with the calcaneus below and lateral. Which plane was used, and which bone lies more lateral in the section? The answer is a coronal plane, and the calcaneus lies more laterally, as well as lower.

Question 1657. What is the name of the bony knob on the inner side of the foot, about halfway along the arch, where the tendon of the tibialis posterior attaches? The answer is the tuberosity of the navicular. The tendon of the tibialis posterior muscle inserts upon it. It is a useful landmark for the joint between the talus and the navicular.

Question 1658. A described oblique radiograph of the foot shows one line of bones running from the heel to the base of the first toe and another line crossing it at the middle of the foot. Which joints does the crossing line mark? The answer is the midtarsal joint, where the talus meets the navicular and the calcaneus meets the cuboid, also called the transverse tarsal joint.

Question 1659. Which tarsal bone articulates with the bases of the fourth and fifth metatarsals: the medial cuneiform, the intermediate cuneiform, the lateral cuneiform, or the cuboid? The answer is the cuboid. Its front surface carries two facets for those two bases. The lateral cuneiform receives the third metatarsal instead.

Question 1660. A constructed illustration: a foot turns inward sharply, the outer edge is painful, the bones of the midfoot stay whole, and the tenderness lies over the ligaments on the outer side of the ankle. Which structures have been stretched? The answer is the lateral ligaments of the ankle. Turning the sole inward stretches them, while the deltoid ligament is slackened.

Question 1661. Turning the sole of the foot inward and outward takes place at which joint: the ankle joint, the subtalar joint, the midtarsal joint, or the tarsometatarsal joint? The answer is the subtalar joint. The ankle moves only up and down, and the midtarsal joint adds only a slight twist.

Question 1662. Which of these pairs belongs together: the medial cuneiform with the first metatarsal, the intermediate cuneiform with the first metatarsal, the cuboid with the third metatarsal, or the navicular with the fifth metatarsal? The answer is the medial cuneiform with the first metatarsal. The intermediate and lateral cuneiforms receive the second and third metatarsals, and the cuboid takes the fourth and fifth.

Question 1663. How many metatarsal bones are there in one foot, and how are they numbered? The answer is five, numbered from the first on the inner side to the fifth on the outer side. Each one has a base near the ankle, a body or shaft, and a rounded head at the ball of the foot.

Question 1664. Which metatarsal bone is usually the longest, and which is the shortest and thickest? The answer is that the second is usually the longest and the first is the shortest and thickest. The first metatarsal carries the two sesamoid bones beneath its head and bears the weight of the great toe. The order of length varies a little between healthy people.

Question 1665. Which bone articulates with the base of the first metatarsal: the cuboid, the navicular, the medial cuneiform, or the calcaneus? The answer is the medial cuneiform. The first metatarsal rests upon the largest of the three cuneiform bones and carries the great toe. It usually has no facet toward the second metatarsal.

Question 1666. Which tendon inserts on the tuberosity at the base of the fifth metatarsal: the tibialis anterior, the fibularis brevis, the fibularis longus, or the tibialis posterior? The answer is the fibularis brevis. It pulls upon that bony prominence, which projects from the outer border of the foot. The fibularis longus tendon runs beneath the foot to the medial cuneiform instead.

Question 1667. A described radiograph of the forefoot shows five long bones side by side, one of them short and stout and set a little apart. Which one is it, and what lies beneath its head? The answer is the first metatarsal, with two sesamoid bones beneath its head. Those small bones lie in the tendon of the flexor hallucis brevis.

Question 1668. Which metatarsal lies immediately lateral to the medial cuneiform, with its base set into a recess between two bones? The answer is the second metatarsal. Its base is wedged between the medial and the lateral cuneiforms. That recess makes the base of the second metatarsal a keystone of the transverse arch at that level.

Question 1669. A constructed illustration: a recruit who has begun long marches develops pain over the shaft of a metatarsal, and a fine crack appears across it. Which bone is usually the one involved: the first, the second, the third, or the fifth? The answer is the second metatarsal, the longest of the five and so under the greatest bending strain. The crack comes from repeated stress, not one injury.

Question 1670. True or false: the first metatarsal lies on the outer border of the foot and is the longest of the five. The answer is false. The first metatarsal lies on the inner border beside the great toe, and it is the shortest and thickest of them. The fifth metatarsal forms the outer border of the foot.

Question 1671. The sesamoid bones beneath the head of the first metatarsal lie within which structure: the flexor hallucis longus tendon, the flexor hallucis brevis tendon, the tibialis posterior tendon, or the plantar fascia? The answer is the tendon of the flexor hallucis brevis. Almost every foot has two of them, one medial and one lateral, gliding over a groove on the underside of the bone.

Question 1672. Which part of a metatarsal bone forms the rounded surface that presses on the ground at the ball of the foot? The answer is the head, the distal end of the bone. It is covered with cartilage and moves against the base of the first phalanx. The base lies at the other end of the bone and joins the tarsus.

Question 1673. Which metatarsal bases articulate with the cuboid rather than with a cuneiform bone? The answer is the fourth and the fifth. Their bases rest upon the cuboid on the outer side of the foot, while the first three metatarsals rest upon the three cuneiform bones. The joints between them are the tarsometatarsal joints.

Question 1674. A constructed illustration: a person stumbles off a step, the foot turns inward, and a small fragment is pulled from the base of the fifth metatarsal. Which tendon did the pulling, and what kind of injury is illustrated? The answer is the fibularis brevis, which inserts upon that tuberosity. The fragment is an avulsion at the tendon rather than a break across the shaft.

Question 1675. A described radiograph taken across the sole of the foot shows the bases of the metatarsals in a row with a gap between the first and the second. What occupies that gap in the living foot? The answer is a strong ligament running from the medial cuneiform to the base of the second metatarsal, which is recessed into the tarsus there.

Question 1676. What is the name of the joint line between the bases of the metatarsal bones and the tarsal bones: the midtarsal joint, the tarsometatarsal joint, the metatarsophalangeal joint, or the subtalar joint? The answer is the tarsometatarsal joint. It runs across the foot at the level of the cuneiforms and the cuboid. The midtarsal joint lies behind it.

Question 1677. Which of these pairs belongs together: the first metatarsal with the medial cuneiform, the first metatarsal with the cuboid, the fifth metatarsal with the navicular, or the third metatarsal with the cuboid? The answer is the first metatarsal with the medial cuneiform. The cuboid receives the fourth and the fifth metatarsals, and the lateral cuneiform receives the third.

Question 1678. What is the name of the bony prominence on the outer border of the foot, about halfway along, where the tendon of the fibularis brevis attaches? The answer is the tuberosity of the base of the fifth metatarsal. It can be felt easily and is often struck or bruised. It is also the site of an avulsion when the foot turns inward.

Question 1679. Which structures form the transverse arch of the foot at the level of the tarsometatarsal joints: the calcaneus and the talus, the cuneiform bones with the bases of the metatarsals, the heads of the metatarsals, or the phalanges? The answer is the cuneiform bones together with the bases of the metatarsals. The arch is held by ligaments and by the tendons crossing it.

Question 1680. A constructed illustration: a heavy object falls across the top of the foot and several metatarsals are broken at once. Why is the swelling between them so severe? The answer is that the metatarsals are bound together by ligaments and lie within tight compartments. Blood and fluid cannot spread freely, so the pressure between the bones rises.

Question 1681. Name the metatarsal whose base is set into a recess and acts as the keystone of the tarsometatarsal line, spoken as one name. The answer is the second metatarsal. Its base is wedged between the medial and the lateral cuneiforms. For that reason it moves less than any of the other four.

Question 1682. A described section across the middle of the metatarsals shows five bony rings joined by ligaments, the first and second bound closely together while the first metatarsal moves freely at its base. What does that arrangement permit? The answer is that the first metatarsal moves more freely than the others, since its base has no facet toward the second metatarsal.

Question 1683. How many phalanges, or toe bones, are there in one foot? The answer is fourteen. The great toe has two of them, and each of the other four toes has three. Every one of them has a base at the back, a body, and a head or a tuberosity in front.

Question 1684. Which toe has only two phalanges, and which ones are they? The answer is the great toe, which has a proximal and a distal phalanx but no middle one. Because it is short and stout it carries the greatest load in walking. The other four toes each have three phalanges.

Question 1685. How many phalanges does the second toe of a normal foot have: one, two, three, or four? The answer is three, a proximal, a middle and a distal phalanx. Only the great toe has two. A foot therefore holds fourteen phalanges in all, and the middle bone of the little toe is sometimes joined to the distal one as a normal variation.

Question 1686. Which bone of the foot forms the tip of a toe and carries a rough tuberosity on its under surface? The answer is the distal phalanx. The rough tuberosity gives the pulp of the toe something to rest against. The nail lies above the flattened upper surface of the same bone.

Question 1687. A described radiograph counts two bones beyond the metatarsal in the great toe and three in the second toe. How many joints does each toe show, and which joint is missing from the great toe? The answer is two joints in the great toe and three in the second toe, Each toe has one joint fewer than it has bones, and the great toe has no middle joint.

Question 1688. Which bone articulates with the base of the proximal phalanx of the great toe, and which two small bones lie beneath that joint? The answer is the first metatarsal, with the two sesamoid bones beneath its head. The joint between them is the first metatarsophalangeal joint. The sesamoid bones lie within the tendon of the flexor hallucis brevis.

Question 1689. A constructed illustration: a bare foot stubs against a stone, the tip of the second toe is bent sharply upward, and the joint at the base of the toe is painful. Which joint is illustrated, and which structure holds it steady? The answer is the metatarsophalangeal joint. The plantar plate and the ligaments beneath it steady the joint, stretched here but not torn.

Question 1690. True or false: the great toe has three phalanges, just as the other toes do. The answer is false. The great toe has two phalanges, a proximal and a distal one. It is the only toe without a middle phalanx, although it is the thickest and strongest of them all.

Question 1691. What is the name of the bone at the very tip of each toe, the one that bears the nail? The answer is the distal phalanx. Its base joins the middle phalanx, or in the great toe the proximal phalanx. Its far end is flattened into a rough tuberosity for the pulp of the toe.

Question 1692. Which of these statements about the toe bones is correct: every toe has three phalanges, the fifth toe has none, the great toe has a proximal and a distal phalanx, or the phalanges lie behind the metatarsals? The answer is that the great toe has a proximal and a distal phalanx. The middle bone of the little toe is sometimes fused with the distal one.

Question 1693. Which of these pairs belongs together: the great toe with two phalanges, the great toe with three phalanges, the fifth toe with two phalanges in every foot, or the second toe with two phalanges? The answer is the great toe with two phalanges. The other four toes each have three, though the bones of the fifth toe are sometimes joined.

Question 1694. A constructed illustration: a dancer forces the great toe upward at its base, the joint becomes painful, and the small bones beneath it are bruised. Which structures lie in that position and are injured here? The answer is the two sesamoid bones within the tendon of the flexor hallucis brevis, beneath the head of the first metatarsal.

Question 1695. A described dissection of a toe shows a short bone with a broad base, a slender body and a rounded head that lies within a fibrous sling. Which bone is it, and what is the sling? The answer is a proximal phalanx, and the sling is the fibrous sheath of the flexing tendons. The sheath holds the tendons close against the bone.

Question 1696. Which part of a phalanx forms the joint at its proximal end, and which part forms the joint at its distal end? The answer is the base proximally and the head distally. The base is broad and hollowed to receive the head of the bone behind it, and the head is rounded to fit the base of the bone in front.

Question 1697. Which joint lies between the head of a metatarsal and the base of a proximal phalanx: the tarsometatarsal joint, the metatarsophalangeal joint, the proximal interphalangeal joint, or the midtarsal joint? The answer is the metatarsophalangeal joint, which lies at the ball of the foot. The interphalangeal joints lie further forward, between the phalanges themselves.

Question 1698. Which structure lies immediately beneath the head of the first metatarsal and the base of the proximal phalanx of the great toe? The answer is the pair of sesamoid bones within the tendon of the flexor hallucis brevis. They are embedded in the tendon and glide over the underside of the metatarsal head, one medial and one lateral.

Question 1699. A described specimen shows the bone at the tip of a toe, flattened from above, with a rough tuberosity at its far end and a broad base behind. Which bone is it, and what lies over its flat upper surface? The answer is a distal phalanx, with the nail above it. The rough tuberosity gives the pulp something to attach to.

Question 1700. A described radiograph of a whole foot shows seven tarsal bones, five metatarsals and the toes, and the examiner asks how many bones the foot contains. Which count fits: twenty-four, twenty-six, twenty-eight, or thirty? The answer is twenty-six. Seven tarsal bones, five metatarsals and fourteen phalanges make that number, and the sesamoid bones beneath the first metatarsal are counted apart.

To summarize, the tibia is the thick medial bone of the leg and carries the weight of the body from the femur to the talus, while the fibula is slender, carries none of it, and serves for muscle attachment and the stability of the ankle. The medial malleolus belongs to the tibia and the lateral malleolus to the fibula, and the common fibular nerve winds around the fibular neck. The tarsal bones are seven in number, with the talus taking the weight from the leg, the calcaneus forming the heel, the navicular as a keystone of the medial arch, and the cuboid articulating with the fourth and fifth metatarsals. Five metatarsals and fourteen phalanges complete the foot, twenty-six bones in all, and the two sesamoid bones beneath the first metatarsal lie within the tendon of the flexor hallucis brevis. Naming each bone aloud together with its neighbors is what fixes this map in memory, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.
