# Chapter 12 — Thoracic Cage

Every breath expands and settles the thoracic cage, and this chapter takes that cage apart piece by piece. It covers the twelve pairs of ribs and their parts, the sternum, the costal cartilages that join the two, the true ribs, the false ribs and the floating ribs, and the costal angles that mark the bend of each rib. The chapter holds one hundred questions, and each one gives its answer immediately after the question and then explains why that answer is right, so nothing has to be held in mind until the end. This is educational material, and it supports rather than replaces an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 1101. How many pairs of ribs form the thoracic cage, and which of them are described as typical ribs? The answer is twelve pairs, with the third to the ninth called typical. A typical rib carries a head, a neck, a tubercle and a shaft with an angle and a costal groove. The first, second, tenth, eleventh and twelfth ribs depart from that pattern and are called atypical.

Question 1102. Which ribs are the typical ribs: the first to the sixth, the third to the ninth, the seventh to the tenth, or the eighth to the twelfth? The answer is the third to the ninth. Each of them shows the full set of features, with a head that carries two facets, a neck, a tubercle and a costal groove. The ribs above and below that range are atypical.

Question 1103. Name the parts of a typical rib in order from the vertebral column to the sternum. The answer is the head, the neck, the tubercle and the shaft. The head is the rounded end that meets the vertebral bodies, and the neck is the narrow part beyond it. The tubercle is the small bump past the neck, and the shaft is the long curved body of the bone.

Question 1104. A described radiograph of the chest shows a break in the sixth rib on the right, in the part of the bone that lies furthest back, just beside the shadow of the spine. Which part of the rib has broken? The answer is the angle of the rib. The angle is the bend where the shaft turns from its backward course into its forward course.

Question 1105. Which structures lie in the costal groove along the lower border of a rib: the intercostal artery alone, the intercostal nerve alone, the vein, the artery and the nerve together, or the pleura only? The answer is the vein, the artery and the nerve together. From above downward the order is vein, then artery, then nerve, so the nerve is the lowest of the three.

Question 1106. Within an intercostal space, does the neurovascular bundle lie nearer the rib above or the rib below? The answer is that it lies in the costal groove of the rib above. The groove runs along the lower border of that rib, so the bone shelters the bundle. The space itself is named after the rib above it.

Question 1107. Which small ridge on the upper surface of the first rib gives attachment to the scalenus anterior muscle? The answer is the scalene tubercle. It lies near the middle of that surface, between two shallow grooves. The first rib is the shortest, the broadest and the most sharply curved of the twelve.

Question 1108. On the first rib, which vessel lies in the groove in front of the scalene tubercle: the subclavian artery, the subclavian vein, the axillary artery, or the internal thoracic artery? The answer is the subclavian vein. The subclavian artery lies in the groove behind the tubercle instead. The vein therefore lies in front of the artery.

Question 1109. A described specimen shows the shortest and most sharply curved of the ribs, lying almost flat rather than sloping, with a fine ridge between two grooves on its upper surface. Which rib is it, and which muscle marks that ridge? The answer is the first rib, and the ridge is the scalene tubercle for the scalenus anterior.

Question 1110. A constructed illustration: a young adult reports aching in the arm and tingling along the little finger, and examination shows a weak hand grip and a reduced pulse at the wrist. A described radiograph shows an extra rib arising from the seventh cervical vertebra. Which structures are compressed? The answer is the lower trunk of the brachial plexus and the subclavian artery. A cervical rib is a recognized variation.

Question 1111. The head of the fourth rib articulates with which vertebrae: the fourth alone, the third and fourth, the fourth and fifth, or the third, fourth and fifth? The answer is the third and fourth. A typical rib head carries two facets, one for each of those bodies. A ridge between the facets meets the disc that lies between them.

Question 1112. The tubercle of a rib articulates with which part of a vertebra, and what kind of joint is formed? The answer is the transverse process of the vertebra of the same number, in a small synovial joint that lets the rib turn. The eleventh and twelfth ribs have no tubercle and therefore no such joint. The head forms the other joint between a rib and the vertebral column.

Question 1113. How many thoracic vertebrae carry ribs, and how does each rib reach the vertebra numerically corresponding to it? The answer is twelve, and each rib curves around to meet the vertebra of its own number. The first pair meets the first thoracic vertebra, the second pair the second, and so on. The twelfth rib is the last of the series.

Question 1114. True or false: the first rib is the longest and the least curved of the twelve. The answer is false. The first rib is the shortest, the broadest and the most sharply curved, and it lies almost flat. The seventh rib is the longest of the series, and the ribs below it grow shorter again.

Question 1115. Which ribs are described as atypical: the first, second, tenth, eleventh and twelfth, the third to the ninth, the fifth to the eleventh, or the seventh to the twelfth? The answer is the first, second, tenth, eleventh and twelfth. Each departs from the typical pattern in its own way. The tenth rib, for instance, usually carries a single facet on its head.

Question 1116. A described radiograph of the chest shows twelve pairs of ribs, with the lower ones sloping steeply down and forward and the upper ones running almost level. Which rib lies closest to the horizontal? The answer is the first rib. Its slope is the least, and each rib below it slopes more steeply. That is why the lowest ribs reach so much further down at the front.

Question 1117. A constructed illustration: a person falls against the edge of a step and breaks the seventh rib on the left in two places along its shaft. Which structure lying immediately deep to that rib is at risk? The answer is the lung and the pleural cavity around it. The inner surface of the rib faces the pleura, and a sharp fragment can tear it.

Question 1118. Which ribs are most often broken: the first and second, the fourth to the eighth, the ninth and tenth, or the eleventh and twelfth? The answer is the fourth to the eighth. They are the longest and the least sheltered by other structures. The first rib lies deep behind the shoulder girdle, and the floating ribs move freely.

Question 1119. An intercostal space is named after which rib, the one above it or the one below it? The answer is the rib above it. The space between the fifth and sixth ribs is therefore the fifth intercostal space. The neurovascular bundle of that space runs in the groove of the fifth rib.

Question 1120. Which muscle has its origin on the rough tuberosity on the outer surface of the second rib? The answer is the serratus anterior. Its other slips arise from the outer surfaces of the upper ribs behind it. Tracing the muscle backward leads to the medial border of the scapula.

Question 1121. Which muscle arises from the outer surfaces of the upper eight or nine ribs and passes backward to the medial border of the scapula: the pectoralis minor, the serratus anterior, the external oblique, or the latissimus dorsi? The answer is the serratus anterior. Its upper slips come from the first and second ribs, and the rest follow below.

Question 1122. A described specimen shows a rib whose head carries only one facet instead of two, whose neck is short, and whose shaft has a small angle but no groove on its lower border. Which rib is most likely shown? The answer is the eleventh rib. It meets the body of the eleventh thoracic vertebra alone. Its tip ends free among the muscles of the abdominal wall.

Question 1123. Which of these is not a feature of a typical rib: a head with two facets, a neck, a tubercle, or a spine? The answer is a spine. A spine belongs to a vertebra and not to a rib. The head, the neck, the tubercle and the shaft with its angle and groove make up the typical rib.

Question 1124. A described illustration shows five ribs on the left, each broken in two places, so that the piece of chest wall between the breaks moves inward when the person breathes in. Which statement explains that movement? The answer is that the free segment loses its bony continuity with the rest of the cage and follows the pressure around it.

Question 1125. Which muscle arises from the outer surfaces of the lower eight ribs and runs down and forward to the front of the abdomen: the pectoralis major, the external oblique, the serratus anterior, or the latissimus dorsi? The answer is the external oblique. The muscle ends in a broad sheet that forms the front wall of the abdomen.

Question 1126. On which ribs is the costal groove absent or poorly formed? The answer is the eleventh and twelfth ribs, and the groove is also short in the first. The groove shelters the intercostal vessels and nerve on the inner surface of the shaft. A rib that carries no groove gives them no such shelter.

Question 1127. Which features are missing from the eleventh and twelfth ribs: the head and the shaft, the neck and the tubercle, the angle and the shaft, or the head alone? The answer is the neck and the tubercle. Without a tubercle they form no costotransverse joint. Their heads carry a single facet, which meets one vertebral body.

Question 1128. A described radiograph of the chest shows twelve curved bones on each side, running from the spine downward and forward, with the lowest ones crossing the shadow of the abdomen. What are these bones, and what do the lowest two do at their front ends? The answer is the ribs, and the eleventh and twelfth ribs end freely. They have no costal cartilage reaching the sternum.

Question 1129. The costal part of the diaphragm arises from which structures? The answer is the inner surfaces of the lower six ribs and their costal cartilages. Those fibers arch upward to the central tendon of the diaphragm. Interdigitating with them are the fibers of the transversus abdominis below.

Question 1130. A constructed illustration: a described incision in the lower chest passes along the upper border of a rib, and the person later reports numbness in a band around the trunk at that level. Which structure was most likely divided? The answer is an intercostal nerve. The nerve lies in the costal groove of the rib above, lowest of the three structures there.

Question 1131. Which statement about the shape of the adult thoracic cage is correct: it is widest at the top, it is narrow above and wide below, or it is a cylinder of equal width throughout? The answer is that it is narrow above and wide below. The superior aperture is its smallest opening. The cage is widest at about the level of the eighth or ninth rib.

Question 1132. In which direction do the fibers of the external intercostal muscle run: downward and forward, downward and backward, straight up and down, or horizontally? The answer is downward and forward. The internal intercostal fibers run downward and backward, crossing them at right angles. The external muscle fills each space from the tubercle of the rib back to the costal cartilage in front.

Question 1133. Name the three parts of the sternum from above downward. The answer is the manubrium, the body and the xiphoid process. The manubrium is the broad upper part and the body is the longest. The manubrium and the body meet at the sternal angle, which can be felt through the skin.

Question 1134. The hollow felt at the top of the sternum between the two clavicles has which name: the jugular notch, the sternal angle, the xiphisternal joint, or the costal margin? The answer is the jugular notch, also called the suprasternal notch. It lies on the upper border of the manubrium. On each side of it is a notch for the medial end of the clavicle.

Question 1135. At which costal cartilage can the sternal angle be felt? The answer is the second costal cartilage. The sternal angle is the joint between the manubrium and the body of the sternum, and the second rib meets the sternum at that level. The first rib lies above it and is hidden by the clavicle.

Question 1136. The sternal angle lies at which level of the vertebral column: the junction of the second and third thoracic vertebrae, the junction of the fourth and fifth thoracic vertebrae, the junction of the seventh and eighth thoracic vertebrae, or the level of the first lumbar vertebra? The answer is the junction of the fourth and fifth thoracic vertebrae. The jugular notch lies at the level of the second thoracic vertebra.

Question 1137. Which large airway structure divides at the level of the sternal angle? The answer is the trachea, which splits into the two main bronchi there. The arch of the aorta also begins and ends at that level. The azygos vein joins the superior vena cava just below it.

Question 1138. A described lateral radiograph of the chest shows the sternum as a straight band at the front, with a slight step where the upper part joins the lower. Which part of the sternum forms the upper band, and what is the step called? The answer is the manubrium above, meeting the body at the sternal angle. The angle marks the level of the second costal cartilage.

Question 1139. A constructed illustration: a driver is thrown forward against the steering wheel and afterward has pain over the middle of the chest, with a step felt in the bone there. Which bone is most likely broken, and which structure lies behind it? The answer is the body of the sternum, with the heart behind it. The illustration shows how a direct blow to the front of the chest is transmitted.

Question 1140. Which costal cartilages articulate with the body of the sternum: the first and second only, the second to the seventh, the third to the sixth, or the eighth to the tenth? The answer is the second to the seventh, with the second divided between the manubrium and the body. The first cartilage joins the manubrium alone.

Question 1141. At which vertebral level does the xiphisternal joint lie? The answer is the level of the ninth thoracic vertebra. The joint lies between the body of the sternum above and the xiphoid process below. It also lies at the apex of the angle formed by the two costal margins.

Question 1142. True or false: the jugular notch of the manubrium lies at the level of the second thoracic vertebra. The answer is true. The notch is the upper border of the manubrium, and the second thoracic vertebra lies behind it. That is why the notch is used as a starting point when counting down the chest.

Question 1143. Bone marrow samples are taken from which part of the sternum: the manubrium, the body, the xiphoid process, or the sternal angle? The answer is the body of the sternum. It lies just under the skin, and the marrow cavity within it is wide. The xiphoid is avoided because it is small and lies over the upper abdomen.

Question 1144. Which of these pairs belongs together: the manubrium with the jugular notch, the body with the clavicular notch, the xiphoid with the second costal cartilage, or the manubrium with the xiphisternal joint? The answer is the manubrium with the jugular notch. The second costal cartilage meets the sternum at the sternal angle.

Question 1145. A described specimen of a sternum shows a small round hole through the middle of the body, closed by a thin membrane. What is this feature, and why does it matter when a needle is passed into the bone? The answer is a sternal foramen, a variation in which the two plates of bone fail to fuse. The heart lies immediately behind it.

Question 1146. Which structure lies immediately behind the body of the sternum, and which gland occupies the same space in a child? The answer is the heart, with the thymus in front of it in the child. The heart rests behind the sternum and the costal cartilages. The thymus regresses with age.

Question 1147. A described examination finds a small ridge on the front of the chest at the level of the second rib, and the examiner counts downward from it. Why is that ridge chosen rather than the first rib? The answer is that the first rib lies behind the clavicle and cannot be felt. Counting down from it gives the number of each rib and space reliably.

Question 1148. What kind of joint is the sternal angle, between the manubrium and the body of the sternum? The answer is a secondary cartilaginous joint, the kind that has a disc of fibrocartilage between two surfaces covered by cartilage. It allows only a little movement. The joint between the body and the xiphoid is of the same kind.

Question 1149. A described illustration shows a young person in whom the lower part of the sternum is hollowed inward, so that the chest looks sunken in the middle. Which part of the sternum is displaced, and is the number of ribs changed? The answer is the body and the xiphoid, and the number of ribs is unchanged. Such a depression is a variation of the chest wall.

Question 1150. Which costal cartilage is the lowest that reaches the sternum: the fifth, the sixth, the seventh, or the eighth? The answer is the seventh. Its cartilage joins the lower end of the body of the sternum. The cartilages of the eighth, ninth and tenth turn upward and join the seventh instead.

Question 1151. Name two structures that lie at the level of the sternal angle. The answer is the second costal cartilage and the division of the trachea into the two main bronchi. The arch of the aorta also begins and ends at that plane. The disc between the fourth and fifth thoracic vertebrae lies there as well.

Question 1152. A described radiograph taken from the front shows a flat vertical band of bone in the middle of the chest, overlying the shadows of the heart and the spine. Which bone is shown, and which of its parts can be made out? The answer is the sternum, with the manubrium above and the body below. The xiphoid often cannot be seen because it is cartilage.

Question 1153. What kind of tissue forms the costal cartilages? The answer is hyaline cartilage. Each cartilage continues its rib forward to the sternum or to the cartilage above it. The cartilage is flexible, so the chest wall can move with every breath. In later life it often becomes calcified and less yielding.

Question 1154. The costal cartilages of the eighth, ninth and tenth ribs join which structure in front: the sternum directly, the cartilage of the seventh rib, the xiphoid process, or the clavicle? The answer is the cartilage of the seventh rib. They join one another in turn to form a continuous sloping border. That border is the costal margin.

Question 1155. Which ribs contribute to the costal margin? The answer is the seventh to the tenth. Their cartilages join in a continuous arch on each side, sloping down and forward. The margin can be felt through the skin along the lower edge of the chest, and the eleventh and twelfth ribs take no part in it.

Question 1156. A described radiograph of an older adult shows thin lines of bone running from the front ends of the ribs to the sternum, where young cartilage would have cast no shadow. What are these lines? The answer is the calcified costal cartilages. Calcification of hyaline cartilage is a normal change with age. It does not mean that the ribs themselves have changed.

Question 1157. At the costochondral junction, which structure meets which? The answer is the bony shaft of the rib and its costal cartilage. The junction lies a short distance from the sternal end of the rib. The joint is immobile, because the bone and the cartilage are united directly.

Question 1158. A constructed illustration: a blow to the front of the chest leaves a person with sharp pain at one point on the costal margin, worse on deep breathing, and the tender spot is at the junction of bone and cartilage. Which junction is involved? The answer is a costochondral junction. The bone and the cartilage may separate there under a direct blow.

Question 1159. Which costal cartilages join one another in series rather than each reaching the sternum: those of the first and second, those of the eighth, ninth and tenth, those of the eleventh and twelfth, or those of the third and fourth? The answer is the eighth, ninth and tenth. Each turns upward and joins the cartilage above it. The eleventh and twelfth have no cartilage in front at all.

Question 1160. True or false: the costal cartilage is a piece of hyaline cartilage that continues the rib forward. The answer is true. It is the same tissue as the cartilage on the articular surfaces of joints. Its flexibility lets the chest wall bend during breathing.

Question 1161. Which of these pairs belongs together: the costochondral junction with the rib and its cartilage, the costovertebral joint with the rib and the sternum, or the costotransverse joint with the rib and the clavicle? The answer is the costochondral junction with the rib and its cartilage. The costovertebral joint lies between the head of the rib and a vertebral body. The costotransverse joint lies between the tubercle and a transverse process.

Question 1162. A described radiograph of the lower chest in an older adult shows the costal cartilages outlined by fine deposits of calcium, so that the front ends of the ribs appear capped by a faint shell. What does that appearance represent, and is the underlying rib diseased? The answer is calcification within the costal cartilages, a change of normal aging. The bony ribs themselves are unchanged.

Question 1163. In some people two adjacent costal cartilages are joined by a bridge of cartilage, so that the front of the chest is stiffer on that side. Which variation is that? The answer is fusion of two adjacent costal cartilages. It is seen most often among the lower cartilages. It is a normal variation rather than a sign of disease.

Question 1164. A constructed illustration: a person reports sharp pain along one costal margin, brought on by twisting, with a clicking sensation as one cartilage slips over the one above it. Which cartilages are most often involved? The answer is the eighth, ninth and tenth costal cartilages, where they join one another. Their junctions are the ones that give way.

Question 1165. In a transverse section across the chest at the level of the fifth rib, does the costal cartilage lie in front of or behind the bony shaft of the same rib? The answer is in front. The rib curves forward and downward, and its cartilage continues that course to the sternum. The cartilage therefore lies anterior to the part of the shaft behind it.

Question 1166. Name two joints that a typical rib forms with the vertebral column. The answer is the costovertebral joint and the costotransverse joint. The first lies between the head of the rib and the bodies of two vertebrae and the disc between them. The second lies between the tubercle and the transverse process.

Question 1167. Which ribs are the true ribs? The answer is the first seven pairs. Each has its own costal cartilage, and that cartilage reaches the sternum. They are also called the vertebrosternal ribs, because they join a vertebra behind and the sternum in front.

Question 1168. Why are the first seven ribs called true ribs: because they are longer than the others, because each reaches the sternum through its own cartilage, because they alone join the vertebral column, or because they are straighter? The answer is that each reaches the sternum through its own cartilage. The ribs below do not reach the sternum directly.

Question 1169. A described radiograph of the chest shows seven ribs on each side whose cartilages turn inward and meet the flat central bone at the front, while the ribs below stop short of it. Which ribs are the seven, and what name is given to them? The answer is the first seven, the true ribs. Their cartilages reach the sternum.

Question 1170. Which costal cartilage joins the manubrium directly rather than the body of the sternum? The answer is the first. It is short, thick and firmly united to the manubrium. The second cartilage is divided, part meeting the manubrium and part the body. The joint between the first cartilage and the manubrium allows almost no movement.

Question 1171. A constructed illustration concerns a person who has taken a severe blow to the top of the shoulder and is found to have a break of the first rib. Which large vessels running just above that rib may be injured with it? The answer is the subclavian artery and the subclavian vein. They cross the first rib on its upper surface.

Question 1172. Which costal cartilage reaches the sternum exactly at the sternal angle: the first, the second, the third, or the fourth? The answer is the second. It meets the sternum where the manubrium joins the body. The first lies above that level and the third below it. That is why the second rib is the one used to begin counting.

Question 1173. In the living body, does a true rib run downward from the spine to the sternum, or upward? The answer is downward and forward. The vertebral end of the rib lies higher than its sternal end. The lowest ribs slope the most steeply of all, which is why the costal margin lies so far below the upper chest.

Question 1174. Which of these pairs belongs together: the fourth rib with two facets on its head, the first rib with two facets on its head, or the twelfth rib with a costal groove along its lower border? The answer is the fourth rib with two facets on its head. The first and twelfth ribs each carry a single facet, and the twelfth has no costal groove.

Question 1175. Which ribs are the false ribs? The answer is the eighth, ninth, tenth, eleventh and twelfth. They are false because their cartilages do not reach the sternum directly. The upper three join the cartilage of the seventh rib, and the last two have no cartilage in front at all.

Question 1176. The eighth, ninth and tenth ribs are also known by which name: the vertebrosternal ribs, the vertebrochondral ribs, the vertebral ribs, or the sternal ribs? The answer is the vertebrochondral ribs. They join a vertebra behind and a cartilage in front. The eleventh and twelfth are the vertebral ribs, because they join only the vertebral column.

Question 1177. A described radiograph shows that on each side the shadows of the eighth, ninth and tenth ribs turn upward at their front ends and merge with one another, forming a continuous line that ends in the middle of the lower chest. What is that line? The answer is the costal margin. It is built from the costal cartilages of the seventh to the tenth ribs.

Question 1178. The costal cartilages of the eighth, ninth and tenth ribs all join which single structure? The answer is the costal cartilage of the seventh rib. Each joins the one above it, and the series ends on the seventh. The seventh cartilage then meets the lower end of the body of the sternum.

Question 1179. A constructed illustration concerns a person with a break of the ninth rib on the right, well forward along its shaft, and afterward bleeding into the abdomen. Which organ lies immediately deep to that rib on the right side? The answer is the liver. Its dome rises under the right costal margin. On the left at the same level lie the spleen and the stomach.

Question 1180. Which statement about the eighth, ninth and tenth ribs is correct: each reaches the sternum, each joins the cartilage of the rib above, each ends free in muscle, or each joins the thoracic vertebra below it? The answer is that each joins the cartilage of the rib above. The series ends on the seventh cartilage, which reaches the sternum.

Question 1181. True or false: the costal cartilage of the eighth rib joins the sternum directly. The answer is false. It joins the cartilage of the seventh rib, which in turn reaches the sternum. That is the reason the eighth rib is classed as a false rib.

Question 1182. Which of these pairs belongs together: the first to the seventh ribs with the true ribs, the eighth to the twelfth ribs with the true ribs, or the eleventh and twelfth ribs with the vertebrochondral ribs? The answer is the first to the seventh ribs with the true ribs. The eighth to the twelfth are the false ribs.

Question 1183. Name the false ribs and say where their cartilages end. The answer is the eighth, ninth, tenth, eleventh and twelfth. The cartilages of the eighth, ninth and tenth join the seventh cartilage and form the costal margin. The eleventh and twelfth have no cartilages reaching the front of the chest at all.

Question 1184. A described examination finds that the lowest intercostal space with a complete front wall lies between the seventh and eighth ribs. Which space is that, and in the groove of which rib does its bundle run? The answer is the seventh space, with its bundle in the groove of the seventh rib. The cartilage of the seventh rib reaches the sternum, so the space has a complete front wall.

Question 1185. Which ribs are the floating ribs? The answer is the eleventh and twelfth pairs. They are the last two and have no costal cartilage reaching the front of the chest. Each ends in a pointed tip among the muscles of the abdominal wall. They are also called the vertebral ribs.

Question 1186. Why are the eleventh and twelfth ribs called floating ribs: because they move with breathing, because their front ends are free, because they are not joined to the spine, or because they are the shortest? The answer is that their front ends are free. They have no costal cartilage joining the sternum or the costal margin. Every rib moves with breathing, so movement is not the distinguishing feature.

Question 1187. A described radiograph of the upper abdomen shows two short curved bones on each side, below the costal margin, tapering to points that end over the soft tissues of the flank. Which ribs are they, and what lies just behind them? The answer is the eleventh and twelfth ribs, with the kidneys behind them. They are the only ribs with no attachment in front.

Question 1188. The upper part of the kidney lies against the inner surface of which ribs? The answer is the eleventh and twelfth ribs, with the twelfth crossing the kidney from behind. The kidney therefore lies partly under the shelter of the floating ribs. The pleura may also reach down over the twelfth rib behind.

Question 1189. A constructed illustration concerns a blow to the flank that breaks the twelfth rib, after which blood appears in the urine. Which organ has most likely been injured? The answer is the kidney, which lies behind the lower ribs. The twelfth rib crosses its upper part from behind. The pleura may lie between the rib and the kidney as well.

Question 1190. The head of the twelfth rib articulates with which structures: the eleventh and twelfth thoracic vertebrae, the twelfth thoracic vertebra alone, the first lumbar vertebra alone, or the eleventh thoracic vertebra alone? The answer is the twelfth thoracic vertebra alone. The rib carries a single facet on its head. For the same reason it forms no costotransverse joint, because it has no tubercle.

Question 1191. A described specimen shows a short, sharply curved rib with a pointed front end, a single facet on its head, no neck, no tubercle and no costal groove. Which rib is it, and how does it differ from the eleventh? The answer is the twelfth rib, which is shorter and has no angle. The eleventh rib is longer and keeps a shallow costal groove.

Question 1192. True or false: the eleventh rib carries a shallow costal groove on its lower border, while the twelfth does not. The answer is true. The eleventh rib still has a small angle and a groove for the intercostal vessels and nerve. The twelfth is shorter and smoother, with neither a groove nor an angle.

Question 1193. Which of these pairs belongs together: the eleventh rib with a single facet on its head, the eighth rib with no cartilage in front, or the seventh rib with no attachment to the sternum? The answer is the eleventh rib with a single facet on its head. It meets only the eleventh thoracic vertebra. The eighth rib has a cartilage that reaches the costal margin, and the seventh reaches the sternum.

Question 1194. What name is given to the bend on the shaft of a rib where it turns from its backward course into its forward course? The answer is the angle of the rib, or the costal angle. It is the most sharply curved part of the bone. It lies only a short distance beyond the tubercle.

Question 1195. Which part of a rib is most often the site of a fracture: the head, the neck, the angle, or the costal cartilage? The answer is the angle. It is the point of greatest curvature and the weakest part of the bone. A break there usually follows a direct blow or a crush.

Question 1196. What is the name of the angle formed beneath the xiphoid process where the right and left costal margins meet? The answer is the subcostal angle, also called the infrasternal angle. It opens downward and lies at the apex of the two costal margins. Its size varies with the shape of the chest.

Question 1197. A described radiograph of the chest shows, on each rib, the point where the curve changes direction most abruptly, lying close to the shadow of the spine. Which part of the rib is shown, and is it nearer the vertebral or the sternal end? The answer is the angle, and it lies much nearer the vertebral end. It sits only a short distance beyond the tubercle.

Question 1198. A constructed illustration: after a fall, a person has pain at a point on the back of the chest beside the spine, and pressing there reproduces it exactly. Which part of which rib lies under the finger? The answer is the angle of a rib. The angle lies at the outer border of the long muscle that runs up the spine.

Question 1199. Which statement about the subcostal angle beneath the xiphoid process is correct: it is wider in the infant than in the adult, it is always a right angle, or it lies above the xiphoid? The answer is that it is wider in the infant than in the adult. The infant chest is rounder, and its costal margins flare outward more. With growth the cage lengthens and the angle narrows.

Question 1200. Which ribs provide the cartilages that form the costal margin, and where do the two margins meet? The answer is the seventh to the tenth ribs, and the margins meet at the xiphisternal joint. Each margin slopes down and forward from the seventh cartilage. The angle between the two margins is the subcostal angle.

To summarize, the thoracic cage is built from twelve pairs of ribs, the sternum, and the costal cartilages that join the two. The first seven ribs are the true ribs, because each reaches the sternum through its own cartilage, while the eighth to the twelfth are the false ribs, and among those the eleventh and twelfth end free as the floating ribs. The head of a typical rib meets two vertebral bodies and the disc between them, its tubercle meets the transverse process of the same number, and its costal groove carries the intercostal vein, artery and nerve in that order from above downward. The sternal angle, felt at the second costal cartilage and level with the disc between the fourth and fifth thoracic vertebrae, is the landmark from which the ribs are counted. The costal margin is formed by the cartilages of the seventh to the tenth ribs, and the angle of a rib, just beyond its tubercle, is both the most sharply curved part of the bone and the one most often broken.
