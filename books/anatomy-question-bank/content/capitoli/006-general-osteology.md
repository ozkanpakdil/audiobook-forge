# Chapter 6 — General Osteology

This chapter examines bone as a tissue and as an organ, from the cells that build and dissolve it to the regions of a long bone and the marks that its attachments leave on the surface. It covers the structure of bone, the difference between compact and spongy bone, the periosteum, the endosteum, the medullary cavity, and the epiphysis, the metaphysis and the diaphysis, and it closes with the bone markings that a listener can name from a description. It holds one hundred questions in that order, and each gives its answer immediately and then explains why that answer is right. Nothing needs to be held in mind, and nothing needs to be looked at, because each item describes what a section or a radiograph would show. This is educational material, and it supports rather than replaces an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 501. Which living cell of bone lies within a small cavity in the matrix, maintains the matrix around it, and communicates with its neighbors through fine channels? The answer is the osteocyte. A cell that buries itself in the matrix it has made becomes an osteocyte and lies in a lacuna. Its processes run in canaliculi, so it is fed without leaving its cavity.

Question 502. Which of these cells dissolves bone matrix and releases the mineral stored in it: the osteoblast, the osteocyte, the osteoclast, or the osteoprogenitor cell? The answer is the osteoclast. It is a large multinucleated cell formed by the fusion of monocyte precursors, and it sits in a shallow pit that it has eaten into the bone.

Question 503. A described ground section of compact bone shows a central hole surrounded by a series of rings, with small dark spaces lying between the rings. Which unit of bone is being described? The answer is the osteon. Its central canal carries a small vessel and a nerve, and the rings are concentric lamellae. The small spaces are the lacunae that hold the osteocytes.

Question 504. What name is given to the lengthwise channel that runs through the middle of each osteon and carries its blood vessel? The answer is the central canal. Each canal is lined by endosteum and holds a small vessel and a nerve fiber. Neighboring canals are joined by perforating canals that cross the cortex.

Question 505. Which substance gives the matrix of bone most of its hardness and its resistance to compression: collagen fibers, calcium phosphate in the form of hydroxyapatite, water, or the fat of the marrow? The answer is calcium phosphate in the form of hydroxyapatite. The crystals lie along and between the collagen fibers and give bone its stiffness, and the collagen gives it tensile strength.

Question 506. Within the wall of a long bone, the vessel in the periosteum reaches the central canal of a deeper osteon by which route, and how do those canals run? The answer is by the perforating canals, which cross the cortex at right angles to the long axis. They join the canals of neighboring osteons and reach the deepest part of the wall.

Question 507. Which part of the matrix lets a bone bend without breaking and so resists pulling forces: the mineral crystals, the collagen fibers, the ground substance, or the fat of the marrow? The answer is the collagen fibers. They make up about one third of the dry weight of the matrix and lie in a felted pattern. A bone with defective collagen becomes brittle.

Question 508. Which fine channels connect one lacuna to the next and allow the processes of neighboring osteocytes to meet? The answer is the canaliculi. They are very small tunnels radiating from each lacuna in every direction. The processes inside them touch at gap junctions, so the osteocytes reach the vessels of the central canal.

Question 509. Which of these statements about the mineral of bone is correct: it is calcium carbonate, it is sodium chloride, it is calcium phosphate laid down as hydroxyapatite, or it is pure calcium? The answer is calcium phosphate laid down as hydroxyapatite. The crystals lie in and around the collagen fibers, and their presence is what makes a bone cast a dense white shadow on a radiograph.

Question 510. A described section of a shaft wall shows several systems of rings, each with its own canal, and between them irregular patches of lamellae that belong to no single canal. Lamellae also lie flat against the outer and inner surfaces. What are the irregular patches called? The answer is interstitial lamellae. They remain from older osteons that have been partly removed during remodeling.

Question 511. True or false: the osteocytes lie in lacunae and communicate with one another through canaliculi rather than by direct contact of their cell bodies. The answer is true. Each cell body occupies its own small cavity, and only the thin processes leave it. The arrangement feeds the cell inside a hard matrix.

Question 512. Which cell of bone produces the unmineralized organic matrix that is called osteoid? The answer is the osteoblast. Osteoblasts line the surfaces of growing or remodeling bone and secrete collagen and ground substance. Mineral is laid down in the osteoid soon afterwards, and a cell buried in it becomes an osteocyte.

Question 513. Around which structure do the concentric lamellae of an osteon lie, and how is that structure supplied? The answer is the central canal, which holds a small blood vessel and a nerve. The lamellae are laid down around it, so the innermost lies nearest the vessel. Perforating canals join it to the vessels of the periosteum and the marrow.

Question 514. Which of these is the origin of the osteoclast: the osteoblast, the osteocyte, the periosteal fibroblast, or the fusion of monocyte precursors? The answer is the fusion of monocyte precursors. The osteoclast is a large cell with several nuclei and a ruffled border, and it belongs to the same family as the macrophage.

Question 515. True or false: about two thirds of the dry weight of bone matrix is mineral and about one third is organic material. The answer is true. The mineral is calcium phosphate in the form of hydroxyapatite, and the organic part is mainly collagen with ground substance. The proportion changes a little with age and with the bone examined.

Question 516. A described specimen taken from a healing fracture shows a disorganized meshwork of collagen with many irregular cells and no orderly rings. Which kind of bone is shown, and what is its fate? The answer is woven bone, also called immature bone. It is laid down quickly and is weak, so it is later replaced by lamellar bone.

Question 517. In which part of a long bone is the compact bone thickest: the epiphysis, the diaphysis, the metaphysis, or the medullary cavity? The answer is the diaphysis. Its wall is a tube of compact bone, thickest at the middle of the shaft, where bending forces are greatest.

Question 518. What name is given to the thin plates of bone that form the lattice of spongy bone? The answer is the trabeculae. They run in many directions and cross one another, leaving spaces that hold marrow. Each is only a few cell layers thick, so its cells are fed by diffusion.

Question 519. A described radiograph of the upper end of the femur shows a fine crisscross pattern of lines running through the neck. What does that pattern represent? The answer is the trabeculae of spongy bone arranged along the lines of stress. One group carries compression from the head toward the shaft, and another resists tension along the upper surface.

Question 520. Which statement about spongy bone is correct: it is built of trabeculae with marrow spaces, it is built of closely packed osteons, it lies only on the outer surface of a long bone, or it contains no cells? The answer is trabeculae with marrow spaces between them. Spongy bone fills the ends of long bones and the interior of flat and short bones.

Question 521. In the upper end of a long bone, which kind of bone lies immediately deep to the articular cartilage, and where does the compact bone lie? The answer is spongy bone, with a thin shell of compact bone at the surface. The trabeculae carry the load inward to the cortex, and the shell is thin because the force is spread widely.

Question 522. Which of these contains the greatest amount of compact bone: the body of a vertebra, the shaft of the tibia, the sternum, or the calcaneus? The answer is the shaft of the tibia. A shaft carries bending and twisting forces and needs a thick cortical wall, while the vertebral body and the calcaneus are mainly spongy bone and the sternum is spongy bone between thin tables.

Question 523. What name is given to the layer of spongy bone that lies between the two compact tables of a flat bone of the skull? The answer is the diploe. The outer table is thicker and curves smoothly, and the inner table is thin and brittle. A blow can crack the inner table without breaking the outer one.

Question 524. Which statement about the trabeculae of spongy bone is correct: they contain no lamellae, they are lamellar but do not form osteons, they remain woven bone throughout life, or they contain no living cells? The answer is that they are lamellar but do not form osteons. Their lamellae lie in flat or slightly curved plates, and the cells are fed by diffusion from the marrow.

Question 525. A described section of a shaft wall shows tightly packed rings, while a section from the upper end shows a loose network of thin plates. Which tissue is shown in each place? The answer is compact bone in the shaft wall and spongy bone in the upper end. The two are the same tissue arranged differently, each following the forces it must carry.

Question 526. A constructed illustration: a limb kept in a cast for weeks afterwards shows a thinner cortex and a wider marrow cavity on a radiograph. Which surface has been resorbed, and which bone has lost the most material? The answer is the endosteal surface, with the greatest loss from spongy bone, because bone that is not loaded is removed by osteoclasts. Management belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 527. Which name is given to the dense outer layer of bone, the layer that forms the thick wall of a shaft and is also called cortical bone? The answer is compact bone. It is built of osteons packed closely together, with lamellae filling the spaces between them. Its density makes it appear white on a radiograph.

Question 528. In the wall of a shaft, which vessels supply the outer part of the cortex and which vessel supplies the inner part? The answer is the periosteal vessels for the outer part and the nutrient artery for the inner part. The nutrient artery enters the marrow cavity and sends branches outward through the endosteum, and the two supplies meet within the cortex.

Question 529. Which statement about the direction of the trabeculae is correct: they run at random, they follow the lines of stress, they lie only vertically, or they parallel the periosteum? The answer is that they follow the lines of stress. In the neck of the femur one group carries compression and another resists tension, and their crossing pattern keeps the bone strong while light.

Question 530. A constructed illustration: a vertebral body in an older person loses height under a moderate load while the cortex of the long bones stays intact. Why does the vertebral body fail first? The answer is that it is built mainly of spongy bone, whose trabeculae have a large surface area and are lost early when bone mass falls.

Question 531. A described radiograph of the spine shows the vertebral bodies with prominent vertical lines and a loss of the fine horizontal lines between them. Which tissue has been lost, and why do the vertical lines stand out? The answer is spongy bone, in which the horizontal trabeculae are lost first. The remaining vertical trabeculae carry the load and so appear more prominent.

Question 532. Which membrane covers the outer surface of a bone everywhere except where the bone is covered by articular cartilage? The answer is the periosteum. It is a tough fibrous sheath firmly fixed to the bone surface. It is absent from the articular surfaces and from sesamoid bones such as the patella.

Question 533. Which of these describes the two layers of the periosteum: an outer fibrous layer and an inner osteogenic layer, two fibrous layers, an inner fibrous layer and an outer bony layer, or a single layer of cartilage? The answer is an outer fibrous layer and an inner osteogenic layer. The outer layer is fibrous tissue with vessels and nerves, and the inner layer, the cambium, holds the osteoprogenitor cells.

Question 534. A described radiograph of a growing bone shows a tumor near the surface, with the membrane lifted off the cortex and a small triangle of new bone at the edge of the lifted membrane. Which structure has been raised? The answer is the periosteum. Its inner layer lays down new bone as it is pushed from the cortex, and that calcified new bone forms the triangle.

Question 535. Which fibers pass from a tendon through the periosteum and into the outer lamellae of the cortex, and what do they achieve? The answer is the perforating fibers, also called Sharpey fibers. They anchor the tendon so that the pull is spread over a wide area. The periosteum itself is fixed to the cortex the same way.

Question 536. Which of these surfaces of a bone is not covered by periosteum: the shaft of the tibia, the articular surface of the head of the femur, the outer surface of the ilium, or the mastoid process? The answer is the articular surface of the head of the femur. That surface is covered by hyaline cartilage instead, and periosteum is also absent from sesamoid bones.

Question 537. What is the name of the inner layer of the periosteum, the layer that contains the cells able to form new bone? The answer is the osteogenic layer, also called the cambium layer. It lies against the bone, deep to the fibrous layer. Its osteoprogenitor cells give rise to the osteoblasts that widen a bone and heal a fracture.

Question 538. A constructed illustration: a person is struck on the front of the shin, where the bone lies just under the skin, and the pain is severe although a radiograph shows no break. Which structure is injured? The answer is the periosteum. It is richly supplied with pain fibers, unlike the bone beneath it. A small bleed raises the membrane and causes sharply localized tenderness.

Question 539. Which statement about the periosteum in a child is correct: it is thicker and more active than in an adult, it is thinner and inactive, it is absent from all bones, or it is replaced by cartilage? The answer is that it is thicker and more active. Its osteogenic layer is busy widening the shaft throughout growth.

Question 540. A described dissection shows a membrane being lifted from the shaft of a bone; it comes away easily in the middle but stays fixed at the two ends. What is the membrane, and what holds it at each end? The answer is the periosteum, held at the joint margin where the cartilage begins and by the fibers of the tendons and ligaments that blend into it.

Question 541. The outer layer of the periosteum is built of which tissue, and what does it contain? The answer is dense irregular connective tissue, carrying blood vessels, lymphatics and nerves. Its fibers run in several directions, so the layer resists a pull from any direction.

Question 542. Which statement about the cortical blood supply is correct: periosteal vessels feed the outer part and the nutrient artery the inner part, the nutrient artery feeds only the marrow, the marrow feeds all the cortex, or the cortex has none? The answer is that periosteal vessels supply the outer part and the nutrient artery the inner part. The two sets meet within the cortex, so the wall survives when one route fails.

Question 543. In a growing bone, with which covering of the cartilaginous end is the periosteum continuous, and where does that covering stop? The answer is the perichondrium, the fibrous layer over the cartilage, with which the periosteum is continuous. The covering stops at the joint margin, where the articular cartilage is left bare.

Question 544. A constructed illustration: a direct blow lifts the periosteum from the shaft of the femur and blood collects beneath it in a long thin sheet. Which space has filled, and why does the blood not enter the marrow? The answer is the subperiosteal space between the membrane and the cortex. The cortex is intact, so the blood stays outside it.

Question 545. Which thin membrane lines the medullary cavity and covers the surfaces of the trabeculae inside a bone? The answer is the endosteum. It is one cell thick and has no fibrous layer. It also lines the central canals of the osteons and the perforating canals.

Question 546. Which of these best describes the endosteum: a single thin layer of cells with osteoprogenitor cells and osteoclasts, a tough sheet of dense connective tissue, a layer of hyaline cartilage, or a plate of compact bone? The answer is a single thin layer of cells. It contains flattened osteoprogenitor cells and osteoblasts, and osteoclasts lie against it where bone is removed.

Question 547. A described section shows the inner surface of the cortex and the surface of every trabecula lined by a thin sheet one cell thick, with marrow on one side and bone on the other. Which membrane is shown? The answer is the endosteum. It is seen on every internal surface, and that surface is very large.

Question 548. A constructed illustration: an older person who has been confined to bed loses bone, and the radiograph shows a thin cortex with a wide marrow cavity. Which surface is being resorbed to produce this change? The answer is the endosteal surface. Osteoclasts working there enlarge the cavity from within.

Question 549. Which statement about the endosteum is correct: it lines the canals inside compact bone as well as the marrow cavity, it covers only the outer surface, it is a layer of cartilage, or it is present only in children? The answer is that it lines the canals inside compact bone as well as the marrow cavity, reaching the deepest part of the cortex.

Question 550. Which cells lie against the endosteum where bone is being broken down, and what do they do to dissolve the mineral? The answer is the osteoclasts, which pump acid and enzymes into a sealed pocket against the matrix. The mineral dissolves in the acid and the collagen is digested. The cell then moves on, leaving a shallow pit.

Question 551. The periosteum covers the outer surface of the cortex and the endosteum its inner surface; where are the two layers continuous with each other? The answer is at the openings through which vessels pass and within the perforating canals. The lining follows the vessel as it crosses the cortex, so the internal and external surfaces share one cellular lining.

Question 552. True or false: the endosteum is a tough fibrous sheet that can be lifted from the bone in the same way as the periosteum. The answer is false. The endosteum is a thin layer of cells with no fibrous coat, so it cannot be peeled away.

Question 553. What name is given to the space that runs through the shaft of a long bone and contains the marrow? The answer is the medullary cavity, also called the marrow cavity. It is bounded by the cortex and lined by endosteum, and in an adult it holds mainly fat.

Question 554. Which of these is the main function of the red marrow in the medullary cavity: fat storage, blood cell formation, mineral storage, or the growth of cartilage? The answer is blood cell formation. Red marrow produces red cells, white cells and platelets, and yellow marrow stores fat.

Question 555. A described section along a shaft shows a hollow tube of dense bone around a central space filled with fatty tissue and a few darker islands. What is the space, and what fills it? The answer is the medullary cavity, filled mainly with yellow marrow and holding islands of red marrow, which can form blood cells when the need arises.

Question 556. A constructed illustration: after severe blood loss the fatty marrow of the long bones becomes active again and forms blood cells. Which cavity is involved? The answer is the medullary cavity. Yellow marrow can turn back into red marrow when the demand is high, giving the body extra surface for making new cells.

Question 557. In an adult, which of these sites holds red marrow: the shaft of the tibia, the sternum and the vertebral bodies, the middle of the femur, or the medullary cavity of the radius? The answer is the sternum and the vertebral bodies. Red marrow persists in the skull, ribs, sternum, vertebrae and pelvis, and in the upper ends of the femur and humerus.

Question 558. Which opening in the cortex of a long bone admits the artery that supplies the marrow? The answer is the nutrient foramen. The artery runs a slanting course through the cortex into the marrow cavity, and the canal points away from the growing end of the bone.

Question 559. Which of these pairs belongs together: red marrow with fat storage, yellow marrow with blood cell formation, red marrow with blood cell formation, or the medullary cavity with articular cartilage? The answer is red marrow with blood cell formation. Red marrow is the blood forming tissue of the adult skeleton, and yellow marrow stores fat.

Question 560. Which statement about the marrow of a young child is correct: the medullary cavities hold red marrow throughout, the cavities are filled with air, the cavities hold cartilage, or the cavities are empty? The answer is that they hold red marrow throughout. All the marrow of the skeleton is red at birth and forms blood cells, and yellow marrow appears later in the shafts.

Question 561. In the shaft of a long bone, which structures lie between the marrow cavity and the skin, in order from the cavity outward? The answer is the endosteum, then the compact bone of the cortex, then the periosteum. The endosteum lines the cavity, the cortex forms its wall, and the periosteum wraps the outer surface.

Question 562. A described radiograph of a long bone shows a dark band running down the middle of the shaft, wider in the middle than at the ends. What is the band, and why is it dark? The answer is the medullary cavity. Marrow contains much fat and little mineral, so it absorbs fewer x-rays than bone and appears dark on the film.

Question 563. In which of these bones does a true medullary cavity fail to develop: the humerus, the femur, the sternum, or the tibia? The answer is the sternum. Flat and short bones are built of spongy bone with marrow spaces between the trabeculae and have no central cavity.

Question 564. Which part of a long bone is the expanded end that lies beyond the growth plate and ossifies from its own center? The answer is the epiphysis. It carries the articular surface of the joint. Its own center of ossification appears after birth and fuses with the shaft when growth ends.

Question 565. Which kind of cartilage covers the articular surface of an epiphysis: hyaline cartilage, fibrocartilage, elastic cartilage, or calcified cartilage? The answer is hyaline cartilage. It is smooth and tough and has no blood vessels of its own, being nourished by the synovial fluid that bathes the joint.

Question 566. A described radiograph of a child's knee shows a dark band across the end of the femur, and beyond it a rounded mass of bone with its own outline. Which two structures are shown? The answer is the epiphyseal plate, which is the dark band, and the epiphysis beyond it. The plate is cartilage, so it absorbs fewer x-rays than bone.

Question 567. A constructed illustration: an adolescent feels sudden hip pain, and the radiograph shows the head of the femur still in its socket while the neck and shaft have shifted. Which structure gave way? The answer is the epiphyseal plate. The head is the epiphysis, and it has slipped on the plate while the metaphysis and shaft moved. Management belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 568. Which statement about the blood supply of the growing epiphysis is correct: vessels cross the growth plate freely, the epiphysis has vessels of its own, the plate supplies it, or it has no blood supply? The answer is that the epiphysis has vessels of its own that do not cross the plate, which is why an infection of the shaft seldom reaches the joint.

Question 569. What name is given to an epiphysis that serves only as the attachment of a tendon and takes no part in a joint, such as the greater trochanter? The answer is a traction epiphysis, or apophysis. It grows in response to the pull of the muscle attached to it. The tibial tuberosity is another example.

Question 570. In a growing long bone the epiphyseal plate separates two regions; which lies on the joint side of the plate and which on the shaft side? The answer is the epiphysis on the joint side and the metaphysis on the shaft side. Growth in length occurs at the plate, so the epiphysis is pushed away from the shaft. When growth stops, the plate becomes a line.

Question 571. In the adult, what remains at the site of a closed epiphyseal plate: a band of hyaline cartilage, a thin line of bone called the epiphyseal line, a gap filled with marrow, or a layer of periosteum? The answer is a thin line of bone, the epiphyseal line. It marks where the plate lay and can often be seen on a radiograph.

Question 572. Name the two cartilages that lie in the region of the epiphysis of a growing long bone. The answer is the articular cartilage at the joint surface and the epiphyseal plate at its base. The cartilage covers the joint for life, and the plate is the site of growth in length.

Question 573. Which vessels supply the epiphysis of a growing bone, and which supply the metaphysis on the other side of the plate? The answer is the epiphyseal arteries for the epiphysis and the metaphyseal arteries for the metaphysis. The metaphyseal vessels are branches of the nutrient artery of the shaft.

Question 574. A constructed illustration: an infection begins in the shaft and spreads to the metaphysis but does not enter the joint. Which structure has held it back? The answer is the epiphyseal plate, which is avascular cartilage. In the older child it bars the way from the metaphysis to the epiphysis and the joint, while in a newborn the vessels cross it.

Question 575. What is the name of the region of a long bone that lies between the epiphyseal plate and the shaft? The answer is the metaphysis. It is the flared part of the shaft next to the plate. In the adult its site is marked by the epiphyseal line.

Question 576. Which of these is found in the metaphysis of a growing bone: the zone where calcified cartilage is replaced by bone, the smooth articular surface, the nutrient foramen, or the fibrous layer of the periosteum? The answer is the zone where calcified cartilage is replaced by bone. The metaphysis is the region of most active bone formation in the growing skeleton.

Question 577. A described longitudinal section of a growing bone shows a band of cartilage across the end, and below it a zone of bony spicules that widen into the shaft. Which two regions are shown below the joint? The answer is the epiphyseal plate and the metaphysis. The band is the plate, and the zone of new bone beneath it is the metaphysis.

Question 578. A constructed illustration: a tumor of bone arises in an adolescent at the lower end of the femur, just above the knee. Which region is the usual site, and why? The answer is the metaphysis. It is the region of fastest cell division in the growing skeleton, which is why a bone tumor is more common in a growing person than in an adult.

Question 579. Which statement about the metaphyseal arteries is correct: they are branches of the nutrient artery and end in loops at the plate, they supply the epiphysis, they cross the plate, or they supply the joint cartilage? The answer is that they are branches of the nutrient artery ending in loops at the plate, where the flow is slow and infection is favored.

Question 580. True or false: the metaphysis lies between the epiphyseal plate and the shaft, and it becomes the flared end of the shaft in the adult. The answer is true. In the child the metaphysis is the zone of newly formed bone next to the plate, and when growth ends the plate closes and the metaphysis merges with the epiphysis.

Question 581. Which of these pairs belongs together: the epiphysis with the growth plate, the metaphysis with the flare of bone next to the plate, the diaphysis with the joint surface, or the medullary cavity with the periosteum? The answer is the metaphysis with the flare of bone next to the plate. The epiphysis is the part beyond the plate and the diaphysis is the shaft.

Question 582. Which pair belongs together: the epiphyseal plate with the region between the epiphysis and the shaft, the epiphyseal line with the closed plate of the adult, the metaphysis with the smooth joint surface, or the endosteum with the outer surface of the bone? The answer is the epiphyseal line with the closed plate of the adult, which is the scar the plate leaves behind.

Question 583. Which statement about the diaphysis of a long bone is correct: it is the shaft, a tube of compact bone around the cavity, it is the end next to the joint, it is a plate of cartilage, or it is a thin shell of spongy bone? The answer is that it is the shaft, a tube of compact bone with a wall thickest in the middle.

Question 584. A described radiograph of a long bone shows a thick white tube, widest at its middle, with the ends expanding into a lacework of bone. Which region is the white tube, and what is its wall made of? The answer is the diaphysis, and its wall is compact bone. The cortex is thickest at the middle of the shaft, where bending forces are greatest.

Question 585. What name is given to the shaft of a long bone, the part that lies between the two ends? The answer is the diaphysis. It is built of compact bone around the medullary cavity, and it develops from the primary center of ossification. Its periosteum carries the vessels that supply the outer part of its wall.

Question 586. A cut is made straight across the middle of the diaphysis of a long bone. Which layers does the cut pass through, from the outside inward? The answer is the periosteum, the compact bone of the cortex, the endosteum, and then the medullary cavity with its marrow. The cut meets compact bone all around the cavity, and no spongy bone is present at that level.

Question 587. Name, in order from one end to the other, the three regions into which the length of a long bone is divided. The answer is the epiphysis, the metaphysis, the diaphysis, and then the metaphysis and the epiphysis of the other end. The epiphyses lie nearest the joints, the metaphyses flank the shaft, and the diaphysis is the shaft itself.

Question 588. In the development of a long bone, around which part of the cartilage model does the bony collar form, and which part of the adult bone does that collar become? The answer is that it forms around the middle of the model and becomes the diaphysis. Bone is laid down in the perichondrium there before the primary center appears. The ends stay cartilage and ossify later.

Question 589. What name is given to a large rounded projection on a bone that gives attachment to a muscle, such as the one on the humerus for the deltoid? The answer is a tuberosity. A smaller and sharper projection of the same kind is a tubercle. Both are marks left by the pull of a muscle or a ligament.

Question 590. Which of these is a depression in a bone that lodges another structure: a fossa, a process, a crest, or a tubercle? The answer is a fossa. A process, a crest and a tubercle are projections rather than depressions. The olecranon fossa of the humerus lodges the tip of the ulna when the elbow straightens.

Question 591. A described radiograph of the lower end of the femur shows two rounded masses separated by a deep notch, with a rough prominence above each. Which markings are described? The answer is the two condyles with the intercondylar fossa between them, and the epicondyles above. The condyles are smooth articular surfaces, while the epicondyles give attachment to ligaments.

Question 592. A constructed illustration: a blow to the outer side of the knee forces the joint inward, and the ligament attached to the inner side of the lower femur is torn. Which marking anchors that ligament? The answer is the medial epicondyle, a roughened projection just above the medial condyle. The ligament attached to it holds the joint against a force from the outer side.

Question 593. Which of these markings is a passage through bone for a vessel or a nerve: a foramen, a spine, a crest, or a tuberosity? The answer is a foramen. A nutrient foramen admits the artery of the marrow, and other foramina admit nerves and veins. A spine, a crest and a tuberosity are projections.

Question 594. Which of these pairs belongs together: a foramen with a passage for a vessel or a nerve, a fossa with a smooth surface, a crest with a hole, or a tubercle with a groove? The answer is a foramen with a passage for a vessel or a nerve. A fossa lodges a structure, a crest is a ridge, and a tubercle is a small projection.

Question 595. What name is given to a prominent ridge of bone that gives attachment to muscles and fascia, such as the ridge along the top of the hip bone? The answer is a crest. A smaller ridge is called a line. Both are raised marks produced where a muscle or a sheet of fascia pulls on the bone.

Question 596. Which of these is a small, smooth, flat or slightly concave surface where one bone meets another: a facet, a tubercle, a spine, or a foramen? The answer is a facet. A facet is covered by cartilage and takes part in a joint or in a sliding contact. A tubercle and a spine are projections, and a foramen is an opening.

Question 597. True or false: the size and the roughness of a bone marking usually reflect the size and the pull of the muscle attached there. The answer is true. A large muscle, or one that pulls at a poor mechanical advantage, leaves a larger mark. The mark grows through life as the bone responds to the load it carries.

Question 598. Which of these pairs belongs together: the greater trochanter with the attachment of hip muscles, the fovea with a nerve passage, the notch with a smooth joint surface, or the meatus with a pad of fat? The answer is the greater trochanter with the attachment of hip muscles. A fovea is a small pit and a notch is a depression at the edge of a bone.

Question 599. Name three kinds of opening or depression found on bones, with one example of each. The answer is a foramen, such as the nutrient foramen of a long bone, a fossa, such as the olecranon fossa of the humerus, and a groove, such as the groove on the back of the humerus for the radial nerve. Each lodges or transmits a named structure.

Question 600. Which statement about bone markings is correct: they are arbitrary, they record the attachments and passages of the bone, they appear only in old age, or they bear no relation to function? The answer is that they record the attachments, passages and articulations of the bone. A projection marks a pull, an opening marks a passage, and a smooth surface marks a joint.

To summarize, bone is a living tissue whose cells lie in lacunae and keep in touch through canaliculi, with osteoblasts forming the matrix, osteocytes maintaining it and osteoclasts dissolving it. Its matrix is about one third organic, mainly collagen, and two thirds mineral, the collagen giving toughness and the mineral hardness. Compact bone is built of osteons whose concentric lamellae surround central canals joined by perforating canals, while spongy bone is a lattice of trabeculae that follow the lines of stress. The periosteum covers the outside of a bone except at the articular surfaces, the endosteum lines every internal surface, and the medullary cavity holds the marrow. A long bone is divided into the epiphysis beyond the growth plate, the metaphysis next to it, and the diaphysis or shaft, and every bone is written over with the markings of its muscles, vessels and joints, from tuberosities and crests to foramina and fossae.
