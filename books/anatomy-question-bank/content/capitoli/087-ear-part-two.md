# Chapter 87 — Ear, Part Two

This chapter continues the ear and then turns to the chemical senses. It takes up the cochlea and its spiral organ of hearing, the vestibular apparatus of the inner ear, and the vestibulocochlear nerve that carries both to the brainstem. The second half follows taste from the taste buds of the tongue and smell from the olfactory epithelium in the roof of the nose. It holds one hundred questions, and each one gives its answer immediately after the question and then explains why that answer is right. The material is educational and does not replace an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 8601. Which membrane of the inner ear carries the spiral organ of hearing? The answer is the basilar membrane. It runs from the bony spiral lamina to the outer wall of the cochlear duct, separating that duct from the scala tympani below. The hair cells of the spiral organ rest on it and move when a sound wave passes.

Question 8602. Which compartment of the cochlea contains endolymph: the scala vestibuli, the scala tympani, the cochlear duct, or the modiolus? The answer is the cochlear duct. The scala vestibuli and the scala tympani both contain perilymph and join each other at the apex of the cochlea. The modiolus is bone, not a space.

Question 8603. Which fluid-filled space lies immediately above the basilar membrane, and which lies immediately below it? The answer is the cochlear duct above and the scala tympani below. The cochlear duct holds endolymph and the scala tympani perilymph. The scala vestibuli lies above the cochlear duct, parted from it by the vestibular membrane.

Question 8604. A described microscopic section through the cochlea shows a triangular tunnel resting on the basilar membrane, with one row of hair cells on its inner side and three rows on its outer side. What is that tunnel called? The answer is the tunnel of Corti, formed by the pillar cells. It separates the inner hair cells from the outer ones, and the tectorial membrane overhangs both rows.

Question 8605. Which bony core does the spiral canal of the cochlea wind around? The answer is the modiolus, and the spiral ganglion lies within it. The canal makes about two and a half turns around this core. The fibers of the cochlear nerve leave the ganglion and pass toward the brainstem.

Question 8606. Which of these structures contains perilymph: the cochlear duct, the utricle, the semicircular canals, or the scala tympani? The answer is the scala tympani. The cochlear duct, the utricle, and the semicircular canals all belong to the membranous labyrinth and contain endolymph. Perilymph, by contrast, resembles ordinary tissue fluid in its salts.

Question 8607. A constructed illustration: a worker who has spent years in a very loud workplace has lost the hearing of high-pitched sounds first. Which cells of the spiral organ are damaged earliest? The answer is the outer hair cells. They are the most easily injured by loud sound, and they amplify the movement of the basilar membrane.

Question 8608. True or false: the hair cells of the spiral organ are named for the fine bristles on their upper surface. The answer is true. Each cell carries a bundle of stereocilia, and the tallest of them touch the tectorial membrane. Bending those bristles opens the channels that begin the nerve signal.

Question 8609. The cell bodies of the sensory neurons that carry hearing lie in which small mass of gray matter inside the modiolus? The answer is the spiral ganglion. Each neuron there is bipolar, with one process reaching the hair cells and the other forming the cochlear nerve.

Question 8610. At the apex of the cochlea, which two spaces join through a small opening called the helicotrema? The answer is the scala vestibuli and the scala tympani. Both contain perilymph, so pressure applied at the oval window can travel up one and back along the other.

Question 8611. Where is the basilar membrane widest and most flexible: at the base near the oval window, at the apex, at the middle turn, or at the helicotrema? The answer is at the apex. The membrane is narrow and stiff at the base and broad and slack at the apex. High-pitched sounds move the base and low-pitched sounds move the apex.

Question 8612. A described preparation of the cochlea shows that the basilar membrane near the oval window is narrow and tightly stretched, while near the far end it is broad and slack. Which sounds move the narrow part? The answer is high-pitched sounds. Its stiffness makes the narrow membrane vibrate best at high frequencies, and the slack far end answers low ones.

Question 8613. Which thin membrane separates the cochlear duct from the scala vestibuli? The answer is the vestibular membrane, also called Reissner's membrane. It is only a few cells thick and carries no hair cells. It keeps apart two fluids that differ in their salts.

Question 8614. Name the two fluids of the inner ear and say which one fills the membranous labyrinth. The answer is endolymph and perilymph, and endolymph fills the membranous labyrinth. Perilymph surrounds it in the bony labyrinth. Endolymph is rich in potassium, and that difference makes the hair cells work.

Question 8615. A constructed illustration: a person suddenly develops deafness and unsteadiness on one side after the blood supply of the inner ear fails. Which small artery supplies the cochlea and the vestibular apparatus together? The answer is the labyrinthine artery. It is an end artery, so its blockage damages hearing and balance at once. What led to the blockage belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 8616. Which structure secretes the endolymph that fills the cochlear duct: the spiral ganglion, the stria vascularis, the tectorial membrane, or the spiral lamina? The answer is the stria vascularis. It lies on the outer wall of the cochlear duct and is rich in small vessels. The ganglion holds the sensory neurons and the spiral lamina is bone.

Question 8617. Which delicate membrane hangs over the hair cells of the spiral organ and is touched by the tallest of their bristles? The answer is the tectorial membrane. It is fixed at one edge and floats free at the other. When the basilar membrane moves, the hair cells are pressed against it and their bristles bend.

Question 8618. Which small bone of the middle ear sits in the oval window and passes the vibrations of sound into the perilymph? The answer is the stapes. Its footplate is held in the window by a ring of ligament. The malleus and the incus lie further out, in the middle ear, and carry the vibration inward.

Question 8619. In a described transverse section across one turn of the cochlea, three fluid spaces appear one above another. Which lies in the middle, and what does it contain? The answer is the cochlear duct, and it contains endolymph. The scala vestibuli lies above it and the scala tympani below, and both hold perilymph.

Question 8620. Why does a high-pitched sound excite the base of the cochlea while a low-pitched sound excites the apex, when both enter by the same oval window? The answer is the changing width and stiffness of the basilar membrane. Narrow and stiff at the base, it vibrates best at high frequencies, and broad and slack at the apex, it answers low ones, so the place of greatest movement is read as pitch.

Question 8621. What is the name of the sensory ridge inside the swollen end of a semicircular canal, the structure that answers turning of the head? The answer is the crista ampullaris. It carries hair cells whose bristles project into a gelatinous cap called the cupula. When the head turns, the fluid lags behind and bends the cupula.

Question 8622. Which part of the inner ear detects rotation of the head: the utricle, the saccule, the semicircular canals, or the cochlear duct? The answer is the semicircular canals. Each canal is a loop filled with endolymph, and its ampulla holds the sensory ridge. The utricle and the saccule answer straight-line movement and gravity, so rotation is the business of the canals alone.

Question 8623. A described microscopic section from the inner ear shows a patch of hair cells covered by a gelatinous membrane in which small crystals lie. Which organ is this? The answer is a macula, either of the utricle or of the saccule. The crystals are otoconia of calcium carbonate, and their weight makes the membrane shift when the head tilts.

Question 8624. Which small duct joins the utricle to the saccule, and where does the fluid go from there? The answer is the utriculosaccular duct, from which the endolymphatic duct leads to the endolymphatic sac. That sac lies in the dura on the back of the petrous part of the temporal bone, and the path keeps the pressure of the inner ear steady.

Question 8625. What are the small crystals of calcium carbonate that rest on the maculae of the utricle and the saccule called? The answer is the otoconia, also called otoliths. They sit in the gelatinous membrane above the hair cells and give it extra weight. That weight makes the membrane slide when the head tilts.

Question 8626. Which semicircular canal lies roughly horizontal when a person stands upright: the anterior canal, the posterior canal, the lateral canal, or the endolymphatic duct? The answer is the lateral canal, the shortest of the three and the one tested when a seated person's head is turned. The anterior and the posterior canals lie in vertical planes.

Question 8627. A constructed illustration: a person turns over in bed and feels the room spin for a few seconds, and the clinician suspects that loose crystals have drifted into one canal. Which canal is most often involved? The answer is the posterior semicircular canal, whose lowest part lies below the utricle when the head is tipped back.

Question 8628. True or false: a vestibular hair cell sends more signals when its bristles bend toward the single long cilium on its surface. The answer is true. That long cilium is the kinocilium, and bending the bundle toward it opens channels and excites the cell. Bending the other way reduces the signal, so the cell reports direction as well as amount.

Question 8629. Which division of the vestibular nerve supplies the posterior semicircular canal and the saccule? The answer is the inferior division. The superior division supplies the anterior and the lateral canals together with the utricle. A small tumor can press on one division and disturb only part of the balance system.

Question 8630. Which of these pairs belongs together: the crista ampullaris with straight-line movement, the macula of the utricle with turning of the head, the crista ampullaris with turning of the head, or the macula of the saccule with sound? The answer is the crista ampullaris with turning of the head. A crista sits in an ampulla and answers rotation.

Question 8631. Each semicircular canal lies in a plane that is nearly parallel to one canal of the opposite ear. Which canal of the right ear lies in the same plane as the anterior canal of the left ear? The answer is the posterior canal of the right ear, while the two lateral canals form the other parallel pair. The canals therefore work in pairs.

Question 8632. Name the three semicircular canals of one ear in the order in which a clinician usually describes them. The answer is the anterior canal, the posterior canal, and the lateral canal. The anterior canal is also called the superior canal, and the lateral one the horizontal canal. Each ends in an ampulla.

Question 8633. The endolymphatic sac ends its duct in the dura of which part of the skull? The answer is the posterior cranial fossa, on the back of the petrous part of the temporal bone. The duct travels through the bone from the vestibule to reach it. Pressure in the sac can affect the whole membranous labyrinth.

Question 8634. A described preparation of the inner ear shows the dilated end of a semicircular canal opened to reveal a ridge of hair cells whose bristles stand in a gelatinous cap. What structure is shown, and what does it detect? The answer is the crista ampullaris, and it detects turning of the head. The cap is the cupula, and it holds no crystals, unlike a macula.

Question 8635. The saccule is joined to the cochlear duct by which small tube, a link that explains why infection can travel between the hearing and the balancing parts of the inner ear? The answer is the ductus reuniens. It carries endolymph between the saccule and the cochlear duct. The utricle has no such direct connection.

Question 8636. A constructed illustration: a person has a sudden attack of severe spinning, nausea, and unsteadiness that lasts for days, without any loss of hearing. Which part of the inner ear is most likely inflamed? The answer is the vestibular apparatus, a picture often called vestibular neuritis. Hearing is spared because the cochlear part is not involved.

Question 8637. Which artery supplies the inner ear: the middle meningeal artery, the labyrinthine artery, the occipital artery, or the superficial temporal artery? The answer is the labyrinthine artery, usually a branch of the anterior inferior cerebellar artery. It travels with the vestibulocochlear nerve through the internal acoustic meatus.

Question 8638. A constructed illustration: a person has slowly worsening deafness and ringing in one ear, and imaging shows a small tumor at the internal acoustic meatus. From which cells does it arise? The answer is the Schwann cells of the vestibular division of the vestibulocochlear nerve. It presses on the facial nerve beside it as well.

Question 8639. The macula of the utricle lies in a nearly horizontal plane while the macula of the saccule lies in a nearly vertical plane. Which of the two answers a forward and backward movement of the head, and which answers a sideways tilt? The answer is that the utricular macula answers forward and backward movement and the saccular macula answers sideways tilt.

Question 8640. In a described section of the inner ear cut in the plane of the lateral semicircular canal, that canal appears as a complete ring. In which plane does the ring lie when the head is upright? The answer is the horizontal plane, parallel to the floor. The anterior and the posterior canals would appear as rings in vertical planes.

Question 8641. Which cranial nerve carries the signals of both hearing and balance from the inner ear to the brainstem? The answer is the vestibulocochlear nerve, the eighth cranial nerve. It has a cochlear part and a vestibular part that travel together. Both are sensory, and the nerve has no motor fibers.

Question 8642. Through which opening does the vestibulocochlear nerve leave the inner ear: the jugular foramen, the internal acoustic meatus, the foramen ovale, or the stylomastoid foramen? The answer is the internal acoustic meatus, which the facial nerve and the labyrinthine artery also pass through. The jugular foramen carries the glossopharyngeal, the vagus, and the accessory nerves.

Question 8643. Which two other structures lie beside the vestibulocochlear nerve inside the internal acoustic meatus? The answer is the facial nerve and the labyrinthine artery. The facial nerve runs above and in front of the cochlear nerve. That close company is why a tumor of the vestibular nerve disturbs the face as well as the ear.

Question 8644. A described specimen shows a nerve entering the brainstem in the groove between the pons and the medulla, just behind the olive-shaped swelling. Which nerve is it? The answer is the vestibulocochlear nerve, which enters at the junction of the pons and the medulla. The facial nerve emerges just beside it.

Question 8645. In which pair of brainstem nuclei do the fibers of the cochlear nerve end before any of them cross to the other side? The answer is the dorsal and the ventral cochlear nuclei, on the surface of the medulla at the junction with the pons. From there the signals pass to the superior olivary complex on both sides, and that crossing lets each ear be compared with the other.

Question 8646. Where does the pathway of hearing reach the cerebral cortex: the occipital lobe, the superior temporal gyrus, the postcentral gyrus, or the insula? The answer is the superior temporal gyrus, in the transverse temporal gyrus buried in the floor of the lateral sulcus. The signal arrives after relays in the inferior colliculus and the medial geniculate body.

Question 8647. Which of these pairs belongs together: the lateral vestibular nucleus with eye movements, the medial vestibular nucleus with posture, the lateral vestibular nucleus with posture, or the cochlear nucleus with balance? The answer is the lateral vestibular nucleus with posture, which gives rise to the vestibulospinal tract. The medial vestibular nucleus works with the nuclei of the eye muscles.

Question 8648. A constructed illustration: a person who has had a stroke in the lower brainstem has lost the hearing of one ear, has severe vertigo, and has a weak face on the same side. Which nerve and which part of the brainstem would a clinician consider? The answer is the vestibulocochlear nerve at the junction of the pons and the medulla.

Question 8649. A described microscopic preparation shows bipolar nerve cells in a small ganglion inside a core of bone, each cell sending one process toward a row of hair cells and the other toward the brain. Which ganglion is shown? The answer is the spiral ganglion of the cochlea, whose cells are the first sensory neurons of hearing. A comparable ganglion serves the balance organs.

Question 8650. Why does damage to the cochlear nerve cause permanent deafness, while damage to the conducting apparatus of the middle ear often leaves hearing that can be helped? The answer is that the fibers of the cochlear nerve and the hair cells do not regenerate. The middle ear only conducts sound to a cochlea that is still intact.

Question 8651. Which large papillae, arranged in a V shape at the back of the tongue, carry most of the taste buds of the tongue? The answer is the circumvallate papillae. There are only a few, but each is ringed by a trough that holds many taste buds. The filiform papillae, which cover most of the tongue, carry none.

Question 8652. Which nerve carries taste from the front two thirds of the tongue: the glossopharyngeal nerve, the facial nerve, the vagus nerve, or the hypoglossal nerve? The answer is the facial nerve, which reaches the tongue by a branch called the chorda tympani. The glossopharyngeal nerve serves the back third.

Question 8653. The chorda tympani travels through the middle ear and then joins which larger nerve, which supplies general sensation to the front of the tongue? The answer is the lingual nerve, a branch of the mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve. Cutting it removes touch and pain from the tongue, while taste may survive.

Question 8654. A described microscopic section shows an oval barrel of cells set in the epithelium of the tongue, with a narrow opening at its summit and delicate hairs reaching into it. What is shown? The answer is a taste bud. The opening is the taste pore, and the cells inside are the gustatory cells.

Question 8655. Which papillae of the tongue are the small thread-like projections that give the tongue its rough surface and contain no taste buds? The answer is the filiform papillae, the most numerous of all, which carry only general sensation. The fungiform, foliate, and circumvallate papillae all hold taste buds.

Question 8656. Taste from the back third of the tongue, including the circumvallate papillae, travels on which nerve: the facial nerve, the glossopharyngeal nerve, the trigeminal nerve, or the hypoglossal nerve? The answer is the glossopharyngeal nerve, the ninth cranial nerve. The trigeminal nerve carries only general sensation from the tongue.

Question 8657. A constructed illustration: a person with sudden weakness of one side of the face also reports that food tastes dull on that side. Which nerve explains both findings? The answer is the facial nerve, whose fibers for taste travel in the chorda tympani. The taste loss is felt on the front two thirds of the tongue.

Question 8658. True or false: sweet is tasted only at the tip of the tongue, bitter only at the back, and sour only at the sides. The answer is false. Every taste quality can be detected across the tongue, though the thresholds differ. The old tongue map misreads data from the nineteenth century, and no region is reserved for one quality.

Question 8659. In which brainstem nucleus do the taste fibers of the facial, the glossopharyngeal, and the vagus nerves all end? The answer is the nucleus of the solitary tract, in the medulla, the shared doorway for taste from the tongue, the palate, the pharynx, and the epiglottis. From it the signals pass to the thalamus and then to the insula.

Question 8660. Which of these pairs belongs together: the geniculate ganglion with taste from the back of the tongue, the inferior ganglion of the glossopharyngeal nerve with taste from the back of the tongue, the geniculate ganglion with taste from the epiglottis, or the nodose ganglion with taste from the front of the tongue? The answer is the inferior ganglion of the glossopharyngeal nerve with taste from the back of the tongue.

Question 8661. Which cells of a taste bud divide and replace the gustatory cells as they wear out: the supporting cells, the basal cells, the gustatory cells themselves, or the cells of the taste pore? The answer is the basal cells, which lie at the base and act as stem cells. Supporting cells hold the bud together.

Question 8662. Name the three cranial nerves that carry taste, and say which of them serves the epiglottis. The answer is the facial, the glossopharyngeal, and the vagus nerves, and the vagus serves the epiglottis. The facial nerve serves the front two thirds of the tongue and the soft palate.

Question 8663. A constructed illustration: after an operation on the middle ear, a person notices that food tastes metallic on one side, although the face moves normally. Which small nerve was most likely stretched? The answer is the chorda tympani, which crosses the inner surface of the eardrum on its way to the tongue.

Question 8664. In which part of the cerebral cortex does the conscious sensation of taste arrive, deep in the lateral sulcus? The answer is the insula, together with the adjacent frontal operculum. It receives the fibers that have passed through the thalamus, and it lies close to the areas for smell.

Question 8665. Which statement about the taste qualities is correct: each quality has its own separate patch of tongue, all qualities can be detected over most of the tongue, only the front of the tongue can taste, or only the back can taste bitter? The answer is that all qualities can be detected over most of the tongue.

Question 8666. A described microscopic preparation from the side of the tongue shows folds of epithelium like the leaves of a book, and taste buds sit on the walls of those folds. Which papillae are shown, and which nerve serves them? The answer is the foliate papillae, and the glossopharyngeal nerve serves most of them.

Question 8667. Taste buds lie in the epithelium of the epiglottis and the pharynx. Which nerve carries their signals to the brainstem? The answer is the vagus nerve, the tenth cranial nerve. Its taste fibers have their cell bodies in the nodose ganglion, and the internal branch of its superior laryngeal nerve serves the same region.

Question 8668. Which of these is not part of a taste bud: a gustatory cell, a supporting cell, a basal cell, or a hair cell of the spiral organ? The answer is a hair cell of the spiral organ, which belongs to the cochlea and serves hearing. A taste bud holds gustatory, supporting, and basal cells.

Question 8669. The cell bodies of the taste fibers that travel in the facial nerve lie in which ganglion? The answer is the geniculate ganglion, which sits on the facial nerve as it turns in the bone near the middle ear. From it, fibers pass to the nucleus of the solitary tract, and fibers for the palate travel in the same nerve.

Question 8670. A constructed illustration: a person has lost the sensation of touch and pain on one side of the front of the tongue but still tastes sweetness there. Which nerve has been injured, and which has been spared? The answer is the lingual nerve injured and the chorda tympani spared.

Question 8671. Which taste quality is detected by cells that form a synapse and release a messenger onto the nerve ending, rather than by cells that signal through a receptor on their surface: sweet, bitter, sour, or umami? The answer is sour. Sweet, bitter, and umami act through receptors that set off a chain inside the cell.

Question 8672. What is the name of the narrow opening at the summit of a taste bud, through which dissolved substances reach the gustatory cells? The answer is the taste pore. The sensitive tips of the gustatory cells project into it, and a substance must dissolve in saliva before it can be tasted. A dry mouth therefore dulls taste.

Question 8673. A described electron micrograph shows a narrow channel through the surface of the tongue, and within it fine processes from cells packed below. Which structure is shown? The answer is the taste pore of a taste bud, and the fine processes are the microvilli of the gustatory cells.

Question 8674. Why does a bitter substance often taste strong at a concentration so low that it cannot be seen or smelled? The answer is that bitter is the taste of warning, and the body is built to detect it early. Many plant poisons are bitter, so a low threshold protects the person.

Question 8675. Which of these pairs belongs together: circumvallate papillae with the tip of the tongue, fungiform papillae with the back of the tongue, foliate papillae with the lateral margin of the tongue, or filiform papillae with taste? The answer is foliate papillae with the lateral margin of the tongue.

Question 8676. In which part of the nasal cavity does the olfactory epithelium lie? The answer is the roof, over the superior nasal concha and the upper part of the nasal septum. Air must be sniffed upward to reach it, which is why a gentle sniff sharpens a faint odor.

Question 8677. Through the openings of which bone do the filaments of the olfactory nerve pass on their way to the brain? The answer is the cribriform plate of the ethmoid bone, whose many small holes lie in the roof of the nasal cavity. A fracture there can tear the filaments and remove the sense of smell.

Question 8678. Which glands lie in the olfactory epithelium and secrete the mucus in which odorants dissolve: the serous glands of the tongue, the olfactory glands, the sebaceous glands, or the lacrimal glands? The answer is the olfactory glands, also called the glands of Bowman.

Question 8679. A described microscopic section from the roof of the nose shows a tall columnar epithelium with three kinds of cells, one of them a bipolar neuron whose fine processes reach the surface. Which epithelium is shown? The answer is the olfactory epithelium, whose neurons are the receptor cells of smell.

Question 8680. Which statement about the pathway of smell is correct: it relays in the thalamus first, it reaches the cortex without a thalamic relay, it begins in the brainstem, or it crosses completely to the other side? The answer is that it reaches the cortex without a thalamic relay, because the olfactory tract runs directly to the primary olfactory cortex.

Question 8681. Which of these pairs belongs together: the basal cell with the detection of odors, the mitral cell with the detection of odors, the basal cell with the replacement of worn-out receptor cells, or the supporting cell with the transmission of the signal? The answer is the basal cell with the replacement of worn-out receptor cells.

Question 8682. What are the small structures in the olfactory bulb where the axons of many receptor cells come together on a few relay cells? The answer is the glomeruli. Each receives fibers from receptor cells that answer the same kind of odorant molecule. The relay cells, called mitral and tufted cells, then send the signal onward.

Question 8683. Which cells of the olfactory bulb receive the signal from the receptor cells and carry it out in the olfactory tract: the basal cells, the supporting cells, the mitral cells, or the Schwann cells? The answer is the mitral cells. The tufted cells do the same work beside them.

Question 8684. True or false: the receptor neurons of smell are replaced throughout adult life. The answer is true. They are the only sensory neurons of the body known to be renewed in this way, and they are replaced roughly every month or two.

Question 8685. The olfactory tract ends in the primary olfactory cortex, which lies in which part of the brain? The answer is the temporal lobe, in the region of the uncus and the piriform cortex. The fibers reach it without any relay in the thalamus, and nearby parts of the amygdala receive branches as well.

Question 8686. A described specimen shows the roof of the nasal cavity from above, with a perforated plate of bone and many fine nerves passing through its holes to a flattened swelling above. What is that swelling? The answer is the olfactory bulb, and the perforated plate is the cribriform plate.

Question 8687. A constructed illustration: a person who struck the front of the head in a fall reports that food has lost its flavor, although sweet, sour, salty, and bitter are all still tasted normally. Which fibers were torn, and where? The answer is the olfactory filaments, torn as they pass through the cribriform plate.

Question 8688. Which part of the brain gives smell its strong link with memory and emotion: the cerebellum, the amygdala and the nearby hippocampal region, the occipital cortex, or the precentral gyrus? The answer is the amygdala and the nearby hippocampal region, which the olfactory tract reaches without a relay in the thalamus.

Question 8689. A person who has completely lost the sense of smell still feels a sharp sting when a bottle of ammonia is opened under the nose. Why? The answer is that ammonia also stimulates the trigeminal nerve endings in the lining of the nose. That sting survives the loss of the olfactory fibers.

Question 8690. A described sagittal section of the head shows a stream of air entering the nostril, rising to the roof of the nasal cavity, and then turning down toward the throat. Why does the sense of smell benefit from that upward path? The answer is that the olfactory epithelium lies in the roof of the cavity.

Question 8691. What is the name for the route by which odors from food in the mouth reach the olfactory epithelium from behind and so add to the flavor of a meal? The answer is the retronasal route, by which the odorant travels up behind the soft palate. Blocking the nose during a meal removes much of the flavor even though taste remains.

Question 8692. True or false: the fibers of the olfactory tract stay entirely on the side on which they began. The answer is false. A small bundle crosses to the other side in the anterior commissure, though most fibers do remain on their own side.

Question 8693. A young person who has never been able to smell has also not gone through the usual changes of puberty. Which explanation fits both findings? The answer is that the neurons that carry the sense of smell failed to develop, and the cells that control the reproductive glands travel with them.

Question 8694. A described microscopic preparation from the roof of the nose shows a neuron whose cell body lies within the epithelium, whose upper process ends in a knob with fine cilia, and whose lower process leaves the tissue. Which cell is shown? The answer is an olfactory receptor neuron.

Question 8695. The medial stria of the olfactory tract carries fibers across to the opposite hemisphere. Through which small bundle do they cross? The answer is the anterior commissure. Fibers that stay on their own side travel in the lateral stria and reach the temporal lobe.

Question 8696. The receptor cells of smell are replaced every month or two, yet a familiar odor is still recognized. What explains that constancy? The answer is that each new cell takes up the same kind of receptor as the one it replaced and takes the same place in the epithelium.

Question 8697. Why does the loss of smell often go unnoticed for a long time, while a loss of hearing is reported at once? The answer is that a person can still taste sweet, sour, salty, and bitter without any sense of smell. Much of what is called flavor depends on the olfactory route.

Question 8698. A described specimen of the nasal cavity shows a small yellowish patch of epithelium in the roof, distinct in color from the pink lining beneath it. What is that patch, and what makes it different? The answer is the olfactory epithelium, and its color comes from pigment in its supporting cells.

Question 8699. A tumor that fills the upper part of the nasal cavity and erodes the cribriform plate can cause loss of smell and nosebleeds. From which cells does such a tumor most often arise? The answer is the olfactory receptor neurons of the olfactory epithelium, one of the few cancers that begin in a sensory epithelium of the head.

Question 8700. A described coronal section through the nasal cavity shows three curled shelves of bone on the lateral wall, with the narrow space above the highest shelf opening toward the skull. Which epithelium lines that highest space? The answer is the olfactory epithelium, which lies in the olfactory cleft. Air reaches it only when it is drawn upward, which is why a sniff helps.

To summarize, the cochlea sorts sound by place, because the basilar membrane is narrow and stiff at the base, where high-pitched sounds act, and broad and slack at the apex, where low-pitched sounds act, and the spiral organ on it turns that movement into nerve signals through hair cells whose bristles touch the tectorial membrane. The vestibular apparatus answers movement with three semicircular canals whose cristae detect turning and with the utricle and the saccule, whose maculae detect straight-line movement and the pull of gravity, and both sets of signals leave by the vestibular division of the vestibulocochlear nerve, which travels with the facial nerve through the internal acoustic meatus. Taste arrives by three nerves, the facial through the chorda tympani from the front two thirds of the tongue, the glossopharyngeal from the back third, and the vagus from the epiglottis, and all end in the nucleus of the solitary tract before the signal reaches the insula. Smell begins in the olfactory epithelium in the roof of the nose, whose receptor neurons are renewed throughout life and send their axons through the cribriform plate to the olfactory bulb and then directly to the temporal lobe.
