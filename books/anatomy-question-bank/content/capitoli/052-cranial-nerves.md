# Chapter 52 — Cranial Nerves

Every cranial nerve leaves the skull through a named opening and carries a particular set of fibers, and the first six are the ones a clinician tests at the bedside. This chapter takes them in order: the olfactory nerve, the optic nerve, the oculomotor nerve, the trochlear nerve, the trigeminal nerve and the abducens nerve. Each is described by where its fibers begin, the opening they use and the structures they serve. It holds one hundred questions, and every question gives its answer immediately afterwards and then explains why that answer is right, so nothing has to be carried forward in memory. This is educational material, and it supports rather than replaces an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 5101. Which cranial nerve carries the sense of smell, and where do its receptors lie? The answer is the olfactory nerve, the first cranial nerve. Its receptors are nerve cells in a small patch of specialized lining in the roof of each nasal cavity. Their fibers are unmyelinated and gather into about twenty bundles on each side.

Question 5102. Which opening does the olfactory nerve pass through on its way to the brain: the optic canal, the cribriform plate, the foramen ovale, or the superior orbital fissure? The answer is the cribriform plate of the ethmoid bone. Its many small holes carry the olfactory filaments into the cranial cavity. The other openings carry the optic nerve and the branches of the trigeminal nerve.

Question 5103. Which structure rests on the cribriform plate and receives the olfactory filaments: the olfactory bulb, the trigeminal ganglion, the pituitary gland, or the optic chiasm? The answer is the olfactory bulb. It lies on the orbital surface of the frontal lobe and is the first relay station of the pathway. From it the olfactory tract runs backward along the base of the brain.

Question 5104. A described section through the roof of the nasal cavity shows fine bundles of fibers piercing a perforated plate of bone. Which nerve is shown, and where do those bundles end? The answer is the olfactory nerve, and the bundles end in the olfactory bulb above the plate. The plate is the cribriform plate of the ethmoid bone.

Question 5105. True or false: the olfactory pathway reaches the cerebral cortex without passing through the thalamus. The answer is true. Its fibers relay in the olfactory bulb and then run to the piriform cortex and the amygdala. Every other sensory pathway, including the visual pathway, relays in the thalamus first.

Question 5106. Which structure lies immediately above the cribriform plate, and which lies immediately below it? The answer is the olfactory bulb above and the olfactory lining of the nose below. The filaments cross the holes of the plate between the two. A fracture there can tear them and let cerebrospinal fluid escape through the nose.

Question 5107. A constructed illustration: a person struck on the front of the head loses the sense of smell on one side and reports a clear watery discharge from that nostril. Which nerve is injured, and at which bone? The answer is the olfactory nerve, torn as it crosses the cribriform plate of the ethmoid bone. The watery discharge is cerebrospinal fluid.

Question 5108. A described computed tomography study of the anterior cranial fossa shows a fracture line crossing its floor on one side. Which nerve is at risk there? The answer is the olfactory nerve, whose filaments cross that floor through the cribriform plate. Injury removes the sense of smell on the same side.

Question 5109. Which statement about the cells that detect smell is correct: they are nerve cells replaced throughout life, they are supporting cells that never divide, they are endings of the olfactory tract, or they are branches of the trigeminal nerve? The answer is that they are nerve cells replaced throughout life. They are the only sensory nerve cells known to be renewed in an adult.

Question 5110. Which of these runs backward from the olfactory bulb toward the temporal lobe: the olfactory tract, the optic nerve, the oculomotor nerve, or the trigeminal nerve? The answer is the olfactory tract. It divides into a medial and a lateral stria near the anterior perforated substance. The lateral stria carries most of the fibers to the primary olfactory cortex.

Question 5111. Which of these pairs belongs together: the olfactory nerve with the cribriform plate, the optic nerve with the foramen rotundum, the maxillary nerve with the optic canal, or the mandibular nerve with the superior orbital fissure? The answer is the olfactory nerve with the cribriform plate. The maxillary nerve passes through the foramen rotundum and the mandibular nerve through the foramen ovale.

Question 5112. Name two structures of the olfactory pathway that lie inside the cranial cavity, spoken as a list. The answer is the olfactory bulb and the olfactory tract. The bulb rests on the cribriform plate and the tract runs backward from it. Both lie against the orbital surface of the frontal lobe.

Question 5113. Where do most fibers of the olfactory tract end: in the thalamus, in the piriform cortex and the amygdala, in the occipital lobe, or in the cerebellum? The answer is the piriform cortex and the amygdala. Those areas lie in the temporal lobe and form the primary olfactory cortex. No thalamic relay interrupts the pathway on the way.

Question 5114. Which cranial nerve lies immediately above the cribriform plate, and which artery runs beside it in the same region? The answer is the olfactory nerve, with the anterior ethmoidal artery. That artery passes from the orbit into the cranial cavity and then down through the plate beside the filaments. Bleeding from it can follow a fracture of that floor.

Question 5115. Which cranial nerve carries the images formed by the retina to the brain, and where do its fibers begin? The answer is the optic nerve, the second cranial nerve. Its fibers begin in the ganglion cells of the retina and leave the eye at the optic disc. The nerve is really a tract of the brain drawn out into the orbit.

Question 5116. Which opening in the skull does the optic nerve pass through: the superior orbital fissure, the foramen rotundum, the optic canal, or the foramen ovale? The answer is the optic canal. The ophthalmic artery runs through it as well. The superior orbital fissure carries the nerves that move the eye.

Question 5117. What is the name of the pale round area at the back of the retina where the optic nerve fibers leave the eye? The answer is the optic disc. It holds no rods or cones, so it forms the blind spot of the visual field. The central retinal artery and vein enter and leave the eye at the same place.

Question 5118. A described view of the retina shows a small pit packed with cone cells at the center of a yellow patch. Which region is described, and what is special about it? The answer is the fovea centralis, at the center of the macula lutea. It holds the greatest density of cones and gives the sharpest vision.

Question 5119. Which of these lies above the pituitary gland and carries crossing fibers from both eyes: the optic chiasm, the optic disc, the fovea centralis, or the ciliary ganglion? The answer is the optic chiasm. Fibers from the nasal half of each retina cross there. Fibers from the temporal half stay on their own side.

Question 5120. Which fibers cross in the optic chiasm: those from the nasal half of each retina, those from the temporal half of each retina, those from the macula alone, or all the fibers of the left eye? The answer is the fibers from the nasal half of each retina. After the crossing, each optic tract carries the opposite half of the visual field.

Question 5121. In a horizontal section through the base of the brain, two nerves meet in the midline and cross before they continue backward on each side. Which plane is this, and what is that crossing called? The answer is a transverse plane, and the crossing is the optic chiasm. The two optic nerves and the two optic tracts all appear in that section.

Question 5122. A described view of the back of the eye shows a swollen optic disc with blurred margins. What is that appearance called, and what does it reflect? The answer is papilledema, and it reflects raised pressure inside the skull. The pressure travels along the sheath of the optic nerve, which is filled with cerebrospinal fluid.

Question 5123. Which membranes cover the optic nerve and continue as the coverings of the brain, and what fluid lies between them? The answer is the dura mater, the arachnoid mater and the pia mater, with cerebrospinal fluid in the space between. That fluid-filled sheath is why raised pressure inside the skull shows at the optic disc.

Question 5124. A constructed illustration: a person loses the whole field of vision in one eye while the other eye remains normal. Where does the trouble most likely lie? The answer is in the optic nerve of that eye. Damage to one optic nerve removes the field of that eye alone. Trouble behind the chiasm removes half of each field instead.

Question 5125. A described visual field chart shows loss of the outer half of the field in each eye, with the inner halves normal. Which structure is affected? The answer is the optic chiasm, and the defect is a bitemporal loss. The crossing fibers from the nasal half of each retina are the ones damaged.

Question 5126. Damage to the left optic tract produces which defect: blindness of the left eye, loss of the left half of both fields, loss of the right half of both fields, or loss of the outer halves of both fields? The answer is loss of the right half of both fields, a contralateral homonymous loss. Each tract carries the opposite half of the field from both eyes.

Question 5127. Which structure receives the fibers of the optic tract and sends them on to the occipital lobe? The answer is the lateral geniculate body of the thalamus. From it the fibers run backward as the optic radiation. They end in the visual cortex on either side of the calcarine sulcus.

Question 5128. Which of these pairs belongs together: the optic tract with the lateral geniculate body, the olfactory tract with the ciliary ganglion, the oculomotor nerve with the lateral geniculate body, or the trigeminal nerve with the optic chiasm? The answer is the optic tract with the lateral geniculate body. From there the optic radiation runs to the occipital lobe.

Question 5129. Which vessel supplies the inner part of the retina: the central retinal artery, the ophthalmic vein, the middle meningeal artery, or the posterior communicating artery? The answer is the central retinal artery, a branch of the ophthalmic artery. It enters the eye within the optic nerve and divides at the disc. Its branches are the ones seen through an ophthalmoscope.

Question 5130. A described magnetic resonance imaging study of the head shows the optic chiasm in the midline with a rounded structure lying above it and a gland lying below it. Which structures are those? The answer is the hypothalamus above and the pituitary gland below. A swelling of the gland presses upward on the crossing fibers of the chiasm.

Question 5131. A constructed illustration: a person who has had a stroke cannot see the right half of the world from either eye, yet the pupils of both eyes still react to light. Where is the damage? The answer is in the optic radiation or the visual cortex on the left side. The pupils still react because the reflex leaves the pathway before that point.

Question 5132. A bundle of visual fibers sweeps forward into the temporal lobe before it turns back toward the occipital lobe. Which part of the field do those fibers serve? The answer is the upper part of the opposite half of the field. They come from the lower retina, so a lesion in the temporal lobe removes the upper field in both eyes.

Question 5133. Which cranial nerve supplies most of the muscles that move the eyeball, and also lifts the upper eyelid? The answer is the oculomotor nerve, the third cranial nerve. It serves the medial rectus, the superior rectus, the inferior rectus and the inferior oblique. It also serves the levator palpebrae superioris, which raises the lid.

Question 5134. Which of these muscles is not supplied by the oculomotor nerve: the medial rectus, the superior rectus, the lateral rectus, or the inferior oblique? The answer is the lateral rectus, which the abducens nerve supplies. The superior oblique is the other muscle outside its control, and the trochlear nerve supplies it.

Question 5135. Which muscle raises the upper eyelid, and which nerve supplies it? The answer is the levator palpebrae superioris, supplied by the oculomotor nerve. Loss of that nerve therefore produces a drooping lid, called ptosis. A small muscle of smooth fibers within the lid assists it.

Question 5136. From which part of the brain stem does the oculomotor nerve emerge: the front of the midbrain, the side of the pons, the back of the midbrain, or the front of the medulla oblongata? The answer is the front of the midbrain, in the hollow between the two cerebral peduncles. From there the nerve runs forward and outward toward the orbit.

Question 5137. A described section through the midbrain shows a nerve leaving from its front surface between the two cerebral peduncles. Which nerve is shown, and which opening will it use to enter the orbit? The answer is the oculomotor nerve, which enters the orbit through the superior orbital fissure. It passes within the ring of tendon at the apex of the orbit.

Question 5138. Between which two arteries does the oculomotor nerve pass as it leaves the midbrain? The answer is the posterior cerebral artery above and the superior cerebellar artery below. That narrow gap is where the nerve is most often pressed on by a swelling artery. The abducens nerve also runs a long course nearby.

Question 5139. In which small ganglion do the parasympathetic fibers of the oculomotor nerve end: the ciliary ganglion, the trigeminal ganglion, the otic ganglion, or the geniculate ganglion? The answer is the ciliary ganglion, which lies behind the eye. The trigeminal ganglion is sensory for the face, and the other two belong to other nerves.

Question 5140. Which opening does the oculomotor nerve pass through to enter the orbit: the optic canal, the foramen rotundum, the superior orbital fissure, or the foramen ovale? The answer is the superior orbital fissure. The trochlear, the abducens and the ophthalmic division of the trigeminal nerve use the same opening. The optic nerve has its own canal.

Question 5141. When the oculomotor nerve is completely paralyzed, in which direction does the eye come to rest: straight ahead, down and out, up and in, or up and out? The answer is down and out. The lateral rectus pulls the eye outward and the superior oblique pulls it downward. Those are the two muscles that the damaged nerve does not serve.

Question 5142. A described photograph of a face shows one upper lid drooping over a wide pupil, with the eye turned down and outward. Which nerve is affected? The answer is the oculomotor nerve on that side. The drooping lid comes from the levator, the wide pupil from the muscle that narrows it, and the eye position from the muscles left unopposed.

Question 5143. Which nerve is compressed when a drooping lid and a widely dilated pupil that does not react to light appear together? The answer is the oculomotor nerve. The fibers that narrow the pupil lie near the surface of the nerve, so compression reaches them first. The levator of the lid fails at the same time, which is why the lid droops.

Question 5144. True or false: when the oculomotor nerve is damaged by loss of its blood supply, the pupil often still narrows when light is shone into the eye. The answer is true. Loss of blood supply strikes the core of the nerve, while the pupil fibers lie on its surface. Compression of the nerve does the opposite and dilates the pupil early.

Question 5145. What is the name of the nucleus in the midbrain that gives rise to the parasympathetic fibers of the oculomotor nerve? The answer is the Edinger-Westphal nucleus. Its fibers travel with the nerve to the ciliary ganglion. There they relay, and short ciliary nerves carry them on to the pupil and the lens.

Question 5146. Within the cavernous sinus, where does the oculomotor nerve lie, and which nerve lies immediately below it in the same wall? The answer is that the oculomotor nerve lies in the lateral wall of the sinus, with the trochlear nerve below it. The abducens nerve lies free in the cavity of the sinus beside the internal carotid artery.

Question 5147. Which fibers of the oculomotor nerve lie closest to its surface, so that compression affects them first: the fibers to the medial rectus, the fibers to the inferior oblique, the fibers that narrow the pupil, or the fibers to the levator of the lid? The answer is the fibers that narrow the pupil, which run on the outer part of the nerve.

Question 5148. True or false: the oculomotor nerve supplies both the muscles that move the eyeball and the muscles inside the eye. The answer is true. It serves four muscles that move the eye, the muscle that lifts the lid, the muscle that narrows the pupil and the muscle that focuses the lens.

Question 5149. A described section through the cavernous sinus shows two nerves lying in its lateral wall, one above the other, while a third nerve lies in the middle of the sinus beside an artery. Which nerve lies in the middle? The answer is the abducens nerve, beside the internal carotid artery. It suffers early in disease of that sinus.

Question 5150. Which of these pairs belongs together: the oculomotor nerve and the ciliary ganglion, the optic nerve and the ciliary ganglion, the trigeminal nerve and the ciliary ganglion, or the abducens nerve and the ciliary ganglion? The answer is the oculomotor nerve and the ciliary ganglion. Its fibers relay there on the way to the pupil and the lens.

Question 5151. A constructed illustration: light shone into one eye makes both pupils narrow, but light shone into the other eye makes neither pupil narrow. Which nerve is at fault, and on which side? The answer is the optic nerve of the second eye, for it carries the signal in. Both oculomotor nerves must be working, since both pupils narrow.

Question 5152. Name two muscles supplied by the oculomotor nerve that move the eyeball, spoken as a list. The answer is the medial rectus and the superior rectus. The inferior rectus and the inferior oblique are supplied by it as well. The same nerve lifts the lid and narrows the pupil.

Question 5153. Which cranial nerve supplies the superior oblique muscle of the eye? The answer is the trochlear nerve, the fourth cranial nerve. It carries motor fibers only and serves a single muscle. Every other nerve that moves the eye serves more than one muscle, and the trochlear is the smallest of the twelve.

Question 5154. Which cranial nerve is the only one to leave the brain stem from its back surface? The answer is the trochlear nerve, which emerges from the back of the midbrain below the lower colliculus. All the other cranial nerves leave from the front or the side. Its fibers then curve around the brain stem to reach the base of the skull.

Question 5155. Which opening does the trochlear nerve pass through to enter the orbit: the optic canal, the foramen ovale, the superior orbital fissure, or the foramen rotundum? The answer is the superior orbital fissure. It enters above the ring of tendon at the orbital apex, so it lies outside that ring. The oculomotor and abducens nerves pass within the ring.

Question 5156. A described dissection of the orbit shows a tendon that bends around a small fibrous pulley on the upper medial wall before it reaches the eye. Which muscle does that tendon belong to, and which nerve serves it? The answer is the superior oblique muscle, served by the trochlear nerve. The pulley itself is the trochlea.

Question 5157. The tendon of the superior oblique muscle passes through its pulley and then runs beneath which muscle before it reaches the eyeball? The answer is the superior rectus. The tendon then inserts on the upper and lateral part of the globe behind its widest point. That insertion explains why the muscle turns the eye inward and depresses it when the eye is turned in.

Question 5158. Which of these is the chief action of the superior oblique muscle when the eye is in the primary position: elevation, intorsion, outward turning, or depression? The answer is intorsion, the rolling of the top of the eye toward the nose. Depression and outward turning are its other actions, and they become stronger when the eye is turned inward.

Question 5159. A constructed illustration: a person sees double when reading or walking down stairs, and one image lies above the other. Which nerve is weak, and which muscle is at fault? The answer is the trochlear nerve, and the weak muscle is the superior oblique. Vertical double vision is worst when the eye is turned in and down.

Question 5160. True or false: the trochlear nerve supplies the superior oblique muscle on the same side as the nucleus from which its fibers arise. The answer is false. Its fibers cross completely before they leave the midbrain. The nucleus on one side therefore serves the superior oblique muscle on the other side.

Question 5161. A described photograph shows a person holding the head tilted toward the left shoulder because that position reduces the double vision. Which nerve is weak, and why does the tilt help? The answer is the right trochlear nerve. Tilting the head toward the left takes work away from the weak right superior oblique muscle.

Question 5162. A constructed illustration: after a blow to the head without any fracture, a person has vertical double vision that comes and goes, and both eyes are affected. Which nerve is most likely injured? The answer is the trochlear nerve. It is the thinnest cranial nerve and has the longest course inside the skull, so a head injury often stretches it.

Question 5163. Which cranial nerve is the largest, and what two kinds of fiber does it carry? The answer is the trigeminal nerve, the fifth cranial nerve. It carries general sensation from the face and motor fibers to the muscles of chewing. It has three divisions, which is what its name describes.

Question 5164. From which part of the brain stem does the trigeminal nerve emerge: the side of the pons, the front of the midbrain, the back of the midbrain, or the side of the medulla oblongata? The answer is the side of the pons. It leaves as two roots, a large sensory root and a small motor root. The small root supplies the muscles of chewing.

Question 5165. What is the name of the large sensory ganglion of the trigeminal nerve, and where does it lie? The answer is the trigeminal ganglion, which lies in a small cave on the front surface of the petrous part of the temporal bone. It is the equivalent for the face of a spinal ganglion. From it the three divisions run forward.

Question 5166. Which of the three divisions of the trigeminal nerve carries motor fibers: the ophthalmic division, the maxillary division, the mandibular division, or all three? The answer is the mandibular division. It supplies the muscles that chew and two small muscles of the ear and the soft palate. The other two divisions are purely sensory.

Question 5167. Which of these pairs belongs together: the ophthalmic division with the foramen rotundum, the maxillary division with the foramen ovale, the mandibular division with the foramen ovale, or the ophthalmic division with the foramen ovale? The answer is the mandibular division with the foramen ovale. The ophthalmic division enters the orbit through the superior orbital fissure.

Question 5168. A described view of the base of the skull shows three openings on one side, one leading to the orbit, one into the upper jaw and one just behind and outside the second. Which nerves pass through them? The answer is the ophthalmic, maxillary and mandibular divisions of the trigeminal nerve. They leave by the superior orbital fissure, the foramen rotundum and the foramen ovale.

Question 5169. In a described CT section of the skull base, which of these openings lies farthest forward: the foramen ovale, the foramen rotundum, the foramen spinosum, or the jugular foramen? The answer is the foramen rotundum. It lies in the greater wing of the sphenoid bone, ahead of the foramen ovale, and both lie ahead of the foramen spinosum and the jugular foramen.

Question 5170. Which of these pairs belongs together: the maxillary division with the upper teeth and the cheek, the mandibular division with the upper teeth and the cheek, the ophthalmic division with the lower lip and the chin, or the maxillary division with the lower teeth and the chin? The answer is the maxillary division with the upper teeth and the cheek. The mandibular division supplies the lower teeth and the chin.

Question 5171. Which division of the trigeminal nerve leaves the skull through the foramen ovale: the ophthalmic division, the maxillary division, the mandibular division, or the abducens nerve? The answer is the mandibular division. It carries sensation from the lower jaw and the tongue and motor fibers to the muscles of chewing. The abducens nerve uses the superior orbital fissure.

Question 5172. Which of these muscles of chewing protrudes the lower jaw forward: the masseter, the temporalis, the lateral pterygoid, or the medial pterygoid? The answer is the lateral pterygoid. The masseter and the temporalis raise the jaw, and the medial pterygoid raises it and helps to protrude it. All four are supplied by the mandibular division.

Question 5173. A constructed illustration: a person cannot feel a light touch on the lower lip and the chin on one side, but the muscles of chewing work normally. Which nerve is affected? The answer is the mental nerve, a branch of the inferior alveolar nerve from the mandibular division. It leaves the jaw at the mental foramen.

Question 5174. Which two divisions of the trigeminal nerve run in the lateral wall of the cavernous sinus, and which one never enters that sinus? The answer is the ophthalmic and maxillary divisions in the wall, while the mandibular division passes straight out through the foramen ovale. The ophthalmic division continues to the orbit and the maxillary to the upper jaw.

Question 5175. A constructed illustration: when the cornea of one eye is touched lightly, neither eye blinks, and the person cannot feel the touch. Which nerve carries the sensation, and which nerve should have closed the lids? The answer is the ophthalmic division of the trigeminal nerve for sensation and the facial nerve for the closing. This is the corneal reflex.

Question 5176. True or false: the cornea receives its general sensation through the ophthalmic division of the trigeminal nerve. The answer is true. Its fibers run in the long ciliary nerves, which are branches of the nasociliary nerve. The same division supplies the skin of the forehead, the upper eyelid and the tip of the nose.

Question 5177. Which division of the trigeminal nerve carries sensation from the upper teeth, the palate and the skin over the cheek: the ophthalmic division, the maxillary division, the mandibular division, or the facial nerve? The answer is the maxillary division. The facial nerve is a motor nerve to the muscles of the face and carries no general sensation from the cheek.

Question 5178. Which nerve leaves the skull just below the eye and supplies the upper lip and the side of the nose: the supraorbital nerve, the infraorbital nerve, the mental nerve, or the auriculotemporal nerve? The answer is the infraorbital nerve, a branch of the maxillary division. The mental nerve supplies the chin and the auriculotemporal nerve the side of the head.

Question 5179. Which nerve runs in the canal inside the lower jaw, and through which opening does one of its branches reach the chin? The answer is the inferior alveolar nerve of the mandibular division, and its branch, the mental nerve, leaves by the mental foramen. The same nerve supplies the lower teeth.

Question 5180. Which branch of the mandibular division carries taste fibers from another nerve: the lingual nerve with fibers from the facial nerve, the inferior alveolar nerve with fibers from the optic nerve, the buccal nerve with fibers from the oculomotor nerve, or the auriculotemporal nerve with fibers from the trochlear nerve? The answer is the lingual nerve with fibers from the facial nerve. They carry taste from the front two thirds of the tongue.

Question 5181. Which small muscle of the ear is supplied by the mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve, and what does it do? The answer is the tensor tympani, which draws the eardrum inward and dampens loud sound. The division also supplies the tensor of the soft palate. Both are small muscles served alongside the muscles of chewing.

Question 5182. Name two divisions of the trigeminal nerve in which sudden sharp facial pain most often occurs, spoken as a list. The answer is the maxillary division and the mandibular division. That pain comes in short attacks and leaves no numbness between them. Its cause and its treatment belong to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 5183. When the jaw muscles on the left side are weak, which way does the chin move when the mouth is opened: to the left, to the right, straight ahead, or upward? The answer is to the left, toward the weak side. The healthy lateral pterygoid on the right pushes the jaw across. A lesion of the mandibular division on the left produces this.

Question 5184. Which three branches of the trigeminal nerve emerge on the face in a vertical line, and what are they called? The answer is the supraorbital nerve above the eye, the infraorbital nerve below the eye and the mental nerve on the chin. One belongs to each of the three divisions. All three are purely sensory.

Question 5185. A described section of the pons shows a large nerve leaving its side in two parts, one much thicker than the other. Which nerve is shown, and what does each part carry? The answer is the trigeminal nerve, whose thick part carries sensation from the face and whose thin part carries motor fibers. The thin part supplies the muscles of chewing.

Question 5186. Which division of the trigeminal nerve supplies the dura mater over the tentorium of the cerebellum? The answer is the ophthalmic division. A small recurrent branch runs backward from it to that fold of dura. The other two divisions supply the dura of the middle cranial fossa instead.

Question 5187. Which nerve supplies the masseter, the temporalis and both pterygoid muscles: the ophthalmic division, the maxillary division, the mandibular division, or the facial nerve? The answer is the mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve. It enters the space through the foramen ovale. The facial nerve supplies the muscles of the face, not the muscles of chewing.

Question 5188. Which nucleus of the trigeminal nerve lies in the midbrain and carries the sense of position from the muscles and joints of the jaw? The answer is the mesencephalic nucleus. Its cells are the cell bodies of the sensory fibers themselves, a feature found nowhere else. Their fibers travel with the mandibular division.

Question 5189. Which cranial nerve supplies the lateral rectus muscle, the muscle that turns the eyeball outward? The answer is the abducens nerve, the sixth cranial nerve. It carries motor fibers only, and that single muscle is all it serves. A weak lateral rectus leaves the eye turned inward.

Question 5190. From which part of the brain stem does the abducens nerve emerge? The answer is the junction of the pons and the medulla oblongata, near the midline. Its fibers leave from the front surface of the brain stem. The facial nerve emerges just beside it.

Question 5191. Which opening does the abducens nerve pass through to reach the orbit: the optic canal, the foramen rotundum, the foramen ovale, or the superior orbital fissure? The answer is the superior orbital fissure. It enters within the ring of tendon at the orbital apex. The oculomotor and trochlear nerves use the same opening.

Question 5192. A described MRI study shows a nerve running upward along the slope of the clivus and bending over the ridge of the petrous part of the temporal bone. Which nerve is shown, and where does it go next? The answer is the abducens nerve, which bends over that ridge and runs forward in the cavernous sinus. From there it enters the orbit.

Question 5193. Inside the cavernous sinus, which nerve lies closest to the internal carotid artery? The answer is the abducens nerve. It lies free in the cavity of the sinus beside the artery, while the other nerves lie in the wall. That position is why disease of the sinus often affects it first.

Question 5194. A constructed illustration: a person with a large brain tumor and raised pressure inside the skull has double vision when looking to the right, and the right eye cannot turn outward. Which nerve is failing? The answer is the right abducens nerve. Its long course inside the skull makes it the nerve most often stretched by raised pressure, even far from the tumor.

Question 5195. A described photograph of a face shows one eye turned slightly inward while the other looks straight ahead. Which muscle is weak, and which nerve is at fault? The answer is the lateral rectus, supplied by the abducens nerve. The medial rectus is unopposed and pulls the eye inward, and the double image is side by side.

Question 5196. Which muscle is left unopposed when the abducens nerve is paralyzed: the medial rectus, the lateral rectus, the superior oblique, or the levator of the lid? The answer is the medial rectus. With the outward pull lost, the inward pull turns the eye toward the nose. That inward turn is the commonest sign of a weak abducens nerve.

Question 5197. Through which narrow passage does the abducens nerve pass as it leaves the back of the skull and bends over the ridge of the petrous part of the temporal bone? The answer is a small channel beneath the petrosphenoidal ligament, often called Dorello's canal. The nerve is held tightly there, which is one reason it is easily stretched. The trigeminal ganglion lies in a cave at the same ridge.

Question 5198. A constructed illustration: a person with an infection of the middle ear and the tip of the petrous bone has deep pain behind the eye and double vision when looking outward. Which nerves are involved? The answer is the abducens nerve and the trigeminal nerve. Infection near the tip of the petrous bone reaches both, since they pass close by.

Question 5199. Which two nerves enter the orbit through the superior orbital fissure within the ring of tendon at its apex: the oculomotor and the abducens, the trochlear and the abducens, the optic and the oculomotor, or the trochlear and the optic? The answer is the oculomotor and the abducens. The trochlear nerve enters above the ring and the optic nerve uses the optic canal.

Question 5200. What is the name of the rounded ridge on the floor of the fourth ventricle that overlies the nucleus of the abducens nerve? The answer is the facial colliculus. The fibers of the facial nerve loop around the abducens nucleus beneath it. That nucleus sends its fibers out to the lateral rectus muscle of the same side.

To summarize, the olfactory nerve is the only sensory pathway that reaches the cortex without a thalamic relay, and its filaments cross the cribriform plate to end in the olfactory bulb. The optic nerve is a tract of the brain wrapped in meninges, and its nasal fibers cross at the chiasm so that each optic tract serves the opposite half of the visual field. The oculomotor nerve supplies four muscles that move the eye, lifts the upper lid and narrows the pupil, and its pupil fibers lie at the surface, which is why compression dilates the pupil early. The trochlear nerve is the thinnest cranial nerve, the only one to leave from the back of the brain stem, and the only one that crosses completely before it supplies the superior oblique. The trigeminal nerve is the largest, with three divisions leaving by the superior orbital fissure, the foramen rotundum and the foramen ovale, and its mandibular division alone carries motor fibers. The abducens nerve supplies the lateral rectus, runs a long course inside the skull and lies beside the internal carotid artery in the cavernous sinus, so it is the nerve most often stretched by raised pressure.
