# Chapter 10 — Vertebral Column

Every movement of the trunk begins with the vertebral column, and this chapter takes it apart from the first cervical vertebra at the base of the skull to the tip of the coccyx. It covers the cervical, the thoracic and the lumbar vertebrae in turn, and then the sacrum and the coccyx. It holds one hundred questions, and each one gives its answer immediately after the question and then explains why that answer is right, so nothing has to be held in mind until the end. The column carries the skull, shelters the spinal cord and hands the weight of the body to the pelvis. Taking the regions in order is the easiest way to keep their shapes and their movements apart. This is educational material, and it supports rather than replaces an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 901. Which cervical vertebra has no body and no spinous process, and is no more than a ring of bone? The answer is the first cervical vertebra, the atlas. It carries the skull on two lateral masses, and the body it would have had became the dens of the vertebra below. A groove for the vertebral artery replaces its spine.

Question 902. How many vertebrae lie in the cervical region: five, seven, nine, or twelve? The answer is seven. The cervical vertebrae are the smallest of the movable vertebrae, and they lie between the skull above and the first thoracic vertebra below. Five is the number in the lumbar region and twelve the number in the thoracic.

Question 903. Which feature of a typical cervical vertebra gives it a forked appearance when it is seen from behind? The answer is the bifid spinous process. The spine splits into two tubercles at its tip. The first, the seventh and usually the sixth cervical vertebrae carry a single spine instead.

Question 904. What passes through the transverse foramen of a typical cervical vertebra: the vertebral artery, the spinal nerve, the sympathetic trunk, or the vertebral vein alone? The answer is the vertebral artery. It enters the foramen of the sixth cervical vertebra and climbs through the foramina above it. A vein of the same name follows the artery.

Question 905. A described radiograph of the neck in profile shows a ring of bone with a small anterior arch, a large posterior arch and no body between them. Which vertebra is shown? The answer is the first cervical vertebra, the atlas. It looks like a ring because it has neither body nor spine, and the dens of the second cervical vertebra rises into it.

Question 906. Which structure lies immediately behind the anterior arch of the atlas, held against it by a ligament? The answer is the dens of the second cervical vertebra. The transverse ligament of the atlas stretches across the ring behind the dens and holds it in place. The dens is the pivot on which the head turns.

Question 907. Turning the head to look over one shoulder happens at which joint: the atlanto-occipital, the atlanto-axial, the joint between the fifth and sixth cervical vertebrae, or the joint between the seventh cervical vertebra and the first thoracic? The answer is the atlanto-axial joint. The dens acts as a pivot, and the atlas and the skull turn around it, while the atlanto-occipital joint nods the head.

Question 908. Which cervical vertebra carries the long spine felt at the base of the neck, and is therefore called the vertebra prominens? The answer is the seventh cervical vertebra. Its spinous process is long and does not split at the tip, so it stands out beneath the skin. Its transverse process is the largest in the region.

Question 909. True or false: the first cervical nerve leaves the vertebral canal above the first cervical vertebra. The answer is true. In the cervical region each spinal nerve takes the number of the vertebra below it, so the first cervical nerve lies between the skull and the atlas, and the eighth lies between the seventh cervical vertebra and the first thoracic.

Question 910. A described computed tomography section through the first cervical vertebra shows breaks in both its anterior and its posterior arch, with the lateral masses driven apart. Which injury is shown? The answer is a burst fracture of the atlas, often called a Jefferson fracture. A blow on the crown drives the occipital condyles between the lateral masses, and the ring gives way.

Question 911. A constructed illustration: after a collision the head snaps forward, and the described radiograph shows a break through the bridge of bone between the articular processes of the second cervical vertebra on both sides. Which vertebra is fractured? The answer is the second cervical vertebra, the axis, in the injury called a hangman fracture. Its body slides forward on the third cervical vertebra.

Question 912. Which joints lie at the sides of the bodies of the third to the sixth cervical vertebrae, where a small lip of bone rises: the uncovertebral joints, the costovertebral joints, the atlanto-occipital joints, or the joints of the ribs? The answer is the uncovertebral joints, also called the joints of Luschka. That lip of bone is the uncinate process, and it meets the vertebra above.

Question 913. Which cervical vertebra carries the large anterior tubercle on its transverse process, against which the common carotid artery can be pressed? The answer is the sixth cervical vertebra. Its anterior tubercle is the carotid tubercle, and it is the largest transverse process of the region. The artery lies in front of it.

Question 914. In which plane do the articular facets of the cervical vertebrae face: horizontal, coronal, sagittal, or oblique? The answer is oblique. The facets face downward and forward at about 45 degrees, so the neck bends, extends, tips and turns with ease. Rotation is freest at the atlanto-axial joint, where the dens serves as the pivot.

Question 915. After it leaves the transverse foramen of the atlas, where does the vertebral artery run? The answer is in a groove on the upper surface of the posterior arch of the atlas. It then pierces the dura mater and the arachnoid mater, passes through the foramen magnum, and winds medially behind the superior articular process.

Question 916. A described magnetic resonance imaging scan of the neck shows a disc bulge between the fifth and sixth cervical vertebrae pressing on a nerve root. Which nerve root is compressed? The answer is the sixth cervical nerve root, because in the cervical region a nerve leaves above its own vertebra. The pain runs down the outer arm to the thumb.

Question 917. Which statement about the cervical vertebrae is correct: the first has a large body, the second has a dens, the seventh has a bifid spine, or the transverse foramina lie in the thoracic vertebrae? The answer is that the second cervical vertebra has a dens, the pivot of the head. The atlas has no body, the seventh spine is single, and the transverse foramina are cervical.

Question 918. True or false: the vertebral artery passes through the transverse foramen of the seventh cervical vertebra. The answer is false. It enters the foramen of the sixth cervical vertebra and never passes through the foramen of the seventh, which usually carries only a small accessory vein. A long transverse process there can press on the brachial plexus.

Question 919. What is the name of the strong band that runs from the external occipital protuberance down the back of the neck to the spine of the seventh cervical vertebra? The answer is the nuchal ligament. It is the thickened supraspinous ligament of the neck, and its elastic fibers help to hold the head up in the midline.

Question 920. Which statement describes the first cervical vertebra correctly: it has a body and a bifid spine, it has neither body nor spine, it carries a dens, or it carries facets for ribs? The answer is that it has neither body nor spine. The atlas is a ring of bone with two arches and two lateral masses, and the dens belongs to the second cervical vertebra.

Question 921. A constructed illustration: a person has numbness along the inner border of the forearm and a weak little finger, and the described radiograph shows an extra rib rising from the seventh cervical vertebra. What has happened? The answer is that the extra rib has narrowed the space above the first rib and pressed on the lower trunk of the brachial plexus, which carries the fibers to the little finger.

Question 922. A described open-mouth radiograph of the neck shows a peg of bone standing in the middle of a ring, with even gaps between it and the lateral masses on both sides. Which structure is the peg? The answer is the dens, which belongs to the second cervical vertebra, the axis. Even gaps on both sides show that it has not shifted.

Question 923. Which cervical vertebra lies immediately above the first thoracic vertebra, and what is the shape of its transverse process? The answer is the seventh cervical vertebra, whose transverse process is long and thick and can be felt through the skin at the base of the neck. Below it the thoracic region begins, with facets for the ribs.

Question 924. Through which opening does the vertebral artery enter the skull: through the foramen magnum, through the carotid canal, through the jugular foramen, or through the hypoglossal canal? The answer is through the foramen magnum. The artery has climbed through the transverse foramina and crossed the posterior arch of the atlas. The carotid canal carries the internal carotid artery.

Question 925. Which vertebra forms the pivot on which the head rotates from side to side? The answer is the second cervical vertebra, the axis. Its dens rises into the ring of the atlas and acts as a peg around which the atlas and the skull turn. The alar ligaments tie the dens to the foramen magnum.

Question 926. Which ligament holds the dens against the anterior arch of the atlas: the anterior longitudinal ligament, the transverse ligament of the atlas, the ligamentum flavum, or the alar ligament? The answer is the transverse ligament of the atlas, and if it gives way the atlas slides forward and the dens presses on the spinal cord. The alar ligaments check rotation.

Question 927. Which of these pairs belongs together: the first cervical vertebra with the dens, the second with the transverse foramen, the seventh with the vertebra prominens, or the atlas with a bifid spine? The answer is the seventh cervical vertebra with the vertebra prominens, whose long spine is felt at the base of the neck. The dens belongs to the axis.

Question 928. A constructed illustration: a person with long-standing inflammatory joint disease has a stiff, painful neck, and the described radiograph shows a wide gap between the dens and the anterior arch of the atlas. Which structure has given way? The answer is the transverse ligament of the atlas. Inflammation erodes it, so the atlas slides forward on the axis. Management belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 929. Name the two cervical vertebrae that differ most from the rest, spoken as a list. The answer is the first cervical vertebra, the atlas, and the second cervical vertebra, the axis. The atlas is a ring without body or spine, and the axis carries the dens and the largest spine of the region.

Question 930. A described radiograph of the neck from the side shows small square bodies, each with a spine that ends in two points, except one at the bottom whose spine is a single long bar. Which region is shown? The answer is the cervical region, and the seventh cervical vertebra is the exception, whose long single spine forms the bump at the base of the neck.

Question 931. Which region of the vertebral column is recognized by vertebrae whose bodies carry facets for the heads of ribs? The answer is the thoracic region. Twelve vertebrae lie between the neck and the loins, and the upper ten also carry a facet on the transverse process for the tubercle of a rib.

Question 932. Which statement about the thoracic spinous processes is correct: they are short and square, they are long and slope downward over the one below, they are bifid, or they are absent? The answer is that they are long and slope downward, overlapping like the tiles of a roof. That overlap is one reason a needle is not passed between them.

Question 933. True or false: the body of a typical thoracic vertebra carries a complete facet for the head of a rib on each side. The answer is false, because typical thoracic bodies carry two half facets, an upper and a lower, so the head of a rib rests on two vertebrae and the disc between them.

Question 934. A described radiograph of the chest shows twelve pairs of ribs sweeping down from a column of bones whose bodies grow larger from above downward. Which region is shown, and what shape are the bodies? The answer is the thoracic region, whose bodies are heart shaped. Its canal is narrow, so anything that narrows it further presses on the spinal cord early.

Question 935. Which ribs have no joint between their tubercle and a transverse process: the first and second, the eleventh and twelfth, the fifth and sixth, or the ninth and tenth? The answer is the eleventh and twelfth ribs. Their heads each meet a single vertebra, and their short anterior ends float free in the abdominal wall.

Question 936. Which joint is formed between the head of a rib, the half facets of two adjacent vertebral bodies, and the disc between them? The answer is the joint of the head of the rib, the costovertebral joint. It is a synovial joint divided into two cavities by a ligament from the crest of the rib head to the disc.

Question 937. Which structure lies immediately in front of the bodies of the thoracic vertebrae, and which lies in front of that? The answer is the thoracic duct, with the esophagus in front of it. The duct climbs through the aortic opening of the diaphragm and lies against the vertebral bodies, while the esophagus lies behind the trachea.

Question 938. In which plane do the articular facets of the thoracic vertebrae face, and which movement do they permit most freely: sagittal, coronal, horizontal, or oblique? The answer is coronal, and rotation is the freest movement of the thoracic region. The facets face backward and forward, so the trunk twists about its axis once the ribs are carried along.

Question 939. A described lateral radiograph of the upper back shows that the tip of a spinous process lies at the level of the body of the vertebra below it. Which region is shown? The answer is the thoracic region, because its spines are long and slope steeply downward. In the lumbar region the spine lies at the level of its own body.

Question 940. A constructed illustration: an older person is described as having a rounded upper back and a loss of height, and the described radiograph shows wedging of several vertebral bodies. Which region is affected, and what is the normal shape of its curve? The answer is the thoracic region, whose normal curve is convex backward. That curve is a primary curvature, present from birth.

Question 941. Which of these curvatures is primary, that is, present at birth and convex backward: the cervical, the thoracic, the lumbar, or none of them? The answer is the thoracic curvature. It appears before birth, because the spine of the fetus is C shaped and convex backward. The cervical and the lumbar curvatures are secondary.

Question 942. Which thoracic vertebrae carry a single complete facet on each side of the body for the head of a rib and no facet on the transverse process? The answer is the eleventh and twelfth thoracic vertebrae. Their ribs meet only the body of their own vertebra, and the twelfth has lumbar-shaped articular processes.

Question 943. Which statement about the spinal canal in the thoracic region is correct: it is wider than in the cervical region, it is narrow and holds the spinal cord closely, it holds only nerve roots, or it ends at the eleventh thoracic vertebra? The answer is that it is narrow and holds the spinal cord closely, so a disc or a tumor damages the cord early.

Question 944. The intervertebral foramen of the thoracic region is bounded in front by the body and the disc and behind by the facet joints. Which structure passes through it? The answer is the spinal nerve of the segment. It leaves below its own vertebra and runs with a small artery and vein.

Question 945. A described magnetic resonance imaging scan of the upper back shows a disc bulging backward between the sixth and the seventh thoracic vertebrae and flattening the spinal cord in front of it. Which structure is pressed? The answer is the spinal cord, because the thoracic canal is narrow and holds it firmly against the wall behind. The bodies here are small as well.

Question 946. A constructed illustration: a person lands heavily on the feet after a fall, and the described radiograph shows that one body at the junction of the thoracic and the lumbar regions has lost height at the front. Which junction is injured? The answer is the junction between the twelfth thoracic and the first lumbar vertebrae, where the stiff thoracic spine meets the mobile lumbar spine.

Question 947. Which feature belongs to a thoracic vertebra but not to a lumbar one: a transverse foramen, a facet for the head of a rib, a bifid spinous process, or a large kidney-shaped body? The answer is a facet for the head of a rib. Thoracic vertebrae alone carry those facets, and the upper ten also carry a facet on the transverse process.

Question 948. Which thoracic vertebra carries a complete facet for the first rib on its body together with a facet on its transverse process, and is therefore atypical? The answer is the first thoracic vertebra. Its spine is long and thick and lies at the base of the neck, and the first rib curves in front of it to the manubrium.

Question 949. Which of these pairs belongs together: the thoracic vertebra with a heart-shaped body, the cervical vertebra with a kidney-shaped body, the lumbar vertebra with a ring and no body, or the atlas with a bifid spine? The answer is the thoracic vertebra with a heart-shaped body. The lumbar body is kidney shaped, and the atlas is a ring without a body.

Question 950. Name the parts of a thoracic vertebra that meet a rib, spoken as a list. The answer is the half facets on the body and the facet on the transverse process. The head of the rib rests on the half facets of two vertebrae and the disc between them, and the eleventh and twelfth ribs meet only the body.

Question 951. Which region of the vertebral column is built from five large vertebrae with kidney-shaped bodies and stout, square spines? The answer is the lumbar region. Its vertebrae carry the weight of the whole upper body and so have the largest bodies in the column. Their blunt spines do not overlap, so a needle can pass between them.

Question 952. In which plane do the articular facets of the lumbar vertebrae face, and which movement do they allow: coronal, sagittal, horizontal, or oblique? The answer is sagittal, allowing bending and straightening. The joint surfaces lie in the sagittal plane, so the lumbar region bends forward and backward freely but twists very little.

Question 953. A described radiograph of the lower back shows five large bodies stacked one above another, each with a broad square shadow in the midline, and below them a fused triangular bone. Which region is shown, and which is its lowest vertebra? The answer is the lumbar region, and its lowest vertebra is the fifth, which sits on the sacrum.

Question 954. At which level is a needle usually passed into the vertebral canal to sample cerebrospinal fluid: between the first and second lumbar vertebrae, between the third and fourth, between the eleventh and twelfth thoracic, or between the fifth lumbar vertebra and the sacrum? The answer is between the third and fourth lumbar vertebrae. The spinal cord ends above that level, so only loose roots lie in the way.

Question 955. Which vertebra of the lumbar region is the largest, and what does it rest on? The answer is the fifth lumbar vertebra, which rests on the sacrum. Its body is the largest in the column because it carries the greatest load. Its transverse process is short, thick and cone shaped, quite unlike the long thin processes above it.

Question 956. At what level does the spinal cord end in the adult, and what occupies the canal below it? The answer is that the cord ends at about the first or second lumbar vertebra, and the space below holds the roots of the cauda equina. Those roots descend to their own openings, so a needle can pass safely.

Question 957. A disc bulges backward and to the side between the fourth and the fifth lumbar vertebrae. Which nerve root is compressed: the third lumbar, the fourth lumbar, the fifth lumbar, or the first sacral? The answer is the fifth lumbar nerve root. In the lumbar region a nerve leaves below its own vertebra, so this disc catches the fifth root.

Question 958. A constructed illustration: a person has severe back pain with numbness of the inner thighs and the perineum and difficulty passing urine, and the described scan shows a large central disc bulge between the fourth and fifth lumbar vertebrae. Which structures are compressed? The answer is the roots of the cauda equina, because a central bulge catches many roots at once. Management belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 959. True or false: the lumbar vertebrae carry facets for the heads of ribs. The answer is false. Ribs articulate only with the thoracic vertebrae, and the lumbar transverse processes are the remnants of ribs taken into the column. A long transverse process on the fifth lumbar vertebra can look like a rib.

Question 960. A described oblique radiograph of the lower back shows a break through the narrow bridge of bone between the upper and the lower articular processes of the fifth lumbar vertebra, and that vertebra has slipped forward on the sacrum. What has happened? The answer is a stress fracture of that bridge with forward slipping, called spondylolysis with spondylolisthesis.

Question 961. What is the name of the small rounded bump on the back of the superior articular process of a lumbar vertebra? The answer is the mammillary process. It gives attachment to the deep muscle of the back called the multifidus, and it is a useful landmark when the joints of the spine are exposed.

Question 962. The transverse processes of the lumbar vertebrae correspond to which structure of the thoracic region: the ribs, the transverse processes, the pedicles, or the spinous processes? The answer is the ribs. Each lumbar transverse process is the fused rib element of its segment, which is why a long one can look like a rib.

Question 963. Name two vertebrae that can be mistaken for each other at the junction of the lumbar region and the sacrum, spoken as a list. The answer is the fifth lumbar vertebra and the first sacral vertebra. Fusion of the fifth lumbar vertebra to the sacrum is called sacralization, and a separate first sacral vertebra is called lumbarization.

Question 964. Which ligament joins the laminae of two adjacent lumbar vertebrae, and what is its color? The answer is the ligamentum flavum, which is yellow because it is rich in elastic fibers. It lies just behind the dura mater in a lumbar puncture and stretches when the spine bends forward.

Question 965. A described scan shows the canal of the third and fourth lumbar vertebrae narrowed by bone overgrowth and a thick yellow ligament. Which symptom fits: pain in both legs on walking, pain down one leg to the big toe, pain in the little finger, or pain in the thigh? The answer is pain in both legs on walking, which eases on sitting, because the crowded canal holds the roots of the cauda equina.

Question 966. Which part of a vertebra is the thick block of bone that carries the weight, and which parts arch backward from it? The answer is the body, with the pedicles and the laminae arching backward to form the vertebral arch. The spinous process points backward in the midline, and the arch is removed in the operation called laminectomy.

Question 967. A constructed illustration: a person has pain in the lower back that is worst when leaning backward and is felt to one side of the midline over the joints of the spine. Which joints are involved, and which nerve supplies them? The answer is the zygapophyseal joints, supplied by the medial branches of the dorsal rami of the lumbar spinal nerves. Pain from them is felt in the back.

Question 968. Which structures form the joint between the fifth lumbar vertebra and the sacrum: a disc with facet joints, a pad of fibrocartilage only, a ball and socket joint, or a fibrous seam? The answer is a disc with facet joints. The disc lies between the bodies, and the articular processes meet behind, and this joint carries the greatest load.

Question 969. Which of these pairs belongs together: the lumbar vertebra with a kidney-shaped body, the thoracic vertebra with a transverse foramen, the cervical vertebra with a facet for a rib, or the sacrum with a bifid spine? The answer is the lumbar vertebra with a kidney-shaped body. The transverse foramen and the bifid spine are cervical.

Question 970. A described radiograph of the lower back from the side shows the bodies forming a curve that is convex forward, with the discs thicker in front than behind. What is the name of that curve, and is it primary or secondary? The answer is the lumbar curvature, which is secondary. It appears when the child stands and walks, balancing the curve of the thoracic region.

Question 971. Which bone is formed by the fusion of five vertebrae, is triangular, and lies between the two hip bones at the back of the pelvis? The answer is the sacrum. Its base meets the fifth lumbar vertebra above and its apex meets the coccyx below, and its sides carry ear-shaped surfaces for the hip bones.

Question 972. How many pairs of foramina pierce the sacrum: two, three, four, or five? The answer is four pairs. Four pairs open on the concave front and four pairs on the convex back, and they carry the branches of the sacral spinal nerves. The fifth pair is missing.

Question 973. A described specimen shows a triangular bone with a smooth concave surface on one side and a rough convex surface on the other, with four holes in a vertical row on each side. Which bone is it, and which surface lay inside the pelvis? The answer is the sacrum, and the smooth concave surface lay inside the pelvis.

Question 974. Which prominent point on the sacrum marks the middle of the pelvic brim, and what rests on the base above it? The answer is the sacral promontory, the front edge of the body of the first sacral vertebra. It projects forward into the pelvis, and the fifth lumbar vertebra rests above it.

Question 975. What is the sacral hiatus, and what leaves through it: a pair of openings for the sacral nerves, the lower end of the sacral canal, a groove for the vertebral artery, or the socket for the hip bone? The answer is the lower end of the sacral canal, formed because the laminae of the fifth sacral vertebra fail to meet.

Question 976. Which ear-shaped surface on the sacrum meets the ilium to form the sacroiliac joint, and what kind of joint is it? The answer is the auricular surface, and the joint is part synovial and part fibrous. The front is a synovial joint with a thin capsule, and strong ligaments bind the bones behind.

Question 977. Which feature explains why the sacroiliac joint carries the weight of the body with almost no movement: the smooth cartilage, the irregular interlocking of the surfaces with very strong ligaments, the loose capsule, or the fluid inside it? The answer is the irregular interlocking of the surfaces together with very strong ligaments, which tighten when weight is put on the joint.

Question 978. A constructed illustration: an older person with thin bones has deep pain in the buttock that is worse on standing, and the described scan shows a faint vertical crack through the side of the sacrum. Which structure is fractured? The answer is the sacrum, through the part of its side that meets the ilium. Such a crack is easily missed.

Question 979. True or false: the sacral canal contains the lower part of the spinal cord. The answer is false. The spinal cord ends above the sacrum, so the sacral canal holds only the roots of the cauda equina and the filum terminale, with their covering of meninges. Below the hiatus only that thin filament remains.

Question 980. In the pelvic cavity, does the sacrum form the posterior wall, the anterior wall, the lateral wall or the floor, and which way does its pelvic surface face? The answer is that the sacrum forms the posterior wall, and its smooth concave surface faces forward and downward into the pelvis. The hip bones form the lateral walls and the front.

Question 981. Which crest on the back of the sacrum represents the fused spinous processes of its vertebrae: the median sacral crest, the intermediate sacral crest, the lateral sacral crest, or the sacral cornua? The answer is the median sacral crest, which runs down the midline. The intermediate crest is formed by the fused articular processes.

Question 982. Which two small horns flank the sacral hiatus, and which parts of a vertebra do they represent? The answer is the sacral cornua, the last remains of the arches of the fifth sacral vertebra. They can be felt through the skin and serve as landmarks when a needle is guided into the sacral canal.

Question 983. What are the alae of the sacrum, and how are they formed: two wings above the auricular surface, two plates on the pelvic floor, the facets for the fifth lumbar vertebra, or the horns beside the hiatus? The answer is two wings above the auricular surface, formed by the fused transverse processes of the first sacral vertebra.

Question 984. Which large nerve plexus lies on the pelvic surface of the sacrum, and which roots form it? The answer is the sacral plexus, formed by the lumbosacral trunk from the fourth and fifth lumbar roots together with the first to the fourth sacral roots. Its nerves leave through the greater sciatic foramen.

Question 985. A described magnetic resonance imaging scan through the pelvis shows a triangular bone behind the pelvic organs, with nerve roots running down its front and leaving through four openings on each side. Which bone is shown, and what lies in front of it? The answer is the sacrum, with the rectum in front of it.

Question 986. A constructed illustration: after a heavy blow to the side of the pelvis, the described radiograph shows a crack through the side of the sacrum and a widened gap at the joint between the sacrum and the ilium. Which structure has been disrupted? The answer is the sacroiliac joint and the strong ligaments behind it, so weight can no longer pass from the spine to the hip bone.

Question 987. Which statement about the female sacrum is correct: it is longer and narrower, it is shorter and wider with a greater curve, it lacks auricular surfaces, or it fuses with the ilium? The answer is that it is shorter and wider with a greater curve. The female pelvis is broader and shallower for childbirth, and the male sacrum is longer and narrower.

Question 988. Which structure continues the vertebral canal down through the sacrum, and where does it open? The answer is the sacral canal, which opens below as the sacral hiatus. It holds the roots of the cauda equina and the filum terminale, wrapped in meninges, before they leave through the sacral foramina.

Question 989. Which of these pairs belongs together: the sacral promontory with the pelvic inlet, the sacral hiatus with the hip joint, the auricular surface with the coccyx, or the sacral cornua with the ribs? The answer is the sacral promontory with the pelvic inlet, the midline point where the pelvis brim crosses the sacrum. The auricular surface meets the ilium.

Question 990. Which small triangular bone forms the lowest part of the vertebral column, below the sacrum? The answer is the coccyx. It is built from three to five rudimentary vertebrae that fuse into a single bone, most often four. It is the remnant of a tail, and only its first segment still shows the shape of a vertebra.

Question 991. How many separate bones make up the coccyx in most adults: one, three to five fused together, seven, or twelve? The answer is three to five fused together, most often four. The first is the largest and still carries transverse processes and two small horns. The last is no more than a nodule of bone.

Question 992. A described radiograph of the lower end of the spine shows a small curved bone hanging below the sacrum, angled forward, with a pair of short horns on its first segment. Which bone is shown, and which way does its tip point? The answer is the coccyx, whose tip points downward and forward, and that tilt matters when the body sits.

Question 993. A constructed illustration: a person slipped on a stair and landed on the buttocks, and now has pain at the very bottom of the spine that is worst when sitting and when rising. Which structure is involved, and why does sitting hurt? The answer is the coccyx, which is tender because it is pressed against the seat. Rising hurts because muscles pull on it.

Question 994. Which of these muscles takes attachment from the coccyx: the gluteus maximus, the rectus abdominis, the sternocleidomastoid, or the deltoid? The answer is the gluteus maximus. Part of it rises from the back of the coccyx and the sacrum, and the levator ani and the coccygeus also attach there.

Question 995. Which small horns on the first segment of the coccyx rise to meet the horns of the sacrum? The answer is the coccygeal cornua, which stand at the upper end of the bone beside the sacrococcygeal joint. They are the last remains of the arch of the first coccygeal vertebra and help to keep that joint steady.

Question 996. Which structures lie on each side of the upper part of the coccyx, and what do they help to close? The answer is the sacrotuberous ligaments, which run from the sacrum down to the ischial tuberosity, and the coccygeus muscles, which lie against the bone. Together they help to close the outlet of the pelvis.

Question 997. A described view of the pelvis from below shows a diamond-shaped floor whose posterior corner is a small curved bone, with muscle slung from it to the front. Which bone forms that corner, and what does it do when the trunk bends forward? The answer is the coccyx, which moves backward when the trunk bends forward and in childbirth, when it widens the outlet.

Question 998. In the standing body, does the coccyx lie in front of or behind the sacrum, and where does its tip lie? The answer is that it lies behind and below the sacrum, and its tip lies in the midline just behind the anal canal. It is the lowest point of the vertebral column.

Question 999. Which statement about the coccyx is correct: it always has four segments, it always fuses with the sacrum, it varies in the number of its segments, or it contains a vertebral canal? The answer is that it varies in the number of its segments and may fuse with the sacrum, and five or six segments occur as normal variations.

Question 1000. Which of these pairs belongs together: the coccyx with the pelvic floor, the coccyx with the hip joint, the sacrum with the skull, or the coccyx with the ribs? The answer is the coccyx with the pelvic floor. Muscles of the pelvic floor and the anococcygeal ligament take attachment from it, and it helps to close the outlet of the pelvis.

To summarize, the seven cervical vertebrae are the smallest and the most mobile, the atlas has neither body nor spine, the axis carries the dens on which the head turns, and the seventh vertebra carries the long spine felt at the base of the neck. The twelve thoracic vertebrae are heart shaped and alone bear facets for the ribs, their spines slope downward over one another, and their narrow canal holds the spinal cord closely. The five lumbar vertebrae have the largest bodies, facets in the sagittal plane that favor bending rather than twisting, and a canal that below the first or second lumbar vertebra holds only the roots of the cauda equina. The sacrum is five fused vertebrae that carry the weight of the trunk to the hip bones, and the coccyx is a small fused remnant of a tail that closes the pelvic floor. In every region the shape of a vertebra follows the work it does, and describing that shape aloud is the surest way to remember it.
