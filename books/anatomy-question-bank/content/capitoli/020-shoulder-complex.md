# Chapter 20 — Shoulder Complex

The shoulder is the most mobile joint in the body and among the least stable, and this chapter works through the joints and the ligaments that trade one property for the other. It opens with eight questions on the vertebral joints, then follows the sternoclavicular joint, the acromioclavicular joint, the glenohumeral joint, the rotator cuff, the labrum and the ligaments of the shoulder. It holds one hundred questions, and every one of them gives its answer in the same paragraph as the question and then explains why that answer is right. Nothing has to be held in mind, and nothing depends on a figure or a diagram. This is educational material, and it supports rather than replaces an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 1901. Which joints of the vertebral column are the plane synovial joints formed between the superior articular process of one vertebra and the inferior articular process of the one above? The answer is the facet joints, also called the zygapophyseal joints. Each has a loose capsule and receives branches of the dorsal ramus of a spinal nerve. Their sloping surfaces guide the direction in which the column is able to move.

Question 1902. Which statement about the facet joints of the cervical region is correct: their surfaces lie in a nearly horizontal plane, they lie in a vertical sagittal plane, they lie in a coronal plane, or they are absent above the second cervical vertebra? The answer is that their surfaces lie in a nearly horizontal plane. That slope allows free flexion, extension and rotation of the neck.

Question 1903. In the thoracic region, the facet joints of adjacent vertebrae face mainly in which plane, and which movement does that arrangement favor? The answer is the coronal plane, which favors rotation and side bending. The joint surfaces look backward and forward, so the chest part of the spine twists more readily than it bends.

Question 1904. Which statement about the lumbar facet joints is correct: they lie mainly in the sagittal plane and check rotation, they lie in the coronal plane and check flexion, they face horizontally and allow free twisting, or they are absent at the lowest level? The answer is that they lie mainly in the sagittal plane and check rotation. The lower back therefore bends forward and back freely but twists very little.

Question 1905. Which two parts make up an intervertebral disc, and which of them lies in the center? The answer is the annulus fibrosus on the outside and the nucleus pulposus in the center. The annulus is a ring of fibrocartilage whose fibers cross in layers, and the nucleus is a soft gel that spreads pressure sideways. No disc lies between the first and the second cervical vertebra.

Question 1906. Which ligament runs down the back of the vertebral bodies inside the vertebral canal, and which movement does it check? The answer is the posterior longitudinal ligament, which checks flexion. It lies within the canal and is narrower than the ligament that runs down the front of the bodies.

Question 1907. What are the small joints called that form between the raised lateral lip of a lower cervical vertebral body and the body above it, and why do they matter? The answer is the uncovertebral joints, also called the joints of Luschka. They lie at the sides of the bodies from the third to the seventh cervical vertebra.

Question 1908. Which joint provides most of the rotation of the head: the atlanto-occipital joint, a pivot joint between the first and second cervical vertebrae, the joint between the second and third cervical vertebrae, or a plane joint at the base of the skull? The answer is a pivot joint between the first and the second cervical vertebrae. The dens of the second turns inside a ring on the first.

Question 1909. Which joint is the only true bony connection between the upper limb and the axial skeleton? The answer is the sternoclavicular joint. It lies between the medial end of the clavicle and the manubrium of the sternum, with the first costal cartilage taking part. Every other link between the arm and the trunk is muscular or fascial.

Question 1910. Which kind of synovial joint is the sternoclavicular joint: a hinge joint, a saddle joint, a pivot joint, or a plane joint? The answer is a saddle joint, though it behaves like a ball and socket. Its two curved surfaces sit at right angles to each other, and a disc divides the cavity in two. That disc is what allows movement in so many directions.

Question 1911. A described specimen cut across the sternoclavicular joint shows two separate cavities with a fibrous plate between them, dividing the joint from side to side. Which structure is the plate, and what does it do? The answer is the articular disc, which divides the joint and checks inward movement of the clavicle. A blow on the outer shoulder drives the clavicle inward against it.

Question 1912. A described dissection at the base of the neck shows a short strong band running from the first rib and its cartilage upward and outward to the underside of the clavicle, with a small bursa splitting it into two parts. Which ligament is shown? The answer is the costoclavicular ligament, the strongest ligament of the sternoclavicular joint. It checks elevation of the shoulder.

Question 1913. Which statement about the movements of the sternoclavicular joint is correct: the clavicle can be raised, lowered, thrust forward, pulled back and rotated, it can only be raised and lowered, it becomes fixed after birth, or it allows no rotation at all? The answer is that the clavicle can be raised, lowered, thrust forward, pulled back and rotated. It turns about its own long axis as the arm goes overhead.

Question 1914. A constructed illustration: after a fall onto the point of the shoulder, a person has difficulty in swallowing and the arm feels heavy and swollen. Which injury explains the picture? The answer is a backward dislocation of the sternoclavicular joint, in which the medial end of the clavicle presses on the trachea, the esophagus and the great vessels. Management belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 1915. A described radiograph of the upper chest shows the medial end of the clavicle sitting above and in front of the manubrium, out of line with the other side. Which joint has moved, and in which direction? The answer is the sternoclavicular joint, with the clavicle displaced forward. Forward displacement is the common one. The medial end normally lies level with the manubrium.

Question 1916. Which ligament crosses the top of the manubrium between the two clavicles: the costoclavicular ligament, the interclavicular ligament, the coracoclavicular ligament, or the transverse humeral ligament? The answer is the interclavicular ligament. It joins the two medial ends of the clavicles over the upper border of the manubrium and blends with both joint capsules. It helps to check downward movement of the shoulder.

Question 1917. Which structures lie immediately behind the sternoclavicular joint and are at risk when the clavicle is driven backward? The answer is the trachea, the esophagus, the great vessels of the neck and the brachial plexus. They lie directly behind the joint, with only a thin layer between them and the bone. That is why a backward dislocation here is treated as an emergency.

Question 1918. Which nerves supply the sternoclavicular joint: the suprascapular and axillary nerves, the medial supraclavicular nerve with the nerve to the subclavius, the long thoracic nerve alone, or the radial nerve? The answer is the medial supraclavicular nerve with the nerve to the subclavius. The first comes from the cervical plexus and the second from the upper trunk of the brachial plexus. Every shoulder joint takes fibers from more than one source.

Question 1919. True or false: the medial end of the clavicle is the first part of the bone to ossify. The answer is false. The shaft ossifies first and the medial end last, and its growth plate does not close until the middle of the third decade of life.

Question 1920. Which of these pairs belongs together: the costoclavicular ligament with the first rib, the coracoclavicular ligament with the first rib, the transverse humeral ligament with the acromion, or the coracoacromial ligament with the clavicle? The answer is the costoclavicular ligament with the first rib. It passes from that rib and its cartilage to the underside of the clavicle. The coracoclavicular ligament runs down to the coracoid process instead.

Question 1921. Which joint lies between the lateral end of the clavicle and the medial border of the acromion? The answer is the acromioclavicular joint. It is a small plane synovial joint at the top of the shoulder.

Question 1922. Which kind of joint is the acromioclavicular joint: a plane synovial joint, a hinge joint, a saddle joint, or a ball and socket joint? The answer is a plane synovial joint. Its flat surfaces glide on one another as the scapula turns.

Question 1923. Which ligament holds the lateral end of the clavicle down to the coracoid process, and which two parts does it have? The answer is the coracoclavicular ligament, made up of the conoid part and the trapezoid part. The conoid lies more medial and the trapezoid more lateral.

Question 1924. True or false: the superior part of the acromioclavicular ligament is weaker than the inferior part. The answer is false. The superior part is the stronger of the two and blends with the fibers of the trapezius muscle.

Question 1925. A constructed illustration: a person falls from a bicycle onto the point of the shoulder, and a tender step can be felt where the outer end of the clavicle meets the shoulder blade. Which joint is injured? The answer is the acromioclavicular joint. When the coracoclavicular ligament is torn as well, the clavicle rides higher than the acromion. Management belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 1926. A described radiograph of the shoulder shows the outer end of the clavicle projecting above the acromion, with the gap between the coracoid process and the clavicle wider than on the other side. Which structure has failed? The answer is the coracoclavicular ligament. Its conoid and trapezoid parts hold the clavicle down against the weight of the arm.

Question 1927. Which movements take place at the acromioclavicular joint: gliding with a little rotation, pure flexion and extension, abduction and adduction alone, or no movement at all? The answer is gliding with a little rotation. The scapula slides and turns on the outer end of the clavicle as the arm is raised. The movement is small, but without it the shoulder blade could not rotate.

Question 1928. Which ligament runs from the coracoid process to the acromion and forms an arch above the head of the humerus? The answer is the coracoacromial ligament. With the acromion and the coracoid process it completes the acromial arch.

Question 1929. A described section through the shoulder shows a flat fibrous band running above the head of the humerus from one bony point to another, with a bursa and a tendon lying beneath it. Which structures are described? The answer is the coracoacromial ligament above, with the subacromial bursa and the tendon of the supraspinatus below. The arch protects the tendon.

Question 1930. Which nerves supply the acromioclavicular joint? The answer is the suprascapular nerve, with a contribution from the lateral pectoral nerve. Fibers reach the joint from behind and from the front.

Question 1931. The clavicle is described as turning about its own long axis as the arm is raised overhead. In which plane does that movement take place, and about which axis? The answer is the transverse plane, about the long axis of the bone. The bone turns so that its curve follows the scapula as the shoulder blade rotates. The sternoclavicular and acromioclavicular joints share the movement between them.

Question 1932. Which statement about the two parts of the coracoclavicular ligament is correct: the conoid lies medial to the trapezoid, the trapezoid lies medial to the conoid, both lie in front of the clavicle, or both are attached to the acromion? The answer is that the conoid lies medial to the trapezoid. The conoid is the more vertical band and the trapezoid the flatter one. Both run from the coracoid process to the clavicle.

Question 1933. Which joint is the ball and socket joint of the shoulder, formed between the head of the humerus and the shallow cavity of the scapula? The answer is the glenohumeral joint. The head is about one third of a sphere and far larger than the socket that receives it. That mismatch is what gives the shoulder its great range of movement.

Question 1934. Which of these is the socket of the shoulder joint: the acromion, the glenoid cavity, the coracoid process, or the greater tubercle? The answer is the glenoid cavity, a shallow pear-shaped depression on the lateral border of the scapula. It is deepened only a little by a ring of fibrocartilage.

Question 1935. The shoulder joint is described as having more movement than any other joint in the body. Which feature of the glenoid cavity explains this? The answer is that the cavity is shallow and small compared with the head of the humerus, so the head can swing widely before bone checks it. The labrum, the capsule and the cuff make up the difference in soft tissue.

Question 1936. Which tendon lies within the cavity of the shoulder joint and passes over the top of the head of the humerus? The answer is the tendon of the long head of the biceps brachii. It begins at the supraglenoid tubercle and leaves through the groove between the tubercles. It lies inside the capsule but outside the synovial lining.

Question 1937. Which factors together keep the head of the humerus in its socket: the depth of the socket by itself, the rotator cuff with the labrum, the capsule and the pressure inside the joint, the ligaments by themselves, or the deltoid muscle by itself? The answer is the rotator cuff with the labrum, the capsule and the pressure inside the joint. No single one of them is enough on its own.

Question 1938. Which structure lies between the acromion and the tendon of the supraspinatus, and with which cavity does it often communicate: the subscapular bursa, the subacromial bursa, the subcoracoid bursa, or the axillary pouch? The answer is the subacromial bursa, which often communicates with the cavity of the shoulder joint. It also lies beneath the coracoacromial ligament.

Question 1939. A constructed illustration: after a fall the arm is held a little away from the side and turned outward, and the normal rounded contour of the shoulder has gone. Which injury is described? The answer is a forward dislocation of the shoulder joint, in which the head of the humerus lies in front of and below the glenoid. Management belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 1940. A constructed illustration: after a shoulder dislocation, the outer part of the shoulder cannot be lifted and the skin over it has lost sensation. Which nerve is involved, and where does it run? The answer is the axillary nerve, which winds around the surgical neck of the humerus through the quadrangular space. A fracture of that same neck can injure the nerve.

Question 1941. A described radiograph taken from the front shows the head of the humerus lying below and medial to the glenoid, with the shaft turned outward. Which joint is dislocated, and in which direction has the head moved? The answer is the glenohumeral joint, with the head displaced forward and downward, which is the common direction. The arm is usually held away from the side.

Question 1942. In which position is the shoulder joint most tightly packed: full abduction with lateral rotation, full adduction, the arm hanging at the side, or full medial rotation with the arm across the chest? The answer is full abduction with lateral rotation. There the capsule and the ligaments are wound tightly around the head. The arm hanging at the side is the loosest position of all.

Question 1943. Which muscles lie immediately behind the glenohumeral joint and form its posterior wall? The answer is the infraspinatus and the teres minor, with the long head of the triceps below them. They cover the back of the capsule.

Question 1944. Which arteries form the ring of supply around the surgical neck of the humerus and the tissues of the joint: the suprascapular artery alone, the anterior and posterior circumflex humeral arteries with the suprascapular artery, the radial artery alone, or the deep artery of the arm? The answer is the anterior and posterior circumflex humeral arteries with the suprascapular artery. They meet around the surgical neck and keep the head supplied.

Question 1945. Which movement of the shoulder joint carries the arm forward and upward in the sagittal plane? The answer is flexion. Extension carries the arm backward in that same plane.

Question 1946. Which muscle begins abduction of the arm during the first part of the movement: the deltoid, the supraspinatus, the infraspinatus, or the teres major? The answer is the supraspinatus. It pulls the head of the humerus into the socket and sets the arm on its way. The deltoid then carries the greater part of the movement.

Question 1947. Which tubercle of the humerus lies immediately lateral to the groove that carries the long head of the biceps tendon, and which muscles are attached to it? The answer is the greater tubercle, to which the supraspinatus, the infraspinatus and the teres minor are attached. The lesser tubercle lies medial to the groove and receives the subscapularis.

Question 1948. A constructed illustration: a person cannot raise the arm above the head, and the shoulder blade begins to move early and travels much further than usual as the arm is lifted. Which mechanism is disturbed? The answer is the scapulohumeral rhythm, in which the scapula and the humerus normally move together in a fixed proportion. When the shoulder joint is stiff, the scapula does more of the work.

Question 1949. A described magnetic resonance imaging scan of the shoulder shows the head of the humerus riding high beneath the acromion, with the tendon above it thinned and drawn back. What does that appearance mean? The answer is a long-standing tear of the rotator cuff, which has allowed the head to rise. The space between the head and the acromion is narrowed.

Question 1950. Name two movements of the scapula that help the arm to reach above the head, spoken as a list. The answer is upward rotation and elevation. Upward rotation turns the glenoid upward so that the humerus can follow it, and elevation lifts the whole shoulder blade.

Question 1951. Which statement about the capsule of the shoulder joint is correct: it is lax and redundant below, it is tight in every position, it is attached to the surgical neck, or it has no synovial lining? The answer is that it is lax and redundant below. The lower part hangs in folds at the side as the axillary pouch. Above it is attached to the glenoid margin, below to the anatomical neck.

Question 1952. True or false: the shoulder joint is less stable than the hip joint because its socket is shallow. The answer is true. The glenoid cavity is far smaller than the head that sits in it, while the acetabulum wraps around most of the femoral head.

Question 1953. Which four muscles make up the rotator cuff, and what do their tendons have in common? The answer is the supraspinatus, the infraspinatus, the teres minor and the subscapularis. Their tendons blend with the capsule and merge into one sleeve. All four are inserted on the upper end of the humerus.

Question 1954. Which muscle of the rotator cuff lies in the fossa above the spine of the scapula and sends its tendon over the top of the shoulder joint: the infraspinatus, the supraspinatus, the teres minor, or the subscapularis? The answer is the supraspinatus. Its tendon passes beneath the acromion to the top of the greater tubercle. It begins abduction and steadies the head in the socket.

Question 1955. Which rotator cuff muscle lies in the fossa below the spine of the scapula and turns the arm outward: the subscapularis, the infraspinatus, the teres major, or the supraspinatus? The answer is the infraspinatus. It covers the back of the scapula below its spine.

Question 1956. Which rotator cuff muscle is the only one that lies on the front of the scapula, and on which tubercle does it insert? The answer is the subscapularis, which inserts on the lesser tubercle. It fills the hollow of the subscapular fossa and crosses the front of the shoulder joint. It is the most powerful medial rotator of the arm.

Question 1957. Which muscle lies immediately below the infraspinatus on the back of the scapula, and which nerve supplies it? The answer is the teres minor, supplied by the axillary nerve. It runs from the lateral border of the scapula to the lowest facet of the greater tubercle. The infraspinatus above it is supplied by the suprascapular nerve instead.

Question 1958. Which nerve supplies both the supraspinatus and the infraspinatus: the axillary nerve, the suprascapular nerve, the upper subscapular nerve, or the radial nerve? The answer is the suprascapular nerve. It passes through the notch above the spine of the scapula and then through the notch beside the spine.

Question 1959. A described view of the shoulder from above shows a tendon running under a bony arch, frayed and thinned at the point where it meets the bone, with the joint surface below it still smooth. Which structure is damaged, and why there? The answer is the tendon of the supraspinatus, damaged in the zone beneath the acromion, where its blood supply is poorest. That zone is also where the tendon rubs as the arm rises.

Question 1960. A constructed illustration: a person has pain when the arm is raised from about 60 to 120 degrees from the side, and the pain eases above that arc. Which structure is most likely involved? The answer is the tendon of the supraspinatus rubbing beneath the acromion. That painful arc is a described illustration and not a diagnosis. Management belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 1961. A described section through the shoulder shows a tendon passing beneath a bony arch, a fluid-filled sac between the two, and a small bony spur projecting from the arch above the tendon. Which structures are described? The answer is the tendon of the supraspinatus, the subacromial bursa and a spur on the acromion. The spur narrows the passage through which the tendon must move.

Question 1962. A described cross section through the upper end of the humerus shows four flat tendons around the bone, merging above with the capsule, one in front and the others behind and above. Which structures form that sleeve? The answer is the tendons of the rotator cuff, the subscapularis on the front and the supraspinatus, the infraspinatus and the teres minor behind. Together they form a sleeve that holds the head in the socket.

Question 1963. Which statement about the rotator cuff is correct: its tendons fuse with the capsule of the shoulder joint, its tendons lie outside the capsule altogether, it has no muscle on the front of the scapula, or its tendons are inserted far down the shaft of the humerus? The answer is that its tendons fuse with the capsule. That blending is why a torn cuff and a painful joint often appear together.

Question 1964. Which of these pairs belongs together: the subscapularis with the lesser tubercle, the supraspinatus with the lesser tubercle, the infraspinatus with the coracoid process, or the teres minor with the spine of the scapula? The answer is the subscapularis with the lesser tubercle. The three muscles on the back of the scapula all reach the greater tubercle instead.

Question 1965. A constructed illustration: a heavy pack strap presses for hours into the hollow above the spine of the scapula, and afterward the arm cannot be turned outward and the muscle in that hollow feels wasted. Which nerve has been pressed upon? The answer is the suprascapular nerve, compressed at the notch above the spine of the scapula. Management belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 1966. Which of these muscles turns the arm inward and lies on the back of the scapula: the teres major, the teres minor, the infraspinatus, or the deltoid? The answer is the teres major, which is not part of the rotator cuff. It runs from the lower angle of the scapula to the crest below the lesser tubercle. The teres minor beside it turns the arm the other way.

Question 1967. A tendon is described as lying inside the capsule of the shoulder joint but outside its synovial lining. What does that mean about where it lies? The answer is that it runs within the fibrous capsule while the synovial membrane wraps around it like a sleeve. It therefore glides in a tunnel of its own.

Question 1968. Which of these pairs belongs together: the teres minor with the lowest facet of the greater tubercle, the supraspinatus with the lowest facet, the subscapularis with the greater tubercle, or the infraspinatus with the lesser tubercle? The answer is the teres minor with the lowest facet of the greater tubercle. The supraspinatus takes the top facet and the infraspinatus the middle one.

Question 1969. Name the three muscles that are attached to the greater tubercle of the humerus, spoken as a list. The answer is the supraspinatus, the infraspinatus and the teres minor. They occupy the top, the middle and the lowest facets of the tubercle in that order.

Question 1970. True or false: when the rotator cuff is torn through, the head of the humerus tends to ride upward under the acromion. The answer is true. The cuff pulls the head into the socket, while the deltoid tends to lift it.

Question 1971. Which ring of fibrocartilage is attached around the rim of the glenoid cavity, and what is its shape in section? The answer is the glenoid labrum, which is triangular in section with its broad base on the bone. It follows the rim of the socket and blends with the capsule above.

Question 1972. Which structure blends with the glenoid labrum at the top of the socket and then runs away through the joint: the long head of the triceps, the long head of the biceps, the coracohumeral ligament, or the transverse humeral ligament? The answer is the long head of the biceps. It arises from the supraglenoid tubercle and from the labrum beside it. The long head of the triceps arises below the socket.

Question 1973. A described view from inside the shoulder joint shows a triangular rim of tissue lifted away from the front and lower part of the glenoid, with the head of the humerus beside it. Which structure is detached? The answer is the glenoid labrum, torn at its front and lower part. That is the injury found after the head dislocates forward.

Question 1974. What name is given to the detachment of the front and lower part of the glenoid labrum that follows a forward dislocation of the shoulder? The answer is the Bankart lesion. The labrum is torn from the rim of the glenoid and the socket loses the depth it needs.

Question 1975. Which of these best describes the work of the glenoid labrum: it deepens the socket and gives the capsule and the ligaments a hold, it makes synovial fluid, it forms the head of the humerus, or it moves the scapula on the chest wall? The answer is that it deepens the socket and gives the capsule and the ligaments a hold. It increases the area over which the head and the socket touch.

Question 1976. Where does the glenoid labrum lie in relation to the capsule of the shoulder joint? The answer is between the joint cavity and the capsule, since the capsule and the synovial membrane both take hold at the rim. The fibrous capsule is attached along the outer edge of the labrum.

Question 1977. A described dissection of the shoulder from the front shows a tendon taking origin from a small bony point above the glenoid cavity, where its fibers blend with a ring of fibrocartilage. Which tendon is it, and which bony point gives it origin? The answer is the long head of the biceps, arising from the supraglenoid tubercle and from the labrum. That bony point lies just above the rim of the socket.

Question 1978. A constructed illustration: a person who has dislocated the shoulder several times feels the joint begin to slip whenever the arm is turned outward with the elbow at the side. Which structure has most likely failed? The answer is the front and lower part of the labrum together with the ligament beside it. Repeated dislocation stretches both until they no longer hold the head.

Question 1979. The glenoid labrum is described as having a poor blood supply, especially along its inner free edge. Which statement follows from that: a tear of it heals badly, a tear heals quickly, it makes the synovial fluid, or it carries the main supply to the head of the humerus? The answer is that a tear of it heals badly. Only the outer part, close to the bone, receives a useful supply.

Question 1980. Which part of the glenoid labrum lies nearest the origin of the long head of the biceps tendon, and which part is torn when the head of the humerus dislocates forward? The answer is that the upper part lies nearest the tendon's origin, while the front and lower part is torn in dislocation. Both are parts of the same ring.

Question 1981. A described MRI scan of the shoulder shows the upper part of the labrum separated from the rim of the glenoid, with fluid tracking into the gap, while the lower part of the rim looks intact. Which injury is described? The answer is a tear of the upper labrum at the point where the biceps tendon begins. The fluid outlines the split.

Question 1982. Name the ring of fibrocartilage that deepens the socket of the shoulder joint, and name the tendon that blends with its upper edge. The answer is the glenoid labrum, blending with the tendon of the long head of the biceps. The two are continuous where the tendon leaves the socket. A pull on one is felt by the other.

Question 1983. Which three thickenings of the fibrous capsule of the shoulder joint are named from their position? The answer is the upper, the middle and the lower glenohumeral ligaments. They are seen best from inside the joint, where no muscle covers the capsule. The lowest of the three is the strongest.

Question 1984. Which of the glenohumeral ligaments is the chief check against a forward dislocation when the arm is raised and turned outward: the upper one, the middle one, the lower one, or the transverse humeral ligament? The answer is the lower glenohumeral ligament. In that position it lies in front of the head and stops it from sliding forward.

Question 1985. Which ligament runs from the coracoid process to the humerus and bridges the gap between the supraspinatus and the subscapularis? The answer is the coracohumeral ligament. It passes over the front and top of the capsule to reach the greater and the lesser tubercles.

Question 1986. Which ligament bridges the groove on the front of the humerus and holds the tendon that runs in that groove? The answer is the transverse humeral ligament. It spans the gap between the greater tubercle and the lesser tubercle. Beneath it runs the tendon of the long head of the biceps.

Question 1987. Which ligament of the shoulder region is not part of the capsule of the joint, yet forms an arch over the head of the humerus from the coracoid process to the acromion? The answer is the coracoacromial ligament. It protects the cuff beneath it and limits how far the head can rise. It takes no part in holding the acromioclavicular joint together.

Question 1988. Which ligament connects the coracoid process to the clavicle: the costoclavicular ligament, the coracoclavicular ligament, the coracoacromial ligament, or the acromioclavicular ligament? The answer is the coracoclavicular ligament. Its two parts, the conoid and the trapezoid, hold the outer end of the clavicle down. The coracoacromial ligament runs to the acromion instead.

Question 1989. A constructed illustration: with the arm hanging at the side and the elbow bent, the head of the humerus can be drawn downward much further than it should be, while the arm can still be raised. Which structures have failed? The answer is the coracohumeral ligament with the upper glenohumeral ligament. They hold the head up when the arm hangs at the side.

Question 1990. A described view from inside the shoulder joint shows three bands running down the front of the capsule, the lowest of them the thickest. Which structures are shown? The answer is the upper, the middle and the lower glenohumeral ligaments, of which the lowest is the strongest. They lie within the fibrous capsule itself.

Question 1991. Which ligament resists elevation of the shoulder and is the strongest ligament of the sternoclavicular joint: the interclavicular ligament, the costoclavicular ligament, the coracoclavicular ligament, or the transverse humeral ligament? The answer is the costoclavicular ligament. It runs from the first rib and its cartilage up to the underside of the clavicle.

Question 1992. Which ligament lies immediately above the capsule of the shoulder joint, runs from the coracoid process to the two tubercles, and blends with the cuff? The answer is the coracohumeral ligament. It roofs the gap between the supraspinatus and the subscapularis.

Question 1993. Which ligament is the chief support of the outer end of the clavicle, and where does each of its parts lie relative to the other? The answer is the coracoclavicular ligament, whose conoid part lies medial to its trapezoid part. Both run from the coracoid process below to the clavicle above.

Question 1994. Which of these pairs belongs together: the transverse humeral ligament with the intertubercular groove, the coracoacromial ligament with the first rib, the costoclavicular ligament with the acromion, or the conoid ligament with the lesser tubercle? The answer is the transverse humeral ligament with the intertubercular groove. It roofs the groove and keeps the tendon of the long head of the biceps in place.

Question 1995. Which statement about the attachment of the capsule of the shoulder joint is correct: it is attached to the anatomical neck of the humerus, to the surgical neck, to the shaft, or to the glenoid rim only? The answer is that it is attached to the anatomical neck. The surgical neck lies outside the joint, so a fracture there does not open the cavity.

Question 1996. Which of these pairs belongs together: the costoclavicular ligament with the inner end of the clavicle, the coracoclavicular ligament with the first rib, the transverse humeral ligament with the glenoid rim, or the coracoacromial ligament with the lesser tubercle? The answer is the costoclavicular ligament with the inner end of the clavicle. It ties that end to the first rib, while the coracoclavicular ligament ties the outer end down.

Question 1997. A constructed illustration: a fall onto the outstretched hand drives the head of the humerus upward against the arch above it, and the person feels pain at the top of the shoulder. Which ligament receives the force, and which tendon beneath it is at risk? The answer is the coracoacromial ligament, with the tendon of the supraspinatus beneath it. The arch stops the head from rising further.

Question 1998. True or false: the glenohumeral ligaments are separate cords lying outside the fibrous capsule of the shoulder joint. The answer is false. They are thickenings of the capsule itself and are seen best from within the joint. Because muscle covers most of the capsule, these bands show only where no muscle lies.

Question 1999. A described dissection of the shoulder from the front, after the deltoid has been removed, shows a flat band running from the coracoid process across to the top of the humerus, lying over the gap between two cuff tendons. Which structure is described? The answer is the coracohumeral ligament, which roofs the front of the capsule and closes that gap. It also resists downward movement of the head when the arm hangs.

Question 2000. Which structure finally checks the head of the humerus when the arm is fully raised to the side: the coracohumeral ligament, the lower glenohumeral ligament, the acromion with the coracoacromial arch, or the transverse humeral ligament? The answer is the acromion with the coracoacromial arch. The bony arch above the head is the last limit of pure elevation.

To summarize, the vertebral column moves differently at every level because its facet joints face in different planes, nearly horizontal in the neck, coronal in the chest and sagittal in the lower back, and the posterior longitudinal ligament inside the canal checks flexion. The sternoclavicular joint is the only bony link between the arm and the trunk, it holds an articular disc, and the costoclavicular ligament is its strongest check against elevation. The acromioclavicular joint is a plane joint held down chiefly by the coracoclavicular ligament, whose conoid part lies medial to its trapezoid part, and the coracoacromial ligament arches over the head of the humerus. The shoulder joint is a shallow ball and socket whose head is much larger than the glenoid, steadied by the labrum, by the capsule with its upper, middle and lower glenohumeral ligaments, and by the four tendons of the rotator cuff. The supraspinatus begins abduction and is the part most often torn beneath the acromion, the subscapularis medially rotates from the lesser tubercle, and the infraspinatus with the teres minor laterally rotates from the greater tubercle.
