# Chapter 37 — Heart Anatomy, Part Two

This chapter continues the study of the heart by following the vessels that supply and drain its own muscle and the sac that holds it. It covers the coronary arteries and the territories they serve, the coronary veins and the channels that return their blood, and the pericardium with its layers, its cavity, its sinuses and its attachments. It holds one hundred questions, and every question is followed at once by its answer and by the reason that answer is right, so nothing has to be carried in mind from one item to the next. This is educational material, and it supports rather than replaces an anatomy course, a dissection, a clinical training program, or the judgment of a teacher.

Question 3601. From which aortic sinus does the left coronary artery arise: the right coronary sinus, the left posterior aortic sinus, the non-coronary sinus, or above the sinotubular junction? The answer is the left posterior aortic sinus, often called the left coronary sinus. It lies behind the left cusp of the aortic valve, while the right coronary artery arises from the right, or anterior, sinus.

Question 3602. Which artery runs down the front of the heart toward the apex, in the groove between the two ventricles? The answer is the anterior interventricular artery, also called the left anterior descending artery. It is one of the two terminal branches of the left coronary artery and supplies the front of both ventricles.

Question 3603. Which artery runs in the posterior interventricular groove and supplies the back third of the interventricular septum? The answer is the posterior interventricular artery, also called the posterior descending artery. In most people it is a branch of the right coronary artery, which makes the heart right dominant, and it also supplies the back walls of both ventricles.

Question 3604. In most people, which artery gives the branch that supplies the sinoatrial node: the right coronary artery, the circumflex artery, the anterior interventricular artery, or the left coronary artery? The answer is the right coronary artery. Its nodal branch arises near the beginning of the artery and climbs to the junction of the superior vena cava and the right atrium.

Question 3605. A described coronary angiogram shows a wide vessel running down the front of the heart toward the apex and giving off small branches that dive into the muscle between the ventricles. Which vessel is shown, and which part of the septum do those branches supply? The answer is the anterior interventricular artery, whose septal branches supply the front two thirds of the septum.

Question 3606. In the anterior interventricular groove, which structure lies superficial to the anterior interventricular artery: the great cardiac vein, the middle cardiac vein, the coronary sinus, or the small cardiac vein? The answer is the great cardiac vein. It ascends from the apex beside the artery and crosses over it in the groove.

Question 3607. A constructed illustration: a person has sudden crushing central chest pain, and the tracing shows injury of the front wall of the left ventricle. Which artery is most likely blocked? The answer is the anterior interventricular artery, which supplies the front of the left ventricle, the apex and the front of the septum. Care belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 3608. True or false: each coronary artery opens into the aorta from within an aortic sinus, near the level of the sinotubular junction. The answer is true. The left artery opens in the left posterior sinus and the right artery in the right sinus, and the third sinus gives rise to no artery.

Question 3609. A small artery arises from the right aortic sinus through its own opening and runs down the front of the right ventricle toward the outflow tract. Which vessel is described: the conus artery, the right marginal artery, the sinoatrial nodal artery, or the circumflex artery? The answer is the conus artery. It has its own opening in some people and is then called the third coronary artery.

Question 3610. Which branch of the right coronary artery runs down the sharp lower border of the heart toward the apex? The answer is the right marginal artery. The sharp lower border is called the acute margin, and the artery supplies the front and the back of the right ventricle along it.

Question 3611. Which of these pairs belongs together: the anterior interventricular artery with the anterior interventricular groove, the circumflex artery with the posterior interventricular groove, the right coronary artery with the anterior interventricular groove, or the posterior interventricular artery with the coronary sulcus? The answer is the anterior interventricular artery with the anterior interventricular groove. The circumflex artery runs in the coronary sulcus instead.

Question 3612. A described coronary angiogram shows a long trunk dividing into two branches, one running down the front of the heart and the other turning around the left border. Which two branches are shown? The answer is the anterior interventricular artery and the circumflex artery, the two terminal branches of the left coronary artery, which divides near the left border of the pulmonary trunk.

Question 3613. Name the two arteries that supply the interventricular septum, spoken as a list. The answer is the anterior interventricular artery and the posterior interventricular artery. The first supplies the front two thirds of the septum through its septal branches, and the second supplies the back third.

Question 3614. Which artery most often gives the branch that supplies the atrioventricular node: the circumflex artery, the anterior interventricular artery, the right coronary artery, or the left coronary artery? The answer is the right coronary artery. Its nodal branch arises near the point where the artery turns into the posterior interventricular groove. In a left dominant heart the circumflex artery supplies the node instead.

Question 3615. As the left coronary artery leaves the aorta, which structure lies immediately in front of it and which lies behind? The answer is the pulmonary trunk in front and the left auricle behind. The artery runs in the short space between them before it divides.

Question 3616. A constructed illustration: a young person has chest pain on exertion, and imaging shows part of the anterior interventricular artery dipping into the heart muscle instead of lying on its surface. What is that arrangement called, and why does it matter? The answer is a myocardial bridge. Each contraction compresses the tunneled segment. The arrangement is a common variant and usually causes no trouble.

Question 3617. How many openings do the coronary arteries normally make in the wall of the aorta: one, two, three, or four? The answer is two. One lies in the left posterior sinus for the left coronary artery and one in the right sinus for the right coronary artery, and an extra opening for the conus artery is present in some people.

Question 3618. The circumflex artery runs around the left border of the heart. In which groove does it lie, and does that groove separate the atria from the ventricles or the two ventricles from each other? The answer is the coronary sulcus, also called the atrioventricular groove, which separates the atria above from the ventricles below.

Question 3619. Which branch of the circumflex artery follows the rounded left border of the heart and supplies the lateral wall of the left ventricle? The answer is the left marginal artery, also called the obtuse marginal artery. Its companion on the right side is the right marginal artery, which follows the sharp border.

Question 3620. A described cast of the coronary vessels shows two trunks leaving the aorta, one passing to the right and one dividing almost at once into two branches. How many main stems leave the aorta? The answer is two, the right coronary artery and the left coronary artery. The left one divides early into the anterior interventricular artery and the circumflex artery.

Question 3621. Which part of the interventricular septum is supplied by the posterior interventricular artery: the front two thirds, the back third, the whole septum, or only the membranous part? The answer is the back third. The anterior interventricular artery supplies the front two thirds through its septal branches, and the membranous part at the top is supplied from both.

Question 3622. A constructed illustration: a person has an infarct of the right ventricle, and the blocked vessel runs in the groove between the right atrium and the right ventricle. Which artery is blocked? The answer is the right coronary artery, which supplies most of the right atrium and right ventricle. In a right dominant heart it also gives the posterior interventricular artery.

Question 3623. Where do the openings of the two coronary arteries lie in relation to the cusps of the aortic valve? The answer is within the aortic sinuses, above the attachments of the cusps and below the sinotubular junction. Because they open into the sinuses, the arteries fill mainly during diastole, when the cusps are closed.

Question 3624. Which of these is a branch of the right coronary artery: the circumflex artery, the anterior interventricular artery, the right marginal artery, or the left marginal artery? The answer is the right marginal artery. The circumflex and anterior interventricular arteries belong to the left coronary artery, and the left marginal artery is a branch of the circumflex.

Question 3625. Which branch of the anterior interventricular artery is the largest of the small vessels that run deep into the interventricular septum? The answer is the first septal artery, also called the first septal perforator. It supplies the front of the septum and part of the conducting tissue within it.

Question 3626. A described computed tomography angiogram of the heart shows a vessel passing between the pulmonary trunk and the left auricle before it divides. Which vessel is shown? The answer is the left coronary artery. Its short trunk lies in that space and divides into the anterior interventricular artery and the circumflex artery.

Question 3627. Why does a blockage of one coronary artery damage only its own territory, although the two arteries do meet: because the connections are too small, because the arteries never meet, because the veins carry blood backward, or because the muscle takes oxygen from the blood? The answer is that the connections are too small to carry blood, so the coronary arteries act as functional end arteries, with small channels at the apex and the crux.

Question 3628. Which of these pairs belongs together: the right coronary artery with the right atrioventricular groove, the circumflex artery with the anterior interventricular groove, the anterior interventricular artery with the posterior interventricular groove, or the posterior interventricular artery with the coronary sulcus? The answer is the right coronary artery with the right atrioventricular groove. The circumflex artery runs in the coronary sulcus on the left.

Question 3629. True or false: the left coronary artery carries more blood than the right coronary artery in the resting heart. The answer is true. The left ventricle does more work and receives the larger share of coronary flow, most of it during diastole, when the muscle is relaxed.

Question 3630. Which statement describes a right dominant heart: the right coronary artery gives off the posterior interventricular artery, the left coronary artery gives off the posterior interventricular artery, the right coronary artery gives off the anterior interventricular artery, or the circumflex artery gives off the right marginal artery? The answer is that the right coronary artery gives off the posterior interventricular artery, the arrangement found in most people.

Question 3631. A constructed illustration: a person has an infarct of the back of the left ventricle and of the back third of the interventricular septum. Which artery is most likely blocked? The answer is the right coronary artery, through its posterior interventricular branch. An infarct here often disturbs the atrioventricular node as well.

Question 3632. Which artery usually reaches the apex of the heart and supplies it? The answer is the anterior interventricular artery, which runs to the notch at the apex and turns around it. The posterior interventricular artery may share the supply of the apex from behind.

Question 3633. Which structure lies immediately deep to the epicardium in the atrioventricular groove on the right side of the heart? The answer is the right coronary artery. The grooves of the heart carry the coronary vessels in a layer of fat just under the epicardium.

Question 3634. A described specimen shows a heart in which the artery running in the posterior interventricular groove can be traced back around the right border to the right aortic sinus. What does this arrangement show? The answer is that the heart is right dominant. In a left dominant heart that same groove is supplied from the circumflex artery.

Question 3635. In about 40 per cent of people, which artery gives the branch that supplies the sinoatrial node: the circumflex artery, the anterior interventricular artery, the right coronary artery, or the posterior interventricular artery? The answer is the circumflex artery. In the remaining people that branch comes from the right coronary artery.

Question 3636. A constructed illustration: a tear in the wall of the aorta extends back along the root of the aorta, and blood collects around the heart. Which two vessels lie in greatest danger because of where they open? The answer is the two coronary arteries, whose openings lie in the aortic sinuses. Care belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 3637. Name two places on the surface of the heart where small connections between the two coronary arteries are most often found, spoken as a list. The answer is at the apex of the heart and at the crux, where the posterior interventricular groove meets the coronary sulcus. These channels are narrow and open only when flow falls.

Question 3638. Into which two branches does the left coronary artery usually divide: the anterior interventricular and circumflex arteries, the right marginal and posterior interventricular arteries, the circumflex and right coronary arteries, or the anterior interventricular and right marginal arteries? The answer is the anterior interventricular and circumflex arteries, both branches of the short left trunk.

Question 3639. Which artery runs in the groove between the right atrium and the right ventricle and gives off the right marginal branch? The answer is the right coronary artery. It begins at the right aortic sinus, follows the groove around the right border, and then turns down between the ventricles at the back.

Question 3640. A described cast of the heart vessels shows a fine network lying over the surface of the heart and larger branches entering the wall between the ventricles. In which layer do these vessels lie? The answer is the epicardium, the outer layer of the wall. The coronary vessels lie in the fat beneath it, and only their smallest branches run within the muscle.

Question 3641. In a right dominant heart, which artery supplies both the atrioventricular node and the back third of the interventricular septum: the circumflex artery, the anterior interventricular artery, the right coronary artery, or the left coronary artery? The answer is the right coronary artery. Its posterior interventricular branch and its nodal branch both arise from it.

Question 3642. In the right atrioventricular groove, which chamber lies above the right coronary artery and which lies below it? The answer is the right atrium above and the right ventricle below. The groove separates the atrium from the ventricle, and the same groove carries the small cardiac vein.

Question 3643. What is the name of the point on the back of the heart where the posterior interventricular groove meets the coronary sulcus? The answer is the crux of the heart. The right coronary artery reaches this point in most people and turns down from it into the posterior interventricular groove.

Question 3644. Which statement about the blood supply of the conducting system is correct: the atrioventricular node is usually supplied by the right artery, the sinoatrial node always comes from the same artery, both nodes come from the left artery, or the nodes have no supply? The answer is that the atrioventricular node is usually supplied by the right coronary artery. The sinoatrial node comes from the right artery in most people and from the circumflex in the rest.

Question 3645. A described angiogram shows the branch running down the front of the heart stopping two thirds of the way to the apex, with no filling of the vessels beyond. Which artery is blocked, and which parts lose their supply? The answer is the anterior interventricular artery; the front wall of the left ventricle, the apex and the front two thirds of the septum lose supply.

Question 3646. Which vein begins at the apex of the heart, ascends in the anterior interventricular groove beside the anterior interventricular artery, and then turns into the coronary sulcus? The answer is the great cardiac vein. It is the longest vein of the heart and ends as the coronary sinus.

Question 3647. Into which chamber of the heart does the coronary sinus open? The answer is the right atrium. It opens between the opening of the inferior vena cava and the opening of the tricuspid valve, and a small fold, the valve of the coronary sinus, guards its mouth.

Question 3648. Which structure is the direct continuation of the great cardiac vein: the middle cardiac vein, the coronary sinus, the anterior cardiac vein, or the oblique vein of the left atrium? The answer is the coronary sinus. The great vein turns around the left border and widens into the sinus, which runs in the posterior part of the coronary sulcus.

Question 3649. Which vein runs in the posterior interventricular groove beside the posterior interventricular artery? The answer is the middle cardiac vein. It drains the back of both ventricles and opens into the coronary sinus near its end, and it is the companion of that artery.

Question 3650. Which vein runs in the atrioventricular groove on the right side of the heart beside the right coronary artery? The answer is the small cardiac vein. It follows the right margin and ends either in the coronary sinus or directly in the right atrium.

Question 3651. Which set of veins crosses the front of the right ventricle and opens directly into the right atrium rather than into the coronary sinus? The answer is the anterior cardiac veins. There are usually two or three of them, and they are therefore not seen in a cast of the coronary sinus.

Question 3652. Which channel carries the largest share of the blood that returns from the muscle of the heart: the anterior cardiac veins, the coronary sinus, the smallest cardiac veins, or the middle cardiac vein? The answer is the coronary sinus. Most of the venous blood from the wall of the heart drains through it into the right atrium.

Question 3653. A described cast of the veins of the heart shows a wide channel lying in the groove between the left atrium and the left ventricle at the back of the heart and opening into the right atrium. Which channel is shown? The answer is the coronary sinus, which lies in the posterior part of the coronary sulcus and is about 3 centimeters long.

Question 3654. Which of these pairs belongs together: the middle cardiac vein with the posterior interventricular groove, the great cardiac vein with the posterior interventricular groove, the small cardiac vein with the anterior interventricular groove, or the coronary sinus with the anterior interventricular groove? The answer is the middle cardiac vein with the posterior interventricular groove. The great cardiac vein runs in the anterior groove instead.

Question 3655. Which two structures open into the right atrium close together, one just above the other, with only one of them carrying blood from the muscle of the heart? The answer is the inferior vena cava and the coronary sinus. Both open into the smooth posterior part of the right atrium.

Question 3656. A constructed illustration: a catheter is passed into the main vein that drains the muscle of the heart, and it is briefly held up at the mouth of the vein by a thin fold of tissue. Which channel is being entered? The answer is the coronary sinus, and the fold is its valve. The fold is a normal structure and may be large in some people.

Question 3657. A described specimen shows a needle passed into a wide vein on the back of the heart and threaded until its tip lies in the right atrium. Which vein was entered? The answer is the coronary sinus, which receives the great cardiac vein and runs from left to right in the posterior part of the coronary sulcus.

Question 3658. True or false: the anterior cardiac veins empty into the coronary sinus. The answer is false. They open directly into the right atrium instead, while the coronary sinus receives the great, the middle and the small cardiac veins along with the posterior vein of the left ventricle.

Question 3659. Which of these veins drains directly into the right atrium without passing through the coronary sinus: the great cardiac vein, the middle cardiac vein, the anterior cardiac veins, or the oblique vein of the left atrium? The answer is the anterior cardiac veins, while the other three reach the right atrium through the coronary sinus.

Question 3660. Which small vein descends over the back of the left atrium and joins the great cardiac vein at the point where that vein becomes the coronary sinus? The answer is the oblique vein of the left atrium. It is the remains of the left superior vena cava of the fetus and may be absent in an adult.

Question 3661. The oblique vein of the left atrium joins the great cardiac vein at a particular point. What does that point mark? The answer is the beginning of the coronary sinus, at the left end of the posterior part of the coronary sulcus. The fold of tissue that accompanies the vein is the remnant of a fetal channel.

Question 3662. Which statement about the smallest cardiac veins is correct: they open directly into the chambers of the heart, they all open into the coronary sinus, they drain only the left ventricle, or they carry most of the venous blood of the heart? The answer is that they open directly into the chambers, mostly into the right atrium and the right ventricle.

Question 3663. A constructed illustration: in a person with a raised pressure on the right side of the heart, a little blood passes from the venous side to the arterial side through tiny channels in the wall of the heart. Which channels are described? The answer is the smallest cardiac veins, which open directly into the chambers. They have no valves, so blood can pass either way.

Question 3664. Which of these pairs belongs together: the small cardiac vein with the right atrioventricular groove, the great cardiac vein with the right atrioventricular groove, the middle cardiac vein with the anterior interventricular groove, or the anterior cardiac veins with the coronary sinus? The answer is the small cardiac vein with the right atrioventricular groove. It runs there with the right coronary artery.

Question 3665. Name two veins that empty into the coronary sinus beside the great cardiac vein, spoken as a list. The answer is the middle cardiac vein and the small cardiac vein. The coronary sinus also receives the posterior vein of the left ventricle and the oblique vein of the left atrium.

Question 3666. A described cast of the veins of the heart shows three channels converging on a single wide trunk that opens into the right atrium, one from the front, one from the back between the ventricles, and one from the lower right border. Which three veins are shown? The answer is the great, the middle and the small cardiac veins, all of which end in the coronary sinus.

Question 3667. Two small folds lie close together in the lower part of the right atrium. Which statement is correct: both guard the opening of the coronary sinus, one guards the coronary sinus and the other the inferior vena cava, one guards the superior vena cava and the other the coronary sinus, or both guard the tricuspid valve? The answer is that one guards the coronary sinus and the other guards the inferior vena cava.

Question 3668. As the coronary sinus runs along the back of the heart, which chamber lies above it and which lies below it? The answer is the left atrium above and the left ventricle below. The sinus lies in the posterior part of the coronary sulcus, and it ends by opening into the right atrium.

Question 3669. Which vein accompanies the right marginal artery along the sharp lower border of the heart: the right marginal vein, the middle cardiac vein, the oblique vein of the left atrium, or the great cardiac vein? The answer is the right marginal vein, which drains into the small cardiac vein or directly into the right atrium.

Question 3670. A described specimen shows a vein lying in the groove between the right atrium and the right ventricle, and another vein crossing the front of the right ventricle to reach the right atrium. Which veins are shown? The answer is the small cardiac vein and an anterior cardiac vein, and the anterior cardiac veins open straight into the right atrium.

Question 3671. Which vein is the longest in the heart: the great cardiac vein, the middle cardiac vein, the small cardiac vein, or the oblique vein of the left atrium? The answer is the great cardiac vein. It begins at the apex and runs the length of the front of the heart before becoming the coronary sinus.

Question 3672. Which statement about the venous drainage of the heart is correct: all of the venous blood passes through the coronary sinus, the anterior cardiac veins drain into the right atrium, the smallest cardiac veins carry most of the blood, or the coronary sinus opens into the left atrium? The answer is that the anterior cardiac veins drain directly into the right atrium. The coronary sinus carries the largest share but not all of it.

Question 3673. What is the name of the tough outer sac that encloses the heart and the roots of the great vessels? The answer is the fibrous pericardium. It is a strong layer of connective tissue that holds the heart in place within the chest, and inside it lies a thin serous layer.

Question 3674. What is the name of the thin serous layer that covers the surface of the heart itself? The answer is the visceral layer of the serous pericardium, also called the epicardium. It forms the outer layer of the heart wall, and the coronary vessels lie in the fat beneath it.

Question 3675. Which part of the pericardium is fused to the central tendon of the diaphragm: the visceral layer, the fibrous pericardium, the parietal serous layer, or the pericardial cavity? The answer is the fibrous pericardium. It is bound to the central tendon below and to the sternum in front by small ligaments.

Question 3676. What is the name of the space between the two layers of the serous pericardium, which in health holds only a thin film of fluid? The answer is the pericardial cavity. Its outer wall is the parietal layer and its inner wall is the visceral layer, and the film of fluid lets the two surfaces glide over one another.

Question 3677. Which nerve runs down the side of the fibrous pericardium between it and the parietal serous layer? The answer is the phrenic nerve, which is accompanied there by the pericardiacophrenic vessels. Because it lies on the pericardium, disease of the pericardium can cause pain felt at the shoulder.

Question 3678. Which artery supplies the fibrous and parietal layers of the pericardium: the pericardiacophrenic artery, a coronary artery, a bronchial artery, or a pulmonary artery? The answer is the pericardiacophrenic artery, a branch of the internal thoracic artery. The visceral layer is supplied instead by the coronary arteries.

Question 3679. A described transverse section of the chest shows a thick sac around the heart, a thin layer closely applied to the heart muscle, and a narrow space between the two. Which layers are shown? The answer is the fibrous pericardium outside, the parietal serous layer within it, and the visceral layer on the heart, with the pericardial cavity between.

Question 3680. The transverse pericardial sinus is a passage within the pericardial cavity. Which structures lie in front of it and which lie behind it? The answer is the ascending aorta and the pulmonary trunk in front, and the superior vena cava behind. A surgeon can pass a finger or a tube through the sinus behind the great arteries.

Question 3681. The oblique pericardial sinus lies behind which chamber of the heart, and which vessels bound it? The answer is the left atrium, bounded by the folds of serous pericardium around the four pulmonary veins and the inferior vena cava. It is a blind recess, and the transverse sinus lies above it.

Question 3682. A constructed illustration: fluid collects quickly in the pericardial cavity after a wound to the chest, and the heart cannot fill because the fibrous sac will not stretch. Which chambers suffer first? The answer is the right atrium and the right ventricle, because they are thin-walled and work at low pressure. Care belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 3683. Which statement about the innervation of the pericardium is correct: the visceral layer is sensitive to pain, the parietal layer is sensitive to pain, both layers are insensitive, or only the visceral layer has a nerve supply? The answer is that the parietal layer is sensitive to pain. It is supplied by the phrenic nerve and by the intercostal nerves.

Question 3684. True or false: the fibrous pericardium is continuous above with the outer coat of the great vessels. The answer is true. It blends with the outer coat of the aorta and the pulmonary trunk as they leave the heart, and below it is fused to the central tendon of the diaphragm.

Question 3685. A described chest radiograph shows the shadow of the heart lying inside a slightly wider shadow that follows the same outline, with a thin dark line of fat on each side. Which structure forms the outer shadow? The answer is the pericardium and the fat within it, and a widened shadow of this kind may mean that fluid has collected.

Question 3686. A constructed illustration: a person with a long-standing illness of the pericardium has the two serous layers stuck together by scar tissue, so that the heart is held in a rigid case. Which layer produces the fluid that normally prevents this? The answer is the serous pericardium. The parietal layer produces the fluid, and the visceral layer and the lymphatics remove it.

Question 3687. To which part of the diaphragm is the fibrous pericardium attached: the muscular part, the central tendon, the left crus, or the right crus? The answer is the central tendon. The attachment is firm, and the fibrous layer is also tied to the sternum by the sternopericardial ligaments.

Question 3688. A described transverse section through the chest shows the heart inside its sac, with a nerve running down each side of the sac just under its outer layer. Which nerve is shown, and what does it supply? The answer is the phrenic nerve, which supplies the diaphragm and lies between the fibrous pericardium and the parietal serous layer.

Question 3689. What is the name of the band of fibrous pericardium that ties the sac to the back of the sternum? The answer is the sternopericardial ligament. There are upper and lower bands, and they help to hold the heart forward in the chest.

Question 3690. Which chamber of the heart lies immediately in front of the esophagus and the descending aorta, with the pericardium between them? The answer is the left atrium. It forms the back of the heart, and that close relation is why an enlarged left atrium can press on the esophagus.

Question 3691. Which of these structures lies immediately outside the fibrous pericardium in the middle mediastinum: the parietal pleura and the lungs, the esophagus, the thoracic duct, or the trachea? The answer is the parietal pleura and the lungs, which lie on each side of the pericardium. Behind it lie the esophagus and the descending aorta.

Question 3692. True or false: because the fibrous pericardium is firmly fixed to the diaphragm and to the great vessels, it can stretch enough to hold a large amount of fluid without compressing the heart. The answer is false. The fibrous layer is strong but it is not elastic, so a rapid collection of fluid can obstruct the filling of the heart.

Question 3693. Which of these pairs belongs together: the fibrous pericardium with the central tendon of the diaphragm, the serous pericardium with the sternum, the pericardial cavity with the pleural cavity, or the epicardium with the fibrous pericardium? The answer is the fibrous pericardium with the central tendon of the diaphragm. The epicardium is the visceral serous layer on the heart.

Question 3694. A transverse plane is passed through the chest at the level of the eighth thoracic vertebra, below the division of the trachea. Which structure lies in the midline in front of the vertebral column and behind the pericardium? The answer is the esophagus, with the descending aorta to its left.

Question 3695. A needle is passed from the left front of the chest toward the pericardial cavity. Which statement about the left lung and its pleura is correct: the lung covers the pericardium everywhere, the left lung has a notch where the pericardium meets the chest wall, the left lung lies behind the pericardium, or the pleura never comes near it? The answer is that the left lung has a notch there, and the pleura still lines its edges.

Question 3696. A described lateral radiograph of the chest shows a dark strip of fat lying in front of the shadow of the heart, just behind the lower sternum. Within which structure does that fat lie? The answer is the pericardium, between its fibrous and serous layers, and the strip is a normal finding.

Question 3697. A constructed illustration: after a wound below the left fifth rib close to the sternum, blood collects in the pericardial cavity and the heart cannot fill. Which two structures lie closest to the wound? The answer is the right ventricle and the pericardium, since the right ventricle forms most of the front of the heart behind the sternum. Care belongs to the treating clinician and the local protocol.

Question 3698. When a person lies on the back, fluid in the pericardial cavity collects in the lowest part of the cavity. Which part is that: the transverse sinus, the oblique sinus, the part behind the sternum, or the part behind the left atrium? The answer is the part behind the left atrium, because the posterior part of the cavity is lowest in that position.

Question 3699. Which statement about the pericardial cavity is correct: it is continuous with the pleural cavity, it normally holds only a thin film of fluid, it lies outside the fibrous pericardium, or it contains the coronary arteries? The answer is that it normally holds only a thin film of fluid. The cavity is a potential space between the parietal and the visceral layers of the serous pericardium.

Question 3700. Which sequence correctly gives the layers crossed by a needle passed from the front of the chest into the pericardial cavity: skin, fibrous pericardium, parietal serous layer, then cavity; skin, parietal serous layer, fibrous pericardium, then cavity; skin, visceral layer, then cavity; or skin, pleural cavity, then cavity? The answer is skin, then fibrous pericardium, then the parietal serous layer, then the cavity.

To summarize, the left coronary artery arises from the left posterior aortic sinus and divides into the anterior interventricular artery, which supplies the front of both ventricles and the front two thirds of the interventricular septum, and the circumflex artery, which runs in the coronary sulcus. The right coronary artery arises from the right sinus, runs in the right atrioventricular groove, and in most people gives the posterior interventricular artery, which supplies the back third of the septum; that arrangement is right dominance. The sinoatrial node is supplied by the right coronary artery in about 60 per cent of people and by the circumflex artery in the rest, while the atrioventricular node is usually supplied by the right coronary artery, so the coronary arteries behave as functional end arteries with small connections at the apex and at the crux of the heart. Most venous blood returns through the great, middle and small cardiac veins into the coronary sinus and so into the right atrium, while the anterior cardiac veins and the smallest cardiac veins open directly into the chambers. The pericardium has a fibrous layer fixed to the central tendon of the diaphragm, and a serous layer whose two parts enclose the pericardial cavity, with the transverse sinus behind the aorta and the pulmonary trunk and the oblique sinus behind the left atrium. The phrenic nerve and the pericardiacophrenic vessels run between the fibrous and parietal layers, which is why pericardial pain is felt at the shoulder, and because the fibrous layer cannot stretch quickly, a rapid collection of fluid can obstruct the filling of the heart.
