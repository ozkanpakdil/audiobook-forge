# Fractures and Dislocations — Chapter Briefs

One brief per chapter. Each chapter follows the contract in PLAN.md; the brief says
only what that chapter is about. Chapters run between three thousand eight hundred
and four thousand four hundred words, appendices between two thousand two hundred
and three thousand two hundred.

The course teaches every injury through the same sequence: assess, stabilize,
examine neurovascular status, immobilize, image, reassess, refer or treat
definitively.

---

### Part — Foundations of Musculoskeletal Trauma

1. `# Chapter 1 — Foundations of Musculoskeletal Trauma`

Module 1.1. The tissues a fracture travels through: bone and its cortex, periosteum, cancellous and cortical bone, the joint and its cartilage, ligaments, tendons, muscle, fascia, and the neurovascular bundle that lies against the bone. Anatomical position, the directional terms, and the planes, because every later chapter describes a displacement in those terms.

2. `# Chapter 2 — Types of Musculoskeletal Injury`

Module 1.2. Fracture, dislocation, subluxation, sprain, strain, crush injury and soft-tissue injury, defined one against the other. What distinguishes a sprain from a strain, a subluxation from a dislocation, and why the distinction changes what the responder does next.

3. `# Chapter 3 — Fracture Classification`

Module 1.3. The vocabulary of description: closed and open, stable and unstable, transverse, oblique, spiral, longitudinal, comminuted, segmental, avulsion, impacted, compression, greenstick, buckle, pathological and stress. Each term is taught as a description that predicts behaviour, not as a label to memorise.

### Part — Initial Assessment of the Trauma Patient

4. `# Chapter 4 — The Primary Survey`

Module 2.1. The primary survey in the injured patient: what is looked for, in what order, and what makes the responder stop and treat before moving on. Why the limb never comes before the airway, and what that means for a patient with an obviously deformed leg.

5. `# Chapter 5 — The Secondary Survey`

Module 2.2. The head-to-toe examination, the history of the event, the exposed and log-rolled patient, and the findings that a fracture produces. How a limb is examined without moving it, and what is written down at each step.

6. `# Chapter 6 — Neurovascular Assessment`

Module 2.3. The examination that this whole course turns on: pulse, color, warmth, capillary refill, sensation and movement, before and after every intervention. The documentation of that examination, why it is recorded on the patient and not only in the notes, and what a change means.

### Part — Immobilization and Patient Handling

7. `# Chapter 7 — Principles of Immobilization`

Module 3.1 and 3.3. What immobilization is for: to prevent movement at the fracture, to protect the vessels and nerves, to reduce pain, and to limit bleeding. The position found, the joints above and below, the padding, and the reasons each rule exists.

8. `# Chapter 8 — Splinting in Practice`

Module 3.2. Splints and the materials a responder actually has: the commercially made splint, the padded board, the sling and swathe, the pillow, the rolled blanket, and the limb bound to its neighbour. What each is good for, what each does badly, and how the neurovascular status is checked after it is applied.

9. `# Chapter 9 — Patient Handling and Transport`

Module 3.4. Moving a patient with an unstable injury: the log roll, the scoop stretcher, the long board, the transfer, the vehicle, and the handover. Why a spine is treated as unstable until it is cleared by someone qualified to clear it, and why no single responder performs a manual handling technique alone.

### Part — Pain, Swelling and Comfort

10. `# Chapter 10 — Pain Assessment and Acute Pain Management`

Module 4.1 and 4.2. How pain is assessed in an injured patient, including the patient who cannot describe it, and the principles of analgesia: who may give it, who may prescribe it, why the dose and the route belong to the local protocol, and why pain relief is part of the treatment of the injury and not a courtesy.

11. `# Chapter 11 — Edema and Swelling Management`

Module 4.3. Why an injured limb swells, what swelling does to the tissues inside a closed fascial compartment, and the measures a responder may take: elevation within the limits of the injury, the removal of constricting rings and bandages, the monitoring of the swelling, and the point at which swelling becomes an emergency.

### Part — The Shoulder Girdle and the Shoulder

12. `# Chapter 12 — Clavicle Fractures`

Module 5.1. The clavicle fracture: how it is recognized, how it is immobilized, what the arm sling and the figure-of-eight bandage do and do not do, and the findings that make it more than a simple fracture — skin tenting, a shortening deformity, and any change in the neurovascular status of the arm.

13. `# Chapter 13 — Scapular Fractures`

Module 5.2. The scapular fracture: usually the product of a high-energy blow, and therefore a marker of injury elsewhere, especially in the chest. Recognition, the examination of the shoulder that must accompany it, immobilization, and the referral that follows.

14. `# Chapter 14 — Shoulder Dislocation`

Module 5.3 and 19.2. The anterior shoulder dislocation: the mechanism, the squared-off outline, the arm held away from the body, the nerve at risk, and the examination that must be recorded before anything is done. Immobilization, imaging, the reduction that belongs to supervised practice, and the reassessment that follows it.

### Part — The Humerus

15. `# Chapter 15 — Proximal Humerus Fractures`

Module 6.1. The proximal humerus fracture: the patient it happens to, the arm held against the body, the swelling and bruising that appear late, the nerve and vessel that run beside the bone, and the sling and swathe that immobilize it while the patient is moved toward definitive care.

16. `# Chapter 16 — Humeral Shaft and Distal Humerus Fractures`

Module 6.2 and 6.3. The shaft fracture and its companion at the distal end: the deformity, the shortening, the radial nerve that runs in the groove against the shaft, the very different consequences of a distal injury, and the immobilization that holds the arm across the body without straightening what is bent.

### Part — The Elbow and the Forearm

17. `# Chapter 17 — Elbow Dislocation`

Module 7.1 and 19.2. The posterior elbow dislocation: the mechanism, the deformity, the pulse and the nerve supply that must be examined before and after, and why the elbow is one of the dislocations in which a delay damages the arm. Immobilization, imaging, and referral.

18. `# Chapter 18 — Radial Head and Olecranon Fractures`

Module 7.2 and 7.3. Two fractures at the same joint with opposite personalities: the radial head fracture, where rotation of the forearm is the finding, and the olecranon fracture, where the triceps pulls the fragment away. Recognition, immobilization, and what each one needs from definitive care.

19. `# Chapter 19 — Forearm Fractures`

Module 7.4. Fractures of the two bones of the forearm, alone and together, and why a fracture of one almost always means injury to the other or to the membrane between them. The deformity, the immobilization, the swelling that must be watched, and the referral that a both-bone injury always needs.

### Part — The Wrist and the Hand

20. `# Chapter 20 — Wrist Fractures`

Module 8.1. The distal radius and the distal ulna: the common fracture of the fall on the outstretched hand, the dinner-fork deformity, the median nerve that lies across the front of the joint, and the examination of sensation in the fingers before and after splinting.

21. `# Chapter 21 — Carpal Dislocations`

Module 8.2. The carpus and its dislocations: the subtle injury that a radiograph taken in one projection alone will miss, the swelling that masks it, the median nerve again, and the reason a suspected carpal dislocation is referred rather than splinted and sent home.

22. `# Chapter 22 — Metacarpal Fractures`

Module 8.3. The metacarpal fractures, including the fracture of the neck of the fifth metacarpal that follows a blow with the fist: the dropped knuckle, the rotational deformity that is judged by looking at the fingers rather than the hand, the splinting position that keeps the hand useful, and the referral for rotation.

23. `# Chapter 23 — Phalangeal Fractures and Finger Dislocations`

Module 8.4 and 8.5. The phalanges and the joints of the fingers: the fracture that looks trivial and the dislocation that is not, the buddy strapping and the splint in the position of function, the open injury over a joint, and the signs that the finger is no longer safe to treat as a minor injury.

### Part — The Pelvis and the Hip

24. `# Chapter 24 — Pelvic Fractures`

Module 9.1. The pelvic fracture as a bleeding injury before it is a bone injury: the mechanism, the pain, the inability to weight-bear, the instability of the pelvic ring, and the examination that must not spring the pelvis repeatedly. Why the pelvis belongs to the primary survey.

25. `# Chapter 25 — Pelvic Trauma Management`

Module 9.2. What is done for the patient with a pelvic injury: the pelvic binder and where it goes, the log roll that does not repeat itself, the prevention of hypothermia, the fluid decisions that belong to the physician, and the transfer to a centre that can treat it.

26. `# Chapter 26 — Hip Fractures and Hip Dislocation`

Module 9.3 and 9.4. The fragility fracture of the hip in an older patient — the shortened, externally rotated leg and the pain on any movement — and the far rarer hip dislocation, which is an emergency for the blood supply of the femoral head. Recognition, handling, and urgent referral for both.

### Part — The Femur

27. `# Chapter 27 — Femoral Shaft Fractures`

Module 10.1. The femoral shaft fracture: the thigh that is swollen and deformed, the blood that is lost inside it, the shortening, and the traction that is applied only by those trained and authorized to apply it. Why this injury is treated as a life-threatening one.

28. `# Chapter 28 — Distal Femur Fractures and Traction`

Module 10.2 and 10.3. The distal femoral fracture, and traction as a principle rather than a technique: what traction is for, what it must never be used for, the skin traction and the skeletal traction that only a qualified clinician applies, and the neurovascular examination that accompanies it.

### Part — The Knee

29. `# Chapter 29 — Patellar Fractures and Knee Dislocation`

Module 11.1 and 11.2. The patella, which can be broken or dislocated, and the knee, which can dislocate and threaten the artery behind it. The two injuries share a joint and share almost nothing else: the first is immobilized and referred, the second is an emergency for the limb.

30. `# Chapter 30 — Tibial Plateau Fractures`

Module 11.3. The tibial plateau: the fracture produced by a blow along the line of the leg, the knee that swells and cannot bear weight, the soft tissues around it that decide what can be done, and the imaging and referral that follow.

### Part — The Leg

31. `# Chapter 31 — Tibial Fractures`

Module 12.1. The tibia, the bone just under the skin: the open injury that follows even a modest blow, the deformity, the swelling, the immobilization that holds the leg in the position found, and the neurovascular examination of the foot.

32. `# Chapter 32 — Fibular and Combined Tibial and Fibular Fractures`

Module 12.2 and 12.3. The fibula, which carries little weight and much importance, because the nerve that moves the foot winds around its neck. Fractures of the fibula alone and of both bones together, and why the both-bone injury is a different problem: the unstable leg that must be immobilized before it is moved.

33. `# Chapter 33 — Acute Compartment Syndrome`

Module 12.4. The emergency this course returns to more than any other. Recognition by pain out of proportion, pain on passive stretch, and a tense compartment; the management principles of releasing the constriction within local protocol, not elevating the limb above the heart, and referring urgently; and the reason the diagnosis is clinical and time is muscle.

### Part — The Ankle

34. `# Chapter 34 — Malleolar Fractures`

Module 13.1. The malleolar fracture: the medial and lateral malleoli, the position of the foot at the moment of injury, the swelling that hides the deformity, and the splinting that supports the ankle in the position found while the neurovascular status of the foot is examined.

35. `# Chapter 35 — Syndesmotic Injury and Ankle Fracture-Dislocation`

Module 13.2 and 13.3. The injury of the ligament between the two bones of the lower leg, which is easy to miss and slow to heal, and the ankle fracture-dislocation, in which the joint is out of place and the skin over it is at risk. Recognition, immobilization, and the urgency of referral.

### Part — The Foot

36. `# Chapter 36 — Calcaneal Fractures`

Module 14.1. The calcaneus: the fracture of the fall from a height, the heel that is swollen, widened and painful, the spine that must be examined in the same patient because the same fall broke both, and the elevation and referral that follow.

37. `# Chapter 37 — Talar and Midfoot Injuries`

Module 14.2 and 14.3. The talus and the midfoot: the bone that has almost no blood supply of its own and the joints that must line up for the foot to work. The subtle findings, the difficulty of examining a swollen foot, and the imaging and referral these injuries always need.

38. `# Chapter 38 — Metatarsal and Toe Injuries`

Module 14.4 and 14.5. The metatarsal fractures, including the stress fracture of the marched foot, and the injuries of the toes. What may be managed with a stiff-soled shoe and strapping, what must be referred, and how the circulation of the toe is examined when it is strapped to its neighbour.

### Part — The Spine

39. `# Chapter 39 — Cervical Spine Injuries`

Module 15.1. The cervical spine: the mechanisms that should make any responder suspect it, the manual stabilization of the head, the collar and the board that only a trained team applies, and the important injuries — the fracture of the second vertebra, the injury of the older patient's already narrow canal, and the cord injury that may be present with no bone injury at all.

40. `# Chapter 40 — Thoracic and Lumbar Spine Injuries`

Module 15.2 and 15.3. The thoracic and lumbar spine: the fracture that follows a fall from height or a flexion injury, the tenderness and the step in the line of the spines, the burst fracture, and the handling that keeps the spine in one line from the moment of the injury to the moment it is cleared.

41. `# Chapter 41 — Spinal Neurological Assessment`

Module 15.4. The neurological examination of a patient with a suspected spinal injury: movement and sensation in each limb, the level of any loss, the sacral sparing that separates a complete from an incomplete injury, and the repeated examination that notices deterioration before it becomes permanent.

### Part — The Child

42. `# Chapter 42 — The Child's Skeleton`

Module 16.1. Why a child's skeleton breaks differently: the growth plate, the thicker and more active periosteum, the softer bone, the greater remodeling, and the consequences of all four for recognition, for healing, and for the future of the limb.

43. `# Chapter 43 — Common Pediatric Fractures`

Module 16.2. The fractures a responder meets in a child: the supracondylar fracture of the elbow, the forearm fracture, the clavicle, the pulled elbow, and the toddler's fracture of the tibia. What each looks like, and which of them threatens the limb.

44. `# Chapter 44 — Growth Plate Injuries`

Module 16.3 and 16.4. The Salter-Harris classification taught as a description of what happens to the plate rather than as a set of numbers, the special considerations of the child with an open injury, the child who cannot describe the pain, and the non-accidental injury that must be considered when the story and the injury do not match.

### Part — The Older Adult

45. `# Chapter 45 — Osteoporosis and the Fragile Skeleton`

Module 17.1. Osteoporosis: what it does to bone, who has it without knowing, why the first sign is often the fracture, and what it means for the forces needed to break a bone, for the imaging that may miss the injury, and for the way a patient is handled.

46. `# Chapter 46 — Fragility Fractures in Older Adults`

Module 17.2 and 17.3. The fractures of the frail: the hip, the vertebra, the wrist, the pelvis and the humerus, and the special considerations that surround them — medication that changes the response to injury, the thin skin that tears, the confusion that may be the only sign, and the early mobilization and pain control that determine whether the patient walks again.

### Part — The Open Fracture

47. `# Chapter 47 — Recognizing the Open Fracture`

Module 18.1. The fracture that has communicated with the outside: the wound, the contamination, the destroyed soft tissue, and the small puncture that hides a serious injury. Why an open fracture over a joint, or on a farm, or in a patient who waited, is a different problem.

48. `# Chapter 48 — Initial Management of the Open Fracture`

Module 18.2 and 18.3. What is done and what is never done: control of the hemorrhage that can be controlled, the sterile covering, the neurovascular examination, the immobilization, the antibiotics and tetanus protection that follow local protocol, and the urgent orthopedic referral. No blind exploration, no manipulation of exposed bone, no closure outside the operating theatre, and no delay.

### Part — Dislocations and Reduction

49. `# Chapter 49 — Principles of Dislocation Management`

Module 19.1. The ten steps the source lays out, written as ordered prose: assess the patient, assess the neurovascular status, look for an associated fracture, provide analgesia, image when indicated, decide whether urgent reduction is needed, reduce only within scope of practice, reassess, immobilize, and image again afterwards.

50. `# Chapter 50 — Major Dislocations`

Module 19.2. The joints that dislocate and what each one costs: shoulder, elbow, wrist, fingers, hip, knee, patella and ankle. Which of them threaten the limb within hours, which threaten a nerve, and which may wait for definitive care.

51. `# Chapter 51 — Principles of Reduction`

Module 20.1. Reduction as a principle rather than a procedure: understanding the anatomy, identifying the contraindications and the associated fractures, assessing the neurovascular status, providing analgesia and sedation where that is authorized, using controlled technique, avoiding excessive force, stopping when there is resistance, and reassessing afterwards. The technique itself belongs to supervised training.

52. `# Chapter 52 — When Not to Attempt Reduction`

Module 20.2. The contraindications, each with its reason: suspected complex fracture-dislocation, entrapped fracture fragments, significant neurovascular compromise needing specialist intervention, an open injury, an unclear diagnosis, inadequate training or equipment, and a previous failed attempt.

### Part — Complications

53. `# Chapter 53 — Immediate Complications`

Module 21.1. What goes wrong in the first minutes and hours: hemorrhage, the neurovascular injury that accompanies the fracture, the compartment syndrome that is already developing, and the damage done by the rescue itself.

54. `# Chapter 54 — Early Complications`

Module 21.2. What goes wrong in the first days: compartment syndrome declared, infection, the lung and clotting complications of immobility, the pressure injury of a splint that was too tight or too long, and the loss of position in a splint that was not checked.

55. `# Chapter 55 — Late Complications`

Module 21.3. What goes wrong over weeks and months: the bone that does not unite, the bone that unites crooked, the joint that stiffens, the pain that persists, the bone that dies, and the growth disturbance in a child. Why the follow-up appointment is part of the treatment.

### Part — Imaging

56. `# Chapter 56 — Plain Radiography`

Module 22.1. The radiograph: what is asked for, the two projections that see around a bone, what a fracture looks like on film, what a dislocation looks like, the occult fracture that is invisible at first, and the joint above and below that must always be included.

57. `# Chapter 57 — Computed Tomography, Magnetic Resonance Imaging and Ultrasound`

Module 22.2 to 22.4. The other three examinations: what computed tomography adds about a complex fracture, what magnetic resonance imaging shows about ligament and bone that is hidden, and what ultrasound can and cannot be trusted for in the injured limb. What each one costs the patient in time and transport.

### Part — Definitive Treatment and Rehabilitation

58. `# Chapter 58 — Conservative Treatment`

Module 23.1. Treatment without an operation: the cast, the brace, the splint and the boot, the traction that is still used in some settings, the follow-up radiograph that checks position, and the instructions about weight-bearing that a patient will misunderstand unless they are said plainly.

59. `# Chapter 59 — Surgical Treatment`

Module 23.2. What an operation does for a fracture: the reduction held with plates and screws, the nail inside the bone, the external fixator outside it, the joint replaced, and the wounds and dressings that a responder may later meet. Why the decision belongs to the orthopedic surgeon and to the patient.

60. `# Chapter 60 — Rehabilitation`

Module 23.3. The part of treatment that decides whether the limb works: early movement of the joints that were not injured, the maintenance of muscle, the management of swelling and pain, the physiotherapist's programme, and the honest conversation about how long recovery takes.

### Part — Emergency Scenarios

61. `# Chapter 61 — Scenarios of the Road, the Home and the Workplace`

Module 24, first group. Constructed scenarios — the motorcyclist, the pedestrian, the fall on the stairs, the hand caught in machinery — walked through the sequence of assess, stabilize, examine, immobilize, image, reassess and refer, with the reasoning at each step spoken aloud. Every scenario is an illustration and is described as one.

62. `# Chapter 62 — Scenarios of the Fall, the Crush and the Sporting Injury`

Module 24, second group. Constructed scenarios: the fall from a roof, the crush injury of an industrial accident, the twisted knee and the dislocated shoulder of sport. Each one turns on a decision, and the chapter shows what happens when the decision is right and what happens when it is made too late.

63. `# Chapter 63 — Scenarios of the Child, the Older Adult and the Open Injury`

Module 24, third group. Constructed scenarios in the patients this course is most afraid of getting wrong: the child with a swollen elbow, the frailer patient with hip pain and no fall, and the open fracture that looked like a scratch. Each is an illustration.

### Part — Skills, Decisions and Communication

64. `# Chapter 64 — The Practical Skills Laboratory`

Module 25. The skills that must be practised on a manikin or under supervision and cannot be learned from a book: the assessment of the injured limb, the application of the splint, the sling and the swathe, the log roll, the use of the stretcher, and the repeated neurovascular examination. What each skill is for, how it is assessed, and what the learner must never practise on a patient without supervision.

65. `# Chapter 65 — The Clinical Decision-Making Framework`

Module 26. The thirteen questions the source poses, written out as a framework that can be run in the head at the roadside: what happened, what is injured, is the patient stable, is there a life-threatening problem, is there a limb-threatening problem, is there a neurological injury, is the fracture open, is the injury unstable, does it require reduction, how should it be immobilized, what imaging is required, where should the patient go, and what needs to be reassessed.

66. `# Chapter 66 — Documentation, Communication and Assessment`

Module 27 and 28. The handover that carries the neurovascular findings from the roadside to the operating theatre, the record that protects the patient and the responder, and the way the written and practical assessments of this course are meant to be used. Closes the course by returning to the core principle.

### Appendices

`# Appendix A — Practical Emergency Algorithms`

The emergency pathways of the course written as ordered prose that can be followed by ear: the unstable patient, the threatened limb, the suspected compartment syndrome, the open fracture, the suspected spinal injury, and the dislocated joint. Each algorithm names the finding that starts it, the steps in order, the point at which the responder stops and escalates, and the scope of practice of each step.

`# Appendix B — Practical Skills Laboratory Checklist`

The skills of the course as a checklist for supervised practice, spoken as prose: how the examination of an injured limb is assessed, how splint application is assessed, how the log roll and the transfer are assessed, and how the neurovascular examination is assessed before and after. Each item states what the learner must demonstrate and what counts as unsafe.

`# Appendix C — Quick-Reference Fracture Card`

A reference to be listened to before or after an encounter rather than during one: for each region, the findings that identify the injury, the immediate management, the neurovascular structures at risk, and the urgency of referral. Written as prose a responder can hold in mind, with the reminder that it never replaces the protocol of the service.

`# Appendix D — The Core Principle and Scope of Practice`

The course's closing statement: that the aim is not to make the injury look normal but to protect the patient from further harm, to recognize what threatens life and limb, and to move the patient toward the care that can finish the job. Restates the scope of practice rules, the prohibition on doses and techniques learned from a book, and the educational disclaimer in full.
