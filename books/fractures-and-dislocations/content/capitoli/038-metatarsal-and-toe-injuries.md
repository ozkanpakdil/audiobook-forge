# Chapter 38 — Metatarsal and Toe Injuries

The metatarsals and the toes are the part of the skeleton that meets the ground first, and they are also the part most often dismissed as too small to matter. Five long bones carry the weight of the whole body into the floor at every step, and the toes finish that movement and push the body on. When one of these bones breaks, the shoe no longer fits and the step becomes painful, so the patient loses more than a fracture. Every chapter of this course follows one order, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. What follows is educational material, and it does not replace supervised training, certification, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

The five metatarsals are numbered from the inner side of the foot outward, and each has a base at the midfoot, a shaft, and a head that meets the first bone of a toe. The first is the thickest and the shortest, and with two small sesamoid bones beneath its head it carries most of the body weight through the great toe. The second is the longest and is set back into a notch between the bones of the midfoot, while the third and fourth are more free to move. The fifth ends in a broad base where a tendon from the leg attaches. The junction of the shaft of the fifth metatarsal with its base is supplied by a small branch that enters there and nowhere else. A break there can therefore leave part of the fracture without blood, which is why this bone has a reputation for failing to heal.

The toes contain the phalanges, and their number differs between the great toe and the rest, with two in the great toe and three in each of the others. The great toe carries the load at the end of the step, so its joint must stay straight and in line, while the lesser toes balance the sole and spread its pressure. The nails protect the tips of the toes and the tissue beneath them, and the nail bed must heal flat and smooth if the nail is to grow without a ridge or a split. Nothing in a toe is large, and that is precisely why an injury there is underestimated by the patient and by the examiner alike.

Injuries of the metatarsals and the toes come from two kinds of force, and telling them apart is part of the assessment. A direct blow breaks the bone under the point that was struck, as when an object is dropped on the foot, a foot is crushed under a wheel, or a bare foot strikes a hard edge. These injuries often involve more than one bone, and the swelling may become the more serious part of the injury. An indirect force arrives through the foot rather than against it, as a twist, an awkward landing or a repetitive load, and it usually breaks a single bone at a place the examiner can predict. A twist of the ankle that pulls on a tendon can tear a small piece from the base of the fifth metatarsal, while the same twist often sprains the ligaments beside it, so the examiner who finds tenderness there also feels the bone below it.

Repetitive load without a single moment of injury produces the stress fracture, the injury this chapter knows as the fracture of the marched foot. A person who marches, runs, dances or stands for long hours in boots that are new or worn thin loads the same small segment of bone thousands of times, and bone that is tired faster than it can repair itself develops a fine crack. The second and third metatarsals are the usual sites, because they are the longest and the least mobile of the five. The pain begins as a dull ache in the ball of the foot that appears with activity and fades with rest, and at this stage the foot looks almost normal. If the load continues, the crack can extend across the bone and the injury becomes a complete fracture, so an ache that returns with every march deserves an examination rather than a new pair of socks.

Assessment of the foot begins with the patient's own account, and that account is worth more than any single sign. The primary survey is completed first, in the order remembered as ABCDE, which stands for airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure, and only then does the assessment turn to the limb. The time of the injury, the force behind it, the position of the foot at impact, and whether the patient has been able to walk on it since are the first questions. The examiner also asks about the days before the injury, because a stress fracture has no single moment to report, and about diabetes, loss of sensation in the feet, poor circulation, and medicines that thin the bone. The lay first aider reports what was seen and heard and when, the trained responder adds what was found on examination, and the diagnosis belongs to the clinician.

The local examination looks at the foot before it touches it, and it looks at both feet side by side. The examiner notes the position of the toes, any unnatural bend, a toe that sits above or below its neighbor, a great toe that no longer points straight, and any wound or break in the skin. Swelling appears quickly over the top of the foot and around the base of the fifth metatarsal, and it can hide the shape of the bones within a short time of the injury. Bruising may appear late, and blood that has traveled along the sole or around the heel suggests that the injury reaches deeper than the skin shows. The examiner asks whether the shoe of the injured foot has become tight, because a foot swelling inside a shoe is a foot whose circulation must be checked.

Palpation follows a fixed order so that nothing is skipped, and it begins away from the place the patient points to. The examiner feels along each metatarsal from its base to its head, then presses gently across the row of metatarsals at once, because a crack in a shaft often hurts when the whole row is squeezed. The base of the fifth metatarsal is felt on its own, because a fracture there follows a twisted ankle closely and is mistaken for a sprain of the ligaments beside it. The examiner does not test the stability of a toe by pulling on it and does not move a toe that is clearly deformed, because both are clinical acts that can add damage. A patient who can walk does not thereby exclude a fracture, since many people walk on a broken toe for days before they seek help. When the patient presents days after the injury, the swelling has softened and the foot may look ordinary, and feeling along the bone belongs to the trained responder or to the clinician.

Stabilization of an injured foot in the field is a small set of acts, and the most important of them is restraint. The foot is supported in the position in which it was found, and a deformed toe or a displaced metatarsal is never straightened in the field, because the vessels and the nerves lie close against the bone. Pulling a toe straight can turn a closed injury into an open one and can release the pressure that was holding a torn vessel closed. The lay first aider supports the foot with the hands, keeps the patient warm, and waits for help, while a shoe that has become tight as the foot swells is cut away by someone authorized to do it. The trained responder and the paramedic may apply a splint within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, and because cold is a complication of trauma, the foot is insulated from the ground.

The neurovascular examination of the foot is performed before any splint or strap is applied, repeated after every intervention, and recorded in words. The examiner feels for the pulse on the top of the foot and for the pulse behind the inner ankle, and remembers that in some healthy people one of those pulses cannot be felt at all. The color and the warmth of the skin are noted, and the examiner presses briefly on a nail and watches how quickly the color returns. Sensation is tested in the web of skin between the toes and along both borders of the foot, and the patient is asked to describe numbness or tingling in ordinary words. Movement is tested by asking the patient to spread the toes, to curl them and to bend the great toe upward, and any movement lost since a moment earlier is recorded at once. Numbness, tingling, a foot that has become cold and pale, and pain far greater than the injury explains are the findings that stop the examination and send the patient for urgent assessment.

A lesser toe that must be held still is often strapped to the toe beside it, and the neighbor then acts as a splint, which means that two toes depend on one piece of tape. The examiner records the color, the warmth, the capillary refill and the sensation of the injured toe and of its neighbor before the strap is placed, and again afterwards. A finding that was not written before the tape went on cannot be attributed to the tape afterwards. The strap is placed around the middle of the toe rather than across a joint, so that the joint can still bend, and a small pad is placed between the two toes where skin would otherwise press on skin. The tape is never wrapped so tightly that it leaves a groove in the skin or stops the flow through the small vessels, and the tip of every strapped toe is left visible. Strapping is used only on a toe that has been assessed as stable, because a toe that is rotated or badly displaced is held in the wrong position by a strap and then heals there. A trained first responder, an emergency medical technician, a paramedic or a nurse applies the strap only within what the local protocol allows, and the toe is examined by a clinician when there is any doubt about it.

The circulation of a strapped toe is examined by looking, by feeling and by asking, in that order. The examiner looks first at the color of the tip and of the nail, comparing the strapped toe with the same toe on the other foot and with its neighbor. The neighbor is now part of the same dressing and can be harmed by it. The nail is pressed briefly, and the time the color takes to return is compared between the two toes, while the skin is felt for warmth with the back of the examiner's hand. The patient is asked whether either toe feels numb, tingling, painful or different from before the strap was applied. If either toe is pale, blue or cold, or cannot be felt, the strap is released by someone authorized to do it within the limits of the local protocol and the patient is referred urgently. A toe lost to a piece of tape is a complication caused by treatment, and the great toe is generally not strapped to its neighbor, because it is wide and carries the weight at the end of the step.

Immobilization of the forefoot has one purpose, which is to stop the metatarsals and the toes from bending while the bone heals, because bending keeps a crack open and keeps it painful. The device most often used is a shoe with a stiff sole that does not bend at the ball of the foot, so that the metatarsals are protected from the force of the step. A walking boot, a rigid sandal or a plaster shoe may be chosen instead, and practice differs between countries and clinics. The physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides which device suits a particular fracture, while the nurse or the trained responder may fit the device and teach its use within the local protocol. Casting, which means molding a rigid shell around the foot and the ankle, is learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and it is never taught from a book, because a cast applied too tightly can cause the very complication it was meant to prevent. Whatever is chosen is checked for fit and for pressure over the bony points, and the skin beneath it is inspected regularly.

Imaging confirms what the examination has already suggested, and it is ordered by a clinician trained to interpret it. A radiograph is usually the first study, taken in at least two directions and including the joint above and the joint below the injury. Views taken while the patient stands and puts weight through the foot may show a widening that a picture taken lying down cannot show, and they are taken only when the clinician judges them safe. Computed tomography shows the detail of a complex fracture, particularly one that enters a joint, while magnetic resonance imaging can find a stress fracture before it is visible on a radiograph. The first radiograph of a stress fracture is often reported as normal, and a repeat study after a period of protected weight bearing may show the new bone, so that period is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. The lay first aider never orders or interprets imaging, the trained responder does not decide which study is needed, and an image is only a picture of one moment.

Many injuries of the metatarsals and the toes are treated without an operation, and the two devices that do most of that work are the stiff-soled shoe and the strap. A single crack through the shaft of the second, third or fourth metatarsal that has not moved is usually held in a stiff-soled shoe while the bone heals. A fracture at the base of the fifth metatarsal that was pulled off by a tendon, and in which the fragment has not separated widely, is often treated in the same way. A simple fracture of a lesser toe, and a toe that a clinician has put back into place and that is sitting straight, may be held with strapping to the next toe and a stiff sole. Protected weight bearing, rest from the activity that caused the injury, and a follow-up appointment are part of the treatment rather than advice added at the end. The decision about which injury belongs in a shoe and which belongs in an operating room belongs to the clinician, and the responder's part is to immobilize in the position found and to arrange the assessment.

Some fractures of the metatarsals must be referred, and each reason explains a rule that would otherwise sound arbitrary. A fracture of the first metatarsal is referred because this bone carries the weight of the step together with the great toe and forms part of the arch, so a break that heals out of line changes the way the whole foot accepts load. A fracture of the fifth metatarsal at the junction of the shaft and the base is referred because the blood supply enters there and only there, so the bone may fail to heal. Several metatarsals broken at once, a fracture that has moved, a fracture that enters a joint, a fracture with a wound near it, and a foot crushed so badly that the swelling is tense and spreading are all injuries for the orthopedic surgeon. A fracture in a patient whose bone is weakened by disease or by a medicine is referred with a lower threshold, because the usual signs of trouble may never appear. Children are referred as well, because the ends of these bones contain growth regions that can be injured without a visible break and can later disturb the shape of the foot.

The toes bring their own list of injuries that have stopped being minor. A fracture of the great toe that enters its joint or that has moved, a dislocation of any toe, and a fracture that has a wound over it are referred rather than strapped. A nail torn from its bed, a nail bed that has been cut, and blood collected beneath a nail that lies over a fracture are injuries that need a clinician, because a nail bed that does not heal flat leaves a nail that grows in two pieces. The examiner does not pull off a partly detached nail and does not probe beneath it. A crush injury of the toes, an injury in which the skin is broken over a joint, and an injury in which the patient cannot feel or move a toe are also referred urgently. Any toe injury in a patient with diabetes or loss of sensation is treated as serious from the first moment, and when the diagnosis is unclear the patient is referred rather than reassured.

A dislocated toe is a joint whose surfaces are no longer in contact, and the toe may look shortened, bent to one side or crossed over its neighbor. The responder supports the toe in the position in which it was found, straps it only if a clinician has judged it stable, and never pulls it back into place. Reduction, which restores the joint surfaces to their normal position, is performed by a clinician according to the local protocol, and the technique itself belongs to supervised clinical training. It is not attempted when a complex fracture with a dislocation is suspected, when a fragment of bone is caught in the joint, when the vessels or the nerves are failing and a specialist is needed, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, when the responder lacks the training or the equipment, or when an earlier attempt has already failed. In each of those situations an attempt can turn an injury that would have recovered into one that cannot, and force is never a technique. After a toe has been reduced, the clinician decides whether strapping and a stiff sole will hold it or whether a surgical device is needed.

An open injury of the foot deserves particular care, because the skin here is thin and the bones lie close beneath it, so a modest wound can expose bone. An open fracture is contaminated even when it looks clean, because the organisms on the skin reach the bone and the deep tissues at the moment of injury. The part played by the responder is limited and precise, which is to cover the wound with a sterile dressing, to control the bleeding that can be controlled, to record the neurovascular status, to immobilize the foot in the position found, and to arrange urgent transfer. An embedded object is never removed and exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, because the object may be holding pressure on a torn vessel. A wound is never explored blindly, and definitive cleaning and closure belong to the operating room and to nobody else. Antibiotics, tetanus protection and pain relief are decisions of the clinician that follow the local protocol, and the substance, the route and the amount belong to the prescriber.

Reassessment is performed after every intervention, and it is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the record, and the strap, the shoe and the splint are each checked for tightness while the foot is checked for color, warmth, pulse, sensation, movement and pain. Pain that increases instead of settling, pain out of proportion to the injury, numbness, tingling, weakness, and a foot that becomes tense and shiny are the findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation. The foot is divided into several closed compartments by sheets of fibrous tissue, and bleeding or swelling inside one of them raises the pressure against the muscles, the nerves and the small vessels, so the earliest dependable sign is pain that grows worse when the toes are gently moved by someone else, and an intact pulse does not exclude it. The constricting strap, bandage, shoe or cast is released by someone authorized to do so within the limits of the local protocol, the foot is not elevated above the level of the heart, and the patient is kept warm and referred urgently, because time is muscle.

Some patients deserve a lower threshold for imaging, immobilization and referral, and the reason lies in what their feet cannot tell them. A child's bones contain growth regions near the ends, and an injury there may be invisible on a radiograph while it can still disturb growth, so a child who refuses to walk is treated with great caution. An older adult whose bone has lost density may break a metatarsal in a fall from standing height, and the fracture may hurt far less than its severity suggests. A patient with diabetes, with poor circulation or with loss of sensation in the feet may not feel the injury at all, and a foot that is warm, swollen and painless in such a patient is an emergency that needs a clinician. The same caution applies to a patient who cannot return for follow-up, because the safety of treatment in a stiff-soled shoe depends on someone looking at the foot again. The response is not a different sequence but a lower threshold for immobilization, for imaging and for referral, while the decisions themselves belong to the clinician.

Advice at the end of the visit is part of the treatment, and the patient is told how long the stiff sole is to be worn, a period to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. The patient is told to keep the shoe dry, to check the skin under it, and to return if it becomes loose or if the skin becomes red or painful. The person who marches is warned that a return to full training too early can reopen a stress fracture, and that the return is guided by the clinician. Some complications are common enough to be expected rather than surprising, and a fracture of the fifth metatarsal may fail to heal, so that the pain over the outer border of the foot never settles. A toe or a metatarsal that heals in the wrong position leaves a toe crossing over its neighbor or a shoe that rubs in a new place. Post-traumatic arthritis produces pain and stiffness that appear gradually, infection may follow any open injury, and in a foot that has lost its sensation a poorly fitting shoe can produce a pressure sore that becomes an ulcer.

The record and the handover are part of the treatment rather than a formality that follows it. The time of the injury, the force behind it, the position in which the foot was found, the state of the skin, and the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention are written or spoken in a fixed order. The record also names the device that was applied, whether it was a strap around two toes, a stiff-soled shoe, a splint or a dressing, and who applied it. An injury of the spine is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has done so, and no lone manual handling technique is safe, so a patient whose foot was injured in a fall is moved by a trained team. Everything described in this chapter is learned with a teacher and certified by an authority that also defines what may be done without supervision, and any period or threshold named here is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

To summarize, the metatarsals carry the weight of the step and the toes finish that step, so a small break there changes the way a person walks and can cost months of pain and altered gait. The findings to keep are swelling over the top of the foot, bruising that appears late, tenderness on one spot along a metatarsal, tenderness at the base of the fifth metatarsal after a twisted ankle, and the dull ache in the ball of the foot that comes with marching. The sequence is always the same, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status and record it before and after every intervention, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. A deformed toe or foot is splinted in the position found and is never straightened in the field, force is not a technique, an embedded object is never removed, exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, and an open wound is never explored blindly. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and reduction is not attempted when the joint is shattered, when a fragment is caught in it, when the vessels or the nerves are failing, when the injury is open, or when the diagnosis is unclear. A strapped toe is examined together with the neighbor it is strapped to, and the constricting strap or shoe is released within the limits of the local protocol when a toe becomes pale, cold or numb, when pain increases, or when the foot becomes tense and shiny. The foot is not elevated above the level of the heart, the patient is kept warm and referred urgently, and the first metatarsal, the fifth metatarsal at the junction of the shaft and the base, and every open or crushed injury go to the orthopedic surgeon. This chapter is educational material, and it does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.
