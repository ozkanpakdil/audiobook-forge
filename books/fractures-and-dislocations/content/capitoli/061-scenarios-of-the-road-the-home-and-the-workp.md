# Chapter 61 — Scenarios of the Road, the Home and the Workplace

Most of the fractures and dislocations that fill emergency departments begin in an ordinary place at an ordinary hour, on a road at dusk, on a staircase in a familiar house, or on the floor of a familiar workshop. The three settings are different, yet the injury is not, because bone fails at the same load wherever the energy arrives, and what changes is the scene, the hazards, the number of patients and the help available. This chapter walks through four constructed scenarios, which are illustrations rather than descriptions of real patients, and it speaks the reasoning at each step, because a rule without a reason is forgotten and then broken. Every scenario is followed through the same order that this course uses everywhere, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

Safety comes before assessment and before everything else, because a responder who becomes a second patient has helped nobody. The lay first aider stops at the edge of the scene, looks for moving traffic, spilled fuel, broken glass, live electricity and unstable machinery, and calls for help before moving closer. The trained first responder and the emergency medical technician wear the protective equipment their service provides and position a vehicle to shield the scene when the local protocol allows it. The mechanism of injury is read from the scene rather than guessed, from where the patient was found, what the patient struck and whether a belt or a helmet was worn, because energy tells the examiner where to look for the injury that is not yet visible. Nobody at any level straightens a deformed limb, removes an embedded object, or pushes exposed bone back into a wound, because each of those acts can convert an injury that would have healed into one that cannot.

The first scenario is a constructed illustration of a motorcyclist who has left his machine on a wet road and is lying on his side near the curb, still wearing his helmet. The lay first aider does not lift the head, does not roll the patient and does not remove the helmet, because the head and the neck are treated as injured until someone qualified to clear them has done so. The trained responder manages the airway with the maneuvers and the equipment the service authorizes, and if the airway cannot be kept open with the helmet in place, the decision to remove it belongs to that trained level and the local protocol, not to a bystander. Breathing and circulation are checked next, and catastrophic bleeding from a torn vessel is controlled at once by direct pressure, with a tourniquet used only by a responder trained and authorized to use one. The patient is kept warm from the first minute, because a person lying on wet asphalt loses heat quickly, and the neck is supported in the position found while help is organized. Only when the patient is stable enough does the assessment turn to the limbs.

The thigh is swollen, and the leg lies at an angle that the roadside rather than the patient's anatomy has decided, so the limb is assessed by looking and by gentle feeling and never by pulling it straight. The lay first aider supports the limb in the position found, with the hands, with a rolled blanket, or with whatever is at hand, because a movement that looks helpful can tear a vessel or a nerve that is still intact. The trained responder and the paramedic may apply a splint within the procedures their service authorizes, and a splint holds the joint above and the joint below the fracture rather than the fracture alone. Traction splints are used for a break of the thigh bone in some services and not in others, so their use follows the local protocol instead of a rule from a book. Before any of this is done, the neurovascular status is examined and written down, and it is examined again afterwards, because a finding that was not recorded before a splint was applied cannot be attributed afterwards. What is recorded is the color and the warmth of the foot, the pulse beyond the injury, the ability to move the toes, the sensation on the top of the foot and the sole, and the pain the patient reports.

A break of the thigh bone bleeds into the soft tissues around it, so a patient who is pale and restless and whose pulse is rising is treated as a patient who is losing blood even when the skin is unbroken. The trained responder and the paramedic monitor the breathing, the pulse and the level of consciousness within the procedures their service authorizes and prepare for urgent transfer, while the lay first aider keeps the patient still, warm and reassured. The physician in the receiving facility decides what imaging is required, and the usual first study is a radiograph taken in at least two directions, with the joint above and the joint below included so that a second injury is not hidden. Computed tomography is used when the shape of the break or an injury to the trunk needs more detail, and magnetic resonance imaging is reserved for the soft tissues, when the answer it gives will change what is done. Reassessment is performed after every intervention and repeats the same observations in the same order, because the comparison with the earlier record is what carries the information. Definitive treatment of that thigh bone, whether it is held with a rod, a plate or a frame, belongs to the orthopedic surgeon in an operating room, and reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol rather than from a book. A transport time or a pressure belongs to the local service, and any such figure must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because it differs between countries and editions.

The second scenario is a constructed illustration of a pedestrian struck by a car while crossing a street, and her injuries follow the direction of the energy rather than the place where the bumper touched her. A pedestrian struck from one side often has an injury to that leg, an injury to the pelvis or the chest, and a head injury from the secondary impact with the road, so the examiner looks at the whole patient rather than at the obvious wound. The primary survey comes first, and its steps are remembered as ABCDE, meaning airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure, and each step is completed before the next begins, because a problem in the airway makes every later finding irrelevant. The lay first aider keeps the patient still, speaks to her to judge whether she is awake and answering sensibly, and covers her against the cold while someone calls for help. The trained responder examines the pelvis once and gently, because repeated springing of a broken pelvis can restart bleeding, and a pelvic binder is applied only where the local protocol authorizes a trained responder to apply one. An arm or a leg that is shortened and rotated may indicate a break in the thigh bone or the hip, and it is splinted as found, without any attempt to restore its length.

Blood lost into the pelvis or the abdomen can be considerable without a wound to be seen, so the findings that matter are those of the whole patient, which are a pulse that becomes faster and weaker, skin that becomes pale and cool, and a level of consciousness that drifts. The lay first aider reports those changes as they are observed instead of judging them, and the trained responder measures and records them within the procedures the service authorizes. A patient with a suspected injury of the spine is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has done so, and no lone manual handling technique is safe, so movement happens only with a trained team and the precautions the local protocol requires, and only when movement is necessary. The physician orders the imaging and interprets it, and the receiving team repeats the examination, because a patient who was talking a minute ago and is quiet now has given the most important finding of the assessment. The responder also passes on the mechanism, the position in which the patient was found and the time of the injury, because the next team should continue a story instead of starting a new one. Pain relief is decided by the prescriber and given according to the local protocol, and this course gives no substance, no amount and no route, because a listener must not be able to take a prescription from a book.

The third scenario is a constructed illustration of an older woman who misses the last step of the staircase in her own house and is found by her daughter sitting on the floor and unable to stand. The home is the most common place for a fracture in an older adult, and the height of the fall is a poor guide to the severity of the injury, because bone that has lost density can break in a fall from standing. The lay first aider does not try to lift her into a chair, because a hip that has broken can be displaced further by that effort, and because the effort also places the helper's own back at risk. The trained responder assesses the scene, asks what happened and when, and completes the primary survey before turning to the limb, remembering that a patient who is confused after a fall may have struck her head. Pain in the groin, a leg that lies shortened and turned outward, and an inability to bear weight all suggest a break in the hip, but the sign may be absent or slight, and an older patient with a painful knee after a fall deserves the same caution as one with an obviously deformed hip. The wrist and the shoulder are examined as well, because a patient who puts out a hand to break a fall commonly injures both the hip and the wrist.

The examination of the injured limb begins with the neurovascular status, which is recorded before anything is applied and repeated after everything is applied, and that record is the reason the examiner can tell later whether a change was caused by the treatment. The limb is supported in the position found, with a pillow under the leg and a rolled blanket at either side, so that it is held without being straightened, and only a responder trained and authorized by the service applies a splint. The physician decides which radiograph is needed and whether an image of the chest or the pelvis is also required, because a fall in an older patient can injure more than the bone that hurts most. Reassessment follows each step, and the findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation are pain that increases rather than settles, pain out of proportion to the injury, numbness, tingling, weakness, and a limb that becomes tense and swollen. A responder who suspects a compartment syndrome does not wait for a diagnosis to arrive, and acts within the local protocol, which may allow a constricting bandage or splint to be released by someone authorized to release it, while the limb is not elevated and the patient is referred urgently. The definitive treatment of a broken hip is an operation performed by an orthopedic surgeon, and the choice of operation and its timing belong to that surgeon and to the team that knows the patient's other illnesses. Casting, traction and reduction are described here so that a listener understands what is being done and why, but the techniques themselves are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol.

The fourth scenario is a constructed illustration of a worker whose hand has been caught between the rollers of a machine in a workshop, and the assessment begins with the machine rather than with the hand. The lay first aider does not touch the controls, because starting a machine that has stopped, or reversing one that has jammed, can pull the hand further in or release it suddenly, so the power is isolated by someone authorized to isolate it and the emergency services are called at once. Machinery that has trapped a person is a rescue problem before it is a medical one, and the responder's task is to keep the patient still, warm and talking while a trained team performs the extrication. The trained first responder and the paramedic assess the airway, the breathing and the circulation while the hand is still in the machine, and they control the bleeding that can be controlled without exploring the wound, because blind exploration of an open wound adds harm. Where the local protocol allows it and the responder has been trained, a tourniquet may be applied for bleeding that pressure cannot control, and the time it was applied is written down. An object embedded in the hand is left where it is, and exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, because the object may be holding pressure on a torn vessel.

If a part of the hand has been separated, it is wrapped in clean gauze that is only slightly dampened with a sterile salt solution, and the sealed bag that holds the gauze is cooled in a container of cold water and ice, so that the tissue is cooled but never frozen and never in direct contact with the ice. The lay first aider can do this, and can keep the patient warm and still, and the trained responder carries it out within the procedures the service authorizes. The decision about whether a separated part can be rejoined belongs to the hand surgeon, and no responder should promise that it can, because the outcome depends on the level of the injury, the crushing and the time that has passed. A duration or a temperature named in that teaching is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since such figures differ between services and countries. Nothing is applied to the wound except a sterile dressing, no antiseptic, no ointment and no powder, because the receiving surgeon must see the tissue as the accident produced it. The patient is given nothing by mouth in anticipation of anesthesia, since that decision belongs to the treating team, and the patient is reassured while transport is arranged.

The hand repays a careful examination, because a finger that cannot be moved or a patch of skin that cannot feel tells the examiner where the damage lies before any image is taken. The trained responder tests sensation along both sides of each finger and asks the patient to bend and to straighten the fingers, and the result is written down before a splint is applied and checked again after it. Immobilization of the hand follows the position of function, which is the softly curved position the hand takes when it is at rest, and the splint is padded where it presses and secured firmly enough to hold without narrowing the circulation. The lay first aider supports the hand on a padded surface and does not attempt to shape it, and the trained responder applies the dressing and the splint that the equipment authorized by the service provides. The clinician orders the radiograph, and a hand that has been crushed may need more than one view. The reassessment after immobilization is not optional, because a crushed hand swells quickly, so the responder checks the color, the warmth, the pulse, the sensation, the movement and the pain, and loosens what the local protocol permits loosening.

Referral for this patient is to a facility with a hand surgeon, and it is arranged early, because the repair of a tendon, a nerve or a vessel, and any attempt to rejoin a separated part, are time dependent and cannot be performed in a clinic room. The definitive care of a crushed hand takes place in an operating room, where the surgeon explores the wound under anesthesia, removes what is dead, fixes what is broken and closes what can be closed, and closure is the surgeon's decision and never the responder's. The workplace itself becomes part of the medical history, because the machine, the guard that was missing and the task the worker was performing are recorded for the safety investigation that will follow. The lay first aider writes nothing about cause and nothing about blame, and simply reports what was seen, what was done, at what time and by whom. The record carries the time of the injury, the position in which the hand was found, and the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention. That record travels with the patient, and the handover is spoken in the same order, so that the receiving team continues a story instead of starting a new one.

Four settings, four mechanisms and one order, and that order is what a listener should be able to say aloud while working. The first step is assessment, which begins with the safety of the scene and continues through the mechanism of injury and the primary survey, whose steps are remembered as ABCDE. The next step is stabilization, which means keeping the patient warm, controlling the bleeding that can be controlled, and supporting the injured part in the position in which it was found. The neurovascular examination follows and is performed before any intervention and repeated after it, and its result is written down, because a finding that was not recorded before a splint was applied cannot be attributed afterwards. Immobilization then holds the joint above and the joint below the injury rather than the injury alone. Only then does imaging begin, and it is ordered and interpreted by a clinician, because a picture confirms what the examination has suggested and does not replace it. Reassessment comes after every intervention, and the last step is to refer the patient or to treat definitively, decided by the clinician who knows the injury, the patient and the resources of the facility. The order is not a ritual, because every step in it protects the next, and the step that protects the patient from the treatment is the reassessment.

The complication this course is most afraid of is the one caused by the treatment, and in each of these scenarios it has the same name. Compartment syndrome occurs when bleeding or swelling raises the pressure inside a closed space bounded by bone and fascia, and the muscle and the nerve inside that space suffer first while a pulse at the wrist or the ankle can still be felt, so an intact pulse does not exclude it. The findings that raise the suspicion are pain that increases rather than settles, pain out of proportion to the injury, a limb that is tense and swollen, numbness, tingling and weakness. The trained responder and the clinician do not wait for a measurement to confirm the diagnosis, because the diagnosis is clinical and time is muscle. Where the local protocol allows it, a constricting bandage, splint or cast is released by someone authorized to release it, the limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, the patient is kept warm, and the patient is referred urgently to a facility that can operate. A patient who improves after the bandage is loosened has given useful information, and a patient who does not has given an instruction to escalate at once. Anyone at any level who is unsure whether a limb is becoming tighter should ask early, because the answer to a question costs nothing and the answer to a missed compartment costs a limb.

Two of these scenarios carry an open wound, and that finding changes the management, because the deep tissues have met the outside world and are contaminated even when the wound looks clean. The part played by the responder is limited and precise: cover the wound with a sterile dressing, control the bleeding that can be controlled, record the neurovascular status, immobilize the limb in the position found, keep the patient warm, and arrange urgent transfer. The wound is not explored with a finger or an instrument, no antiseptic is poured into it, and exposed bone is never pushed back into it, because the surgeon needs to see the injury as the accident produced it. Antibiotics and protection against tetanus are decisions made by the prescriber according to the local protocol, and the responder's part is to report the time of the injury, the state of the wound and the environment in which it happened, since a wound from a farmyard and a wound from a clean kitchen floor do not carry the same risk. The question of how soon the wound must reach an operating room belongs to the surgical team, and any window of hours is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. What the responder must not do is to close the wound, to delay the transfer in order to clean it further, or to give the patient anything to eat or drink, and pain relief remains the prescriber's decision.

Cold is a complication of trauma rather than a detail of comfort, and every one of these four patients is at risk of it. The motorcyclist lies on wet asphalt, the pedestrian lies on a cold road, the older woman lies on a bare floor, and the worker stands in an unheated workshop, and each of them loses heat through wet clothing, exposed skin and a long wait. The lay first aider insulates the patient from the ground, replaces wet clothing with dry covering, and covers the head, and the trained responder does the same within the procedures the service authorizes. Transport is arranged by the service that owns the protocol, and the choice of the receiving facility and of the route belongs to those who know the local capacity and the condition of the patient. An injury of the spine is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has cleared it, and no single manual handling technique is safe on its own, so such a patient is moved by a trained team using the precautions the local protocol requires. Anyone at any level who is uncertain about the spine says so at the handover, and the receiving team decides how the patient will be moved from that point onward.

Children, older adults and pregnant patients appear in these settings more often than any other group, and each of them changes the threshold for acting rather than the order of the steps. The bone of a child bends further before it breaks and has a growth region near each end that can be injured without the bone breaking, so a child who is unwilling to use an arm or a leg after a fall down the stairs deserves the same caution as a child with an obvious deformity. An older adult whose bone has lost density may break a hip or a wrist in a fall from standing height, and the fracture may be far less painful than its severity suggests, so the absence of severe pain is not a reason to wait. A pregnant patient who has fallen is assessed with the same sequence, and the decision about monitoring the pregnancy belongs to the obstetric team, while the responder's task is to keep the patient still and warm and to arrange transport without delay. In all three groups the sign that a book treats as reliable may be absent, so the responder lowers the threshold for immobilization and referral rather than trusting a single finding. The judgments about imaging, about pain relief and about definitive treatment in these patients belong to the clinicians who know them, and the responder's part is to recognize that the ordinary rule may not hold and to say so out loud.

To summarize, the road, the home and the workplace produce the same injuries by different routes, and the responder meets all of them with one order, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. The motorcyclist is not moved and his helmet is not removed by a bystander, the pedestrian is examined as a whole patient because the energy leaves more than one injury, the older woman with a painful hip is not lifted into a chair, and the worker whose hand is in the machine is helped by people who isolate the power before they touch the hand. The neurovascular examination is performed before any intervention and repeated after it and is written down, because a finding that was not recorded before a splint was applied cannot be attributed afterwards, and the reassessment after every intervention is what catches the complication caused by the treatment. A deformed limb is splinted in the position found and is never straightened in the field, an embedded object is never removed, exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, an open wound is never explored blindly, and definitive closure belongs to the operating room. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, a reduction is not attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, or when the vessels and the nerves are failing and need a specialist, and no substance, amount or route for pain relief appears anywhere in this course. Cold is treated as a complication, the spine is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has done so, and the record travels with the patient to the handover.
