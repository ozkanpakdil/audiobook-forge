# Chapter 9 — Patient Handling and Transport

Moving a patient is a treatment rather than a pause between treatments, and it deserves the same care as any other intervention in this course. A patient dragged by the shoulders, rolled by one pair of hands, or lifted by the arm of the injured side can reach the hospital with an injury that no longer matches the one assessed at the scene. Handling therefore runs through the sequence every chapter of this course follows, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. This chapter explains what each device and each maneuver is for, who may perform it and what can go wrong, rather than teaching the technique, because every technique named here is learned on a course, on a model and under supervision. It is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

The spine is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has cleared it, and that rule governs every lift, roll and transfer described here. Clearing the spine is not a single test but a judgment that combines the mechanism of injury, what the patient feels and can move, what the examination shows and what the imaging shows, and it is made by a clinician working within a defined protocol. A patient may have an unstable spine and still be awake, talking and free of pain, so an examination that finds nothing is not the same as an examination that excludes the injury. Spinal precautions are applied on the basis of the mechanism and of what the patient reports, and they are removed only by the person the local protocol authorizes to remove them. The lay first aider and the trained first responder do not clear a spine, the nurse and the physician clear it only as their protocol allows, and no responder clears it because the patient asks to sit up.

Assessment comes before any pair of hands touches the patient, and it begins with the scene rather than with the body. Traffic, fire, electricity and unstable debris are removed or avoided before the responder commits, and personal protective equipment is worn for every patient, because an injured responder becomes a second patient. The number of patients, the mechanism of injury, the time that has passed and the level of consciousness are noted, and then the primary survey proceeds in the order remembered as ABCDE, which stands for airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure. Only when the patient is stable enough to tolerate it does the assessment turn to the limbs, which are examined by looking, by feeling and by asking the patient to move them, never by forcing them. The lay first aider observes and reports, the trained first responder and the emergency medical technician assess within the procedures their service authorizes, and the clinician interprets what the findings mean.

The first decision in handling is whether the patient needs to be moved at all, because the safest place for an unstable injury is usually the position in which it was found. Movement is justified when there is a hazard that cannot be removed, when the airway cannot be protected where the patient lies, when a qualified team has decided that care requires a transfer, or when the space is needed for a patient who is more seriously injured. When two correct actions compete for the same pair of hands, the principle that decides is that life comes before limb, because a brain and a heart wait for nobody while a limb can wait for minutes. A patient lying on a floor with hands holding the head and the pelvis still is safer than the same patient halfway through an unnecessary move. The lay first aider moves a patient only to escape immediate danger, the trained responder moves within the procedures the service authorizes, and the decision to move for a medical reason belongs to the clinician directing the care.

Stabilization in the field is a small set of acts, and the first of them is restraint, which means holding the injured part still rather than pulling it into a better position. The trained responder supports the head in the position found, holds the pelvis square, or takes the weight of the injured limb with both hands, and keeps that hold until a device has taken over the work. A deformed limb is splinted as it lies and is never straightened in the field, because vessels and nerves lie against the bone and a movement that looks helpful can tear what was still intact. Holding is tiring, so the holders are exchanged one at a time, and the new holder takes the load before the old one releases it. The lay first aider supports with whatever is at hand and keeps the patient warm, while formal spinal restraint is a skill of the trained responder, the emergency medical technician and the paramedic.

The neurovascular examination is performed before the patient is moved and repeated after every movement, and its result is recorded on both occasions. The examiner looks at the color and the temperature of the skin, feels the pulse beyond the injury, presses briefly on a nail to watch the color return, and asks about numbness, tingling and weakness. Sensation is tested on both sides and compared, and the record uses the words the patient used, because a note that says numb tells the next clinician less than a note that says where the numbness begins. A pulse that is present does not exclude serious injury to the vessels or to a compartment, and a pulse that is absent turns the move into an emergency. The lay first aider observes color, warmth and the ability of the patient to move and to feel, the trained responder performs the formal examination, and the interpretation belongs to the clinician.

No single responder performs a manual handling technique alone, and this is a rule about physics and about attribution rather than about etiquette. An unstable spine moves as a unit only if the people holding it move as a unit, so a roll, a lift onto a device and a transfer between surfaces all require a team whose members know their roles before the first count. The team has one leader who gives the commands, one person at the head who owns the alignment of the neck, and further pairs of hands along the body, and nobody adds a pull that was not part of the plan. The number of people needed depends on the size of the patient, the equipment and the local protocol, and any figure offered for that is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the procedure of the service. The lay first aider calls for help rather than attempting a roll, the trained first responder joins a team without commanding it, and the emergency medical technician and the paramedic lead the move within their procedures.

The log roll is a way of turning a patient onto the side as a single unit, so that the back can be examined, a device can be placed beneath the body, or a blocked airway can be cleared. What the maneuver is meant to achieve is a turn in which the head, the shoulders, the pelvis and the legs move together, and what it produces when it is done badly is a twist between two parts of the spine, which is the movement the spine tolerates least. The head holder keeps the neck in line with the body throughout, the leader controls the count, and the patient is turned only as far as the task requires and for no longer. A log roll does not move a patient across a room, and it is not attempted on a patient who is fighting the hands that hold the neck. If the patient resists, if a limb cannot move with the body, or if the holders lose their alignment, the leader stops the maneuver and the team returns the patient to the position found, because resistance and a complication are both signals to stop.

The scoop stretcher is a device that separates into two halves, each of which slides under the patient from one side, and the halves are then locked together so that the patient can be lifted without being rolled. It is chosen when a patient found on the ground must be raised onto a stretcher with as little movement of the trunk as possible, and it suits a suspected injury of the pelvis or the hip, where turning can increase bleeding inside the pelvis. The halves are placed by a team that keeps the spine aligned by hand, the locking points are checked before the lift, and the skin is inspected where the device meets the body, because a device that presses on skin causes an injury of its own. The scoop stretcher is applied by the trained responder, the emergency medical technician and the paramedic within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, and the choice between it and a simple lift belongs to whoever is leading the move.

The long board is a rigid flat device whose greatest value lies in freeing a patient from a confined space such as a vehicle, so its purpose belongs to the moment of extrication rather than to the journey that follows. A hard surface that does not give transmits every bump of the road to the body, and the pressure it applies to the skin of the back, the heels and the head can cause an injury within a short time, so practice differs between services and countries about how long a patient is carried on one. Some services remove the board as soon as the patient is on a padded stretcher and the spine has been assessed, while others transport on it, and the answer belongs to the current guideline and the local protocol. The board is applied by the trained responder, the emergency medical technician and the paramedic as part of a coordinated move with the neck held, and the lay first aider never places one.

The transfer from the ground to the stretcher and from the stretcher into the vehicle is the part of the journey in which the most injuries are caused by handling. It is planned aloud before it begins, so that every pair of hands knows which part of the body it owns and where the patient is going. The injured part is supported by the responder who has held it from the start, and the fractured bone is never used as a handle, because the bone is a lever and the soft tissues attached to it take the strain. Where a slide is possible, the patient is moved along the surface rather than above it, since a slide needs fewer hands and moves the spine less than a lift. The patient who is confused, frightened or intoxicated is the patient most likely to be harmed by the hands that are trying to help, so that patient is told what is about to happen and moved with enough people to hold the body securely. The leader watches the patient rather than the device and stops the move the moment the alignment is lost, the patient reports new pain, or a strap or a lock is found to be undone.

A patient trapped in a vehicle presents the clearest example of a decision made for the patient rather than for the injury. When there is fire, the risk of an explosion, rising water or a patient who is not breathing, the patient is removed as quickly as the trained responders can manage, and the spine is protected as well as the situation allows rather than perfectly. When there is no such hazard, the patient is stabilized where the patient lies, the engine is switched off, the neck is held, and the extrication proceeds as a planned move with the equipment the service carries. Rapid extrication is a technique of the emergency medical technician and the paramedic, performed within the procedures their service authorizes and learned in supervised training, and it is not a technique a listener can safely assemble from a description.

An open injury changes the handling of a patient without changing the sequence of care. The wound is covered with a sterile dressing, the bleeding that can be controlled is controlled by pressure around the wound rather than over the bone ends, the neurovascular status is recorded, and the limb is immobilized in the position found. An embedded object is never removed and exposed bone is never pushed back into the wound, because the object may be pressing on a torn vessel and the bone may be the only thing holding the wound closed. An open wound is never explored blindly, since the exploration adds contamination, and definitive cleaning and closure belong to the operating room and to nobody else. The lay first aider covers the wound with the cleanest material available, the trained responder dresses it with the supplies the service carries, and the physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides what is done about it in the hospital.

Immobilization during handling means that the injured part, together with the joints above and below it, is prevented from moving while the patient is transferred, and the device is chosen to fit the injury rather than the other way around. A pelvic binder is a device that encircles the pelvis to reduce its volume and to limit movement of the broken ring, and it is applied by responders trained and authorized to apply it, in the place and in the manner the local protocol specifies. A traction splint applies a gentle continuous pull to the leg, and it is described here only in terms of what it is meant to achieve, which is to hold the thigh in line and to reduce movement of the broken ends against each other, because its application is a supervised skill and is not taught from a book. Splinting with a padded board or a sling is the work of the trained responder and the paramedic within their procedures, and the physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides what definitive immobilization is required.

A deformed limb is never straightened in the field, and the rule is repeated here because handling is the moment at which the temptation is greatest. Pulling a limb straight may convert a closed fracture into an open one, may tear a vessel or a nerve that was intact, and may release the pressure that was holding a torn vessel closed. Reduction, which restores the position of the bone ends, and traction, which holds them in line with a gentle continuous pull, are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and neither is taught as a technique in this course. A reduction must not be attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when the vessels or the nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, when the responder lacks the training or the equipment, or when an earlier attempt has failed. In each of those situations the attempt can turn an injury that would have healed into one that cannot, and force is not a technique.

Some patients change the handling without changing the sequence, and the responder adjusts the threshold rather than the order of the steps. A child is not a small adult, because the head is large in proportion to the body and a device sized for an adult will not hold a small body in line, so equipment and technique are chosen for the size of the patient. An older adult brings thin skin that tears under shear, bone that is fragile and breaks under a hand that grips a rib cage in order to lift, and a stiff spine that will not produce a straight line when a flexible neck is assumed. A pregnant patient is two patients for the purpose of assessment and one patient for the purpose of handling, so the position in which she is carried is decided by the clinician or by the protocol of the service. In all of these patients the lay first aider keeps the patient warm and still and calls for help, while the decisions about imaging and about treatment belong to the clinician.

Cold is a complication of trauma and not a detail of comfort, and a patient lying on the ground, uncovered for an examination or waiting in a vehicle loses heat faster than anyone expects. The patient is insulated from the ground, wet clothing is replaced with dry covering, a hat and blankets are used, and the temperature of the vehicle is set for the patient rather than for the crew. Exposure is necessary for the examination and for the transfer, but it is kept as brief as the task allows and the patient is covered again as soon as the hands have finished. Where a service names a temperature or a time for warming, that figure is an illustration to be confirmed against the guideline in force and the protocol of the service, because such numbers differ between services and editions.

The vehicle is a treatment space, and it is prepared before the patient is placed in it. The stretcher is locked into its mount, the patient is secured with the straps the service provides, the equipment that may be needed on the way is within reach, and the attendant sits where the face of the patient and the monitor can be seen without unfastening anything. An unsecured patient is a moving mass in a sudden stop, so the injury can be completed by the journey rather than by the fall. Smooth driving is part of the care, which means that the driver avoids the hard braking and the sharp corner that a healthy passenger would not notice, and the attendant asks for a gentler route when the pain of the patient rises with every bump. The driver works within the law and the procedures of the service, and the decision to pass the nearest hospital for one that can treat the injury belongs to the paramedic or the clinician under the protocol in force.

Reassessment happens after every intervention, which in this chapter means after every movement, every application of a device and every transfer. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the record, and it is the comparison rather than any single measurement that carries the information. The limb is checked for color, warmth, pulse, sensation, movement and pain, the device is checked for tightness and for pressure on the skin, and the record is written at the time rather than reconstructed at the end of the shift. Pain that increases instead of settling, pain out of proportion to the injury, numbness, tingling, weakness and a limb that becomes tense and swollen are the findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation. A responder who suspects a compartment syndrome does not wait for the diagnosis to be confirmed and acts within the local protocol, which may allow a constricting bandage, splint or cast to be released by someone authorized to release it, and the limb is not elevated above the level of the heart while the patient is kept warm and referred urgently.

Imaging is the step that confirms what the examination has already suggested, and in the care of a patient who has been moved it has one further purpose, which is to show whether the spine or the pelvis was injured in the first place. A radiograph is usually the first study and is taken in at least two directions so that a displacement in the third direction is not hidden, while computed tomography shows the detail of a complex break and magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for the soft tissues. Imaging is ordered by a clinician trained to interpret it, the lay first aider never orders or interprets it, and the trained responder does not decide which study is needed. An image does not clear a spine by itself, because that decision also depends on the mechanism, the examination and the patient, and it is made by the person the local protocol authorizes. The journey to the imaging room is itself a handling event, so the patient is transferred with the same plan and the same record of neurovascular findings before and after.

Referral or definitive treatment is the last step of the sequence, and for the patient who has just been moved it begins with the choice of destination. A patient with an open fracture, a suspected compartment syndrome, a dislocation, a damaged nerve or vessel, a fracture that enters a joint, a serious injury of the pelvis or an unstable spine is referred to a facility with imaging, an operating room and an intensive care unit. That decision belongs to the clinician and to the receiving service under the local protocol, and the paramedic reports the findings on which the decision rests rather than making the choice alone. Definitive treatment includes reduction, which restores the position of the bone ends, traction, which holds them in line with a gentle continuous pull, and casting or surgical fixation, which holds the position while the bone heals, and each of those acts is learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol. A surgeon may hold the fragments with metal devices, an operation called open reduction and internal fixation, and the choice among these treatments belongs to the orthopedic surgeon who has examined the patient and seen the images.

The handover is part of the treatment rather than a formality that follows it, and it is where the work of handling is either preserved or lost. The time of the injury, the mechanism, the position in which the patient and the limb were found, the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention, the devices that were applied and by whom, and any change in the condition of the patient are spoken in a fixed order. It ends with the two things most often left out, which are the findings that remain uncertain and the acts that were not performed because they lay outside the scope of practice of the responder. A constructed illustration is a patient found on the ground, turned once to clear the airway, lifted onto a scoop stretcher and carried to the vehicle, and the useful handover says what was found before the turn, what changed after it and what has not yet been checked, offered as an illustration rather than as a description of any real patient. What is written is written in the same order as what is spoken, so that the receiving team continues a story instead of starting a new one.

Everything described in this chapter is learned with a teacher, practiced on models and on colleagues, and certified by an authority that also defines what may be done without supervision. The lay first aider supports, covers, keeps the patient warm and calls for help; the trained first responder adds the acts the service trains and authorizes; the emergency medical technician and the paramedic carry out the moves, the devices and the transfers their procedures cover; and the physician and the orthopedic surgeon make the decisions that require a license. Nobody in that list performs a manual handling technique alone, and nobody performs an act outside the scope the law and the employer have given to the role. A responder stops when there is resistance, when the alignment is lost, when the pain of the patient changes, when a device does not fit, or when a complication appears, and calls for a higher level of care rather than forcing the move. The record is kept because a finding that was not written before an intervention cannot be attributed afterwards, and the spine remains unstable until someone qualified to clear it has done so.

To summarize, moving a patient is a treatment, and the safest place for an unstable injury is usually the position in which it was found. Assess the scene and the patient before touching anyone, stabilize by holding the injured part still, examine and record the neurovascular status before every movement and again after it, immobilize with a device that fits the injury, transfer only with a team and a plan, and reassess after each of those steps. Never straighten a deformed limb in the field, never remove an embedded object, never push exposed bone back into a wound, never explore an open wound blindly, and never perform a manual handling technique alone. The log roll, the scoop stretcher and the long board are tools for particular problems, used by a trained responder or a paramedic within the procedures the service authorizes, while the lay first aider supports, covers, keeps the patient warm and calls for help. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and a reduction is not attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when vessels or nerves are failing, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, or when the training and the equipment are lacking. A patient who is cold, a patient whose pain is rising and a patient whose spine has not been cleared are all referred urgently, because the complication this course fears most is the one caused by the treatment. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.
