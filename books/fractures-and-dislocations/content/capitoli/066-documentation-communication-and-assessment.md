# Chapter 66 — Documentation, Communication and Assessment

This final chapter is about the two things that decide whether everything you have heard in this course ever reaches a patient: the story that carries the findings from the roadside to the operating room, and the way your competence in all of it is judged. A fracture can be assessed perfectly and still be treated badly, because the team that received the patient never learned what the first responder saw, and a course can teach a sequence flawlessly and still fail if the listener is never asked to perform it. Every chapter of this course has followed one order, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and this chapter follows that order once more, though it follows it through the record rather than through the limb. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

The record exists because memory is the least reliable instrument in trauma care, and the more urgent the situation the less it can be trusted. Adrenaline narrows attention to the task in front of the hands, so a responder who has just splinted a leg may genuinely believe that the foot was warm and the toes moved, when what was actually noted was the absence of an obvious problem. The neurovascular examination is performed before any intervention and repeated after it, and its result is recorded, because a finding that was not written before a splint was applied cannot be attributed afterwards, and no one can tell later whether a nerve was injured by the bone or by the bandage. A written or spoken note therefore protects the patient, who receives care based on facts rather than on a reconstruction, and it protects the responder, who is not asked to defend a memory against a bad outcome. The lay first aider, who carries no chart and no pen, still contributes to the record by reporting aloud, in the same fixed order, what was seen and what was done. The trained first responder, the emergency medical technician and the paramedic write within the forms and the procedures their service authorizes, and the nurse and the physician write in the clinical record.

A handover is not a fresh beginning, and the patient is not a new patient to each team that touches the stretcher. The receiving team is handed a story already in progress, and the value of the handover lies in the fact that the story continues in the order in which the events happened. When that order is kept, the receiving clinician can see the trajectory, so a pulse that was present at the roadside and absent on arrival means something very different from a pulse that was never recorded at all. The same sequence that governs treatment governs the telling of it, which is why the assessment findings come first, then what was done to stabilize, then the neurovascular status before and after each act, then the immobilization, then the images, then the reassessment, and last what is being asked of the receiving facility. A handover that begins with a conclusion and works backwards leaves the listener unable to judge how much of it to trust. The record travels with the patient, and the patient is never separated from the account of what has already been done.

Assessment begins with the scene and with the mechanism, and both belong in the record because the mechanism predicts the tissues that were loaded. The time of the injury is the first thing anyone will ask for, and it is written as a clock time rather than as a duration, because the window for certain treatments is measured from the moment of the injury; the exact figure that defines that window is confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since it differs between services and editions. The primary survey, whose steps are remembered as ABCDE, meaning airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure, is recorded in the order it was performed, with the level of consciousness and any change in it written in the same place every time. The limb assessment is then written as findings rather than as a diagnosis, which means what the examiner saw, what the examiner felt, what the patient said about the pain, and which movements the patient could and could not perform. Describing findings rather than naming a diagnosis is not a matter of modesty, because a label written early travels far and is believed long after the finding that produced it has changed. The lay first aider reports the mechanism and the time as well as the appearance of the limb, while the primary survey and the examination of the limb belong to the trained responder, to the nurse or to the physician according to local protocol.

What was done to stabilize belongs in the record in the plainest words available, because the next team has to know not only that the limb was supported but how. The position in which the limb was found is written down before anything is applied, since a deformed limb is splinted in the position found and never straightened in the field, and the reason is the risk to the vessels and the nerves that lie against the bone. The record notes that bleeding was controlled by direct pressure where it could be controlled, that an embedded object was left in place and stabilized, that exposed bone was not pushed back into a wound, and that an open wound was covered with a sterile dressing and not explored. Warmth belongs in the same paragraph as the splint, because cold is a complication of trauma and not a detail of comfort, so the patient was insulated from the ground and wet clothing was replaced with covering. The lay first aider performs the acts that need no equipment, while the trained responder and the paramedic act within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes.

The neurovascular examination is written in the words of the examination itself, and it is written twice. Color, warmth, the pulse felt beyond the injury, the color of a pressed nail as it refills, sensation compared on both sides, and the movements the patient can perform are recorded before the limb is immobilized and again after it, because the comparison between the two notes is the only thing that can later separate an injury of the nerve from an injury of the treatment. The strength of a pulse is described rather than graded, so that the next examiner reads that it was present and equal to the other side rather than a mark whose meaning was never defined. Sensation is recorded where the patient can point to it, and the patient's own words for numbness or tingling are written down where possible, because a paraphrase loses the difference between a limb that feels asleep and a limb that feels nothing at all. The result is entered even when it is normal, and a normal entry is not a formality, because the normal record of a finding before an intervention is what allows the same finding to be trusted after it. The lay first aider observes and reports what can be observed without equipment, which is color, warmth and the ability to move and to feel, and the formal examination belongs to the trained responder, to the nurse or to the physician within the limits of their practice. A pulse that is present excludes nothing, and a pulse that is absent is an emergency that ends the examination and begins the referral.

Some findings are recorded precisely because they are unreliable, and the record must say what was found rather than what was concluded from it. An intact pulse at the wrist is compatible with a compartment syndrome that was already established when it was felt, because the pressure that closes the smallest vessels may not be enough to silence a large artery, so a note that reads that the circulation was intact conceals the observation that mattered. A patient whose limb is swollen and tense and whose pain is out of proportion to the injury has a finding and not yet a diagnosis, and the note should carry that finding forward without attaching a name to it that the next clinician may accept too quickly. The same discipline applies to the older adult whose fracture hurts less than its appearance suggests, and to the child who will not use a limb after an injury that looked minor, because in both the expected sign is missing and the record of its absence is what will change the next decision. Where a number is written, whether a temperature, a pulse rate or a distance, it is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol rather than a threshold that this course has established.

Immobilization is recorded as an act with an author, so the note names what was applied, to which joints it extended, and who applied it. A splint holds the injured part and the joints above and below it, it is padded where it presses on bone, and it is secured firmly enough to hold without becoming a band that stops the circulation, and the record should say which of those was checked and when. An improvised device, such as a folded magazine, a padded board or a sling, is recorded as improvised, because a device of that kind shifts and must be checked more often than a manufactured one, and the check belongs in the record as well. The trained responder and the paramedic apply splints within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, while the physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides what definitive immobilization is required. Casting belongs to that definitive stage, it is learned under supervision, and it is performed according to local protocol, and no chapter of this course teaches the technique of applying one.

Imaging is recorded as a question and an answer, because a study is requested when the answer it gives will change what is done. The record names who requested the study, what question it was meant to answer, and who interpreted it, since interpreting an image is the work of a clinician trained to do it and is never the work of the responder who carried the patient. A radiograph is usually the first study, it is taken in at least two directions so that a displacement in the third direction is not hidden, and it includes the joint above and the joint below so that a second injury is not missed. Computed tomography shows the detail of a complex break, particularly around a joint, and magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for the soft tissues, including ligaments, cartilage, tendons and muscle. An image is a picture of one moment, and the patient must still be examined and reassessed after the picture has been taken, which is why the note of the reassessment follows the note of the study and never replaces it.

The reassessment is written immediately after every intervention, and it repeats the same observations in the same order so that the comparison can be made by whoever reads the note next. Pain that increases instead of settling, pain out of proportion to the injury, numbness, tingling, weakness, a limb that becomes tense and swollen, and a pulse that changes are the findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation. When a compartment syndrome is suspected, the constricting bandage, splint or cast is released within the limits of local protocol by someone authorized to do it, the limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, and the patient is referred urgently, because the diagnosis is clinical and time is muscle. The record then states what was found after the release, what changed, and whom the finding was reported to by name or by role, so that the next team does not have to reconstruct whether anyone acted. Reassessment is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment, and a course that feared only the injury itself would have no reason to insist on it.

Referral is where the record changes hands, and the questions asked at the receiving end are always the same. They are what the injury is, when it happened, what has been done, what the neurovascular status is now and what it was before, whether the patient is warm and stable, and what the referring clinician believes is needed. Reduction, which restores the position of the bone ends, traction, which holds them in line with a gentle continuous pull, and casting or surgical fixation, which hold the position while the bone heals, are all described rather than taught, because the technique of each is learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol. A reduction is not attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when the vessels or the nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, when the responder lacks the training or the equipment, or when an earlier attempt has failed, and the note should state which of those applies rather than only that no attempt was made. A patient with an open fracture, a suspected compartment syndrome, a dislocation, a damaged nerve or vessel, a fracture that enters a joint, or an injury that crosses the growing part of a child's bone is referred to a facility with imaging, an operating room and an intensive care unit. Force is not a technique, and a responder who meets resistance stops, splints what is there, and escalates. What is written at that moment is what the surgeon reads first.

The spoken handover is a performance with a structure, and the structure is worth practicing aloud until it becomes automatic. The first step is to identify yourself and the patient and to say where the patient is going, the next is to describe what happened and when, only then to give the relevant background of illness and treatment, then to report the findings of your examination in the order this course has used, and finally to state clearly what you are asking for. The receiving clinician is told the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention in the same breath as the interventions themselves, because a handover that lists what was done without the state of the limb around each act gives the listener actions with no results. A closed loop is used for anything that must be acted upon, which means that the instruction is repeated back by the person who will carry it out. The lay first aider gives a short spoken report in the same order and hands over the patient rather than the story. Where the patient is transferred from one service to another, the same order is used again, in speech and in writing, so that nothing depends on which form the receiving service happens to prefer.

Communication is not only professional, and the way the findings are told to the patient and to the family belongs to the same discipline as the record. The patient is told what has been found, what is being done, what will happen next and when, in the ordinary words of the language the patient speaks, and an interpreter is used rather than a passing bystander when that language is not shared. The patient's own account of the pain and of the mechanism is valuable evidence and is recorded as the patient tells it, since a description that comes from the person experiencing it is often sharper than anything the examiner can see. Consent for an examination or for an intervention is obtained by whoever is authorized to obtain it, it is explained in terms of what will be done and what the alternative is, and the fact that it was obtained is written down. Relatives are told what can be said within the limits of confidentiality and by the person designated to speak with them, because a course of treatment announced to a family by three different people in three different versions is a harm of its own.

A handover fails for ordinary human reasons far more often than for want of knowledge, and naming those reasons is part of preparing for them. Noise is the first, because a handover shouted across a moving vehicle competes with an engine and a siren, so the receiver must be close enough to hear and unhurried enough to ask. Hierarchy is the second, because a junior responder may not correct a senior clinician who has misstated a finding, and a service that treats the record as the authority rather than the rank removes that problem. The third is the shift change, where a patient arrives at the moment when attention is divided, and the answer is to write before speaking rather than after. Fatigue and the end of a long shift change how much is remembered rather than how much is known, so the note is written while the details are fresh and is never reconstructed from memory hours later. Where a note is corrected, the correction is made openly and visibly, so that the record shows what was thought at the time and what is known now.

The record has a legal and an ethical life of its own, and the rules that govern it are local, so they must be learned where you work rather than assumed from a course. What is written is written to be read by those who need it for the care of the patient and by no one else, so confidentiality is a duty and not a courtesy. An entry is made at the time or as close to the time as the situation allows, it is dated and signed by its author, and it states what was observed rather than what was inferred. Notes are never altered long afterwards to improve the story, and a later correction is added as a separate entry that says what changed and why, because a record that has been rewritten is worth less than a record that was imperfect and honest. The record may be requested by a court, by an insurer, by a regulator or by the patient, and in each case what stands is what was written at the bedside. No real patient, no identifiable case and no institutional detail belongs in any teaching example, which is why the examples used in this course are constructed illustrations and are described as such.

There are places where the record has no printed form, no computer and sometimes no electricity, and the duty to document does not disappear with the stationery. The minimum set of facts is always the same, and it is the time of the injury, the mechanism, the position in which the limb was found, what was done and by whom, the neurovascular status before and after each act, the images taken and what they showed, the findings on reassessment, and what is being asked of the receiving facility. Those facts can be written on a card, on a dressing wrapper, in a notebook or on a part of the patient that will not be washed before arrival, and the note travels with the patient rather than in a pocket. Where nothing can be written, the handover is spoken at the receiving door in the fixed order and repeated back, and the receiving clinician is asked to write it down while the account is fresh. The patient's own identification and the name of the person who made the note are the two details most often lost, and they are the two that make everything else traceable.

The written assessment of this course is meant to be used as a rehearsal of the sequence rather than as a test of memory, and it will ask you what you would look for, what you would do, in what order, and who may do it. It will not ask you for a dose, because no dose has been given anywhere in these chapters, and it will not ask you to describe the technique of a reduction, because a technique learned from a page and never practiced under supervision is a technique that has not been learned. Questions about an intervention are answered by naming the step of the sequence it belongs to, the finding that indicates it, the provider level that is authorized to perform it, and the reassessment that must follow it. A question about an open fracture is answered with covering, bleeding control, the neurovascular record, immobilization in the position found, warmth, and urgent referral, and a question about a suspected compartment syndrome is answered with escalation and with release of the constricting device within local protocol by someone authorized to do it. The reason the assessment is written this way is that a listener who has internalized the sequence can apply it to an injury the course never described, while a listener who memorized a list of answers cannot.

The practical assessment is where the course is finally used, and it is conducted on models, on simulators and on willing colleagues under the supervision of an instructor who is qualified to certify competence. The candidate is observed performing the sequence whole, which means assessing, stabilizing, examining the neurovascular status, immobilizing, obtaining and discussing imaging, reassessing, and then stating the referral or the definitive plan, while naming the provider level for each act as though the act were real. A candidate who skips the neurovascular examination before applying a splint fails that station regardless of how neat the splint is, and a candidate who straightens a deformed limb in order to make it easier to splint fails the whole scenario, because the examination exists to protect the patient from exactly that instinct. Feedback is given against the sequence rather than against the candidate, so that the same order can be used at the next station and in the next hospital. Certification is issued by an authority that also defines what may be done without supervision, and that definition differs between countries, between services and between professions, so it is checked rather than assumed. Skills decay quietly, so drills, briefings and debriefings are scheduled rather than hoped for, and none of them substitutes for the authorization that the local authority requires. Nothing performed in the practical assessment of this course is a license to perform it on a patient.

Documentation and communication come last in this course because they carry everything that came before, and they carry one idea above all, which is that the patient is the whole person who was healthy the day before and the fracture is only the part of the injury that can be seen. Life comes before limb, because a limb can wait for minutes that a brain and a heart cannot, and the complication this course fears most is the one caused by the treatment. The neurovascular examination before and after every intervention is written down so that the course of the injury can be told apart from the course of the care, and the reassessment exists so that the first sign of harm caused by a splint, a bandage or a cast is caught while it can still be reversed. Immobilization in the position found, an embedded object left alone, exposed bone left where it lies, an open wound covered and not explored, and a deformed limb never straightened in the field are rules that survive only when the reason travels with them, which is why every chapter of this course has attached a reason. The spine is treated as unstable until it is cleared by someone qualified to clear it. Reduction, traction and casting remain acts of supervised clinical training performed according to local protocol, and this course has described them without ever teaching them.

To summarize, the record and the handover are part of the treatment rather than a formality that follows it, and a finding that was not written before an intervention cannot be attributed after it. Write and speak in the order the course has used, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and say who among the lay first aider, the trained responder, the paramedic, the nurse, the physician and the orthopedic surgeon may perform each act. Describe findings rather than diagnoses, name the provider level for every intervention, keep the patient warm, treat the spine as unstable until it is cleared by someone qualified to clear it, and stop and escalate at the first sign of resistance or of a complication. Never straighten a deformed limb in the field, never remove an embedded object, never push exposed bone back into a wound, never explore an open wound blindly, and never take a dose, a route or a regimen from anything you have heard in this course, because the substance and the amount belong to the local protocol and the prescriber. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and no chapter of this course has taught them. The sequence is not a ritual to be recited but a habit of mind that protects the patient at every step from the scene of the injury to the operating room, and if you keep it, you will have taken from these chapters the one thing they were written to give. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.
