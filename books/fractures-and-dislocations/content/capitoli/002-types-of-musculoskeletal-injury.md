# Chapter 2 — Types of Musculoskeletal Injury

Every injury of the musculoskeletal system belongs to a small family of named types, and the name is a decision about what happens next. A fracture, a dislocation, a subluxation, a sprain, a strain, a crush injury and the broader group of soft-tissue injuries are told apart by which tissue failed and how completely. That name sets the urgency, the destination, who is called and what will be done on arrival, so the order of the steps matters as much as the words. This chapter follows the order used throughout the course, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and it is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

Energy delivered to a limb does not choose one tissue, and the damage is decided by where the force entered, how fast it arrived and how the tissues were loaded. A pull along a ligament tears fibers, a twist through a joint tears the capsule, a direct blow crushes muscle against bone, and a bending force breaks what resists bending least. The same fall from a bicycle can fracture a wrist in one patient, dislocate a shoulder in another and tear a ligament in a third, and none of them looks dramatic at the roadside. What the responder sees is the sum of all of that, so the examination is aimed at the tissues rather than at the bone alone.

A fracture is a break in the continuity of a bone, whether the bone is cracked, bent, crushed or divided into pieces. A closed fracture leaves the skin intact over the break, while an open fracture has communicated with the outside through a wound that may be tiny enough to mistake for a scratch. The break is complete when the bone is divided across its whole width and incomplete when part of the outer shell stays in one piece, which is common in children, and a bone broken into many pieces is called a comminuted fracture. A fragment pulled away by a tendon or a ligament is called an avulsion, and the findings that suggest a fracture are deformity, swelling, tenderness at one point, a wound over the bone and a limb the patient will not use.

The teaching that a broken bone can be felt to grate is a poor guide, because the sign is often absent and the movement can injure what survived. Being able to bear weight, moving the fingers and feeling only mild pain do not exclude a fracture, and the patients in whom those signs are least reliable are the ones with the most to lose. A child with a crack, an older adult with fragile bone and a patient whose awareness is clouded by alcohol or by an injury of the head may report far less than the injury deserves. Where this chapter names a distance, a time or a threshold, it names an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between services, countries and editions.

The words used to describe how a broken bone has moved are few, and each answers a different question about the far end of the bone. Angulation describes a bend at the fracture and is named for the direction in which that end points, while translation, also called displacement, describes the broken ends lying beside one another rather than end to end. Shortening, distraction and rotation describe a limb that has lost or gained length and a bone that has twisted about its long axis, and comminution describes a bone broken into more than 2 pieces. These words matter less to the responder than to the surgeon, but the position in which the limb was found is worth recording, because the limb is splinted as it lies.

A dislocation is the complete loss of contact between the surfaces of a joint, so the bone ends that should meet no longer touch at all. The joint looks wrong, it is held in a position the patient cannot change, and the movement that should be free is blocked by the displaced bone. A dislocation tears the capsule that encloses the joint and damages at least one of the ligaments that steady it, and the nerves and vessels passing close to the joint are at risk. The bone end that has lost its blood supply may die if the joint is not put back within a time that teaching offers as an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, and nothing in the field is done to put it back.

A subluxation is a partial loss of contact between the surfaces of a joint, so the bones are still touching but are no longer in their proper relationship. It is often less obvious than a dislocation, because the shape of the limb may be almost normal and the patient may be able to move the joint a little. The capsule and the ligaments are torn in a subluxation too, so the joint is unstable even when it looks well and the injury is a different point on the same scale. A joint that has slipped partly out may return by itself, which is called a spontaneous reduction, and the responder then sees only swelling and tenderness while the injury has already happened.

Why does the distinction change what the responder does next? Because the type of injury sets the urgency, the destination and the limits placed on movement. A dislocated or partly dislocated joint can threaten a nerve, a vessel or the blood supply to the bone end, and a delay of hours can turn a recoverable injury into one that is not. A torn ligament with the joint surfaces still in contact is managed with protection, support and a plan made after imaging, while a torn muscle or tendon bleeds into its own compartment and can be more disabling than its appearance suggests. Two patients with the same swelling and the same pain may therefore need to go to different places, and the difference is the tissue that failed.

A sprain is an injury of a ligament, the band of fibrous tissue that joins one bone to another and guides a joint through its range of motion. The ligament is stretched or torn when the joint is forced beyond the range it allows, and the injury is described in words rather than in grades of number. In the mildest form the fibers are stretched and the joint remains stable, in the middle form some fibers are torn and the joint is still held, and in the most severe form the ligament is torn right through. The responder sees swelling, bruising and tenderness over the ligament rather than over the bone, and testing how loose the joint has become is a clinical skill learned under supervision and never a field test.

A strain is an injury of the muscle and tendon unit, in which the fibers of muscle, of tendon or of the junction between them are stretched or torn. It happens when a muscle contracts hard against a load it cannot move, which is why strains cluster in muscles that cross 2 joints and in tendons that carry sudden loads. The patient feels a sharp pain at a specific place, often describes a tearing sensation, and afterward cannot use that muscle with the same power, while bleeding into the muscle produces swelling and bruising that may appear a day later. When a tendon pulls a piece of bone away the injury is called an avulsion and is treated as a fracture, and when a muscle is struck directly and bleeds within its fascia the result is a contusion with a different risk.

The distinction between a sprain and a strain is simple in words and useful in practice, because a sprain is an injury of a joint and a strain is an injury of the muscle and tendon unit that moves it. A sprain of the ankle, the knee or the wrist sits over a joint whose bone ends may also have broken, so an inability to bear weight or tenderness over the bone raises the suspicion of a fracture and sends the patient for imaging. A strain sits over a muscle or a tendon away from the joint line, and the urgent question there is whether the muscle or tendon has torn completely or pulled a fragment of bone with it. Both are soft-tissue injuries, both are painful, and neither is a diagnosis that a responder can confirm by hand in the street.

Soft-tissue injury is the name for damage to everything in the limb that is not bone, which includes skin, fat, fascia, muscle, tendon, ligament, joint capsule, blood vessels and nerves. It is sometimes spoken of as the mild part of trauma, and that habit is dangerous, because the soft tissues decide how a fracture heals, whether a limb keeps its blood supply and whether the patient returns to work. A fracture always includes a soft-tissue injury, since bone lies within a sleeve of muscle, fascia and skin that is torn or compressed by the same force. Damage to the soft tissues is often invisible at first, because the blood that has escaped lies deep inside the limb, and the responder who records a wound, a bruise or a change in the color of the skin is documenting what the radiograph cannot show.

A crush injury is the result of a body part being compressed between 2 surfaces, and the damage extends far beyond the point of contact. The force is usually high and applied slowly, so the tissues are squeezed and deprived of blood for as long as the compression lasts, and the visible wound may be small compared with what has happened beneath it. When a large mass of muscle has been crushed and then released, the breakdown products of that muscle enter the circulation and can damage the kidneys and disturb the rhythm of the heart, a condition called crush syndrome. The patient may look surprisingly well in the first minutes after release and then deteriorate quickly, so a limb that has been trapped for a long time is a problem for a physician and for a team rather than for a lone responder.

An open injury deserves a word of its own, because the skin's being broken changes the whole plan. Any wound over a bone may communicate with a fracture, and a small puncture wound from a fall onto a spike or from a bite can reach the bone while looking trivial. An embedded object is never removed, because it may be holding pressure on a torn vessel, and exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, which is then covered with a sterile dressing if one is available. The bleeding that can be controlled is controlled, the neurovascular status is recorded, and the limb is splinted as it lies, because exploring the wound blindly adds harm and definitive cleaning and closure belong to the operating room.

Assessment begins before the patient is touched and follows the same order in every case. The first concern is the safety of the scene and the responder, because a rescuer who is struck becomes a second patient, and protection against infection is part of that same thought. The mechanism of injury and the time it happened come next, together with the age of the patient and any illness or medicine that might have made the bone easier to break. Then comes the primary survey, whose steps are remembered as ABCDE, meaning airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure, and only when the patient is stable enough does the assessment turn to the limb. The limb is assessed by looking, by feeling and by asking the patient to move it, never by forcing it, and when two correct actions compete for the same pair of hands, life comes before limb, because a limb can wait for minutes that a brain and a heart cannot.

Looking is done before touching, and it is done with both sides in view so that the injured limb is compared with the other one. The responder notes the position in which the limb is held and whether it lies at an angle the patient cannot correct, because a limb that is deformed is splinted as it lies rather than straightened. Swelling, bruising, a wound, blood soaking through clothing and skin that has changed color are recorded, with the time each was first seen. A limb that is pale, mottled or cold, or one whose skin has become tense and shiny, is described exactly as it appears, and any ring or tight sleeve is removed if it can be removed without force, since a finger that swells inside a ring can lose its circulation within hours.

Feeling begins away from the site of greatest pain so that the patient does not guard the whole limb, and it moves toward the injury with a light hand. The responder feels for the temperature of the skin, for swelling, for tenderness and for the pulse beyond the injury, and compares one side with the other. Feeling for the grating of broken bone is not a useful test, because the sign is often absent and the movement can injure a nerve or a vessel that survived the accident. Pressing hard on a suspected fracture changes nothing about the treatment and adds to the pain, so the examination is as gentle as it can be and stops as soon as the question is answered.

Stabilization in the field is a small set of acts, and restraint is the most important of them. The limb is supported as it lies, with the hands or with whatever is at hand, and it is not straightened, because vessels and nerves lie against the bone and a movement that looks helpful can tear what remained intact. Bleeding that can be controlled without exploring the wound is controlled, the wound is covered, and the patient is kept warm, because cold is a complication of trauma and not a detail of comfort, and the lay first aider does it all with what is available. The trained responder and the paramedic may apply a splint and a dressing within the equipment and the procedures the service authorizes, and nothing in stabilization requires a diagnosis, which is why it can begin before the type of injury is known.

The neurovascular examination is performed before any intervention and repeated after every one, and its result is written down, because a finding that was not recorded before a splint was applied cannot be attributed afterward. The examiner looks at the color and the temperature of the skin, feels the pulse beyond the injury, and presses briefly on a nail to watch the color return. Sensation is tested on both sides rather than at a single spot, and the patient is asked to move the fingers or the toes against resistance that is only the weight of the examiner's hand. Numbness, tingling, weakness, an inability to move a part and a sensation that is reduced or absent are recorded in the words the patient uses, and the lay first aider observes and reports color, warmth and movement while the formal examination belongs to the trained responder or to the clinician.

Immobilization means preventing movement of the injured part and of the joint above and the joint below it, because a bone that is splinted alone can still be moved at either end. A splint is padded where it presses on a bony point, it is applied over the position found, and it is secured firmly enough to hold without becoming a band that stops the circulation. The material matters less than the principle, since a folded magazine, a padded board, a sling or the body of the patient can each serve, and an improvised device must be checked more often because it shifts. The trained responder and the paramedic apply splints within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, while the physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides what definitive immobilization is required, and casting and surgical fixation are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol.

Imaging is the step that confirms what the examination has already suggested, and it is ordered by a clinician trained to interpret it. A radiograph is usually the first study, and it is taken in at least 2 directions so that a displacement in the third direction is not hidden, while the joint above and the joint below are included so that a second injury is not missed. Computed tomography, usually called CT, shows the detail of a complex break, particularly around a joint, and magnetic resonance imaging, usually called MRI, is used mainly for ligaments, cartilage, tendons and muscle. The lay first aider never orders and never interprets imaging, and a radiograph taken soon after an injury does not always exclude a fracture, so a clinician may arrange a further study after a period that teaching offers as an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

Reassessment is performed after every intervention, and it is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the record, because the comparison is what has value rather than any single measurement. The limb is checked for color, warmth, pulse, sensation, movement and pain, the dressing is checked for bleeding that has soaked through, and the splint is checked for tightness and for pressure at its edges. Pain that increases instead of settling, pain out of proportion to the injury, numbness, tingling, weakness and a limb that becomes tense and swollen are the findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation, and reassessment also includes the patient as a whole, because a rising pulse or a falling level of consciousness outranks any question about the limb.

A compartment is a closed space bounded by bone and fascia, and it holds muscle, nerves and blood vessels with very little room to spare, so bleeding or swelling inside it raises the pressure on everything within. The first structures to suffer are usually the muscle and then the nerve, and the flow through the smallest vessels fails while a pulse at the wrist or the ankle can still be felt. The diagnosis is clinical and is made by a clinician, and time is muscle, so a responder who suspects it does not wait for a confirmation that will arrive too late. The acts that follow are limited and are defined by the local protocol, because a constricting bandage, splint or cast may be released by someone authorized to do it, the limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, the patient is kept warm, and the patient is referred urgently.

Referral or definitive treatment is the final step of the sequence, and it begins with the recognition that some injuries cannot be managed where the patient lies. A patient with an open fracture, a suspected compartment syndrome, a dislocation or a subluxation, a damaged nerve or vessel, a fracture that enters a joint, a crushed limb or a fracture in bone weakened by disease is referred to a facility with imaging, an operating room and an intensive care unit. The receiving facility is told what the responder found rather than what the responder concluded, so that the next team can form its own view. The lay first aider does not decide where a patient goes, the trained responder follows the triage rules of the service, and the physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides on definitive care.

Three words describe the acts that restore and hold the position of an injured part, and all three belong to supervised clinical training rather than to a book. Reduction means restoring the position of the bone ends or of the joint surfaces, and it is done because a bone that lies in a poor position may not heal, may not work and may press on the structures beside it. Traction means a gentle continuous pull that holds the ends in line while the soft tissues settle, and casting means holding a limb in a molded shell while the bone heals. Each can go wrong, because a reduction can injure a vessel or a nerve, can convert a closed injury into an open one and can push a fragment where it should not be, while a cast that is too tight can cause a compartment syndrome. A reduction must not be attempted when the source lists a contraindication, which includes a suspected complex fracture together with a dislocation, fragments caught in the joint, significant compromise of the vessels or nerves that needs a specialist, an open injury, an unclear diagnosis, training or equipment that is not available, and an earlier attempt that has failed. In each of those situations an attempt can turn an injury that would have healed into one that cannot, and the qualified decision belongs to the physician or the orthopedic surgeon working within the local protocol, because force is not a technique.

Pain is part of the injury and it deserves to be treated, but no chapter of this course gives a substance, an amount, a route or a frequency, and this one will not either. The decision to give a medicine for pain belongs to a prescriber working within the local protocol, and the responder's part is to report the pain in the patient's own words and to keep the limb supported. A finding that makes the decision urgent is pain that is out of proportion to the injury, pain that increases despite support, or pain accompanied by a limb that is becoming tense, pale or numb. Antibiotics and protection against tetanus after an open injury are decided by a clinician and given according to the local protocol, and a listener must not be able to take a prescription from this chapter.

The record and the handover are part of the treatment rather than a formality that follows it. The time of the injury, the mechanism, the position in which the limb was found, the appearance of the skin and the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention are written or spoken in a fixed order, so that the receiving team continues a story instead of starting a new one. An injury of the spine is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has done so, and no lone manual handling technique is safe, so such a patient is moved by a trained team using the precautions the local protocol requires. Everything described in this chapter is learned with a teacher, practiced on models and on each other, and certified by an authority that also defines what may be done without supervision.

Every act described in this chapter belongs to somebody in particular, and the boundaries are part of the teaching rather than a footnote to it. The lay first aider supports the limb, controls bleeding that can be controlled, keeps the patient warm, observes color and warmth and movement, and calls for help, and does nothing that requires training, equipment or authorization. The trained responder and the paramedic examine the limb within their procedures, perform the neurovascular examination, apply splints and dressings with the equipment the service provides, and hand over what they found. The nurse, the physician and the orthopedic surgeon carry the acts that require training, judgment and a license, including the interpretation of imaging, the reduction of a dislocation and the decision about an operation, each according to the local protocol.

To summarize, the injuries in this chapter are told apart by which tissue failed and how completely, because a fracture breaks bone, an open fracture lets the break communicate with the outside, a dislocation separates the joint surfaces completely, a subluxation separates them partly, a sprain tears a ligament while the joint surfaces stay in contact, a strain tears muscle or tendon, a crush injury compresses part of the body and reaches beyond it, and soft-tissue injury is damage to everything in the limb that is not bone. Look for the position in which the limb is held, for swelling, bruising, a wound, an inability to bear weight, tenderness over one point of a bone, a joint that looks wrong, and any change in color, warmth, pulse, sensation or movement. Do the same things in the same order every time, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and record the neurovascular findings before every intervention and after every one. Never straighten a deformed limb, never remove an embedded object, never push exposed bone back into a wound, never explore an open wound blindly, and never test the stability of a joint or the power of a tendon by force. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and a reduction is not attempted when the source lists a contraindication, when the diagnosis is unclear, when the vessels or the nerves are failing, or when the training and the equipment are not at hand. Cold is treated as a complication and the spine is treated as unstable until it is cleared by someone qualified to clear it. Call the trained responder, the paramedic or the clinician according to the local protocol, and call early when the skin is broken, when a joint is out of place, when a limb is tense and painful out of proportion, or when the patient is deteriorating. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.
