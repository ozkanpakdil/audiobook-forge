# Chapter 22 — Metacarpal Fractures

A metacarpal fracture is a break of one of the five long bones that form the frame of the palm, and no other bone of the hand breaks as often. The injury seldom kills, yet the hand is the instrument with which a person works, eats and cares for others, and a hand that heals in the wrong rotation can cost a patient a trade. The energy of the injury travels through muscle, tendon, nerve and vessel as well as through bone, so this chapter is about the whole hand. The order of work is the same as everywhere in this course, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and the provider level is named for every act. This is educational material, and it does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

Five metacarpals run from the wrist to the knuckles and are numbered from the thumb side outward, and each has a base, a shaft, a neck and a head. The base meets the wrist, the neck is the narrow region before the knuckle, and the head forms the knuckle at the metacarpophalangeal joint. The first metacarpal belongs to the thumb and sits apart, with a saddle-shaped base that lets the thumb swing across the palm. The second and third are almost rigidly locked into the wrist and form a fixed pillar that carries the force of a grip, while the fourth and fifth have mobile bases. That difference in mobility decides how much displacement a broken bone can leave behind.

Around the metacarpal heads lies the apparatus that makes the fingers work, and it may be damaged with the bone or instead of it. Each metacarpophalangeal joint is steadied by a collateral ligament on either side and by a tough plate on its palmar surface, and those ligaments are slack when the joint is straight and tight when it is bent. That is why a hand is never left with the fingers held out flat for days, because a ligament that stays short will not easily lengthen again. Between the metacarpals lie the interosseous muscles, which draw the fingers apart and together, and the tendons that straighten the fingers run over the back of the hand in a layer so thin that a small wound can injure them. The nerve that supplies most of the small muscles of the hand runs close to the fifth metacarpal.

The way the hand was injured tells the responder what to suspect before the hand is examined. A direct blow from a hammer, a door or a machine commonly breaks a shaft or a neck, and a fall onto the outstretched hand drives force along the second and third rays and may break a metacarpal base or the wrist. A twisting injury, such as a hand caught in a strap, tends to produce a spiral break of a shaft, and the spiral pattern is the one most likely to rotate. The commonest of all is a blow delivered with a closed fist against a firm object, which breaks the neck of the fifth metacarpal, the bone of the little finger.

When the neck of the fifth metacarpal breaks, the head tilts toward the palm and the knuckle of the little finger sinks, which is why the injury is also called the dropped knuckle. A small prominence may be felt in the palm where the tilted head presses forward, and the shape can resemble a claw, with the knuckle bent backward and the first joint of the finger bent forward. That appearance alone does not prove which structure is injured, and tenderness over the neck of the bone, swelling and pain on gripping are what a responder can act on. A constructed illustration is a patient who struck a wall and can still bend the little finger, because a broken bone does not stop movement.

Rotation is the deformity that decides the fate of the hand, and it is judged by looking at the fingers rather than at the hand. When a metacarpal twists, the whole finger beyond it turns, and a turn of a few degrees at the bone becomes a large overlap at the fingertip, an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. The examiner asks the patient to bend all the fingers toward the palm and looks at where they point, because the fingers of a normal hand converge toward the base of the thumb. When the little finger rides over or under its neighbor, the fracture has rotated. The fingernails give a second view, since their planes should stay roughly parallel, and a third test uses the tendons, because extending the wrist makes the fingers curl, so a finger that takes no part has rotated.

Rotation and angulation are not the same problem, and the difference is why one patient goes home and another goes to a surgeon. Angulation is a bend at the break, and in the fifth ray a bend of some degree may be accepted because the mobile base of that metacarpal lets the hand compensate, with the amount quoted in teaching varying between services and editions. Rotation is a twist, and it does not remodel, because the finger is not turned by a joint that can adapt but fixed by a bone in the wrong plane. Rotation in the index and middle rays is worse still, because those rays have little mobility at the wrist, so any rotation is referred to a clinician.

Assessment begins with the scene and with the patient rather than with the hand. The responder checks for danger, puts on the protection the situation requires, and works through the primary survey, whose steps are remembered as ABCDE, meaning airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure. The rule that decides the order of work when two correct actions compete for the same pair of hands is that life comes before limb, because a limb can wait for minutes that a brain and a heart cannot. The lay first aider then recognizes the injury, supports the hand and calls for help, while the trained responder, the nurse and the physician work within what the law and the local protocol allow.

The history of a hand injury is short to take and it changes what is done. The responder asks which hand is injured, whether it is the dominant hand, and what work the patient does, because those answers set the urgency of the referral as much as the fracture does. The patient is asked how the injury happened, whether the skin was broken, and whether the hand struck anything that could cut it, and the question about the mouth is asked directly rather than left to chance. Protection against tetanus and any illness or medicine that weakens bone are recorded, and the decisions that follow belong to the treating clinician and to the local protocol.

A wound over a knuckle after a blow to the mouth is a human bite, and it is one of the most easily missed emergencies of the hand. The tooth cuts a small opening over the metacarpophalangeal joint or over the tendon that straightens the finger, and it carries organisms from the mouth into the joint, the tendon sheath or the tissue beneath the skin. The wound often looks trivial and may be hidden by the position of the hand at the moment of the blow, because a cut over a knuckle in flexion slides beneath the skin when the finger is straightened. The patient may be reluctant to say what happened, so the examiner looks at the skin over every knuckle of a hand that has been in a fight. A human bite is an open injury and is referred urgently, while the substances used against infection are decided by the prescriber according to the local protocol.

The hand is examined in a fixed order that no responder has to invent, which is to look, to feel and then to ask the patient to move. Inspection begins with the position of the whole hand at rest and compares it with the other side, because an injured hand is often held differently before any swelling appears. Swelling, bruising, a wound, a deformity at a knuckle and a change in the contour of the back of the hand are all recorded as findings rather than as diagnoses. Palpation moves along each metacarpal from base to head, feeling for the one place that hurts most, and the examiner feels the knuckle, the base of the thumb and the wrist in turn. The fracture is never deliberately rocked to produce a grinding sensation, because that test adds pain and can displace what was stable.

Movement is asked for rather than imposed, and the patient is never made to move a hand against pain. The patient is asked to make a fist, to straighten the fingers, to spread them apart and bring them together, and to touch the thumb to the tip of each finger, and the examiner notes which of those acts is lost. A finger that cannot be straightened against resistance suggests a torn tendon over the back of the hand or the knuckle, and a finger that cannot be bent suggests a tendon injury on the palmar side or a bone blocking the movement. Weakness of the small muscles that spread the fingers apart may point to the nerve close to the fifth metacarpal rather than to the bone, and every movement is compared with the other hand.

The neurovascular examination is performed before any intervention and repeated after it, and its result is written down, because a finding that was not recorded before a splint was applied cannot be attributed afterwards. Sensation in the hand belongs to three territories, and the examiner tests the little finger and the ulnar border of the ring finger for the ulnar nerve, the thumb, the index and the middle fingers for the median nerve, and the web between the thumb and the index finger for the radial nerve. Numbness or tingling in the little finger after a fracture of the fifth metacarpal changes the plan, because the nerve to the small muscles of the hand runs close to that bone. Both hands are compared, and the patient's own words for the feeling, such as pins and needles or a dead finger, are written as far as possible.

The blood supply of the hand comes from two arteries at the wrist, which join in the palm to form arches from which the digital arteries arise. The examiner looks at the color of the fingers, feels their temperature with the back of the hand, presses briefly on a fingernail to watch the color return, and feels for the pulse on both sides of each finger. A pulse that is present does not exclude serious injury, because the small vessels and the compartments can fail while the large pulse continues, and a pulse that is absent is an emergency. When a digital pulse cannot be felt, a clinician may test the supply from the two arteries at the wrist by pressing on one and then the other while the patient opens and closes the hand. The finding is recorded for each finger, and a finger that becomes pale, cool or painful after a splint is applied is reported at once.

Stabilization in the field is a small set of acts, and the most important of them is restraint. The hand is supported in the position in which it was found, and a deformed hand is never straightened, because the vessels and the nerves of the hand lie close to the bones and a movement that looks helpful can tear what remained intact. That decision belongs to a clinician who is trained, equipped, supervised and authorized to make it, and no responder at any level performs it outside a properly equipped clinical area. The lay first aider supports, covers a wound with a sterile dressing, keeps the patient warm and calls for help, while the trained responder and the paramedic act within the procedures their service authorizes.

An open injury of the hand is treated as contaminated from the moment it happens, because the skin carries organisms that reach the bone and the deep tissues as the wound is made. The responder covers the wound with a sterile dressing, controls the bleeding that can be controlled with pressure around the wound and not inside it, and never explores the wound with a finger or an instrument. The neurovascular examination is recorded before the dressing is applied where that is possible, and the hand is immobilized in the position found and kept warm. Definitive closure of an open wound of the hand belongs to the operating room and to nobody else.

Immobilization means preventing movement of the injured bone and of the joints above and below it, and the position chosen is the one that leaves the hand useful. The hand is placed in what teaching calls the position of function, in which the wrist is slightly extended, the knuckles are bent, the middle joints of the fingers are gently bent, and the thumb is carried away from the palm. The knuckles are bent because their collateral ligaments are tight in that position and slack when the joint is straight, so a hand held still is held with the joints that will not stiffen. The amounts quoted in teaching, such as a wrist extended by some degrees, are illustrations to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

The practical details of a hand splint decide whether it helps or harms. It is padded wherever it presses on a bony point, applied over the position found, and secured firmly enough to hold without stopping the circulation, and nothing is ever wrapped tightly around a finger, because a finger has little room for swelling. A sling supports the weight of the arm, and rings, watches and tight bracelets come off the injured hand before the swelling arrives, while a ring that cannot be removed is left alone and reported rather than cut or pulled by an untrained hand. The trained responder and the paramedic apply a splint within the procedures their service authorizes, the nurse and the physician choose the definitive immobilization, and the surgeon decides when the position of the bones must be changed. Swelling is expected, so the hand is raised when the patient is sitting or lying, and a cold pack wrapped in cloth may be applied by a responder authorized to do so. The patient is kept warm, because cold is a complication of trauma, and a patient who may need an operation is kept without food and drink by instruction of the clinician.

Imaging confirms what the examination has already suggested, and it is ordered by a clinician trained to interpret it. A radiograph is usually the first study, and the hand is imaged in more than one direction so that a displacement in the third direction is not hidden, with a view from the front, a view from the side and an angled view commonly used together because the bones overlap. The joint above and the joint below the injured bone are included, so that a second break at the base of a metacarpal or in the wrist is not missed. A special angled view of the metacarpal heads may be requested when a break of a knuckle is suspected but not seen, and computed tomography shows the detail of a complex break that enters a joint. Magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for soft tissues, including ligaments, tendons and cartilage, and ultrasound may be used in some services, though its availability differs between countries. The lay first aider never orders or interprets imaging, and the trained responder does not choose the study.

Reassessment is performed after every intervention, and it is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment. The examiner looks at the color and warmth of the fingers, feels the pulses, tests the sensation, asks the patient to move the fingers and asks about the pain, then checks the splint for tightness. Pain that increases instead of settling, pain out of proportion to the injury, numbness, tingling, weakness, a finger that has turned pale or cool, and a hand that has become tense and swollen are the findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation. A patient whose pain is controlled by the measures the local protocol allows, and whose hand stays warm, pink and movable, can be watched, but a patient who is getting worse is not watched further by the responder.

Compartment syndrome of the hand is uncommon and devastating, and it begins with pain that does not match the injury. The hand has several small compartments between the metacarpals, each bounded by bone and by the fascia that wraps the interosseous muscles, and pressure that rises inside one of them shuts down the smallest vessels while the pulse at the wrist continues. The hand becomes tense and swollen, the pain is worse when the fingers are straightened passively, and the patient may describe a deep ache or a burning that is not relieved by the measures the protocol allows. The diagnosis is clinical, so the responder does not wait for a confirmation that will arrive too late, and within the limits of the local protocol the constricting bandage, splint or cast is released by someone authorized to release it, the limb is not elevated above the level of the heart unless the local protocol for the hand directs otherwise, and the patient is referred urgently, because time is muscle.

Pain is treated in the hand as it is treated anywhere else, and the limits of this course are fixed. A patient in pain is assessed, the injury is immobilized, the swelling is managed and the clinician is told, while the choice of substance, the amount and the way it is given belong to the prescriber and to the local protocol, and no dose, no route and no regimen appear anywhere in this course. The finding that makes the decision urgent is pain that rises despite the measures taken, pain out of proportion to the injury, or pain that comes with numbness or with a hand that is becoming tense. The lay first aider gives no substance, the trained responder gives only what the service authorizes and carries, and the nurse and the physician act within their own license and the law of the country.

Definitive treatment begins with a decision about whether the position of the bones must be changed, and that decision belongs to a clinician. Reduction means restoring the broken ends to a better position, and it is done to relieve pressure on skin, nerve or vessel, to let the bone heal in a useful shape and to make later movement possible, and it is performed with the patient made comfortable, in a clinical setting, by a trained clinician whose technique was learned under supervision rather than from a book such as this one. What can go wrong is worth saying plainly, because a listener who understands the risk will not improvise: the bone ends may injure a nerve or a vessel as they move, a closed injury may be opened by the attempt, and a break that was stable may become unstable. A reduction must not be attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when the vessels or the nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, when the responder lacks the training or the equipment, or when an earlier attempt has failed. In each of those situations the attempt can turn an injury that would have healed into one that cannot, and force is not a technique.

Most metacarpal fractures do not need an operation, and the majority are treated by holding the hand in a useful position and then moving it as soon as the clinician allows. The splint or cast, the position it holds and the length of time it stays are prescribed by the clinician, and a figure such as a few weeks is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. A break of the neck of the fifth metacarpal that angulates but does not rotate is often tolerated, and a residual bump on the back of the hand is a cosmetic finding in many patients. Certain fractures are referred to a surgeon, among them any rotation, an open fracture, a wound over a knuckle after a blow to the mouth, more than one bone broken, a break that enters a joint, a break of the base of the thumb metacarpal, and a hand with a nerve, vessel or tendon injury. Surgery may hold the fragments with wires through the skin, with screws, with plates or with a device outside the skin, and every operation on the hand is performed by a qualified surgeon.

The thumb is not a finger, and a fracture of its metacarpal is not treated like the rest of the hand. The first metacarpal is shorter and set apart, and its base carries the whole force of a pinch and a grip. A break that runs through that base and into the joint at the wrist is unstable, because the tendon that pulls the thumb away from the palm drags the lower fragment with it, and teaching gives such breaks names, one for a break into two parts and one for a break shattered into more than two. A patient with a painful, swollen thumb base after a fall is referred for imaging and for a decision by the clinician, and a responder never pulls on the thumb to test it.

The age of the patient changes the urgency of a hand injury even when the sequence does not change. A child's metacarpal still has a growth region near its ends, and an injury through that region can disturb the growth of the bone or of the whole ray, so a child who will not use a hand after a fall is referred even when the first radiograph looks modest. An older adult may break a metacarpal in a fall from standing height because the bone has lost density, and the fracture may hurt far less than its severity suggests. A bone that breaks without a strong injury may be weakened by disease, and such a break is referred for assessment of the bone as well as of the fracture.

Some errors are made so often that they deserve to be named. The first is to treat a hand injury casually because it is small, when the hand is the part of the body that most often decides whether a patient returns to work, and the second is to miss a rotation because the examiner looked at the hand rather than at the fingers. The third is to miss a human bite because the wound was small and the patient did not say, and the fourth is to leave a ring on a hand that is about to swell. The fifth is to apply a splint that is too tight and then fail to look at the fingers again, the sixth is to straighten a deformed hand in the field, and the seventh is to accept a pulse as proof that the vessels are safe. Scope of practice frames all of it, since the lay first aider supports and calls, the trained responder and the paramedic work within the procedures their service authorizes, and the surgeon decides when the bones must be repositioned. The record and the handover are part of the treatment, so the time, the mechanism, the position found and the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention are written in a fixed order, and a possible injury of the spine is treated as unstable until someone qualified clears it.

To summarize, a metacarpal fracture is an injury of the whole hand, and the bone that broke is only one of the structures the energy passed through. The fracture of the neck of the fifth metacarpal after a blow with the fist drops the knuckle and tilts the head toward the palm, and angulation there may be tolerated while rotation never is. Rotation is judged by looking at the fingers rather than at the hand, by the way they converge when the patient makes a fist and by the planes of the nails. The sequence is the same as everywhere in this course, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, with the neurovascular findings recorded before every intervention and repeated after it. The hand is splinted in the position found and never straightened in the field, the knuckles are kept bent in the position that leaves the hand useful, rings come off early, wounds are covered and never explored, and a wound over a knuckle after a blow to the mouth is treated as a human bite and referred urgently. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, no dose or route or regimen appears in this course, and a responder who suspects a compartment syndrome releases nothing outside the local protocol and refers the patient at once. This chapter is educational material, and it does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.
