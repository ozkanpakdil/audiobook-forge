# Chapter 64 — The Practical Skills Laboratory

This course has taught the assessment and the management of fractures and dislocations in words, and words are enough for the reasoning, because a listener who understands why a deformed limb is splinted in the position found will not straighten it in the field. Words are not enough for the hands, because the pressure that holds a fractured forearm firmly without squeezing it, and the difference between a pulse that is faint and a pulse that has gone, are learned by doing the act on a model or on a colleague and by being corrected while doing it. The practical skills laboratory is the place where that correction happens, and it rehearses the same order that every chapter of this course follows, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. It is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

Between knowing a step and performing it lies a distance that is wider than it looks from a page, because knowledge is tested in a quiet room and skill is tested in a noisy one. A responder who can recite the elements of the neurovascular examination may still hesitate when a limb is swelling under the hands, and a responder who has read about a log roll may still pull the shoulders while the pelvis stays behind. The laboratory narrows that distance by making the noise and the clock safe, so that an error costs a correction instead of a limb. Repetition does the rest, and a learner is allowed to fail in front of an instructor precisely so that the failure is not stored up for a patient. The laboratory is run by an instructor the training program authorizes, and what is taught there is defined by the curriculum of that program and by the body that certifies the learner.

A station begins with a briefing, which states what the skill is for, what the patient must gain from it, and what would make the attempt unsafe, so that the learner meets the failure before meeting a patient. The next step is a demonstration by the instructor, performed at the speed of a real patient rather than at the speed of a teaching video. Only then does the learner perform the skill, first on a manikin and then on an uninjured colleague who can say what the hands felt like. The station ends with a debrief in which the learner describes what went well and what went wrong before the instructor adds anything, because a learner who finds the error is more likely to keep the correction. Order matters in the practice room for the same reason it matters at the roadside, since each step protects the next one and a skill assembled out of order will be performed out of order.

One rule governs every station and is spoken before the learner touches anything, which is that no skill is practiced on a patient for the first time. The first attempt belongs to the manikin, to the padded model limb or to the uninjured colleague who has volunteered to take part, and the patient meets the skill only after the learner has performed it correctly under supervision. A learner in a recognized training program may then carry out the act on a patient within the authorization that the program and the local protocol give, with a supervisor present who is qualified to take over. A learner who is not in such a program never performs the act on a patient at all, however well the manikin went, and no learner is ever the most senior pair of hands on an unstable limb. The rule exists because a patient is not a rehearsal, and because the harm done by an untrained hand is done in the moment when help is furthest away.

Assessment of the injured limb is the first skill of the laboratory, and its purpose is to produce a description rather than a diagnosis. The learner looks at the limb as a whole and compares it with the other side, noting the position, the swelling, the color of the skin and any wound. The learner then feels with the flat of the hand and with the fingertips, beginning away from the most painful place, and notes the temperature and where the tenderness begins and ends. The patient is asked to move the limb gently, and the movement is never forced, because the examination is meant to gather information rather than to test the injury. The learner writes down what was seen and what the patient said, using the words the patient used, and leaves the interpretation to the clinician who will see the patient next.

The assessor follows the order rather than the answer, and looks for the comparison with the other side, for movement that was requested rather than imposed, and for a record that names findings instead of conclusions. An attempt ends immediately if the learner forces a joint, pulls a limb to see whether it moves, or hunts for the grinding felt when broken ends rub together, because each of those acts adds pain and can add damage. The attempt also ends if the learner continues while the patient reports pain that is rising, and stopping at the right moment counts as a pass rather than as a failure. In a constructed illustration that describes no real patient, a learner holds a simulated forearm, feels the swelling increase under the fingers, and says aloud that the examination stops there and that the limb will be splinted as it lies, which is exactly the answer the station is designed to hear.

Every station is also a station in dignity, warmth and hygiene, and these are judged as strictly as the technique. The learner wears personal protective equipment when the scenario calls for it, cleans the hands before and after contact, and asks the patient before exposing a limb. The part is uncovered only as far as the task requires, and it is covered again as soon as the hands have finished. A manikin cannot complain of cold, so the learner rehearses the covering as a deliberate act, since cold is a complication of trauma and a patient left uncovered while a splint is adjusted is being harmed by the treatment rather than by the injury. The assessor watches for the blanket and for the quiet words that tell the patient what is about to happen.

Restraint is the word the laboratory uses for the second skill, because stabilization in the field is a matter of holding still rather than of correcting. The learner supports the injured part in the position in which it was found, takes the weight with both hands, and holds the joints above and below the injury still. A deformed limb is never straightened in the field, not even in a practice scenario, because the habit is built by the hands rather than by the intention, and because vessels and nerves lie against the bone where a helpful-looking movement can tear them. Holding is tiring, so the learners practice the exchange of a hold, in which the new pair of hands takes the load before the old pair releases it. The lay first aider performs this skill with the hands, a pillow or whatever is at hand, while formal splinting belongs to the trained responder and the paramedic.

The neurovascular examination is its own station and the one that is repeated most often, because it is performed before every intervention and again after it. The learner looks at the color and the temperature of the skin, feels the pulse beyond the injury, presses briefly on a nail to watch the color return, and tests sensation on both sides so that the two can be compared. The patient is asked about numbness, tingling and weakness, and about any movement that was possible a moment ago and is not possible now. The learner practices saying the findings aloud in a fixed order and writing them at the bedside rather than from memory, because a finding that was not recorded before a splint was applied cannot be attributed afterwards. The lay first aider is taught the simpler version of this observation, which is color, warmth and the ability of the patient to move and to feel, while the formal examination belongs to the trained responder and to the clinician.

An important part of this station is the lesson that a pulse is a poor comfort, because a pulse can still be felt at the wrist or the ankle while the pressure inside a compartment is already strangling the muscle. A present pulse therefore never excludes a serious injury, and an absent pulse turns the moment into an emergency. The examiner should be the same person before and after an intervention wherever the service allows it, since two examiners may disagree about a faint pulse and neither of them will be wrong. Where a service requires the examination to be repeated at a fixed interval, that interval is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because services differ in how often they require it and in who is authorized to perform it.

Applying a splint is the third skill, and it is taught as a way of preventing movement rather than as a way of correcting position. The learner pads the splint where it will press on bone, applies it over the limb as it lies, and includes the joint above and the joint below the injury, because a bone that is held alone can still be moved by the joint at either end. The straps are tightened until the limb is held and no further, and the material matters less than the principle, since a folded magazine, a padded board or a molded splint each serves the same purpose. The learner then checks the fingers or the toes for color, warmth, sensation and movement, and writes the finding beside the earlier one. Splinting in the field is the work of the trained responder and the paramedic within the procedures their service authorizes, while the physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides what definitive immobilization is required.

A splint is a treatment that can injure, so the laboratory teaches its complications with the same care as its benefits. A strap that is too tight, a knot that presses on a nerve where the bone lies close to the skin, or a pad that has slipped while the patient was moved, each causes damage that appears after the splint and is blamed on the fracture. The learner is therefore taught to check the splint as part of the reassessment, to lift the edge and look at the skin, and to ask the patient about new numbness or new pain. If there is resistance, if the skin changes color, or if the pain rises rather than settles, the responder stops, does not force the device into a better position, and calls for a higher level of care. Force is not a technique in the practice room, and it is not a technique in the field.

The sling and the swathe are small skills that are easy to perform badly, and the laboratory treats them as seriously as the splint. A sling supports the forearm and takes the weight of the arm away from the shoulder girdle, and it is used for injuries of the shoulder, the clavicle, the upper arm and some injuries of the forearm and the hand. The learner is assessed on whether the fingertips stay visible so that the circulation can be watched, on whether the knot sits away from the back of the neck, and on whether the arm hangs in the position the injury tolerates rather than in a position the learner finds tidy. A swathe binds the arm to the chest to limit movement, and it is never drawn so tight that breathing becomes an effort. Binding the chest to splint fractured ribs is avoided in many services because it restricts breathing and increases the risk of a chest infection, and where practice differs the local protocol decides.

The log roll is the skill that most often reveals whether a team can work as one body. It is a way of turning a patient onto the side so that the back can be examined, a device can be placed beneath the body, or a blocked airway can be cleared, and what it must achieve is a turn in which the head, the shoulders, the pelvis and the legs move together. The team has one leader who gives the count and one person at the head who owns the alignment of the neck, and every other pair of hands owns a named part of the body. The patient is turned only as far as the task requires and is returned without delay, because the position is a means and not a destination. The log roll is not a technique for the lay first aider, and it belongs to the trained responder, the emergency medical technician and the paramedic, performed within the procedures their service authorizes.

The station is judged on the stop as much as on the turn, because the maneuver is abandoned whenever it becomes unsafe. If the patient fights the hands that hold the neck, if a limb cannot move with the body, if the alignment is lost, or if the team disagrees about the count, the leader stops the maneuver and the team returns the patient to the position found. No single responder performs a manual handling technique alone, and no chapter may describe a lone technique as safe, because an unstable spine moves as a unit only when the people around it move as a unit. The spine is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has done so, and clearing it is a judgment that combines the mechanism of injury, the examination and the imaging rather than a single test. The learner practices the words that ask for that clearance, because the request is a part of the skill and not a pause before it.

The stretcher and the devices that go with it are taught as answers to particular problems rather than as a list of equipment. The scoop stretcher separates into two halves that slide beneath the patient and lock together, so that a patient found on the ground can be raised with as little movement of the trunk as possible. The long board is rigid and flat, and its greatest value lies in freeing a patient from a confined space, while a hard surface that does not give transmits every bump of the road to the skin of the back, the heels and the head. The padded stretcher and the vacuum mattress spread the weight of the body, and the choice among these devices belongs to whoever is leading the move, within the equipment the service carries and the protocol it follows. Practice differs between services about how long a patient may be carried on a hard board, and any figure for that belongs to the current guideline rather than to this course.

The transfer drill is practiced until the plan comes before the hands, which means that the move is spoken aloud before it begins. Every responder knows which part of the body they own, where the patient is going, and who is watching the injured part, and the fractured bone is never used as a handle, because the bone is a lever and the soft tissues attached to it take the strain. Where the patient can be slid along a surface rather than lifted above it, the slide is preferred, since it needs fewer hands and moves the spine less. The straps and the locks are checked before the lift, the skin is inspected where a device meets the body, and the leader watches the patient rather than the equipment. The number of responders a move requires depends on the size of the patient, the device and the protocol, so any figure offered for it is an illustration to be confirmed against the guideline in force and the procedure of the service.

Imaging is the step that the laboratory can teach only from the outside, because interpretation is a clinical skill that belongs to the radiographer who takes the study and to the clinician who reads it. What the learner can practice is everything around the study, which is the safe transfer of the patient to the imaging table, the immobilization that travels with the patient, the warmth that is maintained in a cold room, and the neurovascular examination that is recorded before and after the journey. A radiograph is usually the first study and is taken in at least two directions, while computed tomography shows the detail of a complex break and magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for the soft tissues. The learner is assessed on knowing that a study is ordered by a clinician and that a lay first aider never orders or interprets one. An image is a picture of one moment, and the patient must still be examined after it has been taken.

Reassessment is drilled as a skill in its own right, because it is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment. The same observations are repeated in the same order after every intervention, and the record of the earlier examination is read before the new one is written. Pain that increases instead of settling, pain out of proportion to the injury, numbness, tingling, weakness, and a limb that becomes tense and swollen are the findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation. A responder who suspects a compartment syndrome does not wait for the diagnosis to be confirmed, because the diagnosis is clinical and time is muscle. A constricting bandage, splint or cast is released only by someone the local protocol authorizes to release it, the limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, the patient is kept warm, and the patient is referred urgently.

The laboratory teaches the definitive treatments by name and by purpose, and leaves the technique where it belongs. Reduction restores the position of the bone ends, traction holds them in line with a gentle continuous pull, and a cast or a surgical fixation holds the position while the bone heals, and none of those acts may be learned from a book. The learner may watch a demonstration, may handle the devices, and may be told what each one is meant to achieve, but the technique itself is acquired in supervised clinical training and is performed according to local protocol by a physician or an orthopedic surgeon. A reduction is not attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when the vessels or the nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, or when the responder lacks the training and the equipment. In each of those situations an attempt can turn an injury that would have healed into one that cannot, and the learner is assessed on recognizing those situations rather than on attempting the act.

The open fracture has its own station, because it is the injury in which the wrong reflex causes the most harm. The learner covers the wound with a sterile dressing, controls the bleeding that can be controlled, records the neurovascular status, immobilizes the limb in the position found, keeps the patient warm, and prepares an urgent referral. An embedded object is never removed and exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, because the object may be holding pressure on a torn vessel and the bone may be the only thing closing the wound. An open wound is never explored blindly, since exploration adds contamination and pain, and definitive cleaning and closure belong to the operating room and to nobody else. The learner is assessed on what is not done as much as on what is done, which is the mark of a skill that has been understood rather than memorized.

Pain, tetanus protection and antibiotics appear in the practice scenarios, and they are handled there with deliberate vagueness. The scenario states who decides, which finding makes the decision urgent, and what the scope of practice of the responder forbids, and it says plainly that the substance, the amount and the way it is given belong to the prescriber and to the protocol in force. A learner must not be able to take a prescription from this course, and the laboratory is no different, so the station is assessed on the recognition of severe pain, on the request for help, and on the accurate reporting of what the patient said and what has changed. The nurse and the physician act within the authority the law and the employer give them, and the learner observes that boundary rather than rehearsing an act that is not theirs.

Some patients change the practice without changing the sequence, and the laboratory keeps manikins of different sizes for exactly that reason. The child is not a small adult, because the head is large in proportion to the body and a device sized for an adult will not hold a small body in line, so the learner practices on a child-sized model and practices the words used to explain the examination to a frightened parent. The older adult brings thin skin that tears under a grip, bone that breaks under a hand laid on a rib cage, and a fracture that may hurt far less than its severity suggests. A pregnant patient is two patients for the purpose of assessment and one patient for the purpose of handling, so the position in which she is carried is decided by a clinician or by the protocol of the service. In each of these patients the learner rehearses a lower threshold for immobilization and for referral rather than a different order of steps.

The skills of talking are assessed beside the skills of the hands, because a frightened patient who is not told what is about to happen will tense against the very hands that are trying to help. The learner says what will be done, asks permission before exposure, warns the patient before a movement, and takes the answer seriously even when the answer is a request to stop. The debrief of a station is blame-free, since a learner who is humiliated does not report the near miss, and a near miss reported in the practice room is an injury prevented at the roadside. Every station ends with a written record of the practice, signed by the supervisor who watched it, and the certifying body that recognizes the training defines what the learner may do afterward and what still requires supervision.

The intervals for refreshing a skill differ between services, countries and certifying bodies, so any schedule named in a course or a policy is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the rule of the authority that certifies the responder. Simulation also has honest limits, because a manikin does not bleed, does not feel pain and does not deteriorate, so the feel of a limb that has become tight, or of a pulse that has become hard to find, is learned on real patients under supervision. What the laboratory can give is the order of the actions, the habit of the neurovascular record, and the reflex to stop and to call for help, and those three are worth the hours. The lay first aider learns a smaller set of acts, which are to call for help, to keep the patient still and warm, to cover a wound, and to support a limb as it lies.

To summarize, the practical skills laboratory is where the reasoning of this course becomes the work of the hands, and it follows the same order as every chapter, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. The skills practiced there are the examination of the injured limb, the support of that limb in the position found, the neurovascular record written before and after every intervention, the splint, the sling and the swathe, the log roll, the transfer onto a stretcher, and the reassessment that catches the complication caused by the treatment. No skill is practiced on a patient for the first time, because the first attempt belongs to a manikin or to an uninjured colleague, and a learner acts on a patient only within the authorization of a training program and the local protocol. Never straighten a deformed limb in the field, never remove an embedded object, never push exposed bone back into a wound, never explore an open wound blindly, and never perform a manual handling technique alone. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and a reduction is not attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when vessels or nerves are failing, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, or when the training and the equipment are lacking. Any interval, temperature or number of responders named in a policy is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, the patient is kept warm, the spine is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has done so, and the responder stops and escalates when there is resistance or a complication. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.
