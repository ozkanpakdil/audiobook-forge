# Chapter 50 — Major Dislocations

A dislocated joint is an injury in which the surfaces that should glide over one another lose contact, and at the major joints it can cost a limb within hours. What it costs depends on where the bone end comes to rest, because that is where the vessels and the nerves are lying. This chapter asks of every major joint three questions, whether the injury threatens the limb, whether it threatens a nerve, and whether it can wait for definitive care. The order of work is the order this course follows everywhere, to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

Three clocks start when a major joint leaves its socket, and each runs at its own speed. The first is the clock of the blood supply, because the bone end may have torn the vessel that feeds it, and a bone end that loses its supply can die, which is called avascular necrosis. The second is the clock of the nerve and the vessel that lie against the bone, because they may recover once the pressure is relieved but not if the relief comes days later. The third is the clock of the cartilage, because surfaces out of contact undergo changes that cause pain and stiffness long afterwards. Teaching attaches a number of hours to the first two clocks, and any such figure is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

Ranking the joints by urgency rests on which structure is trapped rather than on how dramatic the deformity looks. The knee, the hip and the ankle threaten the whole limb within hours, because at each of them a major artery passes close to the bone. The shoulder, the elbow and the wrist threaten a nerve more often than the limb, because a nerve lies in a narrow passage that the displaced bone end can compress. The small joints of the fingers, the thumb and the patella usually wait, provided the skin is intact, the pulse is present and no fragment is caught between the surfaces. Waiting is a decision about the clock and never a permission to leave the patient unexamined.

Assessment comes first, and it begins with the scene and with the mechanism of injury. The mechanism matters, because a fall onto an outstretched hand, a blow to the knee and a collision against a dashboard each produce their own pattern of damage. The history adds the age of the patient, the time of the injury, any earlier dislocation of the same joint, and the illnesses that change the strength of bone. The primary survey follows, and its steps are remembered as ABCDE, meaning airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure, because a patient with a dislocated hip may also have injuries to the head or the spine. Life comes before limb whenever two correct actions compete for the same pair of hands. The lay first aider looks for danger and sends for help, while the trained first responder and the emergency medical technician work within the procedures their service authorizes.

Stabilization of a dislocated joint in the field is a short list of acts, and the most important of them is restraint. A deformed limb is supported in the position found and is never straightened, because the vessels and the nerves lie against the bone and a movement that looks helpful can tear what was intact. Pulling a limb straight can also convert a closed dislocation into an open one. Bleeding that can be controlled is controlled with direct pressure, and an open wound is covered with a sterile dressing and never explored blindly. An embedded object is never removed, because it may be plugging the hole in the vessel beneath it. Cold is a complication of trauma rather than a detail of comfort, so the patient is insulated and the injured part is kept warm. The lay first aider supports the limb with the hands or with a pillow, while the trained responder and the paramedic may splint within the equipment their service authorizes.

The neurovascular examination is the step this course will not allow anyone to skip, and at a dislocated joint it is done before any intervention and repeated after every one. The examiner looks at the color and the temperature of the skin beyond the injury, feels the pulse beyond the injury, and asks the patient to move the fingers or the toes. Sensation is compared between the two sides, and numbness, tingling or the loss of a movement is recorded in the words of the patient. The result is written before the support is applied and again afterwards, because a finding not recorded before an intervention cannot be attributed afterwards. The joint tells the examiner what to test, since the axillary nerve is at risk at the shoulder, the median nerve crosses the wrist, the sciatic nerve lies behind the hip, and the peroneal nerve is the one to watch at the knee.

Immobilization means preventing movement of the injured joint and of the bones above and below it, because a supported joint can still be moved by the limb around it. The support is padded where it presses on bone, applied over the position in which the joint was found, and secured firmly enough to hold without stopping the circulation. A folded magazine, a padded board or the body of the patient can each serve, though an improvised device shifts and must be checked more often. A support that is too tight is more dangerous than none, because it can produce the very compartment syndrome that immobilization was meant to prevent. The lay first aider holds and pads, the nurse checks the fingers or the toes and reports any change, the paramedic applies splints within the equipment the service authorizes, and the physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides what definitive immobilization is required.

Imaging is ordered when the answer it gives will change what is done, and its place follows the examination rather than replacing it. The first study is usually a plain radiograph, taken in at least two directions so that a displacement in the third direction is not hidden, and it includes the joint above and the joint below. Computed tomography shows the detail of a complex break and often reveals a fragment lying between the joint surfaces, while magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for the soft tissues. A joint that has been put back is imaged again afterwards, because the surfaces may be in contact while a fragment remains trapped. The study is requested and interpreted by a clinician trained to do it, and the lay first aider never orders and never interprets an image.

Reassessment is done after every intervention, and at a dislocated joint it catches the complication caused by the treatment. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the record, and it is the comparison rather than any single measurement that carries the value. The examiner checks the color, the warmth, the pulse, the sensation, the movement and the pain, and checks the support for tightness. Pain that increases instead of settling, numbness, tingling, weakness and a limb that turns pale and cool are the findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation. When compartment syndrome is suspected, the constricting bandage, splint or cast is released within the limits of the local protocol by someone authorized to do it, the limb is kept at the level of the heart rather than above it, and the patient is referred urgently, because the diagnosis is clinical and time is muscle.

Referral or definitive treatment is the last step of the sequence, and it begins with the recognition that some joints cannot be managed where the patient lies. The patients moved without delay are the one with an open dislocation, the one with a fracture and a dislocation together, and the one whose pulse or nerve deficit has not recovered. The receiving facility needs imaging, an operating room and access to an intensive care unit, and the receiving team is given the story the record tells. Reduction, traction and casting, which restore the joint surfaces and hold them while the tissues heal, are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and the technique is not taught from a book. A reduction is not attempted when the surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is trapped, when the vessels or the nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, when training or equipment is lacking, or when an earlier attempt has failed. Force is not a technique, and resistance during an attempt is a signal to stop and to escalate.

The shoulder is the joint that dislocates most often, because its socket is shallow and it is held mainly by soft tissue rather than by bone. In most cases the bone end leaves toward the front, so the arm is held away from the body and the patient resists any attempt to bring it in. The nerve most often injured winds around the surgical neck of the bone, and the finding is numbness over the outer shoulder with weakness when the patient lifts the arm from the side. That nerve is recorded before anything is done and again after the joint has been put back, because a deficit that appears afterwards is an emergency. The shoulder may also tear the rim of the socket or dent the head of the bone, and none of that can be told apart by looking and feeling alone.

The age of the patient changes what a shoulder dislocation costs, and the change alters the threshold for referral. In a young patient the ligaments are strong and the force is violent, so the rim of the socket tears and the joint is likely to come out again, sometimes repeatedly. In an older patient the tissues are more brittle, so a dislocation that looks less violent may tear the tendons that raise the arm, injure the nerve far more often, and even tear the artery that supplies the arm. A shoulder that has dislocated before comes out again with less force each time, so that detail belongs in the history. The responder supports the arm in the position found, records the neurovascular findings and transfers the patient, and no attempt is made to put the shoulder back in the field, even when the deformity is familiar.

At the elbow the bone end usually leaves toward the back, and because the joint lies between the arm and the forearm it is surrounded by the nerves and the artery that cross its front. The median nerve is the one most often injured, and the ulnar and radial nerves are exposed as well, so the examination tests the ability to make a fist, to spread the fingers and to straighten the wrist. The artery that supplies the whole forearm passes in front of the joint, so a lost or weakened pulse at the wrist after an elbow dislocation is an emergency of the limb. The joint sits in a tight envelope of muscle and fascia, so swelling raises the pressure inside the forearm quickly, and the pressure can rise while a large pulse is still felt. The responder supports the limb in the position found, records the findings, keeps the patient warm and moves the patient to a clinician.

An elbow that has dislocated is treated with urgency in most services, because the artery lies in front of the joint and the compartment around it is closed. When the radial head and the small projection at the front of the ulna are also broken, the joint is unstable in every direction and needs an operating room rather than an emergency department. An open elbow injury is contaminated even when the wound is small, so the responder covers the wound, records the neurovascular state, immobilizes the limb and arranges urgent transfer. Blind exploration of such a wound adds harm, exposed bone is never pushed back into it, and definitive cleansing and closure belong to the operating room. Casting and the surgical repair of the ligaments are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and any figure for how long the joint is held still is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline.

The elbow of a young child is a separate problem, and one pattern of it is quiet. A small child lifted or pulled by the hand may hold the arm still at the side and refuse to use it, without swelling and without any injury to the skin, because the head of one of the forearm bones has slipped out from under the ring of ligament that holds it. That injury is a subluxation rather than a complete dislocation, it is common in the early years of life, and the child is otherwise well. The examination, the decision about imaging and the maneuver that restores the bone to its place all belong to a clinician, because a child's elbow can also hide a break at the growing end of the bone. The lay first aider supports the arm in the position found and takes the child for assessment, and no one pulls on the arm to test it.

Dislocations at the wrist and in the small bones of the hand are less dramatic and far easier to miss. The bone at the center of the wrist can slip out of its place and take its neighbors with it, a pattern called a perilunate dislocation, and the median nerve then loses its supply while the skin looks almost normal. The findings are numbness and tingling in the thumb, the index finger, the middle finger and half of the ring finger, with weakness when the patient brings the thumb away from the palm. The wrist itself can also be driven out of place, and that injury nearly always comes with a break of one of the bones around it, so an apparently simple dislocation is treated as a fracture until a clinician has shown otherwise. These injuries threaten the nerve and the function of the hand more than the limb, so the responder supports the wrist in the position found, records the findings and transfers the patient.

The joints of the fingers and the thumb dislocate far more often than any other joint, and most of them do so toward the back of the hand after the finger is bent backward. One pattern deserves particular caution, because the head of a bone of the hand has passed through the tough sheet of tissue on the palm and is caught there, and that joint cannot be brought back by pulling on the finger. A wound over a finger joint that was made by a tooth or by a fist is treated as a contaminated joint injury even when it looks trivial, and it needs an operating room rather than a dressing alone. These injuries rarely threaten the limb and rarely threaten a nerve, so most of them may wait a few hours for definitive care, but waiting never means leaving the hand unsupported or unreferred. The responder supports the finger in the position found, records the color, the warmth and the sensation, and passes the patient on to a clinician.

The hip is a deep ball and socket joint, and the bone end usually leaves it toward the back after a collision. Because the socket is deep, the bone end cannot leave without tearing the capsule and often without breaking the rim of the socket or the head of the bone itself. The patient typically lies with the injured leg shortened and turned inward, and any attempt to straighten it is painful and dangerous. The nerve at risk runs from the back of the pelvis down the whole leg, and the findings are weakness of the muscles that lift the foot with loss of sensation over the outer part of the foot. The blood supply of the head of the bone is also at stake, because the vessel that feeds it runs in the capsule the dislocation tears, and a head that loses its supply can collapse years later. The hip is therefore treated as an emergency, and any figure for how quickly it must be put back is an illustration to be confirmed against the local protocol.

A hip that is out of place is put back by a physician or an orthopedic surgeon, usually with medicine that relaxes the muscles and often in an operating room, and the responder never attempts it. Before and after that procedure the nerve is examined, the pulse in the foot is checked and an image is taken, because a second injury to the socket or the knee is common. In a child the same presentation may be a slip at the growing part of the thigh bone, and a slip treated as a sprain can lead to a lifetime of hip disease. An older adult whose hip has been replaced with a prosthesis presents a third difficulty, because that joint can come out of place easily and the decisions belong to the surgeon who knows the implant. In all of these groups the responder protects the limb in the position found, keeps the patient warm and still, and moves the patient without delay.

A dislocation of the knee is rare, and it is the most dangerous dislocation in the body, because the artery that supplies the whole leg passes behind the joint and is tethered above and below it. When the bone ends separate, that artery is stretched, torn or blocked, and the leg below the knee begins to lose its supply while the pulse may still seem reassuring. The nerve that runs around the head of the small bone below the knee is injured in a large share of these cases, and the finding is a foot that cannot be lifted with numbness on the outer side of the leg. A large fraction of knee dislocations go back into place by themselves, so the deformity is absent, yet the swelling is tense and the injury is still a vascular emergency. The responder supports the knee in the position found, records the pulse, the sensation and the movement of the foot, and transfers the patient to a facility with vascular surgery.

Because the limb itself is in question, the assessment of the vessels is repeated and recorded at short intervals while the patient waits. The clinician may measure the ratio of the pressure taken at the ankle to the pressure taken at the arm, but the meaning of the result belongs to the surgeon. A pulse that is present does not settle the question, because an artery can be injured while blood still flows through it and the flow can stop later as the vessel clots. The findings that make the situation urgent are a pulse that has become weaker, a limb that has turned pale and cool, increasing pain, and any new weakness or numbness, and any one of them is reported at once. In a constructed illustration, a patient who cannot lift the foot after such an injury is treated as a vascular emergency until a surgeon has said otherwise. The patient is moved to a facility that can repair a vessel, and the time allowed for that transfer is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

The patella is the small bone that sits in front of the knee, and it usually leaves its groove toward the outer side of the leg when the knee is twisted with the leg straight. The joint fills with blood, the knee swells quickly and the patient holds it slightly bent, and the deformity is often obvious. It frequently slips back into its groove by itself, and when it does not, a clinician may restore it with a gentle act, and the responder does not attempt it. The injury is often accompanied by a small break at the inner edge of the patella, which can leave a loose body inside the joint, so an image is always taken. A patella that has come out of place is not usually a threat to the limb or to a nerve, so it may wait a few hours, but the knee around it is examined as carefully as the bone itself. The responder supports the knee in the position found, records the neurovascular findings, keeps the patient warm and passes the patient on.

The ankle is a joint in which the bone of the leg sits in a socket formed by the bones of the foot. A dislocation of the ankle almost always comes with a break of one or both of the leg bones, and the bone end can press the skin from within until the skin gives way, which turns a closed injury into an open one. The skin over the inner side of the ankle is thin and poorly supplied, so an injury that tents it is an emergency even before the skin breaks, and a clinician may need to restore the position of the bone. The bone at the center of the foot can also leave its place, and its blood supply is fragile, so that injury is treated with the same urgency. These injuries threaten the skin and the supply of the bone, and they are transferred urgently for imaging and definitive care. The responder supports the limb in the position found, covers any wound with a sterile dressing, records the findings and keeps the limb warm.

Pain is not only a symptom in this work, because severe pain produces muscle spasm that holds the joint out of place and masks the findings on which the neurovascular examination depends. Relief of pain therefore has a place, and the place is decided by a prescriber rather than by the responder or by the family of the patient. The finding that makes the decision urgent is pain that is severe, escalating or out of proportion to the injury, because that kind of pain is a sign of a developing problem. The lay first aider gives no medicine at all, the trained responder gives only what the local protocol authorizes, and strong analgesia and sedation belong to a physician. No dose, no route and no regimen is given anywhere in this course, because the substance and the amount belong to the local protocol and to the prescriber. Splinting, position, reassurance, warmth and an explanation of what is happening are the comfort measures that stay within every scope of practice.

The record and the handover are part of the treatment rather than a formality that follows it, and at a dislocated joint they carry more weight than usual. The time of the injury, the mechanism, the position in which the limb was found, the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention and any change in the patient are written in a fixed order. Several errors recur in this work, and each has a reason attached. The first is straightening a deformed limb in the field, which risks the vessels and the nerves that lie against the bone, and the second is attempting a reduction more than once after an attempt has failed. The third is delaying referral for an image that will not change the immediate plan, the fourth is failing to record the neurovascular findings, and the fifth is attributing a new numbness or weakness to the original injury.

To summarize, a dislocation of a major joint is an injury of the whole joint, and what it costs depends on which vessel, which nerve and which blood supply lie against the bone end that has moved. The sequence never changes, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine and record the neurovascular status before and after every intervention, to immobilize in the position found, to image when the answer will change what is done, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. The knee, the hip and the ankle can cost the limb within hours, so they are moved without delay to a facility with an operating room, the shoulder, the elbow and the wrist threaten a nerve, so their nerve findings are examined with particular care, and the fingers, the thumb and the patella usually wait. A deformed limb is splinted in the position found and is never straightened in the field, an embedded object is never removed, exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, an open wound is never explored blindly, and definitive closure belongs to the operating room. A reduction is not attempted when the surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is trapped, when the vessels or the nerves are failing, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, or when training is lacking, and reduction, traction and casting are in every case learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol. Compartment syndrome is treated as an emergency, the limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, cold is treated as a complication, and the spine is treated as unstable until it is cleared by someone qualified to clear it.
