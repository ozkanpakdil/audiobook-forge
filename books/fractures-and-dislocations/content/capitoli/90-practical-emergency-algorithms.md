# Appendix A — Practical Emergency Algorithms

These pathways are the emergency routes of the course, set out as ordered prose so that they can be followed by ear when the hands are busy. Each one begins with the finding that starts it, then gives the steps in the order in which they are performed, then marks the point at which the responder stops and calls for a higher level of care, and then names the scope of practice of each act. The same shape runs through all of them, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. The pathways are the unstable patient, the threatened limb, the suspected compartment syndrome, the open fracture, the suspected spinal injury and the dislocated joint. Nothing here replaces training, certification, supervision, local protocol or the law of the country in which you work.

Every pathway opens with the same two acts, and neither of them touches the injured part. The first is to make the scene safe enough to work in, which means traffic, fire, electricity, glass and the personal protective equipment that keeps blood off the skin of the rescuer, and a responder who cannot make the scene safe calls for help and waits, because a second casualty helps nobody. The second is to judge whether the patient is stable, since a patient who cannot breathe will not be helped by a perfect splint. The course therefore puts the primary survey before the limb and names its steps in the order in which they are spoken, which are airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure, remembered as ABCDE, with the lay first aider doing the acts that need no equipment and everyone else working inside the equipment, the authorization and the local protocol that apply to their own level of practice.

The first pathway is for the patient who is unstable, and the finding that starts it is a patient who does not respond, who is not breathing normally, whose pulse is weak and rapid, whose skin is cold and damp, or who is losing blood faster than it can be replaced. The bleeding that kills first is usually outside the body and within reach, so the trained responder looks for a spurting wound, a dressing soaked through, a swelling that grows while the patient grows quieter, or a limb enlarging under its splint. The lay first aider who finds a patient unresponsive and not breathing normally calls for help at once and begins compression and rescue breathing if trained to do so, because CPR is a skill learned on a manikin with a teacher and refreshed in practice. A tourniquet, where it is used at all, is applied by a trained responder or a paramedic under the procedures of the service, and only for life-threatening bleeding from a limb that direct pressure has failed to control.

Once the catastrophic bleeding has been addressed, the order continues with the airway, which the trained responder opens with a simple position of the head or a device the service authorizes, while an advanced airway belongs to the paramedic or to the clinician trained to place it. Breathing comes next, and oxygen and assisted ventilation are given only by those whose authorization and equipment permit it. Circulation is supported by laying the patient flat, by keeping the patient warm, and by treating the injured limb as a source of concealed bleeding that is splinted rather than straightened, because moving the broken ends disturbs the clot and enlarges the loss. Disability is judged by the level of consciousness and by the pupils. Exposure means undressing the patient enough to find every injury and then covering the patient again at once, because cold is a complication of trauma rather than a detail of comfort.

Throughout this pathway the injured limb waits, because life comes before limb in the order of the hands and not only in the order of the words. A deformed thigh or a dislocated ankle is supported as it was found, splinted by the trained responder within the equipment the service provides, and left alone until the patient is stable enough for the limb to be examined. The responder stops and escalates to the highest level available when the airway is lost, when breathing becomes inadequate, when the pulse disappears, when consciousness falls further, or when bleeding cannot be controlled by the means at hand. Reassessment follows each intervention and is compared with what was written before, and the receiving team is told before arrival what was found, what was done and what has changed, so that it continues a story instead of starting one.

The second pathway is for the threatened limb, and it begins with a finding rather than with a diagnosis. The finding is a limb that has turned pale or dusky, that is cool to the touch, that is painful out of proportion to the injury, that is numb or tingling, that is weak, or that has no pulse beyond the fracture, and any one of those signs starts the pathway. A pulse that is still present does not exclude serious injury, because the flow through the smallest vessels can fail while the large artery still carries a beat to the examining fingers. The injury that most often produces this picture is a fracture that has displaced, a break near a joint, a joint that has dislocated, or a fragment lying against a vessel. The lay first aider notices color, warmth and the ability of the patient to move and to feel, keeps the limb supported as found, keeps the patient warm, and sends for help without delay.

The steps follow the order of the course, and the first of them is the neurovascular examination performed before anything else is done. The trained responder or the clinician looks at color and temperature, feels the pulse beyond the injury, presses briefly on a nail to watch the color return, tests sensation on both sides, and asks the patient to move the fingers or the toes, recording every finding as far as possible. The limb is then supported and splinted in the position in which it was found, and it is never straightened in the field, because vessels and nerves lie against the bone and a movement that looks helpful can tear what remained intact. A wound, if there is one, is covered with a sterile dressing, and the bleeding that can be controlled is controlled with pressure applied around the wound rather than into it. The patient is kept warm and the limb is not raised above the level of the heart, because raising it can reduce the flow the limb still has.

The responder stops and escalates at the moment the limb becomes pulseless, or at the moment its color, warmth, sensation or movement is worse than it was before the splint was applied, and any constricting material is then released by whoever the local protocol authorizes to release it. Nobody in the field reduces a fracture or a dislocation in order to restore a pulse, because force applied to an injured joint can turn an injury that would have healed into one that cannot, and because a reduction performed without imaging and without supervised training may trap the very vessel it was meant to free. The warning to the receiving team is spoken before the patient arrives rather than left as a note for later. Where a period of time is named in this pathway, it is an illustration to be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such periods differ between services, countries and editions.

The third pathway is for the suspected compartment syndrome, and it starts with pain rather than with a pulse. The finding is a limb that is tense and swollen, painful out of proportion to the injury, worse when the muscles inside are stretched, and accompanied by numbness, tingling or weakness that grows instead of settling. A compartment is a closed space bounded by bone and fascia, and bleeding or swelling inside it raises the pressure until the flow through the smallest vessels fails, even while a large pulse at the wrist or the ankle can still be felt. For that reason an intact pulse never excludes the diagnosis, and a limb that is painful beyond its appearance is treated as an emergency. The diagnosis is clinical and is made by a clinician, and the responder who suspects it does not wait for confirmation, because time is muscle.

The responder compares the injured limb with the other side, records what is found and the time, and reports it at once to the clinician responsible for the patient. The constricting bandage, splint or cast is released within the limits of the local protocol by someone authorized to release it, and if that person is not on scene the patient is moved toward whoever is. The limb is not raised above the level of the heart, the patient is kept warm and still, and any measure for pain is the decision of the prescriber, because the substance, the amount and the way it is given belong to the local protocol rather than to this course. A clinician may measure the pressure within the compartment in millimeters of mercury and compare it with the pressure at which the heart relaxes, and any threshold quoted for that comparison is an illustration to be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol. The operation that opens the compartment, called a fasciotomy, belongs to the surgeon in an operating room and nowhere else, so escalation begins as soon as the diagnosis is suspected rather than after it is proved.

The fourth pathway is for the open fracture, and the finding that starts it is a wound anywhere near a broken bone, a wound that bleeds steadily, bone visible within the wound, or an object embedded in the limb. An open fracture is contaminated even when the wound looks clean, because the skin carries organisms that reach the bone and the deep tissues at the moment of the injury, so the injury is treated as a contaminated emergency from the first second. The responder puts on personal protective equipment before touching the wound, and where that equipment is not available the responder protects the hands with whatever the service provides and avoids contact with blood. Bleeding that can be controlled is controlled with pressure applied around the wound rather than into it, and the wound is then covered with a sterile dressing. The limb is supported and splinted in the position in which it was found, and the patient is kept warm while transfer is arranged.

Three acts are forbidden in this pathway, and the reason for each is the same, which is that the object or the bone may be holding pressure on a torn vessel. An embedded object is never removed, exposed bone is never pushed back into the wound, and the wound is never explored blindly with a finger or an instrument. The neurovascular status is recorded before the dressing is applied and again afterwards, because a finding that was not recorded before an intervention cannot be attributed afterwards. Antibiotic treatment and protection against tetanus are decisions of a prescriber working to the local protocol, and the substance, the amount, the route and the timing belong to that protocol rather than to this course. Definitive cleaning of the wound, removal of dead tissue and closure belong to the operating room and to the surgeon, and any interval quoted for the arrival of the patient there is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. The responder stops and escalates when the bleeding cannot be controlled with pressure, when the part of the limb beyond the wound is pale, cold or pulseless, or when the patient becomes unstable for any reason.

The fifth pathway is for the suspected spinal injury, and it begins with the mechanism as much as with the patient. The finding that starts it may be a fall from a height, a dive into shallow water, a collision at speed, a blow to the head or the face, a heavy object falling on the back, or any injury in which the patient was found unconscious. Pain or tenderness in the neck or the back, numbness, tingling, weakness, or a change in the control of the bladder or the bowel raises the suspicion further. A patient whose attention is held by another painful injury may not mention the neck at all, which is why a distracting injury is a reason for caution rather than for reassurance. Until someone qualified to clear the spine has cleared it, the spine is treated as unstable, and that rule holds even when the patient feels well and asks to sit up.

The steps begin with the head, which the trained responder holds steady where it was found, without traction and without pulling, while the rest of the assessment continues around it. The patient is moved only by a team using the equipment and the number of trained people that the local protocol requires, because no lone manual handling technique is safe and one pair of hands cannot prevent movement of an unstable spine. The lay first aider keeps the patient still, warm and talking, and moves the patient only when the scene itself has become dangerous. Clearing the spine is a clinical decision based on the examination, on the way the injury happened and on imaging, and the studies used are computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging, ordered by a clinician trained to interpret them. A responder who is unsure whether the spine has been cleared treats it as not cleared, and the patient is carried with the precautions the protocol demands until someone qualified says otherwise. The point of escalation is a patient who vomits, who becomes unresponsive, who cannot breathe, or who must be moved for safety, and in each of those cases the trained team moves the patient rather than the bystander.

The sixth pathway is for the dislocated joint, and the finding that starts it is a joint that no longer matches its partner on the other side. The limb is held in one position, the usual bony landmark is missing or has moved, the joint is swollen and painful, and the patient resists any attempt to move it. Numbness, tingling, weakness or a pulse difficult to find beyond the joint make the injury urgent rather than merely painful, and they are recorded before anything is done. A joint that has dislocated has almost always torn its capsule and at least one ligament, and it may have injured the cartilage or the bone at the edge of the joint. The one act that must never be performed in the field is the attempt to put the joint back, because a dislocation may be accompanied by a fracture that nobody has seen and because the attempt may compress the vessels on which the head of the bone depends.

The steps are to leave the joint alone, to support it as it was found with a sling, a pillow or the hands, which the lay first aider can do, to record the neurovascular status before and after that support, and to keep the patient warm. Any measure for pain is the decision of the prescriber, and the amount and the way it is given are not taught here. The trained responder may apply a sling or a splint within the equipment and the procedures the service authorizes, and the physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides how and when the joint is put back, with imaging available and with the means to manage whatever is found. Reduction is learned under supervision and performed according to the local protocol, and it is not attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when the vessels or the nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, when the training or the equipment is inadequate, or when an earlier attempt has failed. If an attempt has already begun and meets resistance, or if the pain, the sensation or the pulse changes while it is under way, it stops at once, and the responder escalates to the surgeon rather than persisting, because force is not a technique.

All six pathways end in the same discipline, which is reassessment after every intervention and a record the next person can read. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with what was written before, and the record names the time of the injury, the mechanism, the position in which the limb was found, what was released or applied, and the neurovascular findings before and after every step. Cold is treated as a complication in every pathway, so the patient is insulated from the ground and wet clothing is replaced with covering. None of these pathways is a license to act beyond training, and every act in them is performed within the authorization of the service, the supervision of the clinician responsible and the law of the country in which the responder works.

To summarize, six emergency pathways run through this course and each begins with a finding rather than a diagnosis, which are the unstable patient, the threatened limb, the suspected compartment syndrome, the open fracture, the suspected spinal injury and the dislocated joint. In all of them the first acts are the same, which are to make the scene safe, to protect the hands, to follow the order of airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure, and to keep the patient warm, because life comes before limb. The steps that follow are to examine and record the neurovascular status before and after every intervention, to immobilize the limb as it was found, to image when a clinician orders it, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, with the provider level named for each act. What must never be done is also the same in all six, which is never to straighten a deformed limb in the field, never to remove an embedded object, never to push exposed bone back into a wound, never to explore an open wound blindly, never to raise a limb that may be compartmented, never to move a patient with a suspected spinal injury by a lone untrained technique, and never to attempt a reduction that the source forbids. When the responder stops and escalates, the call is to the emergency service, to the trauma team, to the surgeon or to the intensive care unit, and it is made early rather than late. Everything in this appendix is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol or the law of the country in which you work.
