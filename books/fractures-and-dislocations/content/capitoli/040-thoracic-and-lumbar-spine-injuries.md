# Chapter 40 — Thoracic and Lumbar Spine Injuries

The thoracic and the lumbar spine are the part of the skeleton that carries the weight of the body above the pelvis, and an injury there can change a life more completely than any broken limb. The thoracic spine is the segment to which the ribs are attached, and the lumbar spine is the heavier segment below it. A fracture in this region may be painful and obvious, or it may produce no more than a tenderness that a tired examiner misses at the end of a long shift. Because the spinal cord and the nerves that leave it travel inside this column, the same force that breaks a bone can also interrupt the messages that move a leg or empty a bladder. Every chapter of this course follows one order, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

A vertebra is built around a block of bone in front, called the body, and an arch behind it, and that arch encloses a canal through which the spinal cord and its nerves pass down the body. Behind the canal the arch closes into a spinous process, which is the bony point a hand feels in the middle of the back, and on each side of it lie the facet joints, where one vertebra meets the one above and the one below. The bodies of the thoracic vertebrae are smaller and are held by the ribs, which give the chest its stiffness, while the bodies of the lumbar vertebrae are larger because they carry more weight. The discs between the bodies are cushions that let the column bend and absorb load, and the ligaments along the front and the back of the bodies hold the whole stack together. This arrangement explains why a healthy spine bends and twists without harm and why the same spine fails when the force arrives faster than the tissues can yield.

The spinal cord does not run to the bottom of the column, because it ends in the upper part of the lumbar spine, and below that level the canal holds a bundle of nerves that continues downward like a horse's tail. That bundle is the reason an injury at the top of the lumbar spine can injure the cord itself, while an injury lower down injures the nerves of the bundle one by one, and the two produce different pictures. The cord usually ends somewhere between the lowest thoracic vertebra and the second lumbar vertebra, though the exact level differs from person to person, and any figure offered in teaching must be confirmed against the current anatomy text and the local protocol. The nerves of the bundle supply the muscles that move the feet and the ankles, the sensation of the saddle area, and the control of the bladder and the bowel. This is why a patient who cannot feel the inner thighs or who cannot pass urine after a fall from a height is treated as a surgical emergency rather than as a patient with back pain.

Two mechanisms produce most of the injuries in this region, and the history often names one of them before the examination begins. The first is a fall from a height, in which the patient lands on the feet or on the buttocks and the force travels straight up the column, so the vertebra that fails is usually one of the junctional vertebrae where a stiff segment meets a mobile one. The second is a flexion injury, in which the column is bent forward beyond what the ligaments allow, as happens in a collision when a seated body is thrown forward against a belt or when a heavy load falls across the back. A third mechanism combines both, bending the spine and then loading it, and those injuries tend to be the most unstable because more than one column of the spine has failed. The mechanism is not a diagnosis, but a force arriving from above and along the line of the column raises the suspicion of an injury to a vertebral body even when the patient complains only of a sore back.

What the responder looks for in this region is simple, and it must be looked for on purpose. The patient is asked where the pain is, and the answer is often a single point that the patient can cover with one finger, which is more useful than a vague ache across the whole back. The examiner looks at the line of the spinous processes from the side and from above, and looks for a step, which is a place where one bony point sits out of line with the ones above and below it, and for a gap, which is a space where a bony point should be. Swelling, bruising and a tightening of the muscles beside the column are common, and the muscles may stiffen so much that the usual curve of the back is lost. Tenderness is found by pressing gently along the line of the spines and beside it, and by pressing on the pelvis and on the chest to see whether the pain is reproduced from a distance.

None of those findings is reliable on its own, and a listener who trusts one of them will miss an injury. A step in the line of the spines may be absent in a fracture that has collapsed evenly, and it may be present in a patient whose spine was curved before the injury. Tenderness may be masked by another painful injury, by alcohol, by a medicine that dulls pain, or by a patient who is confused and cannot say what hurts. A constructed illustration may help, in which a patient in their thirties who fell a short distance from a ladder, who walked to the ambulance and who has no numbness or weakness still has a fracture of a vertebral body that only imaging will show. The lesson is not that such a patient can be sent home, but that the absence of a dramatic sign is not evidence of an uninjured spine.

The assessment of a patient who may have injured this region begins with the same steps as any other trauma, which are remembered as ABCDE, meaning airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure. The reason is not a formality but a fact about energy, because the same fall that breaks a vertebra often breaks ribs, bruises a lung, tears the spleen or the liver, or fractures the pelvis, and any of those can kill before the spine is considered. A patient who is short of breath, whose chest does not move evenly on both sides or whose abdomen is tender and tight is treated for that problem first while the spine is protected at the same time. Life comes before limb and limb comes before the fine detail of a bony injury, so the examination of the back waits until the patient is stable enough to be turned. The lay first aider keeps the patient still and calls for help, while the trained responder and the paramedic assess within the procedures their service authorizes.

When the patient can speak, the history is taken in a fixed order and written down, because the answers decide how much caution the rest of the care requires. The questions are how far the patient fell, onto what surface, in what position the body landed, whether the patient could move the arms and the legs afterwards, and whether the patient stood up and walked. It matters whether there has been back pain or back surgery before, whether the bones are weakened by age or by disease, and whether a medicine that thins the bone has been taken for a long time. A constructed illustration is a patient in their seventies who slipped on a wet floor at home, who felt a sudden pain in the middle of the back and who has no weakness at all, because a bone that has lost density can break under a force that would leave a younger spine unhurt. Every figure in that history, including the height of the fall, is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

The burst fracture is the pattern for which this region is best known, and its name describes what has happened rather than how severe it is. In a burst fracture the vertebral body is broken into several parts by a force that arrives along the line of the column, and the fragments are pushed outward in all directions, including backward into the canal where the nerves lie. It follows a fall from a height or a heavy load more often than a simple fall on the level, and it may be present in a patient who has no neurologic deficit at all, which is why the examination alone cannot rule it out. When a fragment presses on the cord or on the nerves of the bundle, the patient may have numbness, weakness or a change in the control of the bladder, and those findings make the injury a surgical emergency. Computed tomography is the study that shows the fragments and the canal, and it is requested by a clinician trained to interpret it, while the responder protects the spine and does not try to judge stability from the story alone.

Another pattern follows a flexion force, in which the upper part of the body is thrown forward while the lower part is held, and the injury runs through the back of the spine rather than the front. A seat belt across the abdomen, the handlebars of a bicycle or a heavy weight falling across the back can each produce it, and the injury may open the back of the spine like a book, tearing the ligaments that hold the vertebrae together. Because the tear is in soft tissue, a radiograph taken soon after the injury may look almost normal, and a patient who is walking with pain may be sent away before the imaging that shows the injury is arranged. A fracture of this kind is treated as unstable even when the bones look nearly lined up, because the ligaments that would resist movement have failed. The names given to these patterns belong in the specialist's report, and the task of the responder is to recognize a mechanism that could have produced one and to protect the spine while help is arranged.

The neurovascular examination of this region is recorded before any intervention and repeated after it, and here it has two parts, one for the cord and its nerves and one for the limbs. The examiner asks the patient to move the ankles, the toes and the feet against resistance and compares the two sides, because a difference between them is more telling than an absolute weakness. Sensation is tested with a light touch on both sides of the body from the chest downward, and the patient is asked whether the feeling is the same on the left and on the right and whether the saddle area between the legs feels normal. The tone of the muscles and the reflexes are examined by a clinician, because a limb that is floppy and a limb that is stiff point to different levels of trouble. The most intimate parts of that examination, which include the sensation around the anus and the tightening of the anal muscle, are performed by a clinician, with a colleague of the same gender as the patient present when local protocol requires it, and they are never performed by a lay first aider.

The findings that make an injury of this region an emergency are worth naming slowly, because a patient may describe them in ordinary words rather than medical ones. A patient may say that the legs feel heavy or that one foot drags, that the inner sides of the thighs feel numb as though a band were drawn around them, or that the saddle area has lost its feeling altogether. A patient may say that passing urine has become difficult, that the bladder feels full but will not empty, or that control of the bowel has been lost, and any of those statements is treated as a nerve emergency. Back pain that is worse at night, pain that wakes the patient from sleep and a loss of weight without a reason are findings that suggest a cause other than injury and are referred for assessment. No responder waits for the picture to become complete, because the nerves of the bundle are compressed from the moment of the injury and time is nerve rather than muscle.

Stabilization of a patient with a possible injury in this region is mostly a matter of restraint, and the first act is not to move the patient at all unless there is a reason that cannot wait. A fire, a falling wall, a rising flood or a patient who is not breathing are such reasons, and in every other case the patient is kept in the position found until a team with the right equipment arrives. The head is held in a neutral position by two trained hands when a trained responder is present, but this is not a technique that one person can perform alone while also assessing or moving the patient, and no lone method of handling is safe. A patient lying on the back, on the side or in a heap on the ground is left as found, with the body supported so that the head, the trunk and the pelvis stay in one line. Covering the patient, insulating them from the ground and replacing wet clothing with dry covering are part of this step, because cold is a complication of trauma and not a detail of comfort.

Moving a patient with a possible spinal injury is a team task, and the number of hands required is decided by the equipment and the local protocol rather than by the strength of one rescuer. The aim is that the head, the trunk and the pelvis move together without twisting, and that the patient is not dragged, folded or pulled by the arms. A scoop stretcher, a vacuum mattress or a rigid board is chosen according to what the service provides, and a rigid board is a tool for lifting and for the short distance to the stretcher rather than a bed for a long journey, because pressure on the skin and discomfort increase the longer a patient lies on it. Padding is placed under the head and the shoulders so that the neck is not forced into a bend, and the older patient with a curved upper spine needs more padding rather than less. Straps are checked twice, once before the move and once after it, and the patient is told what is about to happen at each stage, because fear makes muscles rigid and rigid muscles move a broken spine.

Immobilization of the thoracic and the lumbar spine is not the same problem as immobilization of the neck, and a device that is right for one region may be useless or harmful for the other. The whole column is kept in one line by supporting the body from the head to the pelvis, so the head is held, the trunk is secured and the legs are kept straight and together, and a collar alone does not immobilize the thoracic or the lumbar spine. A collar, side blocks and straps are applied by the trained responder and the paramedic within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, and the decision that a patient no longer needs a device belongs to the clinician who clears the spine. The lay first aider supports the head and the body with the hands and with whatever is at hand, keeps the patient warm and still, and waits. Any device is checked for tightness and for pressure on the skin, because a strap that is right at the moment it is applied can become too tight as the patient swells.

Some patients make every part of this care harder, and the responder should recognize them before the move rather than during it. A patient who is agitated, confused, intoxicated or frightened may fight the hands that are holding the head, and such a patient is treated as having an unstable spine while the cause of the agitation is also assessed, because head injury and low oxygen are common reasons for it. A child has a larger head in proportion to the body, so a board that fits an adult tilts the young neck forward unless padding is placed beneath the shoulders, and the spine of a child also bends rather than breaks in some injuries. A pregnant patient is positioned according to local protocol and with the advice of the receiving team, because the precaution that protects the spine may also reduce the flow of blood to the womb. An older patient whose spine has stiffened with age breaks it in a way that resembles a long bone rather than a stack of vertebrae, and that injury is missed when the mechanism seems too minor to cause harm.

Imaging confirms what the examination has already suggested, and it is ordered by a clinician trained to interpret it rather than by the responder who protects the patient. A radiograph is usually the first study, taken from the front and from the side, and it may be repeated with the patient turned in a way the local protocol allows so that a displacement in the third direction is not hidden. Computed tomography shows the fine detail of the bone and of the canal, and it is the study that shows a burst fracture and its fragments most clearly. Magnetic resonance imaging is the study for the cord, the discs and the ligaments, and it is used when the neurologic findings and the plain images do not explain one another. The trained responder does not decide which study is needed and the lay first aider never orders or interprets an image, but both help by reporting the mechanism and the findings they recorded, because those decide what is asked of the imaging.

Reassessment after every intervention is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment, and in this region it has a particular meaning. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the record, and the comparison is what carries the value rather than any single measurement. The patient is asked again about the movement of the legs, about feeling in the saddle area and about the ability to pass urine, because a change in any of those is the finding that stops the responder. The limb is checked for color, warmth, pulse and pain, and a strap is loosened or a device changed only within the limits of the local protocol and by someone authorized to do it. Pain that rises instead of settling, a new numbness, a new weakness and a patient who becomes restless without a reason are all findings that prompt urgent referral, and the patient is kept warm throughout.

Referral begins with the recognition that some patients cannot be cared for where they lie, and a suspected injury of the thoracic or the lumbar spine is one of them. A patient with a step in the line of the spines, a neurologic finding, a high-energy mechanism with pain in the middle of the back, a known weakness of the bone or a fall from more than standing height is referred to a facility with imaging, a spine service, an operating room and an intensive care unit. The receiving team is told the mechanism, the level of the pain, the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention and any change in the condition of the patient, because a story that continues is safer than a story that starts again. Transfer is arranged by the service that owns the decision, and a patient who must wait is kept warm, still and observed rather than left alone. A patient with no pain, no tenderness and no neurologic finding may still need imaging, and the decision about how much imaging is enough belongs to the clinician who examines them.

Definitive treatment of an injury in this region ranges from a brace to an operation, and every decision belongs to a specialist working within the local protocol. A brace holds a stable injury in a position that allows it to heal, and it must be made and fitted by someone trained to do it, because a brace that is too loose does not hold and one that is too tight injures the skin over a bony point. An operation may be needed to take pressure off the cord or the nerves and to hold the bones in line with metal devices while they heal, and its timing depends on the neurologic findings and on the general condition of the patient. Traction may be used for some injuries of the cervical spine to hold the column in line with a gentle and continuous pull, and it is applied only in a unit with the equipment and the staff to watch the patient. None of these acts is learned from a book, so they are described here only as what they are meant to achieve, who performs them and what can go wrong, and the technique itself belongs to supervised clinical training.

Complications in this region arrive in two directions, from the injury and from the care, and a listener should be able to name both. The injury itself can leave a patient with a weak leg, a numb saddle area or a bladder that no longer empties, and those losses may be permanent if the pressure on the nerves is not relieved in time. The care can add harm when a patient is moved with the column twisted, when a device presses on the skin for hours, or when a fracture that no one suspected is loaded by an eager examination. For that reason a patient with a suspected injury here is never asked to sit up, bend forward or lift a leg while lying on the back, the back is never massaged and a hot pack is never applied. The findings that make a responder stop and ask for more help are a new weakness, a new numbness, a loss of bladder control and pain that increases, and anyone who is unsure of the next step stops and calls for the person qualified to decide.

The record and the handover are part of the treatment rather than a formality that follows it, and in this region they carry more weight than in almost any other. The time of the injury, the mechanism, the position in which the patient was found, the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention and every change in the condition of the patient are written or spoken in a fixed order. The spine is treated as unstable until it has been cleared by someone qualified to clear it, and clearing it means an examination, a decision about imaging and a record of that decision rather than the disappearance of the pain. The lay first aider, the trained responder, the paramedic, the nurse and the physician all write down what they saw and did, because a finding that was not recorded before a device was applied cannot be attributed afterwards. Any number spoken in that handover, including a time or a distance, is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

To summarize, the thoracic and the lumbar spine carry the weight of the body, and an injury there threatens the nerves as much as it threatens the bone. The patient is assessed in the usual order of airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure, because a broken spine rarely comes alone and life comes before limb. The responder looks for the tender point, for a step or a gap in the line of the spines and for a mechanism that fits a fall from a height or a flexion force, and remembers that a burst fracture may leave the patient walking and that the absence of a dramatic sign proves nothing. The neurovascular examination is recorded before any device is applied and repeated afterwards, and it includes the movement of the legs, the feeling of the saddle area and the control of the bladder and the bowel. The patient is not moved unless there is a danger that cannot wait, the head, the trunk and the pelvis are kept in one line by a trained team using the equipment and the procedures the service authorizes, and no lone method of handling is safe. Reduction, traction, casting and surgical fixation are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and no part of their technique belongs in a book. The spine is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has done so, every number in this chapter is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, and the patient is kept warm, observed and handed over with the whole story written down.
