# Chapter 29 — Patellar Fractures and Knee Dislocation

The knee is the largest joint in the body and one of the most often injured, and two of its injuries belong together in one chapter because they could hardly be more different. The patella is the small bone that sits in front of the joint inside the tendon of the quadriceps muscle, where it acts as a pulley and gives that muscle a longer lever in the last part of straightening, and it can be broken or dislocated; that injury is immobilized and referred. The knee itself can dislocate, with the thigh bone and the shin bone losing contact, and that injury is an emergency not only for the joint but for the whole limb, because the artery that supplies everything below the knee runs directly behind it. Both are handled through the same sequence that every chapter of this course follows, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

A direct blow, such as the knee striking a hard surface in a fall or an occupant's knee hitting the dashboard of a vehicle, crushes the patella against the thigh bone behind it. The result is often a fracture with several fragments, sometimes described in teaching as a starburst pattern, and the cartilage on the back of the bone is usually damaged at the same time. Because the quadriceps tendon and the fibrous expansions on each side of the knee, called the retinacula, may stay intact, the fragments can remain together and the patient may still be able to straighten the knee. An indirect mechanism works differently, because when the knee is partly bent and the quadriceps contracts violently, or when the patient catches a fall on a partly bent knee, the muscle pulls the patella apart against the attachment below. The break is then usually a transverse one with a gap between the ends, and the tearing reaches the fibrous expansions on each side, so the extensor mechanism fails.

The patient with a broken patella usually arrives holding the knee still, with the joint swollen and the skin over the front of the knee tight and shiny. Blood inside the joint from the fracture, called a hemarthrosis, fills the pouch above the patella and makes the whole knee look round rather than knobbly. The examiner may feel a gap or a step in the bone, and pressing the patella against the thigh bone causes sharp pain, though that is not a test to repeat. The most useful single observation is whether the patient can straighten the knee against gravity and hold it straight, because that movement is the work of the extensor mechanism and it fails when the mechanism is torn. A patient who cannot lift the straightened leg, or who can hold it straight only with effort, is treated as having a disrupted extensor mechanism until a clinician decides otherwise. The sign is not perfectly reliable, because severe pain may prevent the attempt, a partly torn expansion may still allow weak straightening, and a child may simply refuse to try, so the responder records what was seen and leaves the judgment to the clinician.

Dislocation of the patella is a different injury in a different patient, and it is common in young people who play sport. The kneecap almost always leaves the joint outward, driven off its groove by a twist of the body while the foot is planted and the knee is nearly straight. Some patients are more likely to dislocate because their joints are generally loose, because the groove in the thigh bone is shallow, or because the patella sits higher than usual, and a previous episode is a strong clue in the history. The patient holds the knee slightly bent, refuses to straighten it, and describes the kneecap as having moved sideways, and the patella may still be visibly displaced to the outer side of the knee. It often slips back into place by itself as the knee bends, however, so the examiner may see only a swollen and painful knee with tenderness along the inner edge and no obvious deformity at all. That is why the story matters: something moved, the knee became painful and swollen, and it has not felt right since, and a fragment of cartilage or bone may have been knocked loose inside the joint.

A dislocated patella is not a sprain and it is not put back by the responder. The reduction of a patellar dislocation is performed by a clinician, usually with the knee gently straightened and the kneecap guided back into its groove while the muscles are relaxed, and the technique is learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, never from a book. A first attempt can drive a loose cartilage fragment deeper into the joint, and an attempt made against tense muscle can damage the joint surface, which is why the clinician also wants to know whether the reduction was difficult. After the joint has been put back it is held in whatever position and with whatever device the local protocol directs, and the patient is referred, because a knee that has been dislocated must be examined again once the swelling and the pain have settled. The lay first aider and the trained responder do the same thing while they wait: they support the knee in the position found, they keep the patient warm, and they do not test the joint.

Dislocation of the knee itself is a much rarer and much more dangerous injury, and it is the reason this chapter is written. The thigh bone and the shin bone are forced out of contact, and with them go the ligaments that hold the knee, in most cases several at once. It follows high-energy trauma such as a road traffic collision, a fall from a height or a heavy blow to the front of a bent knee, but it also follows injuries of much lower energy, particularly in a patient who is very heavy or whose joints are unusually lax. The energy of the mechanism therefore does not tell the listener how bad the knee is, and a knee dislocation must be suspected whenever the story and the examination do not fit a simpler injury. The joint frequently returns to its own position before the patient is seen, and that is the trap, because the limb may look nearly normal while the artery behind it has already been torn. The responder who hears that the knee was deformed at the scene and is straight on arrival is hearing a dislocation, whether or not anyone used the word.

Behind the knee the popliteal artery passes from the thigh into the calf, and it is tethered above where it leaves the canal of the thigh muscles and below where it passes beneath the fibrous arch of the calf muscle. Because it is fixed at both ends and lies against the back of the joint, it cannot move out of the way when the bones shift, and it is stretched or torn by the displacement. The nerve that lifts the foot, the peroneal nerve, wraps around the neck of the smaller bone of the lower leg just below and outside the knee, and it is injured in a similar way. Its loss is seen as a foot that drops when the leg is lifted and as numbness or tingling over the front and outer side of the lower leg and the top of the foot, and although that injury does not threaten the limb, it changes the patient's life and is recorded in the same way as the pulse.

The neurovascular examination is the step that decides the fate of a leg, and it is performed before the limb is splinted, again after the splint is in place, and again after every other intervention. The examiner compares the two limbs, feels for the pulse at the front of the ankle and behind the inner ankle, and notes the warmth and the color of the skin and how quickly the color returns when a nail is pressed and released. Sensation is tested on both sides, on the top of the foot and between the first two toes, and the patient is asked to pull the foot up and to push it down and to report any numbness or tingling in their own words. A pulse that is present does not exclude injury to the artery, and this is the finding most often trusted wrongly, because the inner lining of the vessel can be torn while a channel remains open. Many services compare the blood pressure measured at the ankle with the pressure measured in the arm, because a value much lower at the ankle points to an injury that needs urgent study, but the threshold used is an illustration for the local protocol rather than a fixed law. The absence of a pulse is an emergency that stops everything else.

Assessment begins away from the limb, because a patient whose knee has been dislocated has often been injured elsewhere as well. The first step is the safety of the scene and of the responder, and the next is the mechanism of injury, which tells the team what to expect and where to look. The primary survey follows, in the order remembered as ABCDE, meaning airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure, and it is performed before the knee is examined in detail, because life comes before limb and a limb can wait for minutes that a brain cannot. Bleeding inside the chest, the abdomen or the pelvis, a head injury or a spinal injury takes precedence, and the knee is supported in the position found while those problems are addressed. Only when the patient is stable enough does the assessment turn to the knee itself, and then it is done by looking, by feeling gently and by asking the patient to move the joint, never by forcing it. A limb that was deformed and is now straight, a knee that is grossly swollen, a calf that feels tense and a pulse that has changed are findings that are recorded at once and reported to whoever will receive the patient.

Stabilization of these injuries is a small set of acts, and the most important of them is restraint. The knee is supported in the position in which it was found and is never straightened in the field, because the artery and the nerves lie against the bone and a movement that appears helpful can complete a tear that was only partial. The lay first aider supports the limb with the hands, with a pillow or with whatever is at hand, keeps the patient still and warm and waits for help, while the trained responder and the paramedic may apply a splint within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes. Any device that presses on the back of the knee is padded so that it does not add pressure to the artery. Cold is a complication of trauma and not a detail of comfort, so the patient is insulated from the ground and wet clothing is replaced with covering. A knee that is open, with bone or deep tissue visible, is covered with a sterile dressing once the bleeding that can be controlled has been controlled, and nothing is pushed back into the wound.

Immobilization means preventing movement of the injured part and of the joints above and below it, and around the knee that means supporting the hip and the ankle as well as the knee itself. The trained responder and the paramedic apply a splint that holds the limb in the position found, and a device that extends from the upper thigh to beyond the ankle is often used for transport because it controls the knee and the ankle together. Padding is placed where the device presses on bone, particularly over the bony points of the ankle and the hip, and the limb is secured firmly enough to hold without becoming a band that stops the circulation. The splint is checked immediately after it is applied and then at intervals, because swelling inside a limb can turn a splint that was comfortable into one that is dangerously tight. Casting, bracing and surgical fixation are different matters altogether, they are performed by clinicians, and the technique of each is learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol.

Imaging confirms what the examination has already suggested, and it is ordered and interpreted by a clinician trained to do so. A radiograph of the knee is usually the first study, and it is taken in at least two directions so that a displacement in the third direction is not hidden, with the joint above and the joint below included so that a second injury is not missed. For a broken patella the radiograph shows how many fragments there are and how far apart they lie, and the clinician may ask for an additional view taken from a particular angle to see the joint surface. Computed tomography gives finer detail of a fracture with many fragments, particularly where it enters the joint, and magnetic resonance imaging shows the ligaments, the menisci, the cartilage and any loose fragment once the limb is safe; the second study never takes priority over the question of the artery. When injury to the artery behind the knee is suspected, the clinician may arrange a study of the vessels, and the decision about which study, and how quickly, belongs to the team responsible for the patient.

Reassessment happens after every intervention, and it is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the record written before, because the comparison is what has value rather than any single measurement. The toes are checked for color, warmth, movement and sensation, the pulse is felt again at both places on the ankle, the splint is checked for tightness, and the patient is asked whether the pain is the same, better or worse. Pain that increases instead of settling, pain out of proportion to the injury, numbness, tingling, weakness and a calf that becomes tense and swollen are the findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation. The knee is one of the places where compartment syndrome follows dislocation even when the artery was never torn, because bleeding inside the closed compartments of the calf raises the pressure until the muscle and the nerve begin to die. A responder who suspects that condition does not wait for confirmation and acts within the local protocol, which may allow a constricting bandage or splint to be released by someone authorized to do it, and the limb is not elevated above the level of the heart. The patient is kept warm and referred urgently, because the diagnosis is clinical and time is muscle.

Definitive treatment of a patellar fracture turns on three questions that belong to the orthopedic surgeon. Does the extensor mechanism still work, so that the patient can straighten the knee against gravity and hold it straight? Is the joint surface still smooth, and is there a step between fragments large enough to wear out the cartilage behind the bone? And is the skin intact, or has the fracture communicated with the outside? When the answers are that the mechanism works and the surface is smooth, many such fractures are treated without an operation, held in the position the surgeon chooses and allowed to heal while the patient follows a program of supervised exercise. When the extensor mechanism is torn, when a fragment has separated, when the joint surface is uneven, when a piece of bone or cartilage is loose inside the joint, or when the injury is open, an operation is usually needed to bring the fragments together and hold them while they heal. The surgeon may use wires, screws or strong sutures, and an operation that opens the joint to see and fix the fragments directly is called open reduction and internal fixation; every one of those decisions and techniques belongs to the surgeon, is learned under supervision, and is performed according to local protocol.

The treatment of a patella that has dislocated depends on what the first episode left behind. A first dislocation in a young patient is often treated without an operation, with a period of protection chosen by the clinician and then a program of rehabilitation supervised by a physical therapist, whose work is to strengthen the muscle that holds the patella in its groove and to teach the patient to use the leg safely again. An operation may be needed when a fragment has been knocked loose, when the joint surface is damaged, or when the patella will not stay in place without help. The responder's part in this is small and does not change: support the knee, immobilize it in the position found, examine and record the neurovascular status, arrange the transfer, and never attempt to reduce the joint.

A knee that has been dislocated is treated as an emergency for the limb from the moment it is recognized. The joint is put back by a clinician in a controlled environment where the airway, the blood pressure and the rest of the patient can be watched, and where the examination of the vessels can be repeated before and after the reduction; this is not an act for the field, for the ambulance, or for anyone working alone in a corridor. If the artery behind the knee is injured, the repair is a matter of hours rather than days, and the interval quoted in teaching is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the time a limb can survive without blood is not the same in a warm patient and a cold one. The joint may be held after reduction with a frame that lies outside the limb, called an external fixator, or with a brace or a cast chosen by the surgeon, while the vessels and the soft tissues are watched. The ligaments that were torn are often repaired or reconstructed later, once the limb is safe, because saving the leg comes before rebuilding the knee. A patient whose knee has been put back and whose pulse is present is still watched with repeated neurovascular checks, because the inner lining of a stretched artery can tear and clot over the hours that follow, and the period of that observation is an illustration for the local protocol.

An open injury at the knee adds urgency to everything already described. When the bone or the deep tissues have communicated with the outside, the wound is contaminated at the moment of the injury even when it looks clean, and the risk to the limb rises sharply, whether the injury is a broken patella or a dislocated knee. The responder covers the wound with a sterile dressing, controls the bleeding that can be controlled, records the neurovascular status, immobilizes the limb in the position found, keeps the patient warm and arranges urgent transfer; nothing is removed from the wound and nothing is pushed back into it. Protection against tetanus and the use of antibiotics are decisions for the clinician and the prescriber, and the substance, the amount, the route and the timing belong to the local protocol, so that a listener cannot take a prescription from this course. Cleaning the wound and repairing the deep structures are done in the operating room, and definitive closure belongs there and nowhere else. A patient with an open injury at the knee is referred to a facility with an operating room, imaging and an intensive care unit, and is not kept for observation in a place that cannot treat what may be found.

Children and older adults bring their own cautions to these same injuries. The patella of a child is largely cartilage, so a violent contraction of the quadriceps can pull a sleeve of cartilage and bone off its lower or upper edge, and because cartilage does not show on a radiograph the picture may look almost normal while the extensor mechanism is torn; a child who cannot straighten the knee, or who will not bear weight, is referred even when the radiograph is reported as showing nothing. Some children also have an extra center of bone formation at the upper outer corner of the patella, called a bipartite patella, which is a normal variant and not a fracture, and deciding which it is belongs to the clinician. In an older adult, bone that has lost density breaks more easily and holds fragments less well, so a patella can be fractured by a fall that would leave a younger person unhurt, and a knee dislocation may follow an injury of surprisingly low energy. Arteries that are already narrowed tolerate a period without blood flow badly, so the threshold for urgent assessment in an older patient is lower rather than higher. The examiner is not reassured by a calm patient, by a knee that looks straight, or by a radiograph that shows no break.

The record and the handover are part of the treatment rather than a formality that follows it, and around the knee they carry the findings that decide whether a limb survives. The time of the injury, the mechanism, the position in which the knee was found, whether it was ever deformed, and the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention are written or spoken in a fixed order, so that the receiving team continues a story instead of starting a new one. A single line saying that the knee was deformed at the scene and straight on arrival is worth more to the vascular surgeon than a page of description, because it converts a swollen knee into a suspected dislocation with an artery at risk. Any change in the pulse, the color, the warmth, the sensation or the pain is reported immediately to the clinician responsible, and where this chapter names a time or a threshold it names an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

Some rules are absolute, and every act in this chapter has a name attached to the level of provider who may perform it. A deformed limb is splinted in the position found and is never straightened in the field; a dislocated knee or patella is never reduced by anyone but a clinician; and reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, never from a book. An embedded object is never removed, exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, and an open wound is never explored blindly. If an attempt at any maneuver meets resistance, or if a new numbness, a new weakness or a lost pulse appears, the responder stops, records what has changed, supports the limb and escalates. The lay first aider supports the limb, keeps the patient still and warm and calls for help, using no splint and no treatment; the trained first responder and the paramedic splint and dress within the procedures their service authorizes; the nurse observes, records and reassesses; the physician examines, orders the images and performs the reductions the local protocol permits; and the orthopedic surgeon, with the vascular surgeon when an artery has been injured, decides about and performs the operations. For the patella, look for a swollen knee, a kneecap that moved and perhaps moved back, and the inability to straighten the knee against gravity; for the knee itself, suspect a dislocation whenever it was deformed at any point in the story or the pulse, the color, the warmth or the sensation below it has changed. Everything in this chapter is learned with a teacher, practiced on models and on each other, and certified by an authority that also defines what may be done without supervision.

To summarize, the patella can be broken or dislocated, and the knee itself can dislocate, and the two injuries share a joint while sharing almost nothing else. A broken patella is examined for the ability to straighten the knee against gravity, immobilized in the position found and referred, and the surgeon decides whether the fragments, the joint surface or the extensor mechanism require an operation. A dislocated knee is an emergency for the limb, because the artery behind the joint is fixed at both ends and is torn or stretched when the bones move, and because the joint often reduces itself and leaves a limb that looks deceptively ordinary. Suspect it whenever the knee was deformed at any point, and treat a pulse that is present as no guarantee, because the inner lining of the artery can be injured while blood still flows. Follow the sequence in every case, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. Never straighten a deformed limb in the field, never reduce a knee or a patella outside a controlled clinical setting, never remove an embedded object, never push exposed bone back into a wound and never explore an open wound blindly. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, an injury of the spine is treated as unstable until someone qualified clears it, cold is treated as a complication, and the patient is reassessed after every intervention and watched until the handover. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.
