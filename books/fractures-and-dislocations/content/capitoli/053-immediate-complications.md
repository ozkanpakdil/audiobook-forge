# Chapter 53 — Immediate Complications

Some fractures kill within the first minutes, and the instrument is rarely the bone itself. The bone breaks, the vessels it has torn continue to bleed, the pressure inside an unyielding compartment continues to rise, and the rescue can add harm to harm when it is hurried or forceful. This chapter is about those first minutes and hours: hemorrhage that may be hidden, injury to the nerves and vessels that travel against the bone, a compartment syndrome already developing while the limb still has a pulse, and the damage the rescue itself can do. Every injury in this course is taught through one order, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and this chapter follows that order while naming who may perform each act. This is educational material, and it does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

The first thing to understand about bleeding from a broken bone is that the skin tells almost nothing. A closed fracture can empty blood into the thigh, the pelvis or the soft tissues around a bone until the circulating volume falls far enough to threaten life, and not one drop appears on the surface. A break of the thigh bone is taught as capable of releasing enough blood to cause shock, and that volume is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because estimates differ between services and editions. The chest, the abdomen and the pelvis can hide a loss just as large, since an injured rib, a lacerated organ or a fractured pelvis can bleed without any deformity of a limb. The pelvis is never rocked, sprung or squeezed to test whether it is unstable, because that maneuver can disturb a clot that was holding the bleeding in check, and where a pelvic binder is used it is placed by a trained responder or a paramedic under the local protocol. This is why the course repeats that life comes before limb, because a limb can wait for minutes that a heart and a brain cannot.

The body defends itself against blood loss for as long as it can, and that defense is what makes the first hour deceptive. A young patient with a strong heart and constricted vessels may keep a normal pulse and a clear mind while much of the circulating volume has already been lost, and then deteriorate very quickly when the compensation fails. The findings that give the loss away are a pulse that climbs, skin that becomes pale, cool and moist, a patient who grows anxious or drowsy, a thirst reported without being asked, and a delay in the return of color to a pressed nail. An older adult whose arteries are hardened, a patient who takes a medicine that prevents clotting, and a frightened child may show those findings late or in an unusual order, so a single normal measurement proves nothing. Bleeding after an injury is therefore treated as present until someone qualified has excluded it, and the examiner who waits for the classic picture has waited too long.

The acts that control bleeding are few, and every one of them belongs to a named level of practice. The lay first aider applies firm direct pressure with a clean dressing to a wound that is bleeding on the outside, and does not remove a dressing that has soaked through but adds another on top of it. The trained first responder and the emergency medical technician work within the procedures their service authorizes, and they may use a pressure dressing or a tourniquet where the protocol provides for one and direct pressure has failed. A tourniquet is not a casual device, because a limb deprived of its blood supply is a limb at risk of being lost, so the time of application is written down and it is not loosened in the field by a responder acting alone. Bleeding that cannot be seen is treated by keeping the patient warm and still and by moving the patient quickly to a hospital, and no volume to be infused and no substance to be given is named here, because those decisions belong to the prescriber and to the surgeon.

Nerves and great vessels do not run through the middle of a limb, since they run along the bone, often pressed against its surface with only muscle and fascia between them and the outside world. That arrangement is efficient in health and treacherous in injury, because a displaced bone end travels exactly through the place where the vessel and the nerve were lying. The familiar illustrations are a break just above the elbow, which can injure the vessel that supplies the forearm, and a dislocation of the knee, which can injure the vessel behind the knee, and either can leave a limb without a blood supply while the bone injury looks unremarkable. A dislocation deserves suspicion, because a joint that has lost contact has already stretched or torn its capsule, its ligaments and whatever vessel or nerve crossed it. A bruised or torn nerve announces itself with numbness, tingling, weakness, or the loss of a movement the patient could perform a moment earlier, while an injured vessel announces itself with pallor, coolness, a diminished pulse, and pain out of proportion to the injury.

A pulse that can be felt beyond the fracture does not exclude serious damage to a vessel or to a compartment, and this is among the most dangerous misunderstandings in musculoskeletal trauma. The smallest vessels that feed muscle and nerve can fail while the large artery still carries a pulse to the wrist or the ankle, so a responder who feels a pulse and relaxes has learned only that one vessel is open. These findings are also less trustworthy in particular patients, including an older adult with hardened arteries, a limb already cooled by exposure, a patient who cannot report numbness, and a frightened child. The neurovascular examination is therefore performed before any intervention and repeated after it, and it is written down, because a finding that was not recorded before a splint was applied cannot be attributed afterwards. The lay first aider reports only what needs no training, such as color, warmth and whether the fingers or the toes still move, while the formal examination of pulses, sensation and movement belongs to the trained responder, the nurse or the physician.

Certain injuries carry a higher risk than others, and knowing which ones keeps the responder alert. A break of the upper arm can injure the nerve that winds around the bone and lifts the wrist, so the finding is a hand that drops when the patient tries to raise it. A break of the forearm can raise the pressure inside a compartment that is already tight, and a break of the shin can threaten the vessel behind the shin bone and the nerves that supply the foot. An open fracture adds contamination to the list, because the moment bone communicates with the outside, organisms from the skin reach deep tissue, and the size of the wound says nothing about the amount of contamination. In every one of these injuries the responder records the finding and refers the patient urgently, the interpretation belongs to the clinician, and the definitive repair belongs to the operating room.

The compartment syndrome is the complication this chapter most wants the listener to suspect early, and it is already developing in the first hours, often before the patient reaches a hospital. A compartment is a closed space bounded by bone and by the tough sheet of fascia, and it holds muscle, nerve and blood vessels with almost no room to spare. Bleeding from a fracture or swelling from a crush injury raises the pressure inside that space, and because fascia does not stretch quickly, the pressure keeps rising instead of dispersing. Muscle suffers first, then nerve, and flow through the smallest vessels fails long before the large pulse at the wrist or the ankle disappears, so a palpable pulse is worthless as reassurance. Pain out of proportion to the injury, pain that increases instead of settling, pain when the muscles of the compartment are gently stretched, a limb that feels tense and hard, and numbness where a nerve crosses the compartment are the findings that should stop the responder. The diagnosis is clinical, time is muscle, and a test that arrives late is no help at all.

A compartment syndrome is hardest to recognize in the patient who cannot complain. A child who is frightened, a patient who is unconscious after a head injury, a patient who has been given sedation, and a patient whose limb has been numbed for a procedure may all be developing one and report nothing. The examiner must then rely on the appearance and the feel of the limb and on the trend of the observations rather than on the patient's words, watching for a limb that becomes more swollen and tense, a hand or a foot that becomes cool and pale, and a pulse that weakens. Where a service measures the pressure inside a compartment with a device, that measurement supports a clinical judgment, and the threshold quoted for it differs between services and editions and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. A single normal reading does not exclude the diagnosis, and a normal reading never outvotes a limb that is tense, swollen and painful.

Once a compartment syndrome is suspected, the acts that follow are few and each has an owner. An encircling bandage, splint, cast or dressing is released within the local protocol by someone authorized to do it, because the device applied to hold a limb still can be the very thing stopping the circulation. The limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, since raising it lowers the pressure that drives blood through already failing capillaries, and this is one of the few places where the instinct to raise an injured limb is plainly wrong. The patient is kept warm, because cold adds constriction and pain to a limb already short of blood, and the patient is transferred urgently to a facility where a surgeon can open the compartments, an operation called a fasciotomy that belongs to the operating room. Pain relief is often needed, and who may give it and in what form are set by the prescriber, the local protocol and the law, and none of those details belongs in this course.

The fourth subject of this chapter is the harm that comes with the help, and it is why every intervention is followed by a reassessment. A deformed limb that is straightened in the field can tear a vessel that was intact, release the pressure holding a torn vessel closed, and turn a closed fracture into an open one, so a deformed limb is splinted in the position found and is never pulled straight. A splint applied too tightly, or a bandage tightened again as the limb swells, can create the very compartment syndrome the splint was meant to prevent. An embedded object is never removed and exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, because the object may be pressing on a torn vessel and the bone end may be the only thing closing the wound. A wound is never explored blindly, because fingers carry organisms into deep tissue and can displace a clot that was doing useful work, and definitive closure belongs to the operating room.

Other harms come from hurry rather than from force. A patient dragged or rolled by untrained hands may have a spinal injury converted into a spinal cord injury, so a spine is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has done so, and no single manual handling technique is safe on its own. Traction applied by untrained hands, or a pull maintained while the patient is moved, can damage the soft tissues holding the fracture together, and traction is a clinical act learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol. Moving a patient before the neurovascular status has been recorded destroys the only comparison that would later show whether the journey or the splint caused a new deficit, so the record comes before the movement. A long rescue in cold weather cools the patient, and a cold patient bleeds more readily, so covering is part of the rescue and not an afterthought.

One more harm in the first hours comes from good intentions rather than from force, and it is the injury that nobody looked for. A patient with an obvious deformity of one thigh may also have a broken wrist from the same fall, a dislocated shoulder, or a rib that has injured the lung, and the obvious injury draws every eye away from the rest. The examination that follows the primary survey therefore looks at the whole patient, and the joints above and below the injured part are examined before the splint is applied, because a splint crossing an unnoticed second fracture can displace it. The second injury is missed most easily in the patient who is unconscious, the patient in shock and the patient distracted by severe pain, and those are precisely the patients in whom it matters most. Recording which areas were examined, as well as what was found, shows the next clinician where the unknowns still lie.

The sequence this course follows begins with assessment, and in this chapter assessment has one purpose above all others, which is to find the patient who is bleeding or losing a limb before the limb is studied in detail. The scene is made safe first, because a responder who becomes a second patient helps nobody, and personal protective equipment is worn whenever blood or another body fluid may be present. The mechanism of injury, the time of the injury, the age of the patient, and the illnesses and medicines the patient takes are gathered next, because a patient whose blood does not clot normally is a different patient. The primary survey follows the order remembered as ABCDE, which stands for airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure. Only when the patient is stable enough does the assessment turn to the limb, and the limb is assessed by looking, by feeling and by asking the patient to move it, never by forcing a movement. Life comes before limb at every step of that order, because a limb can wait for minutes that a brain and a heart cannot.

Looking at the limb means noting its position, its shape and its color and comparing it with the other side, which is the oldest and least expensive comparison available. Swelling, a wound, bleeding, deformity, bruising and the exact position of the limb on the stretcher are recorded as they are found, because the position found is the position that will be splinted. Feeling means touching gently along the bone for tenderness and for a step in the contour and feeling the skin for temperature and moisture, but it never means pressing hard on a suspected fracture or probing a wound. Asking the patient to move the fingers or the toes, and asking whether the limb feels numb or tingling, gives information that no device can supply, and the answer is written in the words of the patient. The lay first aider performs only the acts that need no training and no equipment, such as supporting the limb, covering a wound with a clean dressing and noticing that the patient can no longer move a hand.

Stabilization comes next, and here it means three things, which are to keep the patient warm, to control the bleeding that can be controlled, and to support the injured part in the position in which it was found. Supporting the limb is done with the hands of the first aider, with a pillow, with a rolled blanket or with whatever is at hand, and the aim is to remove the movement that hurts rather than to change the shape of the limb. The trained responder and the paramedic may apply a splint or a commercial immobilizer within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, and both levels protect the limb from further movement while the patient is transferred. Keeping the patient warm is part of stabilization rather than a comfort measure, because cold worsens bleeding, so the patient is separated from the ground and covering is added from the feet upward. Nothing in field stabilization includes straightening a limb, reducing a dislocation, removing an object, or pushing bone back into a wound.

The neurovascular examination is the step that must be performed before any intervention and repeated after every one, and it is the step most often compressed into a single word in the record. Color is compared side to side, temperature is compared with the back of the examiner's hand, and the pulse beyond the injury is sought and its character described rather than simply noted as present. The nail bed is pressed briefly and the time taken for color to return is observed and written as a comparison rather than as an absolute number, because a threshold quoted in one service differs in another and must be confirmed against the local protocol. Sensation is tested on both sides and compared, and the patient is asked to move each finger and each toe, with the result recorded in the patient's own words. The finding before the splint and the finding after the splint are both entered with the time, because a deficit that was not documented before an intervention can neither be attributed afterwards nor defended.

Immobilization means preventing movement of the injured part and of the joints above and below it, and in the presence of a suspected vascular injury it also means resisting the temptation to correct anything. A splint is padded where it presses over a bony point, it is applied over the position found, and it is secured firmly enough to hold without becoming a band that stops the circulation. The material matters less than the principle, since a padded board, a folded magazine, a sling or the body of the patient can each serve, provided the device is checked often because an improvised one shifts. The trained responder and the paramedic apply splints within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, while the physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides what immobilization will hold the bone for healing, which may be a cast, a brace or surgical fixation. Casting, reduction and traction are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and none of them can be learned from a book, so what a book can explain is why they are done and what can go wrong.

Imaging confirms what the examination has already suggested, and it is ordered by a clinician trained to interpret it. A radiograph is usually the first study, and it is taken in at least two directions so that a displacement in the third direction is not hidden, while the joints above and below are included so that a second injury is not missed. Computed tomography shows the detail of a complex break around a joint, and magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for soft tissue, including ligaments, cartilage, tendons and muscle, and neither is ordered merely because it is available. An image is a picture of one moment, and a patient who is bleeding into a thigh or losing the blood supply to a leg must not wait in a radiology department while the picture is taken, so imaging follows resuscitation and never precedes it. The lay first aider never orders or interprets imaging, the trained responder does not decide which study is needed, and the choice belongs to the clinician responsible for the patient.

Reassessment is performed after every intervention, and in a chapter about immediate complications it is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the record, and it is the comparison rather than any single measurement that carries the value. The limb is checked for color, warmth, pulse, sensation, movement and pain, the splint is checked for tightness, and the patient is checked for the general signs of blood loss, which include a climbing pulse, skin that is becoming pale and cool, and a level of consciousness that is drifting. Pain that increases instead of settling, pain far worse than the injury appears to explain, numbness, tingling, weakness, a limb that becomes tense and hard, and a wound that begins to bleed through its dressing are the findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation. A patient who was comfortable ten minutes ago and is distressed now is telling the responder that something has changed, and the answer is to examine again and to call for help.

Referral or definitive treatment is the final step of the sequence, and it begins with the recognition that some injuries cannot be managed where the patient lies. A patient with an open fracture, a suspected compartment syndrome, a damaged nerve or vessel, a dislocation, or a fracture that enters a joint is referred to a facility with imaging, an operating room and an intensive care unit. The referral is made by the clinician responsible for the patient, while the lay first aider and the trained responder call for help early rather than deciding what treatment is needed. Definitive treatment includes reduction, which restores the position of the bone ends, traction, which holds them in line with a gentle continuous pull, and casting or surgical fixation, which holds the position while the bone heals, and all of those acts are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol. A reduction must not be attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when the vessels or the nerves are failing, when the injury is open or the diagnosis is unclear, or when an earlier attempt has failed. In each of those situations the attempt can convert an injury that would have healed into one that cannot, and force is not a technique.

Across all of these complications the division of labor is the same, and it is worth stating plainly. The lay first aider recognizes danger, calls for help, supports the limb, covers a wound, keeps the patient warm and reports what can be seen, and nothing more is safe at that level. The trained first responder, the emergency medical technician and the paramedic work within the procedures and the equipment their service authorizes, which usually include splinting, wound coverage, control of external bleeding and the recording of the neurovascular examination. The nurse monitors, records, gives what has been prescribed and escalates a change to the physician without delay, while the physician assesses, orders imaging, decides about fluids and analgesia within the local protocol, and calls the orthopedic or vascular surgeon. The surgeon repairs the vessel and releases the compartment, and the operating room is the only place where those acts are done. The record and the handover are part of the treatment rather than a formality after it, so the time of the injury and the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention are written down in a fixed order.

To summarize, the first minutes and hours after a fracture or a dislocation are decided less by the bone than by the bleeding, the nerves, the vessels and the pressure inside a closed compartment, and by whether the rescue adds harm to harm. Bleeding from a broken bone may be entirely hidden, so a closed fracture of the thigh or the pelvis can cause shock with no blood on the skin, and a pulse that remains present does not exclude damage to a vessel or a compartment syndrome. The course follows one order in every chapter, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and it names the provider level for every act. A deformed limb is splinted in the position found and is never straightened in the field, an embedded object is never removed, exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, an open wound is never explored blindly, and an encircling bandage or splint is released only within the local protocol by someone authorized to do it. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and a reduction is not attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, or when the vessels and the nerves are failing and need a specialist. Cold is treated as a complication, the spine is treated as unstable until it is cleared by someone qualified to clear it, the patient is reassessed after every intervention, and the surgeon is called early, because time is muscle and time is blood.
