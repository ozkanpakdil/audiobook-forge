# Chapter 59 — Surgical Treatment

An operation is one step in the same sequence this course follows everywhere, and it is neither the first step nor one that a responder performs. That sequence is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. Surgery belongs to the last of those steps, so everything done before it either prepared the patient for an operation or made one unnecessary. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

An operation on a fracture does three things, and any account of one can be judged by asking which of the three it addresses. The first is to put the broken ends into a position the body can heal and the limb can use, which is the act called reduction. The second is to hold that position without depending on a cast or on the stillness of the patient, which is done with a device fixed to the bone or passing through it. The third is to deal with everything else the injury damaged, which means the skin, the muscle, the vessels, the nerves and sometimes the surface of the joint itself.

The decision to operate belongs to the orthopedic surgeon and to the patient, and it is made after a conversation rather than from a picture alone. The surgeon brings the assessment of the bone, the state of the soft tissues and the resources of the facility, while the patient brings the life that has to continue afterward, including work, dependency and fear. A fracture that can be treated in more than one way is treated by explaining the choices, including what each choice asks of the patient and what each choice risks. A responder who tells a patient that an operation will certainly be needed has promised something that belongs to a surgeon's judgment and to a consent conversation.

Assessment comes before any decision about an operation, and it begins with the patient rather than with the radiograph. The mechanism of injury and the energy involved tell the clinician how much tissue the energy passed through, and a fall from a height or a collision at speed does far more than break the bone that finally stops it. The history includes illnesses and medicines that change how bone behaves and how it heals, and the general condition of the patient decides whether an operation is safe to offer at all. The primary survey, whose steps are remembered as ABCDE, meaning airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure, is completed before the limb becomes the priority. The limb itself is assessed by looking, by feeling and by asking the patient to move it, never by forcing it, and the findings are recorded in the words of the patient as far as possible.

Stabilization is what happens while the operation is still being decided, and in the field it is a small and disciplined set of acts. A deformed limb is supported and splinted in the position in which it was found and is never straightened, because the vessels and the nerves lie against the bone and a movement that looks helpful can tear what remained intact. The lay first aider supports the limb, keeps the patient warm and waits for help, while the trained first responder and the paramedic work within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes. Bleeding that can be controlled is controlled by pressure, an embedded object is never removed, exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, and nothing done in the field should make the operation harder.

Cold is treated in this course as a complication of trauma rather than a detail of comfort, and it follows the injured patient into the operating room. A patient who has lain on a cold surface, whose clothing is wet, or whose limb has been uncovered for examination loses heat quickly, and that loss disturbs clotting, the heart and the response to blood loss. Covering the patient, insulating the limb, removing wet clothing and warming the environment are acts within the reach of the lay first aider and of every level above it. Warming belongs to whoever is present, provided the local protocol is followed, while the measurement and the correction of a low body temperature belong to the clinical team.

The neurovascular examination is performed before any intervention and repeated after it, and its result is written down. A finding that was not recorded before a splint was applied cannot be attributed afterward, and that is the whole reason the record matters. The examiner looks at the color and the temperature of the skin, feels the pulse beyond the injury, presses briefly on a nail to watch the color return, and asks the patient to move the fingers and the toes. Sensation is tested on both sides and compared, and numbness, tingling, weakness and the loss of a movement are recorded plainly. A pulse that is present does not exclude serious injury to the vessels or to a compartment, and a pulse that is absent is an emergency.

Immobilization prevents movement of the injured part and of the joints above and below it, and before an operation it serves two purposes at once. It protects the soft tissues from the sharp ends of the bone, and it holds the patient in a condition in which imaging, transfer and preparation for surgery can proceed. A splint is padded where it presses on bone, it is applied over the position found, and it is secured firmly enough to hold without stopping the circulation. The trained responder and the paramedic apply splints within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, while the physician and the orthopedic surgeon decide what immobilization is required next. Casting, traction and surgical fixation are learned under supervision with real patients and performed according to local protocol, and none of them is taught in this course as a technique to be taken from a book.

Imaging confirms what the examination has already suggested, and it is ordered and interpreted by a clinician trained to do both. A radiograph is usually the first study, taken in at least two directions so that a displacement in a third direction is not hidden, and it includes the joint above and the joint below. Computed tomography shows the detail of a break that enters a joint or shatters a segment of bone, and it is often the study that decides which operation is possible. Magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for soft tissues, and an image is a picture of one moment, so the patient is still examined and reassessed after it has been taken.

Reassessment is performed after every intervention, and it is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the record, because the comparison is what has value rather than any single measurement. Pain that increases instead of settling, pain out of proportion to the injury, numbness, tingling, weakness and a limb that becomes tense and swollen are the findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation. A responder who suspects a compartment syndrome does not wait for the diagnosis to be confirmed, and acts within the local protocol, which may allow a constricting bandage, splint or cast to be released by someone authorized to do it. The limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, the patient is kept warm, and the patient is referred urgently, because the diagnosis is clinical and time is muscle.

Referral or definitive treatment is the final step of the sequence, and it begins with the recognition that some injuries cannot be managed where the patient lies. A patient with an open fracture, a suspected compartment syndrome, a dislocation, a damaged nerve or vessel, a fracture that enters a joint, or a fracture in a child that crosses the growing part of the bone is referred to a facility with imaging, an operating room and an intensive care unit. Transfer is arranged by the clinician responsible for the patient and carried out by a team trained and equipped for it, and it is not delayed for a study that will not change what is done. The receiving facility is told what was found, what was done, what has changed and what is suspected, in a fixed order, so that it continues a story instead of starting a new one.

A plate is a strip of metal that lies against the surface of the bone and is fixed to it with screws, and it can hold the broken ends in several different ways. It can press them together so that they unite directly, it can bridge a shattered segment and hold the length while the body fills the gap, and it can lock each screw into the plate so that the plate and the bone behave as one piece. The surgeon chooses among those behaviors according to the shape of the break and the quality of the bone, because a plate that presses works poorly in bone that has lost its density. Screws may also be used without a plate to hold a single fragment, and thin wires may be added where a small piece of bone must be kept in place. Placing any of these devices requires an open operation and a trained surgical team, and the technique is not something this course can teach.

A nail is a long rod passed into the hollow center of a long bone, so that it lies inside the bone rather than against it and holds the broken ends by running through both of them. A break in the shaft of the thigh bone, the shin bone or the arm bone is often treated this way, because a rod along the axis of the bone resists bending and carries load close to the line the bone itself would carry. The rod is introduced through a small opening at one end of the bone, guided into position with imaging used during the operation, and locked with screws across each end so that it can neither slide nor rotate. The operation is performed by an orthopedic surgeon in an operating room, and how much the canal is prepared beforehand belongs to the surgeon and to the current literature rather than to this course.

External fixation takes a different route, because the frame that holds the bone sits outside the limb and reaches it through the skin. Pins or wires pass through the soft tissues into the bone above and below the break, and they are joined by bars that the team can see and reach. Because the frame does not cross the zone of injury with a large implant, it is often chosen when the soft tissues are badly damaged, when the wound is contaminated, when the patient is too unstable for a long operation, or when the pelvis must be held together while bleeding is controlled. It may be the definitive treatment, and it may equally be a temporary measure that holds the limb until the soft tissues are ready for something else. The pins create a route from the outside world into the bone, so the pin sites are a place where infection begins, and a responder who sees spreading redness, increasing pain or discharge there reports it rather than touching the frame. The frame is applied, adjusted or padded only by the team qualified to do it, and it is never leaned on or used as a handle for the patient.

When the surface of the joint itself is destroyed, holding the bone is not enough, and the joint may be replaced. The worn or broken ends of the bones that form the joint are removed and replaced with an artificial joint, and the operation is called an arthroplasty when it redoes the whole joint and a hemiarthroplasty when it replaces only one side of it. This is a common operation in the older adult who has broken the hip, where the blood supply to the head of the thigh bone has been damaged and the bone will not unite, and it allows the patient to stand sooner than a healing fracture would. The choice among fixing the bone and replacing part or all of the joint depends on the age of the patient, the state of the bone, the ability of the patient to cooperate with protected movement and the wishes of the patient. It is decided by the orthopedic surgeon together with the patient or the family, and it is never a decision for a responder.

After the operation a responder may meet the limb again in a ward, in a vehicle or at home, and what is visible is the wound and the dressing rather than the device. A closed wound is usually held with stitches, staples or strips of tape, and it is covered with a dressing whose job is to keep it clean, to absorb what seeps and to let the team see whether bleeding continues. A drain may be left in place briefly to carry collected blood and fluid out of the wound, and it is removed by the team that placed it. A dressing that has become soaked is reinforced rather than taken down, because taking it down opens the wound to the environment and to hands that have no reason to be in it, and a change of dressing is done by a nurse or a physician following the local protocol. The lay first aider does not touch a surgical dressing at all, and the trained responder reports what is seen and leaves it covered. How much weight the patient may put on the operated limb is a decision the orthopedic surgeon makes and records, it is stated in words such as no weight, some weight or full weight, and a responder never decides that a limb looks strong enough to bear weight.

Infection is the complication that turns a good operation into a long illness, and it is watched for in every patient who has had one. The findings are a wound that becomes more painful rather than less, skin that is red and warm around it, swelling that increases, a fever, and a patient who feels unwell in a way that is hard to describe. In the first days after an operation those findings may be caused by something other than infection, and only the clinical team can tell the difference, so the responder reports rather than concludes. Whether an antibiotic is given, which one and by what route are decisions of the prescriber, and this course names no substance and no amount.

A compartment syndrome can develop after an operation as well as before one, and the treatment that was meant to help is sometimes the cause. A cast, a tight bandage, a dressing that becomes soaked and then dries, or bleeding inside a closed space can each raise the pressure within a compartment, and the earliest and most reliable finding is pain that grows instead of settling. The limb may look normal from the outside, the pulse may still be felt, and the patient may still move the fingers or the toes, which is why an intact pulse is not a reassurance. Pain relief that numbs the limb can hide the pain that would otherwise give the warning, so the team watches the limb even when the patient is comfortable. A responder who suspects a compartment syndrome does not wait for confirmation, does not elevate the limb above the level of the heart, keeps the patient warm, and refers the patient urgently, because the diagnosis is clinical and time is muscle.

Two further problems are common enough after an operation to be mentioned in any handover. A clot may form in the deep veins of the leg when a patient is still and has had a major injury, and the signs are a swollen, tender calf, while a clot that travels to the lungs causes sudden breathlessness and chest pain and is an emergency. Whether a medicine is given to reduce that risk, which medicine, in what amount and by which route are decisions of the prescriber working to the local protocol, and this course names none of them. Pain is treated for a reason as well, because a patient in severe pain cannot breathe deeply, move or sleep, but the choice of substance and the amount are again the prescriber's. The responder describes the pain in the words of the patient and reports it, and never takes a prescription from a book.

Repeating the neurovascular examination after an operation is as important as performing it before, and the record of the earlier examination is what gives the later one its meaning. A nerve may be injured by the original trauma or by the operation itself, and a weakness or numbness that was not present before is a finding to be reported at once rather than explained away. The responder records what the patient can feel and move, compares the two sides, and reports any change. Interpretation belongs to the clinical team, and the observation and the record belong to whoever is with the patient.

Rehabilitation begins earlier than most patients expect, and the reason is that a joint held still for a long time becomes stiff in a way that is hard to undo. Movement is often started while the bone is still healing, because the device holds the fracture and the movement keeps the muscle and the joint working. A typical interval for a long bone in the shaft of a limb to unite is given in teaching as 8 to 12 weeks, a figure offered here as an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because it differs with the bone, the patient and the service. The physiotherapist teaches the exercises, the nurse supports them through the day, and the patient, whose understanding matters as much as the device, does the work. In the older adult the goal is to restore walking as early as it is safe, because a person who loses the ability to walk loses independence as well.

The reason the decision belongs to the surgeon and the patient together is that an operation trades one set of risks for another, and only the patient can weigh the trade. The surgeon explains the injury, the options, the likely result of each option, and the complications that are known as well as those that are rare, and the patient answers with the life he or she wants to return to. Consent is not a signature collected at the door of the operating room, and a patient who does not understand what is proposed cannot consent to it, which is why an interpreter, a family member or a simpler explanation may be needed. Where the patient cannot decide and no one is authorized to decide for that patient, the law and the local protocol govern what happens, and those differ between countries and between editions of the same guideline. A responder who is asked whether an operation is necessary answers with the name of the person who can say, and not with an opinion.

Some operations are not a choice but a rescue, and they follow a different logic from the planned ones. A patient who is bleeding into the pelvis or the abdomen, whose limb has lost its blood supply, whose wound is open and heavily contaminated, or who is deteriorating for reasons that are not yet clear may go to the operating room before there is time for every study. In that situation the operation may aim only to control bleeding, to clean a wound, to hold a bone temporarily and to keep the patient alive, with the definitive reconstruction planned for a later day. The decision is made by a surgical team with anesthesia and an intensive care unit behind it, and it is not a decision for a responder to make or to delay. The responder's part is to recognize how fast the patient is changing, to say so plainly, and to keep the patient warm while the transfer proceeds.

Two groups of patients need their own arithmetic, and the reason lies in the bone rather than in the operation. In a child, the growth region near the end of a bone can be injured without the bone breaking, and an operation that crosses it carelessly can slow or stop the growth of that limb, so these injuries belong to a surgeon used to treating children. In an older adult, the bone may have lost density, so a device that holds well in strong bone may cut through it, and the surgeon may prefer a joint replacement to a plate for that reason. Neither fact changes the sequence that begins with assessment, and both mean that a responder should lower the threshold for getting a senior opinion rather than settle for an explanation that the injury is minor.

Carrying the operation into the next phase of care is the work of the record and the handover, which are part of the treatment rather than a formality after it. The time of the injury, the mechanism, the position in which the limb was found, the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention, the operation performed, the device used and the wound care ordered are written or spoken in a fixed order. An injury of the spine is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has done so, and no lone manual handling technique is safe, so the patient is moved by a trained team using the precautions the local protocol requires. What the next team needs is not an opinion but an accurate story with the times attached, because a finding without a time cannot be interpreted. Everything described in this chapter is learned with a teacher, practiced on models and on each other, and certified by an authority that also defines what may be done without supervision.

Consider a constructed illustration, which is not a real patient and is offered only to show how the parts fit together. A man in middle age is struck by a car and breaks the shaft of his shin bone, and the skin over it is torn, so the fracture is open and the soft tissues are badly bruised. At the scene the trained responders control the bleeding, cover the wound, find the pulse and the sensation intact, splint the limb in the position found and keep him warm. In the hospital the wound is cleaned in an operating room, the bone is held temporarily with a frame outside the limb, and the wound is left covered while the tissues settle. Some days later the orthopedic surgeon replaces the frame with a rod inside the bone, and the wound is closed. Weeks afterward a responder meets him at home with a healed wound, a limb that is beginning to bear weight as the surgeon allowed, and exercises taught by a physiotherapist, and nothing in that illustration is a rule.

Certain acts lie outside the scope of everyone except the team that owns them, and the reason is that each of them can convert an injury that would have healed into one that cannot. A responder never straightens a deformed limb, never removes an embedded object, never pushes exposed bone back into a wound, never explores an open wound blindly, and never takes down a surgical dressing to look underneath it. No chapter of this course teaches a listener to reduce a fracture, to apply traction or to apply a cast, because those skills are learned under supervision with real patients and are performed according to local protocol. A reduction is not attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when the vessels or the nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, when the responder lacks training or equipment, or when an earlier attempt has failed. Force is not a technique, and resistance is a reason to stop.

To summarize, an operation is the last step of the sequence this course follows, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and the decision to operate belongs to the orthopedic surgeon and to the patient together. The operations that hold a fracture use a plate and screws against the bone, a nail inside it, a frame outside it, or an artificial joint that replaces the surface the injury destroyed, and each of them is chosen for one patient and one fracture. The neurovascular status is recorded before any intervention and repeated after it, the limb is reassessed after every intervention, and a limb that becomes tense and painful out of proportion to the injury is treated as a compartment syndrome until someone qualified decides otherwise. A responder never straightens a deformed limb, never removes an embedded object, never pushes exposed bone back into a wound, never explores an open wound blindly, and never takes down a surgical dressing. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, no dose and no route appears in this course, the patient is kept warm, the spine is treated as unstable until it is cleared by someone qualified to clear it, and who to call is settled by the local protocol before the injury happens.
