# Chapter 60 — Rehabilitation

Rehabilitation is not the period that begins when the fracture has healed; it begins in the first hour after the injury and continues long after the last radiograph has been filed. It is the part of treatment that decides whether the limb works, and a limb that has united perfectly on an image but cannot reach the mouth, bear weight or hold a cup is a treatment that has failed the patient. Everything this course teaches leads to one question, which is whether the person can do again what they did before, and rehabilitation is the work that turns a healed bone into a working limb. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

The same sequence that governs the acute injury governs the whole of recovery, and it is worth naming again because it does not stop when the splint is applied. Assess, stabilize, examine the neurovascular status, immobilize, image, reassess, and then refer or treat definitively. In rehabilitation the assessment is the progress the patient has made, the stabilization is the protection of what is healing, and the reassessment is the scheduled review that catches the complication before the patient loses function. The sequence is a habit of mind rather than a list to be recited, and it is repeated at every visit, in the clinic, at home and on the telephone.

Healing is the work of the body, and no exercise, machine or program makes bone unite faster than it will. What rehabilitation does is protect the conditions in which healing can happen and preserve everything around the injury that the healing process does not repair by itself. Muscle that has wasted, a joint that has stiffened and a nerve that has been compressed do not recover on their own simply because the fracture has joined. The outcome measure of this phase is therefore function, and the union of the fracture is only one of the conditions that make function possible.

The goals of rehabilitation are few and can be said plainly. The first is to keep moving every joint that the injury did not involve, because a joint that is not moved becomes stiff and the cartilage within it is nourished by movement. The next is to keep as much muscle as the injury and its treatment will allow, because muscle is lost far more quickly than it is regained. Then comes the control of swelling and the control of pain, since both of them, if they are allowed to persist, prevent the movement that the first two goals require. The last is the restoration of the patient's own independence, which is the goal the patient cares about most and the one that a measurement can easily miss.

Before any of this begins, the treating clinician decides what the injury will tolerate, and nothing in this chapter gives a listener permission to make that decision. A bone fixed with metal, a bone healing in a cast, a joint that has been put back into place and a ligament that has been repaired each allow a different amount and a different kind of movement, and the difference is not visible to the person holding the limb. The physical therapist designs and progresses the program, the nurse supports it at the bedside and in the clinic, and the patient performs it. Every one of them works inside the instruction of the treating physician or orthopedic surgeon and inside the local protocol.

The assessment at the start of rehabilitation is a full one, because a patient who has been immobilized for weeks is not the patient who was injured. The examiner asks what the patient can do now that they could not do a week ago, and what they could do a week ago that they cannot do now, because a loss of function is more informative than any single measurement. The limb is looked at, felt and moved, the joints above and below the injury are examined, and the strength of the muscles that cross them is tested against the other side. Nothing in this examination forces a joint or reproduces pain deliberately, because an examination that injures the tissue it is measuring has done harm rather than good.

The neurovascular examination recorded on the day of the injury is repeated at every stage of recovery, and its result is written down in the same form. The color and the temperature of the skin are observed, the pulse beyond the injury is felt, and sensation is tested and compared with the other side. The patient is asked to move the fingers or the toes, and any weakness is recorded in the words of the patient as far as possible. A finding that has changed since the previous visit matters more than one that has always been abnormal, which is why the earlier record is part of the examination. The lay first aider and the trained responder report what they observe, while the interpretation belongs to the clinician.

A new numbness, a new tingling, a new weakness, a limb that has become cool or pale, and pain that increases instead of settling are the findings that stop the program and send the patient back for urgent assessment. The complication this course fears most is the one caused by treatment, and in rehabilitation that usually means a cast, a splint, a bandage or a brace that has become too tight as the limb swelled. A responder who suspects a compartment syndrome does not wait for confirmation and does not elevate the limb above the level of the heart; the constricting device is released only by someone authorized to do it under the local protocol, and the patient is transferred urgently, because the diagnosis is clinical and time is muscle. This is why the neurovascular findings are recorded before a device is applied and again after it is applied or changed.

Swelling after an injury is expected, and it is also a mechanical problem that slows everything else. Fluid that collects in a limb makes the joints stiff, weakens the muscle that has to work against it, and presses on the small vessels and nerves at the edge of the healing tissue. Movement of the joints that were not injured pumps that fluid back toward the trunk, which is one reason early movement of the uninjured parts is worth the trouble it takes. Elevation of the limb above the level of the heart helps in most situations, with the important exception of a limb in which a compartment syndrome is suspected or not yet excluded. Compression, when it is applied by someone trained and authorized to apply it, works in the same direction, and a poorly applied bandage works in the opposite one.

Pain has to be respected, because pain that is not controlled prevents movement and sleep, and a patient who cannot sleep cannot heal well. The physical measures that reduce pain belong to the patient, the nurse, the physical therapist and the trained responder according to what their service authorizes: rest and protection of the injured part, a supported position, elevation when it is appropriate, and the careful application of cold within the limits the local protocol sets. The choice of a medicine, its amount and the way it is given belong to the prescriber, and this course deliberately gives no dose, no route and no regimen. Pain relief becomes urgent when the pain is out of proportion to the injury, increases instead of settling, is not relieved by the measures that usually work, or comes with a change in the neurovascular findings.

A constructed illustration may make the shape of this clearer. A patient with a fracture of the lower leg treated in a cast is taught to move the toes and the knee, to keep the leg supported, to keep the cast dry and to return if the pain increases or the toes change color. Suppose that some days later the toes become pale and the pain becomes worse rather than better; the illustration is meant to show that this finding is not the ordinary course of healing but an emergency, and that the response is urgent assessment rather than loosening the cast at home. An illustration is not a patient, and the details here are invented for teaching.

Early movement of the joints that were not injured is the single most valuable and most neglected part of recovery. A shoulder that is not moved while the forearm is in a cast becomes the joint that limits the arm long after the fracture has healed, and the same is true of a knee left still while the ankle is treated, or of a hip kept flexed in one position for weeks. The movements are chosen by the physical therapist and authorized by the treating clinician, they are performed gently and within the range that causes no more than mild discomfort, and they stop at any moment when the patient feels something give way or when the pain becomes sharp. The lay first aider and the trained responder can encourage and remind, but the prescription of movement belongs to the clinical team.

The order matters, and it can be described without teaching a technique. The first step is to keep the uninjured joints moving through their available range in a way the clinician has approved. The next is to teach the patient to tighten the muscles around the injured part without moving the joint, a contraction that is felt rather than seen, since this keeps muscle working while the bone is protected. Only then, and only when the treating clinician has confirmed that the bone or the repair will tolerate it, is movement of the injured joint itself begun, and even then under the supervision of a physical therapist rather than on the initiative of the patient, because force has no place in any of these steps and a movement that produces sharp pain or a sense of instability is stopped and reported.

Muscle is the tissue that punishes immobility most quickly, and the loss begins within days rather than weeks. Muscle that is not used loses its bulk and its strength, and in an older adult the loss is faster and the recovery slower, so a period of bed rest that would cost a young patient little can cost an older patient the ability to stand unaided. The program therefore keeps every usable muscle working, using contractions that do not move the joint while the fracture is unprotected, and later using movement against resistance once the clinician has authorized it. No weight is borne through an injured bone or an unhealed repair until the treating clinician allows it, because a limb loaded too early can lose the position that the treatment achieved. The physical therapist decides the exercise, the clinician decides when the limb may be loaded, and the patient decides how much effort to give.

The decision about bearing weight is a clinical one, and the words used for it are worth understanding even by those who will never make the decision. Some patients are told to keep the limb off the ground entirely, others that only the weight of the limb may be taken, others that part of the body weight may be taken with support, and others that full weight is allowed. Each of those instructions is specific to one injury, one operation and one stage of healing, and none of them can be transferred from one patient to another or from one week to the next. A patient who is unsure must ask rather than guess, and the nurse or the physical therapist who is unsure must ask the clinician rather than improvise.

Immobilization protects the healing tissue, and it also creates most of the problems that rehabilitation later has to solve. A cast or a brace holds the bone while it knits, and at the same time it holds the joints still, hides the skin, and takes away the sensation that would warn the patient of trouble. Where a limb is found deformed it is supported and splinted in the position in which it was found, and it is never straightened by anyone outside a supervised clinical setting, because the vessels and the nerves lie against the bone. Skin under a device can be rubbed sore, can become macerated by moisture, and can break down without the patient feeling it clearly, so the patient is asked at each visit about itching, burning, a damp feeling, a bad smell or a new pain over a bony point. Nothing is ever pushed under a cast, and the cast itself is never removed by the patient at home. Removing, trimming, splitting or replacing such a device is a clinical act performed by someone trained and authorized to do it, within the local protocol, because the position it holds may be lost as soon as it comes off.

Reassessment is performed after every intervention and at every stage, and in rehabilitation the interventions are spread over weeks rather than minutes. Each visit repeats the same observations in the same order and compares them with what was recorded before, because the comparison is what carries information. The patient is asked about pain, sleep, mood and what they can do for themselves, the limb is examined, the device is inspected, and the neurovascular status is recorded again. When a program is advanced, the patient is observed performing the new activity before being sent home with it, since an exercise understood in the clinic and done differently at home is worse than none. A change in the findings is the signal to stop the progression and to refer the patient back to the clinician responsible for the injury.

Imaging belongs to the clinician and continues to be used during recovery for reasons that are specific. A radiograph may be taken to see whether the bone is uniting, to check that the position has been kept, to look for a break in the metal that holds a repair, or to look again at an area where pain persists without explanation. Computed tomography shows the detail of a bone that is healing slowly or a joint that has become irregular, and magnetic resonance imaging is used when the question concerns the soft tissues, such as a ligament, a tendon, a cartilage surface or an infection deep to the skin. The physical therapist may report a finding and the nurse may ask for an opinion, but ordering and interpreting the image belong to the clinician trained to do both. It is worth saying plainly that a good image and a stiff limb are entirely compatible, which is why the program is guided by function and not by the picture alone.

The times and the thresholds that surround recovery are illustrations and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because they differ between services, between countries and between editions of the same advice. It is reasonable to describe the phases of healing in general terms, such as a period of protection while the tissue is fragile, a period in which controlled movement is introduced, a period of strengthening, and finally a return to the activities the patient wants. The length of each phase depends on the bone, the injury, the operation, the age and the health of the patient, and the presence of any complication, and no chapter can give a listener a number that will be right for the person in front of them. Where a clinician names a time, that time is a plan to be reviewed rather than a promise.

Referral during recovery is as important as referral in the first hour, and the findings that prompt it are not always dramatic. A fracture that is more painful after several weeks rather than less, a limb that will not take weight when the clinician expected it to, a joint that is losing movement instead of gaining it, a wound or a surgical site that becomes red, hot and swollen, a new deformity, and a numbness that does not resolve are findings that send the patient back to the clinician responsible for the injury. Infection, a bone that fails to unite, a repair that has loosened, a tendon that has pulled apart, and a nerve compressed by scar tissue all present in these quiet ways. The decision about what to do next belongs to that clinician and to the orthopedic surgeon, and the task of everyone else is to recognize the finding, record it and arrange the review without delay.

Some patients heal differently, and rehabilitation has to be written for the patient rather than for the injury. A child's bone remodels and a child's rehabilitation is often shorter and better tolerated, but an injury that crosses the growing part of the bone still needs scheduled follow-up until growth is finished, because a problem there can appear long after the child has stopped complaining. An older adult who has lost bone density, or who has lost muscle during even a short admission, needs a program that addresses strength, balance and the prevention of another fall, since the next fracture is often the one that takes independence away. A sign or a measurement that is reliable in a healthy adult may be absent or misleading in any of these patients, so the plan is adjusted and the review is earlier rather than later.

The conversation about how long recovery takes is part of the treatment, and it is the part most often avoided. A patient who is told that a bone takes about 6 weeks to knit, which is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, and who then finds that the limb is still stiff and weak after several months, may conclude that something has gone wrong or that they were misled. The bone unites, then the muscle must be rebuilt, then the joint must regain its movement, and then the patient must regain confidence in the limb, and none of those steps is the same as being able to use the arm or the leg normally. Nerves recover slowly, often over months, and some never recover fully, which the patient deserves to hear early. The honest answer to the question of how long is a description of the phases, an explanation of what can slow them, and a promise of review rather than a date.

Adherence is where most programs quietly fail. The patient who does not do the exercises at home is rarely lazy; they may be in pain, afraid that movement will break the bone again, depressed, sleeping badly, or unable to reach the clinic because of distance, cost or work. A program that a patient cannot perform in their own home is not a program, so the physical therapist builds it around the space, the help and the time the patient actually has, and the nurse asks at every contact what is getting in the way. Returning to work, to driving, to caring for others and to the ordinary tasks of a household are goals that motivate more than any measurement, and they belong in the plan from the beginning.

The mood, the sleep and the confidence of the patient are not a separate subject from the limb, and a patient who is frightened of using an arm will not use it however strong it becomes. Pain that has lasted for weeks disturbs sleep, disturbed sleep lowers the tolerance for pain, and the two together make every exercise harder, which is a circle broken by treating the pain, protecting sleep and progressing slowly enough to succeed. Returning to a job may depend on an employer's willingness to accept modified duties, and returning to driving depends on the limb, the law and the local rules rather than on the opinion of the patient. The occupational therapist addresses the tasks of daily living and the return to work, the physical therapist addresses movement and strength, and the clinician addresses the injury, and none of them can replace the others.

Rehabilitation after a dislocation follows the same logic, with the difference that the joint itself has been injured and the tissues that hold it have torn. The joint is protected while those tissues heal, the muscles around it are kept working in the ways the clinician permits, and movement is restored in a controlled way, because a joint moved too far too early can come out again. The decision about how far and how fast belongs to the clinician and the physical therapist, and it is made for that joint and that patient. Anyone who has been taught to put a joint back into place from a book has been taught something dangerous, because reduction is a procedure learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol.

There are complications that belong specifically to this phase, and the program is designed to catch them. A joint that becomes painful, swollen, warm and stiff some weeks after an injury, with skin that changes color and sweats more than the other side, is a picture that the clinician must assess, because it may not be the ordinary stiffness of recovery. Prolonged immobility can also lead to a deep vein thrombosis in the leg, and a swollen, tender and painful calf in a patient who has been still for days needs urgent clinical assessment rather than massage. Pressure on a nerve can produce weakness and numbness that worsen while the patient is following the program correctly, and that too is a reason to stop and refer.

Throughout this phase the record and the handover carry the same weight as they did in the first hour. What the patient could do at the last visit, what the neurovascular examination showed, what the device looked like, what was changed and who authorized the change are written in a fixed order so that the next clinician continues a story instead of starting a new one. The patient is given the same information in words they can repeat, including what they may do, what they must not do, and which findings should bring them back before the next appointment. A patient recovering from an injury of the spine is still treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has done so, no lone manual handling technique is safe, and the rehabilitation plan for such a patient is written with the team that manages the spine. Protection against cold remains part of care wherever a patient is exposed, waiting, or has a limb that is swollen and immobile, because cold slows healing and adds its own complications to an injury that already has enough.

Two things are deliberately absent from this chapter, and saying so is part of teaching safely. The first is the technique of reduction, of traction and of casting, all of which are learned under supervision, practiced on models and on patients under a teacher, and performed according to local protocol; a listener who learns the steps from a spoken description has learned nothing that is safe to do. The second is any medicine, its amount or the way it is given, because those belong to the prescriber and to the guideline. What a listener may take from this chapter is the shape of recovery, the findings that interrupt it, and the discipline of measuring function again at every visit, and every time it has named a time or a threshold it has named an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

To summarize, rehabilitation begins on the day of the injury and is the part of treatment that decides whether the limb works, so function and not the image is the measure of success. The same sequence continues through recovery, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and the neurovascular findings are recorded before any intervention and repeated after it. Uninjured joints are kept moving, muscle is kept working, swelling and pain are controlled, and the program of the physical therapist is progressed only within the authorization of the treating clinician, because the amount and the kind of movement an injury will tolerate is a clinical decision. A cast or a brace is never removed by the patient, nothing is pushed under it, weight is not borne through an unhealed bone until the clinician allows it, and a limb is not elevated above the level of the heart when a compartment syndrome is suspected. Increasing pain, new numbness, new weakness, a cold or pale limb, a red and swollen surgical site, a swollen and tender calf and a joint that is losing movement are the findings that stop the program and bring the patient back for urgent assessment, and reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol. The honest conversation about time is part of the treatment, because the bone unites long before the limb works, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.
