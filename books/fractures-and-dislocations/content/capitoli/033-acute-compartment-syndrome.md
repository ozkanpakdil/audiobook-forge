# Chapter 33 — Acute Compartment Syndrome

Acute compartment syndrome is the emergency this course returns to more than any other, because it is common enough to meet on an ordinary shift and quiet enough to be missed while the limb still has a pulse. It happens when bleeding or swelling raises the pressure inside a closed space bounded by bone and fascia until the smallest vessels can no longer carry blood to the muscle and the nerve within it. The muscle suffers first and the nerve a close second, so the patient loses feeling and power before the limb looks dramatic, and by then the harm is usually done. Everything in this chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work. The course teaches every injury through one order, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and this chapter follows that order from beginning to end.

A compartment is a box whose walls are bone and fascia, and it holds muscle, nerves and blood vessels with very little room to spare. Fascia is a tough sheet of fibrous tissue that wraps muscles and divides a limb into these boxes, and its important property here is that it does not stretch quickly. The lower leg is usually described as holding four compartments and the forearm three, with several more in the hand, the foot and the thigh, and each holds a group of muscles that share a nerve and a supply of blood. When the pressure rises inside one box the tissue cannot move aside, so the pressure is passed to every structure within it instead of dispersing. This anatomy is not a detail reserved for the surgeon, because it tells the responder which limb is at risk and which finding must be reported without delay.

The pressure inside a compartment rises for a small number of reasons, and a responder who knows them will suspect the problem earlier. The most common is bleeding from a broken bone, especially a break of the shaft of the shin bone or of the two bones of the forearm, because the bone lies within or beside a compartment. A crush injury does the same on a larger scale, and so does the return of blood to a limb whose artery was blocked or repaired, because tissue starved of blood swells when the flow comes back. A burn that encircles a limb creates a tough layer of dead skin that acts like a band, and so can a tight bandage, a splint strap or a plaster cast. Bleeding into a compartment is more likely in a patient who takes a medicine that thins the blood, so the list of medicines belongs in the history. As a constructed illustration, a person pulled free after being trapped under a fallen weight, with a leg that is tense and painful out of proportion to the wound, must be suspected of this at the roadside.

The process is a matter of pressures, and understanding it explains why the limb can look deceptively well. Blood reaches muscle through the smallest vessels, and those vessels stay open only while the pressure inside them is higher than the pressure around them. As the tissue pressure climbs it first narrows and then closes those vessels, so the muscle is starved even though the large artery is open and a pulse can still be felt at the wrist or the ankle. Anything that lowers the patient's blood pressure makes this worse, because the pressure driving blood into the tissue falls while the pressure inside the compartment stays the same. The nerve suffers early, which is why numbness and tingling may come before the loss of a pulse, and the muscle suffers in hours rather than days, though that window differs between patients and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. The diagnosis is clinical, and a responder who waits for the limb to look dead before acting has waited too long.

The findings that matter are few, and the first of them is pain that is out of proportion to the injury. The patient describes pain that is severe, deep and unrelenting, and that grows instead of settling after the limb has been splinted. Gently stretching the muscles of the compartment produces pain, and this pain on passive stretch is the finding that most often raises the alarm, though it is not present in every patient and is lost once the nerve has failed. The compartment itself feels tense and firm, and that tension is compared with the same place on the other limb. Sensation is diminished or changed in the area the nerve of that compartment supplies, and the patient may report pins and needles, numbness or a limb that feels asleep. Weakness, or the loss of a movement the patient could perform minutes earlier, follows, and the responder records each of these findings in the words of the patient where that is possible.

The signs taught as the classic picture of this emergency are the signs of a limb that has already been lost, and that is the trap this chapter most wants to close. Absent pulses, a pale or mottled color, cold skin and paralysis appear late, after the muscle and the nerve have suffered damage that may not be reversible. A palpable pulse does not exclude the diagnosis, because the large vessels remain open while the small ones have closed, and this single misunderstanding sends more patients to harm than any other. The responder therefore treats pain and tension, rather than pulse and color, as the findings that demand action, and reports all of them together. Waiting for the classic picture is the same as waiting for the damage to be done.

Some patients cannot report the pain on which the diagnosis depends, and in them the threshold for suspicion must be lower rather than higher. A young child may cry, refuse to use the hand or become quiet and withdrawn, and may not localize the pain at all, so a limb a child will not move is taken seriously. A patient who is unconscious, confused, deeply sedated or intoxicated cannot describe pain, and the examination must then rest on tension, color and the comparison with the other limb. A patient who has received a nerve block or an epidural anesthetic may feel no pain at all while the muscle is dying, which is why that decision belongs to a clinician who has weighed the risk. Where a sign is unreliable in the patient in front of you, say so in the record, and let the record carry the uncertainty forward.

The injuries that most often produce this emergency are worth knowing, because they set the level of suspicion before the examination begins. A break of the shaft of the shin bone is the classic, and a break of the upper part of the shin bone is close behind it, because both lie within a compartment that cannot expand. A break of both bones of the forearm is the other classic, and it is the injury that most often leaves the lasting contracture of the hand when it is missed. A break just above the elbow in a child, a dislocation of the elbow, a break of the bone at the lower end of the forearm and a break of the heel bone are all followed with particular care. The thigh, the upper arm, the hand and the foot can each be affected, and the thigh is dangerous because a large volume of blood can be lost within it. A compartment syndrome can also occur with no broken bone at all, so a normal image never removes the diagnosis.

The sequence begins with assessment, and assessment begins before the limb is touched. The responder makes the scene safe, notes the number of patients and the mechanism of injury, and records the time the injury happened, because that interval matters to the team that receives the patient. The primary survey comes next, and its steps are remembered as ABCDE, which stands for airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure, and it is completed before the limb is examined in detail. Only when the patient is stable enough does the assessment turn to the limb, and it is done by looking, by feeling and by asking the patient to move, never by forcing a movement. Life comes before limb when two correct actions compete for the same pair of hands, and a falling level of consciousness or a rising pulse sends the responder back to the beginning.

Stabilization of a limb that may be losing its muscle is a short list of acts, and restraint is the most important of them. The limb is supported in the position in which it was found and is never straightened in the field, because the vessels and nerves lie against the bone and a movement that looks helpful can tear what remained intact. Rings, watches, tight sleeves and any band that encircles the limb are removed by the trained responder or the clinician, because a ring that fits now may not fit later. Bleeding that can be controlled is controlled with direct pressure, an open wound is covered with a sterile dressing, and an embedded object is left in place because it may be holding pressure on a torn vessel. The limb is kept at the level of the heart and is never raised above it, for reasons this chapter explains. The patient is kept warm and insulated from the ground, because cold is a complication of trauma and not a detail of comfort.

The neurovascular examination is performed before any intervention and repeated immediately after it, and its result is written down at both times. The examiner looks at the color and the temperature of the skin and at the swelling of the limb, feels the pulse beyond the injury and compares it with the other side, and presses briefly on a nail to watch the color return. Sensation is tested in the area the nerve supplies and compared with the other limb, and the patient is asked to move the fingers or the toes and to say whether the movement is normal. The lay first aider observes color, warmth and the ability of the patient to move and to feel, and reports any change at once. The trained responder and the paramedic perform the formal examination within their training and their service procedures, and the nurse and the physician examine, document and compare. A finding that was not recorded before a splint was applied cannot be attributed afterwards, and that is the whole reason for the rule.

Immobilization means preventing movement of the injured part and of the joints above and below it, so that a splinted bone cannot still be levered by the joint at either end. A splint is padded where it presses on bone, is applied over the position found, and is secured firmly enough to hold without becoming a band that stops the circulation. Nothing that encircles the limb is tied so tightly that a finger cannot be slipped beneath it, and nothing adds pressure over a wound. The trained responder and the paramedic apply splints within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, and the physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides what definitive immobilization is required. Casting and surgical fixation are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and neither of them is taught from a book in this course.

A cast, a splint, a bandage or a dressing that has become tight is the one cause of this emergency that a caregiver can remove, and removing it is treatment rather than an afterthought. The bandage is unwound, the straps of the splint are released, and a plaster cast is opened together with the padding beneath it, because a cut shell still lined with a tight band continues to constrict. That act belongs to the clinician, or to the provider the local protocol authorizes, with the equipment and the training the protocol requires, and it is not a task a lay first aider improvises. The limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, because raising it lowers the pressure that drives blood into the compartment and can deepen the starvation. Cold packs are not applied and the limb is not massaged, because both narrow the vessels the muscle is fighting to keep open, and the patient is not asked to exercise the fingers or toes as a test. After the constriction is released the limb is supported, the patient is kept warm, and the release is judged by repeating the examination.

Reassessment is performed after every intervention, and it is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the record, and it is the comparison rather than any single measurement that has value. The limb is checked for color, warmth, pulse, sensation, movement and pain, the splint is checked for tightness, and the patient is asked whether the pain is better, the same or worse. Pain that increases instead of settling, pain that appears when the muscles are stretched, numbness, tingling, weakness and a limb that becomes more tense and more swollen are the findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation. A patient whose urine has turned dark after a crush injury is reported as well, because that finding belongs to the breakdown of muscle and to the team that will treat it. A responder who suspects a compartment syndrome does not wait for confirmation and acts within the local protocol, or escalates by another route without delay.

Imaging comes after stabilization, and it answers a different question from the one that matters most here. A radiograph is usually the first study, and it is taken in at least two directions so that a displacement in the third direction is not hidden, with the joints above and below included so that a second injury is not missed. Computed tomography shows the detail of a complex break around a joint, and magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for soft tissues such as muscle, ligament and cartilage, while neither of them measures the pressure inside a compartment. The clinician responsible for the patient orders and interprets the study, and requests it only when its answer will change what is done. Imaging never delays the referral of a patient in whom this emergency is suspected, and a report that shows a fracture and nothing more does not remove the diagnosis. An image is a picture of one moment, so the patient is still examined and reassessed after the picture has been taken.

Measuring the pressure inside a compartment is a clinical test rather than a field one, and it is performed by a clinician who has the equipment and the training the local protocol requires. The figures taught as thresholds differ between services and between editions of the guidelines, so a reading within 30 millimeters of mercury of the diastolic pressure is named only as an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. What matters more than any single value is the trend over time, the difference from the patient's own blood pressure, and the clinical picture, because one reassuring reading does not exclude the diagnosis. Only a clinician measures and interprets this pressure, and the responder's part is to report the findings that made the measurement necessary. Where the equipment or the trained person is not available, the patient is referred rather than watched until the test can be arranged.

Several other conditions produce a swollen, painful and tense limb, and a responder does not have to separate them at the roadside, but should know that they exist. A clot in a deep vein produces swelling and pain, usually with a limb that is less firm and a patient in less distress, and its confirmation and treatment belong to a clinician. An infection of the skin and the tissue beneath it reddens and warms the limb and may be accompanied by fever, and that distinction is made by the physician rather than by the responder. An injury to a major artery can produce pallor, coldness and a loss of pulse, and it is a surgical emergency in its own right, so it is reported in the same breath as the suspicion of a compartment syndrome. The responder's task is to describe what is seen and felt, to record it, and to hand both to the team that can decide.

Definitive treatment for an established compartment syndrome is an operation called a fasciotomy, and it is performed in an operating room by an orthopedic or vascular surgeon working with an anesthesia team. The surgeon opens the skin and the fascia over the compartment so that the pressure can escape and the muscle and the nerve can be supplied with blood again, and the wound is usually left open at first and covered with a sterile dressing. The operation is urgent, because muscle that has been without blood for hours does not recover, and the surgeon may return to look at the tissue again before deciding how the wound will be closed. Damage already present when the patient arrived cannot be undone by the operation, which is why it is not a substitute for early recognition. The technique of the operation is learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and it is not taught from a book.

A fasciotomy is a large operation, and like every operation it has complications of its own, which the surgeon explains to the patient and the family. Bleeding, infection, injury to a nerve or a vessel, and a wound that takes weeks to close are all possible, and a patient who becomes unwell after the operation is reported to the surgical team at once. When the diagnosis is made late, muscle that has died is replaced by scar tissue that shortens as it heals, and in the forearm this produces a hand with fingers that curl and cannot be straightened, a condition remembered as a contracture of the forearm. Sensation and strength may never return, and in the worst case the limb itself is lost, which is why this course treats the tense limb as a race against the clock. The chapter asks the listener to suspect the problem early and to say so plainly, because a picture of the pressure will not arrive in time to make the decision.

Every act in this chapter belongs to a named level of practice, and the levels are worth saying together because the listener may be any one of them. The lay first aider recognizes a limb that is swollen, tense and painful beyond the injury, supports it in the position found, keeps the patient warm, does not raise it, does not apply cold, and calls for help without waiting to be certain. The trained first responder and the emergency medical technician perform the same acts and add the splint, the formal neurovascular examination and the reporting of every change, within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes. The paramedic may release a bandage or a strap within those same procedures, and transports the patient urgently while alerting the receiving facility in advance. The nurse examines the limb, records the findings before and after each intervention, keeps it at the level of the heart, and escalates to the physician without delay. The physician makes the clinical diagnosis, orders imaging, may measure the pressure and may open a cast, and the orthopedic or vascular surgeon performs the fasciotomy and any definitive treatment of the fracture.

Medicines appear in this emergency in a few narrow places, and none of them is a regimen this course will give. Severe pain is treated, because a patient in agony is not made safer by withholding relief, but the substance, the amount and the manner of giving it belong to a prescriber working from the local protocol, and this course names none of them. A nerve block or an epidural can remove all feeling from the limb and hide the pain that is the earliest warning, so that decision belongs to a clinician who has weighed the benefit against the risk and who will keep examining the limb. A patient with an open wound over a fracture, or with a crush injury, needs decisions about protection against infection and against tetanus, and those decisions also belong to the prescriber and to the protocol. A listener must not be able to take a prescription from this book, and everything said here about a medicine describes who decides rather than what to give.

A short list of things that are never done in the field protects more limbs than any technique, and each has a reason attached. A deformed limb is splinted in the position found and is never straightened, because the vessels and nerves lie against the bone and because a closed injury can be turned into an open one by a movement that looks helpful. An embedded object is never removed, exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, and an open wound is never explored blindly, because the object may be holding pressure on a torn vessel and exploration adds injury and infection. A reduction is not attempted in the field, nor anywhere when the joint surfaces are shattered, a fragment is caught in the joint, the vessels or nerves are failing and need a specialist, the injury is open, the diagnosis is unclear, the responder lacks training or equipment, or an earlier attempt has failed. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and force is not a technique, so a responder who meets resistance stops and escalates. A patient with a suspected injury of the spine is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has done so, and no lone manual handling technique is safe.

The record and the handover are part of the treatment rather than a formality that follows it, and in this emergency they carry the whole story of a limb. The time of the injury, the mechanism, the position in which the limb was found, its appearance, the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention, and every change in the patient are written or spoken in a fixed order. The receiving team is told plainly that a compartment syndrome is suspected, because that sentence changes how quickly the patient is seen and who sees the patient first. Everything described here is learned with a teacher, practiced on models and on each other, and certified by an authority that also defines what may be done without supervision. Where this chapter names an interval, a pressure or a distance, it names an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between services, countries and editions. A course cannot certify a hand, so the listener is asked to use this chapter to recognize, to report and to refer rather than to act beyond the scope the law allows.

This emergency is quiet, and that is the quality that makes it dangerous, because the limb that is being lost often looks better than the injury would suggest. The patient may be calm, the wound may be small, the pulse may be present and the skin may be pink, while the muscle inside is starved and the nerve is beginning to fail. The responder who has heard this chapter is asked to trust pain, tension and the passage of time over the reassurance of a pulse, and to say the word compartment out loud to the team that receives the patient. Prevention here is not a device or a medicine but a habit of examination, repeated at every stage and recorded at every stage. A limb that is watched by someone who knows what to look for has a chance, and a limb left unexamined because it looks well is the limb this chapter was written to save.

To summarize, acute compartment syndrome is a raised pressure inside a closed space of bone and fascia that starves the muscle and the nerve, and the diagnosis is clinical, so it is made from the history and the examination rather than from a picture. What to look for is pain out of proportion to the injury, pain that grows instead of settling, pain when the muscles are gently stretched, a compartment that feels tense and firm compared with the other limb, and numbness, tingling or weakness in the area the nerve supplies. What to do is to assess the patient first, to support the limb in the position found, to keep the patient warm, to record the neurovascular status before and after every intervention, to immobilize, to image when a clinician orders it, and then to reassess. The constriction is released within the local protocol by the provider authorized to do it, the limb is kept at the level of the heart and never raised above it, cold and massage are not applied, and the patient is referred urgently, because time is muscle. What never to do is to straighten a deformed limb in the field, to remove an embedded object, to push exposed bone back into a wound, to explore an open wound blindly, to raise the limb above the heart, to apply cold, or to delay referral while waiting for a test. Call the emergency service and the receiving facility early, speak the words compartment syndrome when you suspect it, and work only within the scope of practice the local protocol and the law allow, because this course is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.
