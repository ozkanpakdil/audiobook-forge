# Chapter 45 — Osteoporosis and the Fragile Skeleton

Osteoporosis is a disease in which the amount of bone and the arrangement of its material have both fallen far enough that ordinary loads break it. The fracture is the event that brings the patient to care, while the disease is the reason the event was possible, and the two must be kept apart. A bone loses strength in two ways at once: it loses mass, so that less material carries the load, and it loses architecture, so that the material it retains is badly placed. This chapter is educational material for health professionals, students and trained responders, and it does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

Bone is rebuilt throughout life, and the balance of that rebuilding decides how strong it is when a force arrives. Cells called osteoclasts dissolve small packets of bone, and cells called osteoblasts lay down new bone in the space that has been cleared, so the skeleton is not a fixed structure but a surface that is continually resurfaced. In the first three decades the building usually outpaces the taking down, and a person reaches a peak bone mass that is set partly by inheritance and partly by hormones, nutrition, muscle pull and physical activity. After that peak the balance slowly turns, and loss begins in both men and women, though it accelerates in a woman after menopause because the hormone that restrained resorption has fallen away. Nothing in that process can be felt by the patient, and that silence is the first thing to understand about the disease.

A long bone has a dense outer shell called cortical bone and an inner lattice of thin plates and struts called cancellous or trabecular bone. The shell carries bending and twisting loads and gives the bone its stiffness, while the lattice spreads the load of a joint across a wide area at the ends of the bone. In osteoporosis the shell thins from the inside and the lattice loses whole struts rather than merely becoming thinner, and a lattice that has lost its connections does not recover them by gaining thickness later. Strength is therefore not one property but three: how much material is present, how well it is arranged, and how sound the material itself remains.

The disease is common, and most of the people who have it do not know that they do. It causes no pain, no swelling, no limp and no visible change until a bone fails, so a patient cannot be asked whether the skeleton is fragile and cannot be believed if the answer is no. Inheritance matters, so a family history of a broken hip in a parent is a useful piece of information, and a small body frame, a low body weight and a history of weight loss all raise the probability. A previous fracture after a minor injury is one of the strongest warnings available, because it says that the skeleton has already failed once under an ordinary load. The responder who hears any of this does not make a diagnosis, but neither does the responder treat the patient as though the bones were ordinary.

The illnesses and medicines that weaken bone are numerous, and a chapter can only show the shape of the list. The most common medicine cause is the group of steroid hormones used for asthma, arthritis and inflammatory disease, because they reduce the formation of new bone and increase its removal at the same time. Other conditions work through nutrition or absorption, including disease of the small bowel, celiac disease and previous surgery on the stomach, and others work through hormones, including an overactive thyroid, an overactive parathyroid and low testosterone in a man. Chronic kidney disease disturbs the mineral balance of bone, and some cancers settle in bone and hollow it from within, while multiple myeloma does so in a scattered and destructive pattern. Smoking, heavy drinking, long use of some medicines and years of poor diet belong on the same list.

The first sign of a fragile skeleton is usually a fracture, and it is usually a fracture caused by a mechanism that would not have broken a healthy bone. A fall from standing height is the classic example, but the force may be smaller still, and a cough, a sneeze, a bend to pick something up, a stumble off a curb or a hard embrace have each been enough to break a vertebral body or a rib. This is the idea of the fragility fracture: an injury whose severity is out of proportion to the energy that caused it. In a teaching illustration, an older woman who catches her foot and lowers herself to the ground with one hand returns with a painful wrist, and the wrist is treated while the reason the wrist broke is never mentioned; the second visit is the one that changes her life. The responder cannot prevent the second fracture alone, but the responder can make sure that the first one is described accurately enough for someone else to act on it.

Every bone in the body can be involved, but a few sites carry most of the burden. The bones of the spine, especially in the middle of the back and the lower back, are the most common, and the hip, the wrist and the shoulder are next. The pattern has some logic in it, because the sites that carry the richest cancellous lattice lose their strength earliest. A patient who has had two such fractures deserves a careful review of why, and that review belongs to a clinician.

The energy of the injury and the injury itself must always be compared, and a mismatch is a piece of information. A break of a large bone from a fall down a flight of stairs tells the examiner that the forces were large and the bone may be ordinary. A pathological fracture is one in which a disease process in the bone, most often a cancer that has spread there or a disease of the marrow, has weakened it before the injury happened, and it may occur with no remembered injury at all. Pain in a bone that is present before it breaks, or pain that wakes the patient at night, is a finding that must be reported rather than explained away as strain. The responder's work is to notice the mismatch, to describe it in plain words, and to pass it on; the naming of the underlying disease belongs to the clinician who can investigate it.

The spine deserves its own warning because the fracture there is the one most often missed. A vertebral body can lose height quietly and cause nothing more than a dull ache or a change in posture, and the patient may believe that the pain came from lifting a bag or from sleeping awkwardly. Loss of height over the years, a gradually stooped posture and a new hollow at the back of the neck are all visible findings that suggest old compression fractures, and they are found by looking rather than by asking. Because the bone is fragile, the spine of an older adult who has fallen must be treated as injured until someone qualified to clear it has done so. No lone manual handling technique is safe for such a patient, and the precautions are those the local protocol requires.

The hip is the fracture that frightens patients most, and it can present in ways that do not look like a hip at all. A patient may complain only of pain in the knee, because the nerves of the hip joint also supply the knee, and the hip itself may be moved with little discomfort. The classic picture of a leg that is short and turned outward is helpful when it is present, but it is absent often enough that its absence proves nothing, and some patients with a fractured hip can still bear weight a few steps. A radiograph taken on the day of the injury may look normal when the fracture is a crack that has not displaced, and that is why a patient whose pain and inability to walk do not match the image needs further assessment. The responder keeps such a patient still and does not test the limb by asking the patient to walk on it, because the test can complete a fracture that was only a crack.

A break of the wrist is the fragility fracture of middle life, and it usually follows a fall onto the outstretched hand. The hand and the forearm may be visibly deformed, and the patient guards the limb and will not use it, but the amount of deformity is a poor guide to the amount of damage. The upper end of the arm bone is broken in the same way, often by a fall from standing, and the patient may only report that the shoulder hurts and will not lift. A break of the pelvis in an older adult after a low energy fall is a serious injury with a risk of bleeding, and a break of a rib after a cough carries a risk of lung injury. Every one of these findings is handled in the same order, and none of them is handled by force.

Assessment of the patient begins before the limb is touched, and the first questions are what happened, when it happened and how the patient is now. The mechanism of injury, the age of the patient, the time of the injury, the medicines the patient takes and the fractures the patient has had before are all part of the history, and so is a history of falls. A fall that was not explained by a slip must be treated as a medical event until it is explained, because a faint, a stroke or a disturbed heart rhythm can present as a broken bone. Only when the patient is stable enough does the assessment turn to the limb, and the order is deliberate because a limb can wait for minutes that a brain and a heart cannot. The steps of the primary survey are remembered as ABCDE, meaning airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure, and they are performed before any splint is applied.

The limb itself is assessed by looking, by feeling and by asking the patient to move it, and never by forcing it. The examiner looks for swelling, bruising, deformity, a wound, a shortened limb or a limb that lies at an unnatural angle, and compares the injured side with the other side. The examiner feels the temperature of the skin, the tenderness of the bone and the tightness of the muscle compartments, and asks whether the feeling in the fingers or the toes has changed. Pain is a finding and must be recorded in the words of the patient, including where it is, when it began and what makes it worse. A fracture in a fragile bone may be far less dramatic than its severity suggests, and a patient who can move the limb may still have a fracture, so movement alone never excludes one. Equally, an older patient with confusion may not report pain at all, and a new reluctance to move a limb or a new confusion after a fall deserves the same seriousness as a complaint.

The neurovascular examination is performed before any intervention and repeated after every one, and its result is written down. The examiner looks at the color of the skin, feels its warmth, feels the pulse beyond the injury, presses briefly on a nail or a fingertip to watch the color return, and asks the patient to move the fingers or the toes. Sensation is tested on both sides and compared, and numbness, tingling or the loss of a movement the patient could perform a moment earlier is recorded and reported. A pulse that can be felt does not exclude serious injury to a vessel or the start of a compartment syndrome, and a pulse that cannot be felt is an emergency. In a patient with fragile bone the examination is more important rather than less, because a fracture can complete during handling, a nerve can be compressed by a fragment, and a break of the spine can threaten the spinal cord. The formal examination belongs to the trained responder or the clinician, while the lay first aider observes and reports.

Stabilization is a small set of acts, and the most important of them is restraint. The limb is supported and splinted in the position in which it was found and is never straightened in the field, because vessels and nerves lie against the bone and a movement that looks helpful can tear what remained intact. Straightening a deformed limb can also convert a closed fracture into an open one and can release the pressure that was holding a torn vessel closed. In a fragile skeleton the surrounding bone is weak as well, so a force applied to one part of the limb can break another part, and handling must be gentle and deliberate for that extra reason. The trained responder and the paramedic may apply a splint within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes. A wound near the broken bone is covered with a sterile dressing and is never explored blindly, and an object embedded in it is left in place. Cold is a complication of trauma rather than a detail of comfort, so the patient is insulated from the ground, wet clothing is replaced with covering, and an older patient is kept warm from the moment of the injury.

Immobilization means preventing movement of the injured part and of the joints above and below it, because a bone that is splinted alone can still be moved by the joint at either end. A splint is padded where it presses on bone, and in an older patient the padding must also protect thin skin that tears easily and skin that has already been damaged by pressure. The splint is applied over the position found and is secured firmly enough to hold without becoming a band that stops the circulation, and the neurovascular status is checked and recorded after it is in place. The trained responder and the paramedic apply splints within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, while the physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides what definitive immobilization is required, which may be a cast, a brace or a surgical fixation. Casting is a technique learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and it is never learned from a book.

Pain in these patients is real, and it is also a signal that must be respected rather than silenced without thought. The decision to give any medicine for pain belongs to a prescriber working within the local protocol, and this course gives no substance, no amount, no route and no frequency, because a listener must not be able to take a prescription from a book. The finding that makes the decision urgent is pain that is severe, pain that is rising despite the splint, or pain that is out of proportion to the injury, because that combination suggests that the circulation of the limb is failing. An older patient with confusion or memory loss may be unable to describe pain, and a new restlessness or a new confusion after an injury is treated as a possible sign of it. The responder's part is to describe the pain accurately, to record what has already been given as far as it is known, and to escalate rather than to improvise.

Imaging is the step that confirms what the examination has already suggested, and it is ordered by a clinician trained to interpret it. A radiograph is usually the first study, and it is taken in at least two directions so that a displacement in the third direction is not hidden, while the joint above and the joint below are included so that a second injury is not missed. In a patient with fragile bone the image is harder to read, because the bone contains less mineral and therefore casts a fainter shadow, and a fracture line may be visible only as a subtle change in the pattern of the bone. Computed tomography shows the detail of a complex break and is particularly useful around a joint, while magnetic resonance imaging shows soft tissues and can reveal a fracture that a radiograph has not, including a crack in the neck of the thigh bone. Either study is requested when the answer it gives will change what is done, and that decision belongs to the clinician responsible for the care of the patient.

A normal image does not exclude a fracture when the examination suggests one, and this is especially true in a fragile skeleton. A crack that has not separated may be invisible at the moment of the injury, an overlapping shadow may hide it, and pain may prevent the positioning that would reveal it. In the spine, a compression fracture may show only a slight loss of height in one vertebral body, and the change may be missed unless the examiner looks for it deliberately. A second image taken after some days may show the fracture more clearly as the bone edges resorb, and further imaging may be needed for a patient whose pain persists despite a reassuring first study. The lay first aider never orders or interprets imaging, and the trained responder does not decide which study is needed, because an image that is read by the wrong person can close a question that should stay open.

A measurement of bone mineral density is sometimes used to confirm the disease and to estimate the risk of a future fracture, and it is performed and interpreted by a clinician. The report compares the patient with a reference group of young adults and gives a figure that describes how far the patient lies from that reference, and any such number in this course is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the protocol of the service you work in. The measurement is one part of a judgment that also weighs age, previous fractures, medicines, falls and general health, and no single figure decides anything on its own. The responder's task is to know that such a measurement exists, to mention it in the handover when it is known, and to leave the ordering and the interpretation where they belong.

Reassessment is performed after every intervention, and it is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment. Pain that increases instead of settling, pain out of proportion to the injury, numbness, tingling, weakness, and a limb that becomes tense and swollen are the findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation. A splint or a cast can become tight as swelling grows inside it, and a bandage that was comfortable an hour ago can become a tourniquet, so the limb is checked again after every change. A responder who suspects a compartment syndrome does not wait for the diagnosis to be confirmed and acts within the local protocol, which may permit a constricting bandage, splint or cast to be released by someone authorized to do it. The limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, the patient is kept warm, and the patient is referred urgently, because the diagnosis is clinical and time is muscle.

Referral or definitive treatment is the final step, and in this group of patients referral is the rule rather than the exception. A patient with a fragility fracture of the hip, the spine, the pelvis or a joint surface is referred to a facility with imaging, an operating room and an intensive care unit, because these injuries need more than a splint. The timing of an operation for a fractured hip is a matter of local protocol and current guidance, and any interval named in this course is an illustration to be checked against the guideline in force where you work. The receiving team needs to know the mechanism, the time, the position in which the limb was found, what was done, and the neurovascular findings before and after each intervention. The part played by the responder is to recognize, to stabilize, to immobilize, to record and to transfer without delay.

Definitive treatment of the bone may include reduction, which restores the position of the broken ends, traction, which holds them in line with a gentle continuous pull, casting, which holds the position while the bone heals, and surgical fixation, in which a surgeon holds the fragments with metal devices or replaces the worn joint. Each of those acts is learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and none of them is taught from a book in this course, which describes only what they are for, what they must achieve and what can go wrong. A reduction must not be attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when the vessels or nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, when the responder lacks the training or the equipment, or when an earlier attempt has failed. In each of those situations the attempt can convert an injury that would have healed into one that cannot, and force is not a technique. A responder who meets resistance stops, supports the limb in the position found and escalates, because the decision to proceed belongs to someone qualified to make it.

The second half of definitive treatment is aimed at the skeleton itself, because the fracture that has just been treated is a warning about the fracture that has not yet happened. Whether a patient should take calcium, vitamin D or a medicine that slows the removal of bone is a decision for a physician, and this course gives no substance, no amount, no route and no frequency, because those belong to the prescriber and to the local protocol. What the responder can contribute is the recognition that a fracture happened after a minor injury, that the patient has lost height, that a parent broke a hip, or that the patient has been taking a steroid medicine for years, and the habit of saying so in the handover. Preventing the next fall is part of the same work, and it includes reviewing the medicines that cause drowsiness, checking vision, making the home safer and encouraging strength and balance exercise where the patient is able. The first fracture is the diagnostic event, and the second fracture is the one that must be prevented.

Handling and transport deserve special care in a patient whose bones are fragile and whose skin is thin. A patient who has fallen and whose spine has not been cleared is moved only by a trained team using the precautions the local protocol requires, because the spine is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has done so. Pressure injuries begin within a short time on a hard surface, so the patient is padded and lifted rather than dragged, and every contact point is checked during the wait for transport. Fracture of the ribs and of the pelvis makes breathing and coughing painful, so the patient is encouraged to breathe deeply at intervals and is watched for signs of a chest infection. Nothing in this paragraph replaces the manual handling technique that must be practiced with an instructor on a suitable model.

Some patients with fragile bones do not fit the familiar picture, and the responder must keep room for them. A younger adult may have a secondary cause of bone loss, such as a long course of steroid medicine, an eating disorder, an untreated hormonal condition or a period of immobility, and their fracture is as significant as that of an older patient. A child may have a rare condition that weakens bone, and a fracture in a child from a mechanism that seems too small must be reported rather than dismissed, since the same finding may also raise a question about how the injury happened. A patient who lives in a care home may arrive with a fracture that was noticed only when they stopped standing, and the absence of a clear story is not evidence that nothing happened. In all of these patients the sequence is unchanged, and the threshold for imaging and for referral is lower rather than higher.

To summarize, osteoporosis is a disease of bone amount and bone architecture that causes no symptoms until a bone breaks, so the first sign is usually a fragility fracture caused by a force too small to break a healthy skeleton. The responder assesses the scene, the mechanism, the age, the medicines and the history of previous fractures, performs the primary survey whose steps are remembered as ABCDE, and then examines the limb by looking, feeling and asking the patient to move it without ever forcing it. The neurovascular status is examined before any intervention and repeated after every one, and it is recorded, because a finding that was not written down before a splint was applied cannot be attributed afterwards. Such a patient is kept warm, handled gently, and splinted in the position found, and a deformed limb is never straightened and an embedded object is never removed. Imaging is ordered and interpreted by a clinician, and a normal radiograph does not exclude a fracture when the examination suggests one, so a patient whose pain persists is reassessed rather than reassured. Fragility fractures of the hip, the spine, the pelvis and the joints are referred to a facility with an operating room and an intensive care unit, and reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, never from a book. Every number in this chapter is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, the patient is reassessed after every intervention, and the first fracture is the moment to ask why it happened.
