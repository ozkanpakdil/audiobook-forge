# Appendix D — The Core Principle and Scope of Practice

Every injury in this course has been taught through one sequence, and this appendix names the principle that holds the sequence together. The aim of the responder is not to make the injury look normal, and it is not to produce a limb that appears straight and tidy on the outside. The aim is to protect the patient from further harm, to recognize what threatens life and what threatens limb, and to move the patient toward the care that can finish the job. A fracture that looks better after the responder has handled it may be a limb that has just lost its last intact vessel, and a joint that has been forced back into place may now be a wound that the skin can no longer hide. This appendix is placed last on purpose, because the judgment that decides whether to act matters more than any technique, and it is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

The first duty is restraint, and restraint is a skill and not the absence of action. A limb that is deformed is supported and immobilized in the position in which it was found, and it is never straightened in the field, because the vessels and the nerves lie against the bone and the movement that looks tidier may tear what was still carrying blood and feeling to the hand or the foot. Pulling a limb straight may also turn a closed fracture into an open one and release the pressure that was holding a torn vessel closed. The lay first aider supports the limb with the hands, with a pillow or with whatever is at hand, keeps the patient warm, and waits for help. The trained first responder and the emergency medical technician or paramedic may apply a splint within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, and each of those levels works inside a scope that has been defined for it. What none of them does is improvise a reduction, because the act that seems helpful is the act that most often causes the harm this course is written to prevent.

The recognition that must come before every other is that the patient may die while the injured limb is being examined. The assessment begins with the scene and with the mechanism of injury, and it then runs through the primary survey, whose steps are remembered as ABCDE, meaning airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure. A patient who is not breathing, who is bleeding from a wound that a dressing has not controlled, or whose level of consciousness is falling is treated before the fracture, and the fracture is protected in the meantime by the hands of whoever is present. Where two correct actions compete for the same pair of hands, the principle that settles the order is that life comes before limb, because a limb can wait for minutes that a brain and a heart cannot. Only when the patient is stable enough does the examination turn to the limb, and then it looks, it feels and it asks the patient to move, and it never forces.

The second recognition is that a limb can be lost while the patient is otherwise stable, and the findings that warn of that loss are gathered by the hands that will later apply the splint. The examiner looks at the color and the temperature of the skin, feels the pulse beyond the injury, presses briefly on a nail to watch the color return, tests sensation on both sides and compares them, and asks the patient to move the fingers or the toes. Numbness, tingling, weakness, the loss of a movement the patient could perform a moment earlier, pallor, coolness, a diminished pulse and pain out of proportion to the injury are the findings that change the plan. The neurovascular examination is performed before any intervention and repeated after it, and its result is recorded, because a finding that was not written down before a splint was applied cannot be attributed afterwards. A pulse that can still be felt does not exclude a compartment syndrome, and an absent pulse is an emergency that does not wait for a second opinion. Pain that increases instead of settling, and a limb that becomes tense and swollen, send the responder back to the beginning and up the chain of referral.

The whole of this course is contained in a single order of acts, and the order is deliberate because each step protects the next. The sequence is to assess the patient, to stabilize what threatens life, to examine and record the neurovascular status, to immobilize the injury in the position found, to obtain the imaging that a clinician orders, to reassess after every intervention, and then either to refer the patient onward or to treat the injury definitively. Assessment comes first because a treatment given to a patient who has not been assessed is a guess with instruments attached to it. Stabilization comes next, and it means keeping the patient warm, controlling the bleeding that can be controlled, and supporting the limb as it was found. Immobilization comes before imaging, because a fracture that is moved in order to reach a radiograph may be moved into a worse position on the way. Reassessment comes after everything else, because the complication this course is most afraid of is the one caused by the treatment, and a responder who does not look again will not see it.

Scope of practice is not a formality that is settled somewhere else, and it belongs in the same breath as every act this course describes. Each intervention has a level attached to it, which may be the lay first aider, the trained first responder, the emergency medical technician or paramedic, the nurse, the physician, or the orthopedic surgeon. An act that needs equipment, authorization or a protocol is named together with that requirement, so that the listener knows whether the act is available at the roadside or only inside a facility. The lay first aider performs the acts that need no equipment, which are to keep the patient safe and warm, to support the limb, to control the bleeding that can be controlled with a dressing, and to call for help early. The trained responder works within the procedures the service authorizes, while the clinician decides about imaging, about analgesia and about definitive care. When a responder is unsure whether an act lies inside the scope, the safe position is to protect the limb, to record what is found, and to hand the decision to someone who is authorized to make it.

Three techniques recur throughout this course, and none of them can be learned from a book, from a recording or from a constructed illustration. Reduction is the act that restores the position of the bone ends, traction holds them in line with a gentle continuous pull, and casting or surgical fixation holds the position while the bone heals. This course describes what each of them is for, what it is meant to achieve, and what can go wrong, and it says plainly that the technique itself is learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol. What can go wrong is not a short list, and it includes damage to the vessels and the nerves that lie against the bone, the conversion of a closed injury into an open one, the loss of a position that was tolerable, and a limb that becomes tight and swollen after the attempt. An open reduction and internal fixation is an operation performed by a surgeon in an operating theatre, and it is not a field procedure under any circumstances. Casting is likewise a supervised skill, because a cast that is applied too loosely does not hold and a cast that is applied too tightly can cause the very compartment syndrome the responder is trying to prevent.

A reduction is not attempted when the situation forbids it, and each of those situations carries its own reason. It is not attempted when a complex fracture and dislocation is suspected, because the bone ends may be locked in a way that only an operating theatre can unlock. It is not attempted when a fragment of bone is caught in the joint, when the vessels or the nerves are failing and need a specialist, or when the injury is open, because in each of those the attempt may convert an injury that would have healed into one that cannot. It is not attempted when the diagnosis is unclear, when the responder lacks the training or the equipment, or when an earlier attempt has already failed, because a failed attempt leaves the tissues more swollen and the next attempt harder. A limb that resists the movement, or a limb in which something gives way, is telling the responder to stop, and force is not a technique, since force has never been a diagnosis and has never held a position. These prohibitions bind the physician and the orthopedic surgeon as much as the first aider, because the decision belongs to the clinician who has examined the patient and who knows the protocol of the facility.

Where a substance must be considered, this course stops at the reasoning and leaves the substance itself and the amount to the prescriber and to the local protocol. Pain is treated, and pain that is severe, rising or out of proportion to the injury is a finding that makes the decision urgent rather than a matter of comfort. Analgesia, antibiotics, protection against tetanus and sedation each have a place in the management of an injury, and each of them is decided by a clinician who knows the patient, the wound, the allergies and the protocol of the service. This course gives no drug name, no dose, no route, no frequency and no regimen, and a listener must not be able to take a prescription away from it. What it gives instead is who decides, which finding makes the decision urgent, and what the scope of practice forbids to a responder who is not authorized to prescribe. The amount and the interval belong to the guideline that is current today and to the prescriber who signs for them, and a synthesized voice is a poor place to look them up.

A few further prohibitions belong beside the rule about reduction, and all of them are versions of one instruction, which is not to make an injury look tidier than it is. An embedded object is never removed in the field, because the object may be pressing on a torn vessel, and the bleeding that follows its removal is the bleeding that kills. Exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, and the wound is never explored blindly, because the exploration adds contamination and damage without adding information that would change what the responder does next. An open fracture is contaminated even when it looks clean, so the wound is covered with a sterile dressing, the bleeding that can be controlled is controlled, the neurovascular status is examined and recorded, the limb is immobilized in the position found, and the patient is transferred urgently. Definitive cleaning and closure of that wound belong to the operating theatre and to nobody else, and the sooner the patient arrives there the better the chance the limb has. Personal protective equipment is part of these acts rather than an afterthought, because a responder who is injured becomes a second patient and takes hands away from the first.

The steps that catch the complications caused by treatment are reassessment, warmth and the handover, and each of those is an act rather than an attitude. The same observations are repeated in the same order after every intervention and compared with what was written before it, and it is the comparison that carries the value rather than any single reading. When a compartment syndrome is suspected, the constricting bandage, splint or cast is released within the limits of the local protocol by someone who is authorized to release it, the limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, and the patient is referred urgently, because the diagnosis is clinical and time is muscle. Cold is a complication of trauma and not a detail of comfort, so the patient is insulated from the ground, wet clothing is replaced, and the covering is kept in place during the transfer. An injury of the spine is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has done so, and no lone manual handling technique is safe, so a patient who may have such an injury is moved by a trained team using the precautions the local protocol requires. The record and the spoken handover carry the time of the injury, the mechanism, the position in which the limb was found, every neurovascular finding before and after every intervention, and every change in the condition of the patient, so that the receiving team continues a story instead of starting a new one.

Escalation is a clinical act with its own threshold, and that threshold is reached when the condition of the patient or of the limb stops improving or begins to worsen. The call is made to the emergency service, to the trauma team, to the surgeon, to the orthopedic service or to the intensive care unit, depending on the injury, the distance and the protocol of the region, and it is made early rather than late. A limb that is pale, cold, pulseless or numb, a limb that is tense and painful out of proportion to the injury, a wound that continues to bleed through a dressing, a patient whose pulse rises while the skin becomes pale, and a patient whose level of consciousness falls are all reasons to call before the next observation rather than after it. Distance and time matter whenever a patient must be moved, so the responders who work far from a hospital plan the transfer while they are still treating, because the hour spent watching a limb is the hour that limb needed. Nothing in this appendix authorizes an act beyond the training of the person performing it, and nothing in it sets aside the law or the protocol under which that person works.

The figures that appear in this course are illustrations, and the listener is asked to treat them as such. A time, a distance, a temperature, a threshold or a count named here serves to make a principle concrete, and every one of them must be confirmed against the current guideline and against the protocol of the service in which the responder works, because such numbers differ between services, between countries and between editions of the same text. Practice differs in the same way, and where this course describes more than one accepted approach it says that the difference exists rather than declaring one version universal. No real patient, no identifiable case and no institutional detail appears anywhere in these recordings, and every example is a constructed illustration made for teaching. Everything described in this course is learned with a teacher, practiced on models and on each other, and certified by an authority that also defines what may be done without supervision.

To summarize, the aim of everything in this course is not to make the injury look normal but to protect the patient from further harm, to recognize what threatens life and what threatens limb, and to move the patient toward the care that can finish the job. The order that carries those aims is to assess, to stabilize, to examine and record the neurovascular status, to immobilize in the position found, to image when a clinician orders it, to reassess after every intervention, and then to refer or to treat definitively, with the provider level named for every act. A deformed limb is never straightened in the field, an embedded object is never removed, exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, an open wound is never explored blindly, a limb that may be compartmented is never elevated above the level of the heart, and a patient who may have an unstable spine is never moved by a lone untrained technique. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and a reduction is not attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when the vessels or the nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, when the training or the equipment is lacking, or when an earlier attempt has failed. This course gives no dose, no route, no frequency and no regimen, so the substance and the amount belong to the local protocol and to the prescriber. Any number heard in this course is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. This appendix, like the whole course, is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work. The principle you began with is the one to keep, which is that the injury never has to look normal for the patient to be better, and every act is measured by whether it protected the patient from further harm and moved that patient toward the care that can finish the job.
