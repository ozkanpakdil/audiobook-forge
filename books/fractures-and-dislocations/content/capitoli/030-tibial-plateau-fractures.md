# Chapter 30 — Tibial Plateau Fractures

The tibial plateau is the upper surface of the shin bone where it faces the thigh bone inside the knee, and a break through it is an injury of a joint surface that must carry the weight of the body. A patient with this fracture usually arrives with a knee that has swollen within hours, that will not take weight and that hurts when it is bent. The upper end of the shin bone is soft inside and thin at its rim, so a force along the leg drives the surface down rather than breaking it cleanly across. The soft tissues around the knee then decide both how dangerous the injury is and what may be done about it, which is why skin, swelling and pulses matter as much as the bone. This chapter follows the order used throughout this course, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

The tibial plateau is formed by two rounded surfaces on top of the shin bone, the medial plateau on the inner side of the knee and the lateral plateau on the outer side. Between them rises a small bony peak called the intercondylar eminence, where the ligaments that cross the center of the knee take hold. The lateral plateau is smaller and slightly domed, the medial plateau is larger and slightly hollowed, and together they receive the weight of the body through the rounded ends of the thigh bone. Beneath a thin shell of joint surface the upper shin bone is built of spongy bone rather than dense bone, which is why a force down the leg dents the surface instead of cracking it across. The front of the same region carries a bony bump where the tendon of the quadriceps muscle attaches, and a fracture may run into it. In a child the region also contains the growth plate just below the joint, so an injury there can disturb the growth of the leg.

A plateau fracture is rarely a break of bone alone, because the force that drives the joint surface down also stretches or tears the structures that hold the knee together. The menisci are two rings of tough fibrous cartilage between the thigh bone and the shin bone, and they are commonly torn when a plateau is split or driven down. The ligaments that cross the center of the knee and those along each side may be stretched, partly torn or completely torn at the same time, and a piece of the bony peak between the plateaus may be pulled away with one of them. Because the joint surface is damaged, blood collects inside the knee, and that collection makes the joint swell, feel tense and refuse to move. No responder can tell from the outside which of these structures is injured, and that judgment belongs to a clinician who can examine the knee once pain and swelling allow. The responder records what can be seen, which is a swollen knee, a limb that will not take weight and a patient who holds it still.

The force that produces this fracture travels along the line of the leg and is usually a combination of a direct blow and a bending movement at the knee. A common history is that of a pedestrian struck by the bumper of a vehicle on the outer side of the knee, which drives the shin bone outward and breaks the lateral plateau, and that mechanism is why the injury was once taught as the bumper fracture. A fall from a height onto a straight leg and a landing in sport load the joint in the same way. The direction in which the knee bends decides which plateau breaks, because a force that drives the knee outward damages the outer surface while a force inward damages the inner one. A fracture of the medial plateau generally follows a more violent mechanism and is more often accompanied by injury to the ligaments and to the structures at the back of the knee. The energy of the mechanism therefore matters more than the size of the break on an image, and a small crack may belong to a limb with a torn artery behind it.

The soft tissues do not simply cover this fracture, because they decide what can be done and when it can be done. Swelling around the knee can become so great that the skin over it is stretched and shiny, and blisters may appear over the front and sides of the knee within a day or two. Skin stretched by swelling is skin at risk of dying, and the surgeon planning an operation will look at it and decide whether the knee is ready. Where the bone has communicated with the outside through a wound, the fracture is open, it is contaminated at the moment of injury, and it needs urgent surgical care rather than treatment in the field. The leg below the knee is divided into closed compartments of muscle bounded by bone and fascia, and bleeding or swelling inside one of them can raise the pressure enough to suffocate the muscle and the nerve within it. That complication, called a compartment syndrome, is why a swollen leg after a plateau fracture is watched closely rather than merely covered.

The patient with a fractured tibial plateau most often holds the knee slightly bent and refuses to straighten it, because straightening stretches the joint and hurts. The knee looks swollen and round rather than bony, and bearing weight causes sharp pain along the joint line on the side that was struck. A visible deformity is often absent, which is one of the traps of this injury, because a break of the joint surface can hide beneath a knee that only looks bruised and puffy. Some patients describe a knee that gives way, and the injury is mistaken for a sprain until the swelling fails to settle and walking remains impossible. Not every patient tells this story, and one sign taught as always present will mislead. An older adult whose bones have lost density may be treated for a sprain after a fall from standing height, a replaced knee may break around the implant, and a child may hurt so little that the injury is noticed only because the child refuses to run. The responder therefore records what was seen and felt, and leaves the diagnosis to the clinician who can image the limb.

Assessment begins before the knee is touched, with the safety of the scene, the number of patients and the mechanism of injury. The primary survey comes first, and its steps are remembered as ABCDE, meaning airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure, because a patient who has fallen from a height may have injuries that kill faster than a broken knee. Only when the patient is stable enough does the assessment turn to the limb, and the principle that decides the order is that life comes before limb. The history includes the time since the injury, the height of any fall, whether the knee was struck on the inner or the outer side, and whether the patient has been able to stand since. The patient's age, the illnesses that affect bone and the medicines that thin it belong to the same history, because they change how easily the bone broke and how well it may heal. The person who performs this assessment is whoever arrives first, whether a lay first aider, a trained first responder, an emergency medical technician, a paramedic, a nurse or a physician, and each works within the procedures their service authorizes.

Examination of the knee follows the primary survey and is done by looking, by feeling and by asking the patient to move the limb, never by forcing it. The examiner looks at both knees together, comparing their shape, the position of the limb and the color of the skin, and looks for a wound, a blister or an area of stretched and shiny skin. Feeling is done gently and from the unaffected side inward, noting where the pain begins, whether the joint feels tense and whether the skin is warm. The patient is asked to lift the leg and to bend and straighten the knee a little if they can, and an attempt that causes severe pain is stopped rather than repeated. Testing the stability of the knee by pulling it from side to side is a clinical skill learned under supervision and is not performed by a responder in the field. The limb is left in the position in which it was found, and that position is written down together with the time.

Stabilization means keeping the patient alive, keeping the limb from moving and keeping the patient warm, in that order. The painful knee is supported in the position in which it was found and is never straightened in the field, because the vessels and the nerves of the leg lie close to the bone and a movement that looks helpful can tear what remained intact. The lay first aider supports the limb with the hands, with a pillow or with whatever is at hand, keeps the patient still and waits for help. The trained responder and the paramedic may apply a splint within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes. Bleeding from a wound is controlled by direct pressure with a dressing, an embedded object is never removed, and exposed bone is never pushed back inside. A patient with an injured limb loses heat quickly, so wet clothing is replaced with covering and cold is treated as a complication of trauma.

The neurovascular examination is performed before any intervention, repeated after every intervention and recorded both times, because a finding that was not written down before a splint was applied cannot be attributed afterwards. The examiner compares the two legs and looks at the color and the temperature of the skin of the foot and the toes. The pulse is felt where the artery crosses the front of the ankle and again behind the inner ankle, and it is described as strong, weak or absent. A nail is pressed briefly and released, and the time the color takes to return is noted. Sensation is tested on the top of the foot, between the first two toes and on the sole, and the patient is asked to report numbness or tingling in their own words. Movement is tested by asking the patient to pull the foot up, to push it down and to turn it inward and outward, and the injured side is compared with the other.

The artery that supplies everything below the knee passes at the back of the joint and lies against the bone, and because it is held at both ends it cannot move out of the way when the bones shift. The nerve that lifts the foot winds around the small bone of the lower leg just below and outside the knee, and its injury shows as a foot that drops when the leg is lifted and as numbness over the front and outer side of the lower leg. An intact pulse does not exclude damage to a vessel or to a compartment, because a pulse can still be felt while the flow through the smallest vessels has failed, and an absent pulse is an emergency that stops everything else. A compartment syndrome is the complication this chapter most wants the listener to suspect early, and its earliest sign is pain far worse than the injury appears to explain while the leg becomes tense and swollen and stretching the muscles by moving the foot causes severe pain. A responder who suspects this complication does not wait for confirmation, releases any constricting bandage or splint within the local protocol and by someone authorized to do it, keeps the limb at the level of the heart rather than above it, keeps the patient warm and refers urgently.

Immobilization means preventing movement of the injured part and of the joints above and below it, because a bone that is held alone can still be moved from the knee or the ankle. For a suspected plateau fracture the knee and the ankle are both supported, and the limb is held in the position found without any attempt to bring it into a better line. A splint is padded where it presses on bone and secured firmly enough to hold without becoming a band that stops the circulation. The material matters less than the principle, since a padded board, a folded blanket, a vacuum splint or the body of a second person can each serve, and an improvised device must be checked more often because it shifts. The trained responder and the paramedic apply splints within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, while a nurse or a physician may apply a temporary device in a treatment area. Casting, traction and the devices that hold a limb for weeks are applied under the direction of the orthopedic surgeon and are learned under supervision, never from a book.

Imaging confirms what the examination has already suggested, and it is ordered and interpreted by a clinician trained to do it. A radiograph is usually the first study, taken in at least two directions so that displacement in the third direction is not hidden, and it includes the joint above and the joint below so that a second injury is not missed. On a plain radiograph the clinician looks for a step in the joint surface, for a fragment driven down into the bone beneath it and for a split that runs from the joint into the shaft. Computed tomography is used when the joint surface must be seen in detail, and it shows how far the surface has been driven down, how many pieces there are and which way the split runs, all of which change the surgical plan. Magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for the soft tissues, including the menisci, the ligaments and the cartilage, and for a suspected fracture that plain films do not show. An image is a picture of one moment, and the patient must still be examined and reassessed after it has been taken.

Reassessment is performed after every intervention, and it is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the record, because the comparison carries the value rather than any single measurement. The limb is checked for color, warmth, pulse, sensation, movement and pain, and the splint is checked for tightness, for slipping and for points where it presses on the skin. The knee is watched for increasing swelling, for a wound that was not there before, for blisters and for skin that has become shiny and tense. Pain that increases instead of settling, numbness, tingling, weakness and a leg that becomes hard and swollen are the findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation. A patient whose pain is eased but whose leg is tense is not reassured by the absence of pain, and a patient whose knee was comfortable an hour ago and is now rigid is not watched for another hour. Each reassessment is recorded and handed over in the same words, so that the next team continues a story rather than starting a new one.

Referral decides where the patient will be treated, and for this injury the answer is usually a facility with an orthopedic surgeon, an operating room and imaging. A patient with an open fracture, a foot without a pulse, a leg that is tense and painful out of proportion, or a knee that cannot be moved at all is referred as an emergency. So is a patient whose joint surface is displaced, whose knee is unstable, whose skin is stretched over a swelling, or whose fracture is so comminuted that the surface lies in many pieces. A child with a suspected injury across the growth region below the knee belongs with a surgeon who treats children, and an older adult whose bone is thin belongs with a surgeon who can weigh fixation against replacement. A patient with a knee replacement already in place and a patient with several injuries need the same urgent referral, whatever the first radiograph appears to show. Referral does not end the responder's task, because the limb is splinted, the neurovascular status is recorded, the patient is kept warm and the handover is completed before the patient leaves.

Definitive treatment begins with the decision about whether the joint surface can be left to heal without an operation, and that decision belongs to the orthopedic surgeon. A fracture that is not displaced, that leaves the surface of the joint smooth and level and that leaves the knee stable may be treated without surgery, and the patient is then supported in a device that holds the knee while the bone heals. What that device is, how long it stays and how much weight the patient may put on the leg are decisions made by the surgeon and the local protocol, and the periods used for such matters are illustrations to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. Casting and bracing are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and this chapter does not teach the technique of either. A patient treated without surgery is watched closely, because a fracture that looked stable can move as the swelling settles, so the limb is examined and imaged again as the surgeon directs. Exercise of the joints that are not held still, and of the muscles of the thigh, is begun when the surgeon allows it, and a physical therapist carries out the program.

When the joint surface is broken in a way that will not heal smoothly, an operation is needed, and it is performed by an orthopedic surgeon in an operating room. The surgeon opens the knee, restores the position of the fragments, fills the space beneath a driven down surface with bone graft or a substitute material, and holds the pieces with plates and screws in a procedure whose name is open reduction and internal fixation. Sometimes the soft tissues are too damaged for a plate to be placed safely, and the bone is then held from outside the skin with pins and bars in a frame, which may span the knee while the swelling settles and the skin recovers. The technique of every one of these operations is learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and none of it is taught from a book. The operation may be delayed until the soft tissues are ready, and how long that delay may be is a matter for the surgeon and the local protocol rather than a rule in a chapter. The aim of all of it is a joint surface that will carry weight without pain and a limb that can move.

The complications of a tibial plateau fracture are worth naming, because the patient is watched for them rather than merely treated and sent away. The joint surface may settle out of position as the bone heals, and a surface that is not smooth wears the cartilage of the knee and leads to arthritis years later. Infection is a risk of any operation and a greater risk after an open injury, and it may appear days or weeks after the patient has gone home. A blood clot may form in the deep veins of the leg and a piece of it may travel to the lungs, which is why a patient with a swollen painful calf and breathlessness is assessed urgently. Injury to the artery or to the nerves of the leg may be present from the moment of the fracture and may not recover, and a compartment syndrome can leave a foot that does not work. The patient and the family are told what to watch for, and a responder who meets such a patient later records the findings and refers.

Pain after this injury is severe, and relieving it is part of care rather than a courtesy. The decision about which medicine is given, how it is given and how often belongs to a physician, a nurse practitioner or another prescriber working within the local protocol, and the substance and the amount are never taken from a course listened to in a car. A responder who is not authorized to give a medicine does not give one, and a responder who is authorized gives only what the protocol names and only in the way the protocol names. What makes the decision urgent is a patient in severe pain, a patient whose pain is rising despite support and splinting, and a patient whose pain is out of proportion to the injury, because that last finding may be the first sign of a compartment syndrome rather than a call for more medicine. An open fracture also raises the questions of protection against tetanus and of antibiotics, and both decisions belong to the treating clinician and the local protocol. Positioning the limb, splinting it, keeping the patient warm and explaining what is happening are acts of comfort that every level of responder can perform.

Reduction, traction and casting appear in the treatment of knee injuries, and a listener must understand what they are without ever learning to perform them from these words. Reduction means restoring the position of bones that are out of place, and for a fracture of the tibial plateau it means lifting a joint surface that has been driven down and holding it while the bone heals. Traction means holding a limb with a gentle continuous pull, and it is used mainly for injuries of the shaft of the thigh bone rather than for a plateau fracture, though the principle of supervision applies wherever it is used. Casting means holding a limb in a rigid shell while the bone heals, and it is applied by a clinician or a trained technician under the direction of the orthopedic surgeon. All three are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and the technique belongs to that training and never to a book or a recording. A reduction is not attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when the vessels or nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, when the responder lacks training or equipment, or when an earlier attempt has failed. In each of those situations the attempt can turn an injury that would have healed into one that cannot, and force is not a technique.

The record and the handover are part of the treatment rather than a formality that follows it. The time of the injury, the mechanism, the position in which the knee was found, the condition of the skin and the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention are written or spoken in a fixed order, so that the receiving team continues a story rather than starting a new one. Where the patient is transferred, the receiving facility is told in advance which injuries are suspected and what has been done, because an open fracture or a tense leg changes what must be ready on arrival. An injury of the spine is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has done so, and a patient who fell from a height with a broken knee is exactly the patient in whom that rule applies, so movement is performed by a trained team using the precautions the local protocol requires. Everything described here is learned with a teacher, practiced on models and on each other, and certified by an authority that also defines what may be done without supervision. Where this chapter names a time, a distance or a period, it names an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between services and editions.

To summarize, the tibial plateau is the weight bearing surface of the shin bone at the knee, and it is broken by a blow along the line of the leg that drives the joint surface down while the skin, the ligaments and the compartments of the leg are injured with it. What to look for is a knee that swells quickly, that will not take weight and that cannot be straightened, with tenderness along the joint line, stretched and shiny skin, a foot that cannot be felt or moved as well as the other, and pain far worse than the injury appears to explain. What to do is to assess the whole patient first, to support the limb in the position found, to examine and record the neurovascular status before and after every intervention, to immobilize the knee and the ankle, to keep the patient warm and to transfer the patient to a facility with imaging and an orthopedic surgeon. What never to do is to straighten the limb in the field, to remove an embedded object, to push exposed bone back into a wound, to explore a wound blindly, to test the stability of the knee, or to attempt a reduction, because force is not a technique. A responder who suspects a compartment syndrome acts at once, releases any constricting device within the local protocol, keeps the limb at the level of the heart and refers urgently, because time is muscle. No dose, no route and no regimen appears in this chapter, since the substance and the amount belong to the local protocol and the prescriber, and every figure named here is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.
