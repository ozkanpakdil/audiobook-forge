# Chapter 31 — Tibial Fractures

The tibia, the shin bone that runs between the knee and the ankle, carries the weight of the body, and its sharp front ridge can be felt through the skin of every healthy leg. Along most of its length it lies directly beneath the skin, with almost no muscle over its front and inner surfaces, so a blow that would only bruise a thigh can break it and tear the skin from within. The fibula beside it carries little of the load, but the two bones are joined at both ends, so an injury to one is often an injury to the other. This chapter follows the order used throughout this course, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. What follows is educational material, and it does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

Blood reaches the shaft of the tibia mainly through a vessel that enters the bone near its middle and runs inside the canal, so the inner part of the shaft depends on that supply more than on the muscle around it. The periosteum, the tough sleeve that wraps the bone, adds a smaller supply along the surface, and it is stripped away by the same force that breaks the bone. When a fracture tears that sleeve over a distance, a fragment may lose much of its supply, and the lower third of the tibia is where this matters most. That is one reason a fracture of the shin bone heals more slowly than a fracture of the thigh bone, and why a break that looks modest can take months to unite.

The mechanism tells the responder what to expect. A bumper of a car, a falling object, a kick or a heavy door produces a direct blow, and the bone breaks at the point of impact with skin and muscle bruised above it. A twisting force, as in a fall while skiing, produces a long spiral break that may run up to the knee or down to the ankle, while a fall from a height drives the bone along its length. High energy damages soft tissue far beyond the bone, so the energy involved is a better guide to danger than the size of the wound, because skin may be nearly intact over muscle that has been crushed.

Because the front and inner surfaces of the tibia lie directly under the skin, this is the long bone most often broken open, and the wound may follow a modest blow rather than a violent one. A spike of bone tears through the skin from within, so the wound may be no larger than a buttonhole. A small hole in a trouser leg or a stain on a sock may be the only sign, and the wound can be hidden by the position of the limb or by the swelling. Clothing is cut away rather than pulled over the leg so that the whole limb can be seen, and the patient is covered again at once to protect against cold. The search for a wound belongs to the assessment and is performed by the trained responder or the clinician, while the lay first aider covers what is already visible.

Any fracture with a wound that communicates with the broken bone is an open fracture, however small the wound and whatever it looks like. The wound is contaminated at the moment of injury, because the skin carries organisms that reach the bone. An open fracture needs surgical cleaning, and the decisions about infection and tetanus belong to the treating clinician and the local protocol. Surgeons describe such a wound by its size, by the damage to skin and muscle, and by whether a vessel supplying the foot has been torn, and those words belong to the treating team. What the responder contributes is the time, the appearance of the wound, whether bleeding was controlled, the neurovascular findings before and after immobilization, and the treatment given. A wound near a fracture is covered and reported, and the responder does not judge whether it is deep enough to matter.

Assessment begins before the limb is touched. The responder makes the scene safe, notes the mechanism, and works through the primary survey whose steps are remembered as ABCDE, meaning airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure. A patient with a broken shin may also have a broken pelvis, a broken thigh or an injury to the head, and those injuries can kill within minutes while the leg can wait. The rule that decides the order is that life comes before limb, so a falling level of consciousness, a changed pattern of breathing, a weak pulse or skin that has become cold and pale sends the responder back to the beginning. The primary survey and the decisions it forces belong to the trained responder, the paramedic, the nurse or the physician, each within the scope that the local service authorizes.

The history adds what the examination cannot show. The responder asks when the injury happened, how much energy was involved, and whether the leg gave way before the fall rather than because of it. Medicines and illnesses that weaken bone, smoking, diabetes, poor circulation in the legs and previous surgery on the same limb all change what the fracture means for this patient. An older adult may break the shin in a fall from standing height, and a person whose bone is fragile may break it with no fall at all. In a child the story matters as much as the injury, because a story that does not fit the findings is a warning to be passed on and recorded.

Looking at the limb comes next, with the patient covered except for the leg. The leg may lie at an angle, it may be shortened, or the foot may point inward or outward when the knee is straight, and any of those findings means the bone ends are no longer in line. The injured leg is compared with the other one, which is the only useful standard available in the field. Swelling may be modest at first and grow while the responder watches, and blisters may form over skin stretched by fluid beneath it. A wound, a stain of blood, a patch of pale or dusky skin and a foot that is not the same color as its partner are all recorded. Nothing is straightened, nothing is pulled, and nothing is measured by force so that the shape can be written down.

Feeling the limb is done with the flat of the hand and with care, because the examination itself can do harm. The responder feels the temperature of the skin, the swelling and the tension in the calf, and compares each finding with the other leg. The sharp front ridge of the tibia is tender wherever it is broken, but the bone is not moved in order to prove that it moves. Tenderness at the knee and at the ankle is looked for, because a twisting force that breaks the shaft may also injure a joint. The compartments of the calf are felt for a hardness out of proportion to the injury, and a calf that is tense is an emergency. Formal palpation belongs to the clinician, while the responder supports the leg, covers the wound and feels without moving anything.

The neurovascular examination of the foot is performed before any intervention and repeated after every one, and it is recorded in words. The examiner feels for the pulse behind the inner ankle and for the pulse on the top of the foot, and compares both with the other side. The color and the warmth of the foot are noted, and a nail is pressed briefly to see how quickly the color returns. Sensation is tested in the web between the first and second toes, which belongs to the nerve that lifts the foot, and on the sole. The patient is asked to pull the foot up, to push it down and to spread the toes, and any movement that has become weak or lost is recorded as a change. The lay first aider observes color, warmth and the ability of the patient to move and feel, while the formal examination belongs to the trained responder or the clinician.

A pulse can still be felt in a leg that is developing a compartment syndrome, so a present pulse does not replace the search for pain and tension. The pulse on the top of the foot cannot be felt in a small number of healthy people, so it is compared with the other foot and read with color, warmth and sensation. Pain, swelling and distress can make movement and sensation hard to test, and the responder says so in the record instead of writing that sensation was normal. An absent pulse, a foot that is pale and cold, or a loss of movement that was present a moment earlier is an emergency that is reported immediately. The examination is repeated after the splint, after the dressing and after any transfer, because a finding not recorded before an intervention cannot be attributed afterwards.

Stabilization of the limb is a small set of acts, and the most important is restraint. The responder keeps the leg in the position in which it was found, supports it with the hands or with padding, and does not test it. Bleeding that can be controlled is controlled by firm pressure around the wound and by a sterile dressing laid over it, and the dressing is not lifted to look underneath. An embedded object is left in place and stabilized, and exposed bone is never pushed back into the wound, because the bone may be holding pressure on a torn vessel. The wound is never explored blindly, and definitive closure belongs to the operating room and to nobody else. The lay first aider performs the acts that need no equipment, the trained responder and the paramedic work within the equipment their service authorizes, and the physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides what comes next.

Keeping the patient warm is part of stabilization rather than a comfort that follows it. The injured leg is insulated from the ground, wet clothing is replaced with dry covering, and the patient is shielded from wind and rain while waiting for help. Cold worsens bleeding and shock and makes the patient more difficult to assess, and a patient who is already losing blood tolerates cold badly. The covering is checked again each time the patient is moved, and the responder who has only a coat or a blanket uses it. Everything else that might be done for the limb waits until the patient is warm and the life threats are treated.

A deformed leg is not straightened in the field, and this rule has a reason the listener can picture. The vessels and the nerves of the lower leg lie against the bone, and the vessel behind the knee and the nerve at the neck of the fibula are close to the surfaces a broken tibia may injure. Pulling a limb straight may tear what remained intact, and it may also convert a closed fracture into an open one by driving the bone through the skin. It may release the pressure that a collection of blood was placing on a torn vessel, and bleeding that had stopped then begins again. The limb is therefore splinted in the position found, however unnatural it looks, and the correction of the deformity is left to those who can see the vessels and the nerves. Force is not a technique, and resistance is a signal to stop.

Pain is treated as a finding and not as a request. The responder asks about it in the patient's own words, notes where it is and whether it is growing, and reports pain out of proportion to the injury, because that is the earliest warning of a compartment syndrome. Relief of pain is decided by a clinician, or by a paramedic acting under a written protocol, and the substance, the amount, the route and the timing belong to the prescriber and to local protocol. A listener must not be able to take a prescription from these words, and no dose and no regimen is given here. What the responder may do is reduce the pain of movement by immobilizing and supporting the limb and keeping the patient warm. The decision to give nothing, or to wait for a senior clinician, is itself a clinical decision and belongs to the person accountable for it.

Immobilization means preventing movement of the injured part and of the joints above and below it, because a bone splinted alone can still be moved by the knee and by the ankle. A rigid splint is padded where it presses on bone, which for this injury means the heel, the ankle, the knee and the head of the fibula, and it holds the leg from the thigh to below the foot. The splint is applied over the position found and secured firmly enough to hold without becoming a band that stops the circulation, and the toes are checked after it is secured. The trained responder and the paramedic apply splints within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, and a device that applies traction is used only where the service trains and authorizes it. The physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides what definitive immobilization is required, which may be a cast, a brace or a surgical fixation, and each is learned under supervision.

A splint applied too tightly becomes a complication of the treatment, and the leg is where this happens most easily. As the limb swells, a bandage that was comfortable at the roadside can begin to act as a tourniquet, and pressure rises inside a compartment that cannot expand. The responder therefore examines the toes, the color of the foot and the tightness of the straps at the interval the local protocol sets, and compares them with the record made after the splint was applied. A constricting bandage or splint is released only by someone the local protocol authorizes to do it, because releasing it in the wrong way can also cause harm. The patient is not left alone with a fresh splint, and a leg that becomes more painful, more tense or more numb inside its splint is an emergency.

Compartment syndrome of the leg is the complication this chapter fears most, and the responder who suspects it does not wait for confirmation. The lower leg is divided into four compartments by bone and by sheets of fascia, and the front one is affected most often. The earliest finding is pain and tension, and the pain is out of proportion to the injury and grows worse rather than better, while a limb that hurts when the toes are stretched is the classic warning. Numbness between the first and second toes, a weak lift of the foot and a calf that feels hard like wood come later, and the pulses may still be present. The diagnosis is clinical and is made by a clinician, and time is muscle, so a responder who suspects it acts within the local protocol and arranges urgent transfer. The limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, and the patient is kept warm while the transfer is arranged.

The record is part of the treatment and not a formality that follows it. The time of the injury, the mechanism, the position in which the leg was found, the wound, the treatment given and the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention are written or spoken in a fixed order. Pain that is increasing, a change in color or warmth, a loss of movement or sensation and a rise in the pulse are recorded in the same way. The condition of the other leg is the standard against which every later observation is compared. A finding that was not written before the splint was applied cannot be attributed afterwards, and the note travels with the patient.

Imaging confirms what the examination has already suggested, and it is ordered and interpreted by a clinician trained to do it. A radiograph is usually the first study, and it is taken in at least two directions so that a displacement in the third direction is not hidden, with the whole bone and both joints included. The knee and the ankle are examined even when the pain is in the middle of the leg, because the force that broke the shaft may also have broken one of them. Computed tomography shows the detail of a break that reaches a joint or is broken into several pieces, and magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for soft tissues and for a suspected stress injury. The lay first aider never orders or interprets imaging, the trained responder does not choose the study, and the surgeon decides when the images are complete. An image is a picture of one moment, and the patient must still be examined afterwards.

An overuse injury, common in runners, dancers and soldiers who train in new boots, begins as a small crack that hurts with activity and eases with rest, and it may not appear on an early radiograph. If training continues, the crack can grow across the bone and the leg can break completely, and any figure quoted for the time away from training is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and local protocol. The responder advises the patient to stop the activity and arranges assessment, and does not ask the patient to run on it. The decision about imaging and about the return to training belongs to a clinician, and a stress injury that is ignored is a fracture that could have been prevented.

Reassessment happens after every intervention, and it is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the record, because the comparison has value and no single measurement does. Color, warmth, pulse, sensation, movement, pain and the tightness of the splint are checked, and the wound is checked for bleeding that has soaked through. Reassessment follows the dressing, the splint, every move of the patient and every period of waiting, and the result is recorded each time. Pain that increases instead of settling, pain out of proportion to the injury, numbness, tingling, weakness, a tense swollen calf and a foot that has become pale or cold are the findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation.

Referral or definitive treatment is the last step of the sequence, and for the tibia it is rarely a step the responder completes alone. Almost every fracture of the tibial shaft is assessed by an orthopedic surgeon, and a fracture that is open, deformed, pulseless or numb is referred at once. The same is true of a fracture that enters the knee or the ankle, a fracture in a child, a fracture in a leg with poor circulation or with nerve damage from diabetes, and a fracture that cannot bear weight. The receiving facility needs imaging, an operating room and an intensive care unit, and the handover names the time, the wound and the neurovascular findings. The lay first aider calls for help and stays with the patient, the trained responder arranges transport within the local protocol, and the nurse and the physician prepare to receive the patient. Nothing here authorizes anyone to keep such a patient at home.

Definitive treatment is described here so that the listener understands what is being done, and not so that the listener can do it. Fractures of the shaft that are not displaced are held in a cast or in a functional brace while the bone heals, and those devices are applied and monitored by clinicians trained in that work. Many displaced fractures are held with a metal rod passed down the canal of the bone or with a plate and screws, and a severe open injury may be held with a frame outside the limb. Reduction restores the position of the bone ends, traction is the gentle continuous pull that holds them in line, and casting or surgical fixation holds the position while the bone unites, and each is learned under supervision. A reduction is not attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when the vessels or nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, when the responder lacks training or equipment, or when an earlier attempt has failed. In each of those situations a well meant attempt can turn an injury that would have healed into one that cannot.

Healing of the tibia is slower than healing of most other long bones, and the patient should hear that early. The lower third of the bone has the poorest supply and the least muscle cover, and a fracture there may take months to unite, while every figure quoted for the time to union and for weight bearing is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. Weight bearing is allowed only when the surgeon responsible says so, because a leg loaded too early can lose the position won in the operating room. The patient is taught to move the toes and the ankle as far as the splint allows and to report new pain, numbness or a change in the color of the foot. Smoking, poor nutrition and poorly controlled diabetes slow healing, and those are matters for the treating team. The listener's part is to keep the appointments and to bring the patient back when something changes.

A fracture that does not unite, a fracture that unites at an angle so that the leg is not straight, an infection that settles into the bone after an open injury, and stiffness of the knee and the ankle are the outcomes that follow the most severe injuries. Pressure from a splint or a cast can damage skin over the shin, the heel or the ankle, and a patient with nerve damage may not feel it happening. A leg that becomes hot, swollen and more painful days after the injury, a wound that begins to discharge and a fever send the patient back to the clinician rather than to the pharmacy. The responder does not clean, probe or repack a wound in the field and does not adjust a cast.

A child's bone bends before it breaks, and the same fall may produce a crack that is hard to see or an injury to the growing region near the knee, so a child who will not use the leg is treated with the caution owed to an obvious break. An older adult with fragile bone may break the shin in a fall from standing height, and the injury may hurt less than its severity suggests. A patient with diabetes and damaged nerves may not feel the pain of a splint that is too tight, and a patient with poor circulation may lose the foot as well as the fracture. A patient who cannot describe pain because of a head injury, sedation or a language barrier is examined more often and not less. None of these patients receives a different sequence, and all of them receive a lower threshold for immobilization and referral.

The handover is the moment when the story must survive a change of hands. The time of the injury, the mechanism, the position in which the leg was found, the wound, the neurovascular findings before and after each intervention and the treatment given are passed on in a fixed order. Gloves and eye protection are used whenever blood or body fluid may be present, and the level of protection is set by the local protocol, because an open tibial fracture exposes the responder as well as endangering the patient. The receiving nurse repeats the neurovascular examination on arrival and at the interval the local protocol requires, and reports any change at once. A leg that has been splinted is never left unobserved while the patient waits for an operating room. Everyone who touches the patient works within the scope of practice the local service and the law define.

To summarize, the tibia lies just under the skin, so even a modest blow can break it and open the skin, and a fracture with any wound near it is treated as an open fracture and referred urgently. The order never changes: assess the patient and the limb, stabilize by covering the wound, controlling bleeding and keeping the patient warm, examine the neurovascular status of the foot and record it, immobilize the leg in the position found, image when a clinician orders it, reassess after every intervention, and then refer or treat definitively. Look for the wound that may be small, the deformity that is never straightened in the field, the swelling that grows, the pain out of proportion, the tense calf and the foot that is pale or cold. Reduction, traction and casting belong to supervised clinical training and to local protocol, and a reduction is not attempted when the diagnosis is unclear, the injury is open, the vessels or nerves are failing, a fragment is caught in the joint, or the responder lacks training and equipment. Keep the patient warm, record every finding before and after every intervention, and call for a team that can reach an operating room. This chapter is educational material and it does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.
