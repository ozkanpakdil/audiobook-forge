# Chapter 6 — Neurovascular Assessment

The neurovascular examination is the examination this whole course turns on, because it decides whether a limb survives the journey to definitive care. Is blood still arriving at the hand or the foot, and is the nerve still carrying its messages back? A patient can lie still with a calm face and a steady pulse while the tissues beyond the fracture are being starved, and the only warning is this examination. It needs no specialist equipment and takes less than a minute on each limb once the order is learned. It is performed before any intervention and repeated after every intervention, its result is written down, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

A nerve does not run down the middle of a limb where nothing can reach it. It travels along the surface of the bone, held there by muscle and fascia, and the arteries do the same at the elbow, behind the knee and around the ankle. A bone that breaks and shifts moves into the space where the vessel and the nerve were lying. The soft tissues offer no guarantee either, because the energy that broke the bone passed through them first. A limb can lose its nerve supply, its blood supply, or both, while the skin above it stays unbroken and the wound everyone is searching for never appears.

The sequence this course follows places the neurovascular examination third, after assessment and stabilization and before immobilization, imaging, reassessment and definitive referral or treatment. Assessment decides whether the patient is stable enough for a limb to be examined at all, and stabilization buys the minutes in which the examination means something. The examination then sets the baseline against which every later intervention is measured, because splinting, imaging and treatment all change the limb. Nobody can say what a splint did unless somebody recorded what was there before the splint. A listener who can recite the sequence will not skip the step that a hurry makes tempting.

Assessment begins away from the limb, with the scene, the mechanism of injury, the age of the patient and the time since the injury. It continues with the primary survey, whose steps are remembered as ABCDE, meaning airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure, because a patient who cannot breathe has no use for a perfect examination of the wrist. Life comes before limb, so a responder with one pair of hands treats the threatened airway first and returns to the limb a moment later. Only when the patient is stable enough does the limb assessment begin, and the limb is examined by looking, by feeling gently and by asking the patient to move it, never by forcing it. A falling level of consciousness, a changed pattern of breathing or cold clammy skin sends the responder back to the beginning of the survey.

Stabilization is a small set of acts, and restraint is the most important of them. Bleeding that can be controlled is controlled with pressure over a dressing, the limb is supported in the position in which it was found, and the patient is kept warm. A deformed limb is never straightened in the field, because the vessels and the nerves lie against the bone and a movement that looks helpful can tear what remained intact. The lay first aider supports the limb with the hands or with whatever is at hand, keeps the patient warm and reports any change in the color or the warmth of the hand or the foot. The trained responder and the paramedic splint within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, and both record the neurovascular findings before the splint goes on. Cold is a complication of trauma, so wet clothing is replaced and the patient is insulated from the ground.

The examination itself is a short list of observations made in the same order every time: pulse, color, warmth, capillary refill, sensation and movement. Pain belongs in the same list, as the warning sense that usually speaks first. Both limbs are examined, the injured one and the uninjured one, and the injured side is compared with the other rather than with a remembered idea of normal. Findings are described as present, absent, reduced or increased, and never left as the single word normal. The order moves from what the examiner can see and feel to what the patient must report, and it makes a change visible at a glance in the record. The whole examination is repeated after every intervention that follows.

The pulse is felt beyond the injury, on the side of the fracture farther from the heart, because a pulse there is evidence that blood is still arriving. In the arm the examiner feels at the wrist on the thumb side, and in the leg at the top of the foot and behind the inner ankle, comparing the two sides whenever possible. A pulse is described as present, diminished or absent, and an absent pulse beyond a fracture is an emergency rather than a note to be filed. A pulse that is present does not exclude a compartment syndrome, an injury to an artery or a nerve that has stopped working, because pressure inside a limb can destroy muscle while a large vessel still carries blood. The trained responder and the clinician feel for pulses as part of the formal examination, while the lay first aider observes color and warmth and reports what has changed.

Color is the first thing the eye notices and the easiest finding to describe. A limb that is pale, waxy or white has lost its blood supply, while a limb that is dusky or mottled is showing that blood arriving is not draining or is short of oxygen. Bruising and swelling change the color of the skin without any injury to the vessels that matter, so the examiner looks at the whole limb and at the other side before drawing a conclusion. In a patient with deeply pigmented skin, pallor and bluish discoloration are judged best at the nail beds, the palms, the soles and the lips. A change in color that appears after a splint has been applied is a reason to look under the splint and reassess.

Warmth is felt with the back of the fingers on both limbs at the same level. The examiner moves a hand from the shoulder toward the fingers or from the hip toward the toes and notices where the limb becomes cooler, because a line of coldness that falls at the fracture differs from a foot that has been cold since the patient left a warm room. A cool limb beyond an injury suggests a failing blood supply, while cold limbs on both sides with cold skin on the trunk suggest that the patient rather than the limb is in difficulty. The limb is kept warm throughout, and the examiner takes care that the examination does not chill the patient. The comparison with the other limb and with the trunk is recorded beside the pulse and the color, and one finding without the other is a puzzle rather than reassurance.

Capillary refill is tested by pressing on a nail bed or on the pad of a finger or a toe until the skin blanches, then releasing the pressure and watching the color return. A delay of more than a few seconds is the finding that is taught, and the number of seconds is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between services, countries and editions. The test is unreliable in a cold environment, in a patient whose circulation has already shut down and in a darkly pigmented nail bed. It is therefore read with the color, the warmth and the comparison with the other limb, and the trend across repeated examinations is worth more than any single measurement. A capillary refill that becomes slower while the limb lies splinted is a signal to look at the splint before looking for another explanation.

Sensation is tested by asking the patient what the limb feels like, and then by touching the skin lightly in the same place on both sides and asking whether the two sides feel the same. A light stroke with a fingertip is enough for the field, while a pinprick test or the testing of one nerve territory belongs to the trained responder or to the clinician and only where the local protocol allows it. The patient is asked for the words that matter, which are numbness, tingling, pins and needles, a limb that has gone to sleep and a patch that feels dead. It is not enough to touch one spot, because a nerve supplies a territory, so the examiner tests the part of the limb that the nerve in question supplies and compares it with the matching part on the other side. Sensation is unreliable in a patient who has taken alcohol or another sedating substance, in a patient with a head injury and in a frightened child, so those patients need a lower threshold for referral.

Movement is tested by asking the patient to move the part rather than by moving it for them. In the arm the patient is asked to spread and bend the fingers and to lift the wrist up and down, and in the leg to spread the toes and to pull the foot up and push it down. Each movement is compared with the other side, and what the patient could do a few minutes earlier matters as much as what the patient can do now. A movement that has been lost, or that has become weak, stops the examination and sends the responder to the local protocol for a threatened limb. Forcing a joint through a movement the patient cannot perform is never part of this examination, because force may turn a partial injury into a complete one. The lay first aider asks the patient to wiggle the fingers or the toes and reports whether the patient still can.

Pain is the finding that most often arrives before any of the others, and it deserves a place in the neurovascular examination. Pain that is out of proportion to the injury, pain that increases rather than settles, pain made worse when a muscle is stretched gently, and pain that is not relieved as expected are all warnings. The examiner asks the patient to describe the pain and to say whether it is changing, and records the answer with the time. Pain may also be absent or blunted in a patient who has already received a medicine for pain, so its absence proves nothing on its own. Relief of pain is decided by a clinician or by a prescriber working to the local protocol, and this course gives no drug, no dose, no route and no frequency, because the substance and the amount belong to the prescriber. The responder who is not authorized to give such a medicine concentrates on splinting in the position found, on warmth and on the speed of the transfer.

The neurovascular examination is performed before any intervention, and the reason is attribution. If the hand is numb after a splint is applied, nobody can say whether the splint caused the numbness or the fracture did, unless somebody wrote down that the hand was numb before the splint went on. The examination is therefore done before the splint, before the dressing, before the limb is moved and before the patient is lifted, and again after each of those acts. An examination performed once, at the end, after everything has been done, is not a baseline and cannot be used to judge anything. The sequence is repeated at every transfer, because a new team must know what the limb was doing an hour ago.

The record of the examination belongs on the patient and not only in the notes, because the notes travel with a chart while the patient travels on a stretcher. A small card fixed to the splint or a note placed where the receiving team will see it carries the information to whoever unwraps the limb next, and it survives the loss of a folder and the change of a shift. Where local protocol allows writing on the skin, the examiner writes the time and a few plain words rather than a symbol that only one service understands. The card carries the time of the injury, the position in which the limb was found, the findings of each examination and the time of each intervention, so that the next person reads a story rather than a single sentence. Writing the examination is the work of the trained responder, the nurse, the paramedic or the clinician, and the lay first aider reports in the same plain words. A finding that was not written down was not made.

Immobilization means preventing movement of the injured part and of the joints above and below it, and it is done in the position in which the limb was found. A splint is padded where it presses on bone, it is applied over the position found, and it is secured firmly enough to hold without becoming a band that stops the circulation. An embedded object is never removed, exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, and an open wound is never explored blindly, because the object may be holding pressure on a torn vessel, and definitive closure of the wound belongs to the operating room. The trained responder and the paramedic apply splints within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, and the physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides what definitive immobilization is required, which may be a cast, a brace or a surgical fixation. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and no chapter of this course teaches their technique from a book. The neurovascular examination is repeated as soon as the splint is in place and compared with the record made a few minutes earlier.

A splint can cause the very catastrophe it was applied to prevent, and reassessment is the only thing that catches this in time. Swelling continues after an injury, so a splint that was comfortable an hour ago can become a tight band while the patient waits, and the limb beyond it turns pale, cool, numb and painful. The fingers or the toes are left visible and uncovered wherever the design of the splint allows it, so that the next person can look at them without undoing anything. A bandage is checked for tightness, a sling for pressure on the neck, and the padding for the place where it has shifted and left bone uncovered. If the limb changes, the responder stops, reassesses and follows the local protocol for what may be loosened and by whom, because force is not a technique and neither is waiting.

Imaging comes after the examination and the immobilization, and it is ordered by a clinician trained to interpret it. A radiograph is usually the first study, and it is taken in at least two directions so that a displacement in the third direction is not hidden, with the joint above and the joint below included so that a second injury is not missed. Computed tomography shows the detail of a complex break, particularly around a joint, and magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for soft tissues, including ligaments, cartilage and tendons. The neurovascular status is recorded again before the patient leaves for the imaging department, because a limb can become worse in a corridor. An image is a picture of one moment and does not examine anyone, so a normal radiograph never cancels a limb that is pale, cool or numb. The choice of study belongs to the clinician responsible for the care of the patient, and the lay first aider never orders or interprets imaging.

Reassessment is performed after every intervention and at intervals while the patient waits, because the complication this course fears most is the one caused by the treatment. The same observations are repeated in the same order, that is pulse, color, warmth, capillary refill, sensation, movement and pain, and each one is compared with the record rather than judged on its own. The comparison is the examination, since a pulse that has always been weak is a different finding from a pulse that has just become weak. A new numbness, a new pallor, a limb that has turned cold, a movement that has been lost, a pulse that cannot be found and pain that is worse than it was are the findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation. The splint or the dressing is then examined, the other limb is examined again for comparison, and the patient is kept warm while help is arranged.

Compartment syndrome is the diagnosis this whole examination is arranged around, and it is a clinical diagnosis that nobody makes from a single measurement. Bleeding or swelling inside a closed space raises the pressure there, the smallest vessels stop carrying blood, and muscle and nerve begin to die while the large pulse at the wrist or the ankle can still be felt. The limb becomes tense, swollen and painful out of proportion to the injury, pain is produced when the examiner gently stretches the muscles of the compartment, and the patient may describe numbness in the territory of a nerve that passes through it. A pulse that is present does not exclude the diagnosis, and waiting for the pulse to disappear is waiting for damage that may already be permanent. A responder who suspects compartment syndrome does not elevate the limb above the level of the heart, but keeps the patient warm and refers urgently, because time is muscle. A constricting bandage, splint or cast is released only by someone the local protocol authorizes to do it, and the release is followed by another examination.

What a change means depends on when it was first noticed, and that is why the times are recorded. A pulse lost while the limb lies in a splint is first treated as a splint problem, and the responder looks at the bandage before concluding that the injury has worsened. A limb that was warm and pink and is now pale and cool has lost its arterial supply until proven otherwise, and an injury to an artery is a surgical emergency that needs a facility with an operating room. Bleeding that spurts or will not stop under pressure, a swelling that grows while it is watched, a pulse that cannot be felt and a nerve that has stopped working are hard signs that make the transfer urgent rather than soon. A pulse can also be present at the first examination and gone at the second, because a vessel bruised inside its wall may close over the following hours, which is why the examination is repeated and not merely performed once. None of these findings improves by waiting or by an attempt to restore a position by force.

This illustration is invented rather than taken from any real patient. A man in his thirties falls from a bicycle and holds out an arm with a bend in the middle of the forearm that was not there before; the trained responder supports the limb in the position found, records that the fingers are warm and pink, the pulse at the wrist is present, sensation is intact and the fingers can be moved, and then applies a padded splint. Less than an hour later, in the back of the vehicle, the fingers are pale and cool, the patient says the hand is tingling, and the pulse that was easy to feel is now difficult to find. The responder does not straighten the arm and does not wait for the hospital to notice; the splint is checked for tightness within the local protocol, the findings and the time are written down, and the receiving team is warned before arrival. The examination turns a change that would otherwise be discovered too late into a message that arrives early.

Some patients cannot give a reliable account of their own limb, and the examination has to be adjusted rather than abandoned. A young child who is frightened and in pain may cry without answering and may withdraw the limb, so the examiner compares the two limbs, watches how the child uses the arm and keeps a low threshold for splinting and for referral. An older adult may have thin skin, less padding over the bone, poor circulation and a nerve that has been losing function for years, and a fracture that would be painful in a younger patient may be almost quiet. A patient whose feet have been numb for years from diabetes has no reliable sensation left to lose, so the pulse, the color, the warmth and the comparison with the other foot carry more weight. In all of these patients the trend across repeated examinations is worth more than any single finding, and referring earlier than the rule suggests is never wrong.

Referral does not begin when everything else has failed, and in this subject it begins at the moment a threat to the limb is suspected. The findings that demand an urgent destination are a limb that has lost its pulse, a limb that has become pale and cold, a new numbness or a lost movement, pain that is out of proportion to the injury or increasing under a splint, an open fracture, a suspected compartment syndrome, a dislocation, and any injury in which the vessels or the nerves are known to be involved. The receiving facility needs imaging, an operating room and an intensive care unit, and the clinician who accepts the patient is told which of these findings is present rather than only that the patient is coming. The emergency medical service is contacted, the transfer is arranged through the local system, and the time at which the limb was last known to be well is stated plainly. A responder who is unsure whether a finding is serious refers and describes it rather than deciding alone, because a transfer that turns out to be unnecessary costs far less than a limb that might have been saved. Whether the call is made by a nurse or by a physician is decided by the local protocol.

Definitive treatment is the last step of the sequence, and it begins with the recognition that some injuries cannot be managed where the patient lies. Reduction restores the position of the bone ends, traction holds them in line with a gentle continuous pull, and casting or surgical fixation holds the position while the bone heals, and every one of those acts is learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol. A reduction is not attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught within the joint, when the vessels or the nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, when the responder lacks the training or the equipment, or when an earlier attempt has failed. In each of those situations an attempt can turn an injury that would have healed into one that cannot, because force applied to a limb whose vessels are already compromised removes the last of its supply. The technique itself belongs to supervised clinical training and never to a book.

The provider level is named for every act in this chapter, and the law of the country in which the listener works decides the rest. The lay first aider supports the limb in the position found, keeps the patient warm, observes the color, the warmth, the movement and the feeling of the hand or the foot, and reports any change. The trained first responder and the emergency medical technician or paramedic perform and record the examination within the procedures their service authorizes, and apply splints only within their equipment and their protocol. The nurse repeats the examination and escalates to the physician without delay. The physician examines, orders imaging and decides on analgesia and on the urgency of transfer, while the orthopedic surgeon and, where a vessel is involved, the vascular surgeon provide the definitive care. Authorization, equipment and protocol decide what each of these people may do.

To summarize, the neurovascular examination is done before any intervention and repeated after every intervention, and it looks at pulse, color, warmth, capillary refill, sensation, movement and pain on the injured limb and on the other one, in the same order every time. A pulse that is present does not exclude a compartment syndrome, and a finding that was not recorded before a splint was applied cannot be attributed afterwards, so the findings are written on the patient and not only in the notes, with the time beside them. The changes that stop a responder are a lost pulse, a limb that turns pale and cold, a new numbness, a movement that has gone and pain that increases instead of settling. A deformed limb is splinted in the position found and is never straightened, an embedded object is never removed, exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, an open wound is never explored blindly, and a constricting bandage, splint or cast is released only by someone the local protocol authorizes. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, no drug, dose or route is given in this course, and a limb with a suspected compartment syndrome is not elevated above the level of the heart. The provider level is named for every act, the patient is kept warm, and when the limb is threatened the clinician and the receiving facility are told at once, because time is muscle.
