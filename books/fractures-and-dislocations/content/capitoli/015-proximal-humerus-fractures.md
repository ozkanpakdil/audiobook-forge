# Chapter 15 — Proximal Humerus Fractures

The upper arm bone is called the humerus, and the part of it nearest the shoulder is the proximal humerus, where a break is one of the commonest injuries of the shoulder. The shoulder is built for movement rather than for stability, since a shallow socket on the shoulder blade holds a rounded head in place with muscles and ligaments rather than with the shape of the bones. A break at the upper end of the arm bone therefore damages a region through which every movement of the arm passes, and it happens most often to an older person whose bone has lost density and who has fallen at home, and less often to a younger person who has met a far greater force. This chapter follows the order used in every part of this course, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. It is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

Four parts of this small region decide what happens after the break. The head is the rounded part that sits in the shallow socket of the shoulder blade, and it is covered by smooth cartilage that lets the joint glide. The neck just below the head, which surgeons call the surgical neck, is where the bone most often gives way. Two rounded bumps at the top are called the tuberosities, and the tendons of the muscles that rotate the shoulder are attached to them, so a fragment can be pulled out of position by a muscle. A child has a growth plate across the upper end of this bone, from which the arm lengthens, so an injury there is judged differently in a child. The bone here is a thin shell around a lattice rather than a thick tube, which is why it yields to a force that would leave the middle of the bone unbroken.

The patient this happens to is usually not a young athlete, and the reason lies in the bone rather than in the accident. An older adult who falls from standing height at home, often a woman whose bone has thinned with age, can break this part of the arm with a force that would not harm a younger person. A patient whose bone has been weakened by disease, or by long use of a medicine that thins it, may break the bone while turning in bed. In these patients the pain can be far milder than the break suggests, and the patient may sit and talk while holding the arm perfectly still. The other patient is younger and has met a much larger force, such as a fall from a height, a collision at speed, a seizure, or an electric shock, and must be searched for injuries elsewhere. Both need the same sequence, so what differs between them is how low the threshold for referral is set.

The mechanism is worth asking about in detail, because it predicts what else may be broken. The commonest story is a fall onto the outstretched hand, in which the force travels up the arm and drives the head of the bone against the rim of the socket. A direct blow to the outer side of the shoulder is the second common pattern, and it is the one that most often splits the head into several pieces. A sudden violent contraction of the muscles around the shoulder, as in a seizure or an electric shock, can pull the tuberosities away from the rest of the bone. The responder records what happened, when, how far the patient fell, and onto what surface, because those details are used later to judge whether the injury fits the story told. An injury that does not match the described force is reported according to the law and the local protocol of the country.

The way the patient holds the arm is the first thing the responder sees, and it is often more revealing than the swelling. The arm is usually held close against the side of the body, with the elbow bent and the forearm supported by the other hand, and any attempt to lift the arm or turn it outward is resisted. The patient guards the shoulder and turns the whole body rather than the arm, because it is the movement of the shoulder that hurts. That picture is close to the opposite of the one seen in a dislocation, where the arm is held away from the body and the outline of the shoulder looks squared off. Only a clinician can be certain which injury is present, so the responder records the finding rather than naming the injury, in the words the patient used.

Swelling and bruising are late arrivals in this injury, and a shoulder that looks almost normal in the first hour can be marked within a few days. The blood that escapes from the broken bone runs down the arm under the force of gravity, so a bruise may appear at the elbow, along the forearm, or on the chest wall rather than over the shoulder itself. The swelling can increase for a day or two, and tightness of the skin over the shoulder suggests that bleeding within the tissues is continuing. A constructed illustration may help, and it describes no real patient: an older person falls in a garden, keeps the arm still because it hurts, and two days later has a forearm stained with a broad bruise. Because the bruise arrives late, its absence at the first examination never excludes the break.

Assessment begins at the scene, with protection of the rescuer and of the patient, and it continues with the primary survey whose steps are remembered as ABCDE, meaning airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure. The rule that governs the whole survey is that life comes before limb, because a limb can wait for minutes that a brain and a heart cannot. A patient who fell from a height, or who was thrown from a vehicle, is treated as having an unstable spine until someone qualified to clear it has done so, and no lone manual handling technique is safe, so any move is made by a trained team. Personal protective equipment is worn before contact with blood or body fluid, and providing it belongs to the responding service. Exposure means uncovering enough of the patient to see the whole injured region while covering everything else, because cold is a complication of trauma and not a detail of comfort. The survey is repeated whenever the patient changes.

Taking the history is part of the assessment and not a formality that precedes it. The responder asks what happened rather than what hurts, and records the time of the injury, the height of any fall, the surface landed upon, and whether the arm could be used immediately afterwards. Medicines that thin the blood, or that have weakened the bone over years, are recorded because they change the threshold for imaging and for referral. Whether the patient is right-handed or left-handed matters to the plan, since the injury may affect the hand the patient writes and eats with. Whether the patient lives alone also matters, because a shoulder that must be kept still is a shoulder that cannot dress, wash, or cook. The findings of that history travel with the patient to the next provider.

The examination of the shoulder itself begins only when the patient is stable enough to allow it, and it begins with looking. Both shoulders are uncovered so that the two sides can be compared, and the examiner looks for swelling, bruising, a wound, a change in the outline of the shoulder, and the position in which the arm is held. The skin is inspected for a graze, a puncture, or a tenting of the skin over a fragment pressing outward, because any of these makes the injury an open one until a clinician decides otherwise. A wound near the shoulder is covered with a sterile dressing and is never explored blindly with a finger or an instrument, and exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound. Pressing the skin to see how quickly the color returns is an act any trained responder can perform, while the decision about what a wound means belongs to the clinician.

Palpation follows inspection, and it begins away from the place of greatest pain and moves toward it. The examiner feels the collar bone, the joint at its outer end, the bony point of the shoulder, the ridge across the back of the shoulder blade, and the shaft of the arm bone below the injury, because one force can break more than one of them. Tenderness over the upper part of the arm bone, a step in the bone, or a crackling feeling under the fingers suggests a break, and each finding is described in words rather than given a name. The pulse in the arm is felt during the same pass, and the examination never includes pressing hard on the injured part, pulling on the arm, or forcing the joint through its range. A deformed limb is never straightened in the field, because the vessels and the nerves lie against the bone and can be torn by a movement that looks helpful.

Movement is tested by asking the patient and never by forcing the limb. The patient is asked to lift the arm forward as far as it will go, to move it out to the side, to bend the elbow, and to turn the palm up and down, and to say where the pain begins rather than to push through it. In this injury the shoulder usually moves very little, and that absence of movement cannot be used to tell a break from a dislocation or from a torn tendon. Testing the stability of a joint by pulling on it is a clinical skill learned under supervision and is not a field test. A sign that is taught as reliable is often absent in a frightened, cold, or confused patient, so every movement the patient can still perform is recorded.

The neurovascular examination is performed before any intervention and repeated afterwards, and its result is written down, because a finding that was not recorded before a sling was applied cannot be attributed afterwards. An injury present from the beginning can be blamed on the person who applied the support, and a change not compared with an earlier note is a change nobody can prove. The examination covers the nerve supply, the blood supply, and the skin of the whole arm from the shoulder to the fingers, and it is done on both sides so that the uninjured arm serves as the standard. It belongs to the trained responder, the paramedic, the nurse, or the clinician, while the lay first aider reports only what can be seen without touching the injured part. The record notes the time, the side, the pulse, the color, the warmth, the sensation in each area, and the strength of each movement, and it is read aloud at every handover.

The nerve most often injured with this fracture runs around the neck of the bone, lying directly against it, and it supplies the skin over the outer part of the shoulder and the muscle that lifts the arm sideways. Sensation is tested over that outer patch of skin on both sides and compared, and the patient is asked whether the touch feels the same on each side. The responder then asks the patient to lift the arm out to the side against a gentle resistance, but a shoulder that cannot move because the bone is broken gives no information about the nerve, so weakness in a limb held still is an unreliable sign here. Numbness, tingling, or an area of skin that feels deadened is recorded in the words of the patient as far as possible. The nerves that travel on to the hand pass close by as well, so the grip and the spread of the fingers are tested in the same pass, and any change is reported at once.

The vessel that supplies the arm passes close beneath the shoulder joint, on the inner side of the upper arm, and it can be torn by the same force that breaks the bone. The examiner feels the pulse at the wrist, looks at the color and the warmth of the hand, and presses briefly on a nail to watch the color return, comparing one hand with the other. A pulse that is present does not exclude a serious injury to a vessel, because a pulse can survive a tear contained by the tissues around it, and a pulse that is absent is an emergency. An expanding swelling around the shoulder, a bruise that grows while the patient is watched, and a hand that becomes cool and pale are reasons to summon help at once. The formal examination belongs to the trained responder or the clinician, while the lay first aider notices color, warmth, and movement and reports them without delay.

Stabilization means the small set of ordinary acts that keep the patient alive and comfortable while the plan is made. The patient is kept warm, because cold worsens bleeding and shock, wet clothing is replaced with covering, and the patient is insulated from the ground on which the patient lies or waits. Bleeding from a wound that can be controlled is controlled with direct pressure through a dressing, and bleeding that cannot be controlled is a reason for urgent transfer rather than for prolonged effort at the scene. The arm is supported in the position found, with the other hand of the patient, a pillow, a rolled blanket, or a folded garment, and the support is adjusted until the patient reports less pain. A patient who is drowsy, who is vomiting, or who may need an operation is not given anything by mouth, and the decision about any substance belongs to the prescriber. The lay first aider supports the limb, keeps the patient warm, and calls for help.

Pain is treated, and the decision about how belongs to the prescriber rather than to this course. The finding that makes the decision urgent is pain that is severe, rising instead of settling, or out of proportion to the injury seen, because that pattern may signal a compartment syndrome or an injury to a vessel rather than an ordinary break. The responder reports the pain in the words of the patient, records the time of the report and any change in it, and does not offer a substance that has not been authorized for that patient by someone qualified to prescribe it. The name of the medicine, the amount of it, and the way it is given belong to the local protocol and to the prescriber, and a listener must not be able to take a prescription from this book. Cooling the shoulder is decided within the local protocol as well, since cold applied too long to skin that is already numb can cause an injury of its own.

Immobilization is achieved by taking the weight of the arm off the injured shoulder and by preventing the arm from swinging, and the two devices that do this are the sling and the swathe. A sling cradles the forearm and the hand and hangs from the neck, so that the weight of the arm is carried by the trunk rather than by the broken bone. A swathe is a broad band that passes around the chest and holds the arm against the side of the body, and together the two keep the shoulder still during a journey over rough roads. The sling is applied over the position found, it is padded where it presses on the neck and on the bony points, and it is secured firmly enough to hold without stopping the circulation. The lay first aider trained in basic first aid may apply a simple sling, the trained responder and the paramedic apply the supports their service authorizes, and the nurse or the clinician adjusts the device when the patient reaches care.

One caution belongs with the swathe, and the reason is the same one that governs the immobilization of any injury near the chest. The force that broke the shoulder may also have broken ribs or bruised a lung, and a band that encircles the chest limits the movement of the ribs and the depth of every breath. In a patient with a chest injury the band is applied loosely or left off altogether, and the arm is supported by the sling alone, because a shoulder can be treated tomorrow and a lung cannot wait. The fingers are checked for color, warmth, and sensation immediately after any support is applied and again at intervals, and a hand that becomes cool, pale, or numb is a reason to loosen the support within the local protocol rather than to tighten it. What the support must never be used for is to pull on the arm, to straighten the limb, or to reduce the fracture. How long the support stays in place is decided by the clinician, and any interval a listener has heard is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

Imaging is the step that confirms what the examination has already suggested, and it is ordered by a clinician trained to interpret it. The first study is usually a radiograph of the shoulder taken in more than one direction, because a break invisible in one view may be obvious in another, and the joint above and the joint below are included so that a second injury is not missed. Computed tomography shows the detail of a break that is split into several pieces or that crosses the joint surface, and it often decides whether an operation is needed. Magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for soft tissues, including the tendons that rotate the shoulder, and it is requested when the answer it gives will change what is done. An image is a picture of one moment and does not replace the examination, so the patient is examined and reassessed after the picture is taken. Neither the lay first aider nor the trained responder decides which study is needed.

Reassessment is performed after every intervention, and it is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the record, and the comparison is what has value rather than any single measurement. The arm is checked for color, warmth, pulse, sensation, movement, and pain, and the sling and the swathe are checked for tightness and for pressure on the neck and on the ribs. Late findings are expected here, since the bruising spreads over days and the swelling can increase until the skin feels tight, so a shoulder that looked comfortable an hour ago is looked at again. Pain that increases instead of settling, numbness that was not there before, a movement that has been lost, and a hand that becomes cool or pale are the findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation. The reassessment is repeated at each handover, and the findings are spoken aloud in the order in which they were recorded.

Compartment syndrome is unusual around the shoulder, because the bone lies in a broad bed of muscle rather than in a closed space, but it can occur in the arm and the forearm, and the rule is the same everywhere. Pressure inside a closed compartment rises after bleeding or swelling, the flow through the smallest vessels fails, and the first structures to suffer are the muscle and then the nerve. The diagnosis is clinical and is made by a clinician, and the finding that raises the suspicion is pain out of proportion to the injury, worse when the muscles are stretched and not relieved by support. The constricting bandage, sling, or swathe is released within the limits of the local protocol by someone authorized to do it, the limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, and the patient is referred urgently, because time is muscle. A pulse that remains present does not exclude the diagnosis, and waiting for a pulse to disappear is waiting far too long.

Referral or definitive treatment is the final step, and for this fracture the decision belongs to an orthopedic surgeon with the images and the patient in front of them. Many proximal humerus fractures are not displaced, or are displaced so little that the shoulder can be expected to work, and those are treated without an operation, supported in a sling with gentle movement begun early under supervision. An operation is considered when the head is split into separate pieces, when the joint surface is broken and left uneven, when the fragments are displaced enough that the shoulder will not lift, when the bone has lost its blood supply, or when the shoulder has also been dislocated. The surgeon may hold the fragments with metal devices, an operation called open reduction and internal fixation, or may replace the head of the bone with an artificial one, particularly in an older patient whose bone is unlikely to heal well. The decision, the timing, and the technique belong to the surgeon in a facility with imaging, an operating room, and an intensive care unit.

Reduction, traction, and casting are named only so that the listener understands what they are and who may perform them, and the technique belongs to supervised clinical training. Reduction restores the position of the bone ends, traction holds them in line with a gentle continuous pull, and a cast or a surgical fixation holds the position while the bone heals. Each is learned under supervision, practiced on models and on patients under a teacher, and performed according to local protocol by a clinician or an orthopedic surgeon. A reduction is not attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when the vessels or the nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, when the responder lacks the training or the equipment, or when an earlier attempt has failed. In each of those situations the attempt can convert an injury that would have healed into one that cannot, and force is not a technique. The same rule governs a shoulder with both a break and a dislocation, because pulling on such an arm in the field can convert a nerve injury that was recovering into one that will not.

The record and the handover are part of the treatment rather than a formality that follows it. The time of the injury, the mechanism, the position in which the arm was found, the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention, and the support that was applied are written or spoken in a fixed order, so that the receiving team continues a story instead of starting a new one. What each provider may do is decided by training, by authorization, and by local protocol: the lay first aider supports the arm if trained to do so and calls for help, the trained responder and the paramedic apply the supports their service authorizes and record what they find, the nurse observes and reassesses, the physician orders imaging and decides what treatment is needed, and the orthopedic surgeon decides on and performs any operation. Where this chapter names an interval, a threshold, or a size, it names an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between services, countries, and editions. A patient who is injured, immobilized, exposed, or waiting is kept warm and covered, because cold is a complication of trauma, and nothing here describes a dose, a route, or a regimen.

To summarize, a break of the upper end of the arm bone is one of the commonest injuries of the shoulder, and it happens most often to an older person whose bone has lost density and less often to a younger person who has met a great force. The patient usually holds the arm close against the side of the body with the elbow bent and the other hand supporting it, and the swelling and the bruising arrive late and spread down the arm, so a shoulder that looks almost normal at first can never be dismissed. The nerve that runs around the neck of the bone and the vessel that passes beneath the shoulder are examined and recorded before any intervention and again afterwards, because a finding not written down before a sling was applied cannot be attributed later. The arm is immobilized in the position found with a sling and a swathe, the support is checked for tightness and the fingers for color and warmth, and the band around the chest is left loose or omitted when the chest has also been injured. The patient is kept warm, is not given anything by mouth if an operation may be needed, and is reassessed after every intervention, with rising pain, new numbness, a lost movement, or a cool pale hand treated as reasons to escalate. Everyone with a suspected break of this bone is referred to a clinician for imaging, and the decision about an operation, about reduction, about traction, and about casting belongs to an orthopedic surgeon in a facility with an operating room and an intensive care unit. A deformed limb is never straightened in the field, a wound is never explored blindly, an embedded object is never removed, exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, and force is never a technique.
