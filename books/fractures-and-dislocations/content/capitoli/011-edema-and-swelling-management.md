# Chapter 11 — Edema and Swelling Management

Swelling is the injury that continues after the injury has happened, and it is treated rather than only watched, because the fluid itself can cost a limb. A limb that swells inside a closed space bounded by fascia has nowhere to put the extra volume, so the pressure rises against the muscle and the nerve within it, and the damage that follows belongs to the swelling rather than to the bone. Every chapter of this course follows one order, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and swelling runs through every one of those steps. Two of them are never skipped, because the neurovascular examination is performed before an intervention and repeated after it and its result is recorded, and the reassessment after every intervention is what catches the complication caused by the treatment. This chapter explains why an injured limb swells, what swelling does inside a closed compartment, what a responder may do about it, who may do it, and when swelling stops being a finding and becomes an emergency. It is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

Swelling after an injury is not a mistake of the body but part of its repair, and it begins within minutes of the moment the tissue is torn. A broken bone bleeds from its own vessels and from the marrow inside it, and the blood that collects between the broken ends forms the hematoma that is the first stage of healing rather than a complication to be drained. Torn muscle, torn fascia, torn capsule and torn skin each add their own bleeding, and the inflammatory response that follows widens the small vessels so that plasma leaks into the spaces between the cells. That fluid carries the cells that clear debris and the signals that call repair forward, so a limb that does not swell at all is not automatically a lucky limb. The responder meets the total of all of this as a limb that is larger, tighter, warmer and more painful than it was. The question is how fast it is growing, what it is pressing against, and whether the tissue on the other side of the fascia has room to give.

The body has two places to put this fluid, and the difference between them decides whether the swelling is a nuisance or a danger. The first is the tissue between the cells, where fluid spreads along the limb and produces the soft swelling that leaves a dent when a finger presses into it. The second is the compartment, a closed room bounded by bone on one side and by fascia on the other, which holds muscle, nerves and vessels with very little room to spare. Fascia is a tough sheet of fibrous tissue that wraps a muscle or a group of muscles, and it stretches slowly and reluctantly rather than opening to let the pressure out. When bleeding or edema pours into a compartment, the volume inside a room that cannot enlarge raises the pressure within it, and that pressure reaches everything inside. The same amount of blood is therefore a minor problem in the loose tissue of the thigh and a threat in the tight compartments of the forearm or the leg. The responder cannot see a compartment or feel the pressure inside it, so the examination is a search for what a rising pressure does to the tissues it holds.

As the pressure inside a compartment rises, the first vessels to lose their flow are the smallest ones, and the order in which the tissues suffer is fairly constant. The capillaries and the small veins are compressed first, so the blood that arrives through the artery cannot leave through the vein, and the compartment holds more fluid than it drains. Muscle suffers earliest and most, and nerve is next, which is why the earliest symptoms come from muscle that is being starved and from nerve that is being squeezed. The artery is a thick muscular tube that withstands a pressure which has already closed the smaller vessels, so a pulse felt at the wrist or the ankle does not mean that the muscle inside the limb is being perfused. A pulse that is present therefore does not exclude a compartment syndrome, and a pulse that is absent is a separate emergency with its own urgency. Pain comes from the muscle that is ischemic and from the nerve that is compressed, and it is usually the first thing the patient reports and the first thing that must be believed. A limb that is swollen, tense and painful beyond what the injury appears to explain is not a limb to be observed at leisure.

Swelling has a natural history, and knowing its shape helps the responder decide whether what is being seen is expected or alarming. In an ordinary injury the swelling grows over the first hours, reaches a peak and then slowly settles as the torn vessels seal and the fluid is carried away by the veins and the lymphatics. The height of that peak and the time it takes to arrive depend on which bone was broken, on how much soft tissue was torn and on what the patient has been doing since, and they differ between services and between editions. A figure that names a number of hours is therefore an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. What matters at the bedside is the direction of the change rather than the exact size, because a limb whose swelling increases while the bandage stays the same is telling the responder something that a measurement can only confirm. Swelling that is settling is a patient who can be watched, and swelling that is tightening is a patient who must be seen now.

Assessment of a swelling begins before anyone touches the limb, and it begins with the story. The mechanism of injury, the time it happened, the position in which the limb was found and the illnesses and medicines that affect bleeding and healing are all part of it, because a patient whose clotting is impaired or whose bone is weakened may swell far more than the injury suggests. The account the patient gives of the pain is the most valuable single piece of information in the assessment, and it is worth asking whether the pain is deep, whether it burns, and whether it is growing. The lay first aider gathers what the patient says and reports it, the trained first responder and the emergency medical technician assess within the procedures their service authorizes, and the interpretation of the findings belongs to the clinician. Only then does the examination move to the limb, which is looked at before it is touched and compared with the other side, because a difference between the two limbs is easier to see than an absolute size, and nothing is moved into a prettier position before that comparison has been recorded.

Looking at a swollen limb is a skill with several parts, and each part answers a different question. The color of the skin is compared with the other side, because pallor may mean that flow has failed and a dusky or mottled appearance may mean that the blood is no longer moving through. The surface is inspected for tightness and shine, since skin stretched smooth over a tense limb is a different finding from skin that still moves freely over soft tissue. Blisters may appear over swollen skin, and their presence tells the examiner that the tissue beneath has been under stress for some time. Rings, watches, bracelets, tight sleeves and boots are looked for before the swelling makes them impossible to remove, and the mark left by any of them is recorded. The lay first aider observes and reports these findings, the trained responder and the emergency medical technician document them, and the clinician decides what they mean.

Feeling the limb is the next part of the assessment, and the examiner feels the same place on both sides in the same order. The temperature of the skin is compared, the swelling is pressed gently to see whether it pits and springs back, and the examiner notices whether the limb is soft or tense under the hand. A measuring tape is used at a marked and repeatable landmark, such as a fixed distance from a named bony point, and the mark is written on the skin so that the next examiner measures the same place. The choice of landmark and the size of the difference that matters are local conventions rather than fixed truths, so a difference of 2 centimeters between the two sides illustrates a threshold that some services treat as significant and that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol in force. The examiner also presses along each compartment for tenderness out of proportion to the injury, because a compartment that is tense and exquisitely tender is the finding that changes the plan. Measuring swelling has value only when the measurement is repeated by the same method and compared with the record, and the lay first aider needs no tape to report that the limb looks larger than it did.

Swelling does not always mean the same thing, and where it sits helps to say what has happened. A joint that fills quickly after an injury holds blood inside its capsule, and the joint becomes swollen, painful and hard to move, which tells the clinician that something within the joint has torn. A swelling of the whole limb suggests a larger injury or a failure of the drainage that carries fluid away, while a swelling in a limb with no remembered injury, or in both limbs, points toward a cause that is not trauma at all and deserves assessment rather than a splint. The lay first aider reports what is visible, the clinician decides which of these possibilities is being faced, and nobody in the field reopens a wound to look for a cause.

Several of the signs that a responder would most like to rely on are unreliable, and knowing which ones they are protects the patient. A pulse that can be felt beyond the injury does not exclude a compartment syndrome, because the pressure that closes the smallest vessels is far lower than the pressure that closes an artery. The classical findings of advanced compromise appear late, and by the time they are all present the muscle may already be lost, so the responder acts on the earliest trustworthy report rather than waiting for the whole picture. Pain is the earliest warning in a patient who can communicate, but it is a poor guide in a patient who is sedated, intoxicated, confused or unconscious, in a patient whose spinal cord has been injured, and in a small child who cannot describe it. A limb may also swell deep inside the calf, where the fascia is thick and the swelling is far less visible. Pain out of proportion to the injury, pain that increases when the examiner gently stretches the muscle, a limb that is tense to the hand, and a new numbness or weakness are the findings that matter most. The lay first aider watches and reports, the trained responder and the emergency medical technician perform the examination their service authorizes, and the diagnosis of a compartment syndrome belongs to the clinician who is called to the bedside.

Stabilization of a swollen limb is a small set of acts, and the first of them is to stop adding to the swelling while the patient is moved toward care. The limb is supported in the position in which it was found, and it is never straightened in the field, because vessels and nerves lie against the bone and a movement that looks helpful can tear what remained intact. Elevation above the level of the heart is a traditional measure against swelling, and it does reduce the pressure in the small vessels, but it is used within the limits of the injury and never against them. When a compartment syndrome is suspected the limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, because guidance on this point differs between services and the safe course is to keep the limb level and to move the patient toward definitive care. A limb whose position is holding a fracture quiet, a limb with an open wound, and a patient whose spine is being treated as unstable are not repositioned for the sake of a swelling. The lay first aider supports the limb with the hands or with whatever is at hand and keeps the patient warm, the trained responder elevates within the equipment and the procedures the service authorizes, and elevation never delays the transfer of a limb that is tense.

Removing what constricts is the second act, and it is done early because a ring that fits now may not come off in an hour. Rings, watches, bracelets, tight sleeves, tight boots and any elastic garment around the limb are removed before the swelling makes the removal painful and difficult. The lay first aider may remove jewelry and loose clothing that comes away easily, and the trained responder may use the simple measures the service authorizes, such as lubrication, a cool hand or a gentle rotation. A ring that will not move is not pulled, is not forced over a swollen knuckle and is not cut off in the field by anyone who is not authorized and equipped to do it, because a cut ring is a small loss and a torn finger is not. If a constricting item cannot be removed, its presence and its position are recorded and reported before the swelling grows further. Every technique for removing a tight ring or a tight boot is learned under supervision and practiced on a model, and none of it can be learned from listening to a book.

A bandage, a splint or a cast that was comfortable when it was applied can become a tourniquet as the limb beneath it swells, and this is the most common way that treatment causes the very injury it was meant to prevent. The device is checked at the fingers or the toes for color, warmth, sensation and movement immediately after it is applied and again at every reassessment, and the findings are compared with the record made before it went on. A bandage or a splint whose fastenings have become tight may be loosened within the limits of the local protocol, and which provider is authorized to loosen it is written into the protocol that governs the patient. A cast is a rigid shell with no give at all, so a cast that becomes tight is a problem for the clinician, who may split it or remove it with the proper tool and the proper authorization. Cutting a cast blindly is dangerous because the skin beneath the edge may already be broken or blistered, and a cut that reaches the padding and the skin introduces an infection where there was none. Compression wraps carry the same risk, so they are applied only by a provider authorized to apply them, only when the injury and the protocol permit it, and never over an open wound or a limb whose compartments are already tight.

The neurovascular examination is the step that turns a swelling from an impression into a record, and it is performed before any device is applied and repeated after each intervention. The examiner looks at the color and the temperature of the skin, feels the pulse beyond the injury, presses briefly on a nail to watch the color return, and asks the patient to move the fingers and the toes. Sensation is tested on both sides and compared, and the loss of a movement the patient could perform a moment earlier is written down in the words of the patient as far as possible. The result is recorded before the splint and after the splint, because a finding that was not written before the intervention cannot be attributed afterwards, and the change between the two records is what carries the information. The lay first aider observes color, warmth and the ability of the patient to move and feel, the trained responder performs and records the examination the service authorizes, and the nurse and the physician repeat it and interpret it. Numbness, tingling, weakness, pallor, coolness and pain out of proportion to the injury are the findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation.

Immobilization is the step that holds the swelling still, and it is done for the fracture and for the soft tissues at the same time. A splint prevents movement of the injured part and of the joints above and below it, because a bone that is splinted alone can still be moved by the joint at either end. It is padded where it presses on bone, it is applied over the position found, and it is secured firmly enough to hold without becoming a band that stops the circulation. The material matters less than the principle, since a folded magazine, a padded board, a sling or the body of the patient can each serve, and an improvised device must be checked more often. Cooling a swollen limb is used in some injuries and is applied according to the local protocol and never directly against bare skin, because cold can injure tissue that is already short of blood, and cold is treated throughout this course as a complication of trauma. The trained responder and the paramedic apply splints within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, the physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides what definitive immobilization is required, and the patient is insulated and covered before anyone moves.

Imaging is the step that confirms what the examination has suggested, and it is ordered by a clinician trained to interpret it. A radiograph is usually the first study, and it is taken in at least two directions so that a displacement in the third direction is not hidden, while the joint above and the joint below are included so that a second injury is not missed. Swelling makes that first picture harder to read, because the soft tissues are thicker and the bone edges sit further from the recording surface, so a fracture that becomes obvious once the swelling has settled can be invisible on the first study. A patient whose limb remains painful and swollen after a normal first radiograph is therefore examined again, and a further study may be requested later. Computed tomography shows the detail of a complex break, particularly around a joint, and magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for the soft tissues, including ligaments, cartilage, tendons and muscle. The lay first aider never orders or interprets imaging, the trained responder does not decide which study is needed, and the clinician requests a study when the answer it gives will change what is done.

Reassessment is performed after every intervention, and it is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the record, and the comparison is what has value rather than any single measurement. The limb is checked for color, warmth, pulse, sensation, movement and pain, the swelling is compared with the mark and with the other side, and every splint, bandage and wrap is checked for tightness. Pain that increases instead of settling, pain that is worse than the injury appears, pain that a gentle stretch of the muscle makes worse, numbness, tingling, weakness, pallor and a limb that becomes tense and hard are the findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation. A responder who suspects a compartment syndrome does not wait for a confirmatory test and acts within the local protocol, which may allow a constricting bandage, splint or wrap to be released by someone authorized to release it. The limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, the patient is kept warm, and the patient is referred urgently, because the diagnosis is clinical and time is muscle. The lay first aider reports the change, the trained responder escalates to the receiving facility, and the clinician decides what happens next.

Swelling becomes an emergency when it begins to threaten the tissues inside the closed compartment, and the transition is measured in the damage done rather than in the size of the limb. The diagnosis is clinical, which means that it rests on the history, on the examination and on the account the patient gives, and a single pressure measurement is only one part of a picture that a clinician assembles. Where such a pressure is measured, the threshold and the technique differ between services and between editions, so any figure quoted for it is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. The muscle that is starved of blood begins to die before the skin shows anything unusual, and the breakdown products that follow can reach the circulation and the kidneys, which is why the patient is referred to a facility with an operating room and an intensive care unit. Both are reasons to refer urgently rather than to wait for a scheduled appointment, and to write down what was seen and when it was seen. The lay first aider and the trained responder do not make the diagnosis, and their task is to suspect it early, to remove what constricts, to keep the limb level and the patient warm, and to hand the suspicion over in plain words.

Definitive treatment of a swelling that has become dangerous belongs to the operating room, and the operation that relieves the pressure is called a fasciotomy, in which the surgeon opens the fascia that bounds the compartment. That operation is performed by a surgeon with a team, under anesthesia, in a facility with an intensive care unit, and its timing is a surgical decision rather than a field one. Reduction, which restores the position of the bone ends, traction, which holds them in line with a gentle continuous pull, and casting or surgical fixation, which holds the position while the bone heals, are the other definitive acts this course names, and every one of them is learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol. None of them is taught from a book, and a responder who has not been trained and authorized does not attempt them, because a reduction that is forced can convert an injury that would have healed into one that cannot. A reduction must not be attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when the vessels or the nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, when the responder lacks the training or the equipment, or when an earlier attempt has already failed. In each of those situations the right act is to immobilize in the position found, to reassess, and to hand the patient to someone qualified to do more, because force is not a technique.

A swollen limb is also a window on the whole patient, and the responder reads it that way. Swelling that grows while the pulse rises and the skin turns pale may be telling the responder about bleeding that continues somewhere the eye cannot reach, and a closed fracture of a large bone can release enough blood to threaten the circulation. A limb that swells after a long period of stillness or after an operation may hold a clot in a deep vein, which is a different emergency with a different treatment, and it is referred rather than massaged. A limb that is swollen, red, hot and painful with a fever suggests an infection, and a limb that swells without any of those features may have a cause in the heart, the kidneys, the liver or the lymphatics that no splint will help. A small child cannot describe the pain an adult would name, an older adult may blister over a splint that would be harmless on a younger arm, and a patient who is sedated, confused or paralyzed by a spinal cord injury may have a compartment that is tight and silent. The record carries the time of the injury, the mechanism, the position in which the limb was found, the size of the swelling and the landmark used to measure it, the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention, and every change in the condition of the patient, and it is spoken in a fixed order so that the receiving team continues a story instead of starting a new one.

To summarize, an injured limb swells because bone, muscle and fascia bleed and because the inflammatory response leaks plasma into the tissues, and the swelling becomes dangerous when it rises inside a compartment that cannot expand. The neurovascular status is recorded before any intervention and repeated after every one, because a finding that was not written down before a splint was applied cannot be attributed afterwards. The responder supports the limb in the position found, removes rings and constricting clothing early, loosens what the local protocol allows to be loosened, keeps the limb level when a compartment syndrome is suspected, keeps the patient warm, and refers urgently, because the diagnosis is clinical and time is muscle. A deformed limb is never straightened in the field, an embedded object is never removed, exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, and an open wound is never explored blindly. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and they are never attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when the vessels or nerves are failing, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, when training or equipment is lacking, or when an earlier attempt has failed.
