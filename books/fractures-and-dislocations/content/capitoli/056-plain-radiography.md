# Chapter 56 — Plain Radiography

The plain radiograph is the oldest and still the most widely used picture in the care of an injured limb, and it earns that place because it is quick, it is inexpensive, and it is available where nothing else is. It is also a shadow rather than a limb, and what a listener does with it depends on understanding what kind of shadow it is. A radiograph answers a question that someone has already asked about a patient, so it never replaces looking at the limb, feeling it, and asking the patient what happened. Every chapter of this course follows one order, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and the picture comes after the hands for a reason. The reason is that a film can be repeated and a nerve cannot. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

To understand what a plain film can and cannot show, it helps to remember how the image is made. A beam of X-rays is passed through the part, and the tissues absorb different amounts of it, so the picture is a map of absorption rather than a portrait. Bone absorbs a great deal of the beam and appears white, air absorbs almost none and appears black, and muscle, fat and fluid lie between them in shades of gray. The beam crosses the whole thickness of the limb on its way to the detector, so every structure along that path is printed on top of the others and none can be separated afterwards, which is why a radiograph is a projection rather than a slice.

Assessment comes before the picture, and its purpose is to turn a general worry into a precise question. The mechanism of injury, the age of the patient, the point of greatest tenderness, the deformity, the swelling and the loss of function all point toward a particular bone and a particular place on it. A request that says only that the limb hurts asks the radiographer to search an entire region, and a search produces more exposures, more time and more radiation than a question does. The clinician who orders the study is the one responsible for the patient, which in most services means a physician, and in some services a nurse practitioner or a physician assistant working within an agreed protocol may order it. The lay first aider and the trained responder do not order imaging and do not decide which study is needed, and nothing here permits a listener to request a study that the local rules reserve to someone else.

Stabilization comes next, and it decides whether the patient goes to the imaging room at all. A patient whose airway, breathing or circulation is failing is treated where the team can act, and the film waits, because a picture of a limb is worth nothing to a patient who is bleeding somewhere else. When an image genuinely cannot wait, a portable machine may be brought to the bedside, and that decision belongs to the clinician leading the resuscitation together with the team that will perform the study. The responder keeps the limb supported in the position in which it was found, keeps the patient warm, and keeps the patient still while the exposure is made. Pain is not a detail that a patient must endure in order to obtain a better picture, and analgesia is decided by the prescriber, with the substance, the amount and the route belonging to that decision and to the local protocol. A patient with a suspected injury of the spine is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has done so, and no lone handling technique is safe, so such a patient travels with a trained team and the precautions the local protocol requires.

The neurovascular examination is performed before the patient leaves for the imaging department and repeated when the patient returns, because the journey and the positioning can change what the nerves and vessels are doing. The examiner looks at the color and the warmth of the skin, feels the pulse beyond the injury, presses briefly on a nail to watch the color return, tests sensation on both sides and asks the patient to move the fingers or the toes. The result is written in the record before the film and written again afterwards, because a finding that was not recorded before an intervention cannot be attributed to it later. The radiographer positions the limb for the exposure and must never be asked to straighten it or to pull it into line, and a limb that cannot be placed for a standard view is imaged as it lies. A pulse that is present does not exclude serious damage to the vessels, and a pulse that is absent is an emergency that no picture will explain.

Immobilization usually comes before the image, because a limb splinted in the position found is safer to move and easier to radiograph than one that is left free. A splint may obscure the fracture it is meant to protect and may hide the soft tissues the clinician needs to see, so the order bends when the clinical situation requires it, and the decision to image first belongs to the clinician. The trained responder and the paramedic apply splints within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, while the physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides what definitive immobilization is required. Casting and surgical fixation are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and neither is taught from a book in this course. A splint that is tight enough to stop the circulation is worse than no splint at all, which is one reason the film taken after a splint is applied is read for the position of the bone and for the fit of the splint.

What is asked for matters as much as how the picture is taken. The request names the region, the side, the clinical question and the reason the study is needed, and it names the projections the local protocol uses for that region. The side must be marked on the image itself, because a film shown against the wrong side of a patient is a hazard that has harmed people, and the marker is placed by the radiographer rather than added to the record later. Earlier films, where they exist, travel with the patient, because an old study may show the fracture that the new one is too early to reveal. The clinician writes the request, the radiographer chooses the technical factors and may add a view the protocol allows in order to answer the question, and the radiologist or another trained clinician interprets the result. The lay first aider does none of those things and is never asked to hold a film up to a window.

An injury has three dimensions and a radiograph has two, so a single exposure leaves one direction unexamined. The rule that follows is that a fracture is not excluded by one view, and the practical form of the rule is that every injured bone is examined in at least two projections taken at right angles to each other. The common pair is one view taken from front to back and one taken from the side, and in many regions a third view taken at an angle is added because it opens a joint surface that the first two overlap. Displacement of the broken ends can lie entirely within the plane of a single beam and leave no trace in the picture it produces, so a confident answer built on one image deserves distrust. The far end of a broken bone may also be rotated about its long axis, and rotation is the displacement a film shows least clearly, so the two limbs are compared and the patient is examined.

Two projections of the injured bone are still not the whole study, because the joints at either end must be included in the picture as well. A fall onto an outstretched hand can break the wrist and dislocate the elbow, and a blow to the knee can break the shin bone and injure the ankle, so the second injury is missed when the study stops at the ends of the shaft. A long bone is part of a chain, and a break anywhere in the chain changes the forces that reach every other link in it. The clinician who writes the request therefore names the joints above and below the suspected fracture, and the clinician who reviews the images checks that both of them are on the film. The forearm is a special case, because its two bones form a joint with one another along their whole length, so a break in one of them may be accompanied by a dislocation at either end. When any part of that chain is missing, the image is incomplete, and the answer is to obtain the view that was omitted rather than to assume that nothing is there.

A fracture announces itself on a plain film in a small number of ways, and a listener who knows them can follow a clinician's description. The most direct sign is a dark line across the pale bone, which is the gap between the broken ends, and it is often irregular, stepped or branched rather than a clean cut. A break may show instead as a kink or a step in the cortex, the dense white edge of the bone, with no obvious line within it, and an impacted fracture may look brighter rather than darker because the bone is compressed. A small piece pulled away where a tendon or a ligament attaches is called an avulsion, and a bone broken into more than two pieces is described as comminuted. The film also shows how the ends are sitting, since angulation appears as a bend in the line of the shaft, translation as an offset of the outlines, and shortening as a bone that looks shorter than its fellow on the other side. The soft tissues are part of the same picture, so swelling or a joint filled with blood adds to the story rather than decorating it.

A dislocation has its own appearance, and it is usually easier to see than a fracture because a joint that has lost its fit looks wrong even at a glance. The rounded head of one bone no longer sits within the hollow of the other, so the two outlines overlap instead of nesting, or a gap opens where the surfaces should touch. On the view taken from the side the far bone usually lies in front of or behind its socket rather than within it, and the smooth curves that run along the edge of the joint are broken rather than continuous. A joint that has dislocated has almost always torn its capsule and at least one ligament, and a fragment of bone may have come away with it, so the film is read for a chip as well as for the displacement. Reduction restores the position of the joint surfaces, and it is learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol by a clinician qualified to do it, never from the pages of a book. A film taken after a reduction shows whether the surfaces are back in contact, and it never decides by itself whether the joint is stable enough to be left alone.

When a dislocation and a fracture occur together, the picture becomes harder to read, and the combination is one of the injuries in which a reduction attempted by the wrong person does the most harm. A fragment caught between the joint surfaces, a fracture of the socket, or a break of the bone that carries the joint can all be hidden by the displacement itself and appear only after something has been moved. That is one reason a reduction is not attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when the vessels or nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, when the responder lacks the training or the equipment, or when an earlier attempt has failed. In each of those situations the attempt can convert an injury that would have healed into one that cannot, and force is not a technique. The part of the plain film here is to show the clinician what is present before anything is done, and the part of the responder is to support the limb in the position found.

Some fractures are invisible on the first film, and a listener who does not know this will send a patient away with a broken bone. The small bone at the base of the thumb side of the wrist is the classic example, because its fracture line may be too fine to see at first and the bone has a poor blood supply that punishes a delay. The neck of the thigh bone in an older adult can also break without a line that the first film reveals, and the patient may still be able to stand, which makes the picture even more misleading. A stress injury from repeated loading, common in runners, dancers and soldiers who march, often appears only as a faint thickening of the bone or not at all. The signs that point to a fracture the film does not show are indirect but real, and they include tenderness over one exact point, swelling of the soft tissue beside the bone, and fluid within a joint that has no other explanation. A tiny avulsion flake may be dismissed as an artifact, and the growth region of a child can conceal an injury that later disturbs the growth of the limb.

What is done when the film is normal and the patient is not is the test of whether the study is being used well. The patient is treated rather than the picture, so a limb that is painful, swollen and tender over one point is immobilized and protected even when the report says that no fracture is seen. The clinician may arrange a repeat film after an interval, and a commonly taught figure is 10 to 14 days, which is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol because the interval differs between injuries and between services. A child with a tender elbow and a normal film receives the same caution, since a growth region can be injured without a visible break. In each of these situations the decision belongs to the clinician, and the part of the responder is to immobilize, to protect, and to make certain the patient will be seen again.

The soft tissues are not invisible on a plain film, and what they show can change what is done next. Swelling appears as a widening of the shadow around the bone, and a joint filled with blood appears as a bulge over the joint, which at the elbow may push the pad of fat that lies against the bone away from it and produce a dark crescent that warns of an injury within the joint. A foreign body may be visible if it is dense enough, such as metal or glass, while wood and many plastics often are not, so a normal film never excludes a retained object. An embedded object is never removed in the field and exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, because the object may be holding pressure on a torn vessel. An open wound is never explored blindly; it is covered with a sterile dressing, the bleeding that can be controlled is controlled, the neurovascular status is recorded, the limb is immobilized, and the patient is referred urgently.

Reassessment follows every one of those steps, and it is the step this course is most afraid to see skipped. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the record, because the comparison is what has value rather than any single measurement. The examiner checks the color and the warmth of the skin, the pulse beyond the injury, sensation on both sides, the ability to move the fingers or the toes, and the level of pain, and then checks the splint for tightness. Pain that increases instead of settling, pain out of proportion to the injury, numbness, tingling, weakness, and a limb that becomes tense and swollen are the findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation. A tense and painful limb may be developing a compartment syndrome, whose diagnosis is clinical and whose treatment is time critical, so the responder acts within the local protocol rather than waiting for an image or a laboratory result to confirm it. The limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, the constricting bandage or splint is released only by someone the protocol authorizes to release it, the patient is kept warm, and the patient is referred urgently.

The radiograph is not free, and the cost it carries is invisible. The beam that makes the picture also deposits energy in the patient and in anyone who remains in the room, so the study is requested only when its answer will change what is done and never as a routine before a clinical decision. The clinician who orders the study is responsible for asking whether the patient may be pregnant, because the growing child is more sensitive to radiation than the adult, and local protocols differ in how that question is asked and what is done with the answer. The radiographer shields the patient and the team according to the local rules, wears personal protective equipment where those rules require it, and may ask everyone who is not needed to step behind the barrier. A patient is never left unsupported in order to keep a staff member out of the room, and nobody holds a fracture by hand during an exposure when a splint can hold it instead.

A film does not read itself, and the person who takes it is not always the person who interprets it. The radiographer is trained to produce a diagnostic image and to recognize when it is not good enough, and in some services the radiographer may comment on an obvious abnormality within a local protocol, but the formal reading belongs to a radiologist or to another clinician trained to interpret it. A responder who is not trained does not read the film aloud to the patient, does not name a diagnosis from it, and does not use it to decide that treatment is unnecessary. An obvious deformity with a limb that has no pulse is treated as an emergency whether or not a film has been taken, because the examination outranks the picture when the two disagree. A report that has not yet arrived never delays the care of a patient who needs it, and a normal report does not erase the findings that were recorded before the study was taken. When the report and the examination disagree, the patient is examined again and the clinician decides which to trust while the patient is kept safe.

The record of the study is part of the treatment, and it is written before the film is taken and after it is read. The request states the region, the side, the clinical question and the reason the study is needed, and the record states the findings that sent the patient there and the neurovascular status before and after the journey to the imaging room. A normal film of the spine does not clear it, because a spine is cleared only by someone qualified to clear it, using the examination and the rules of the local protocol rather than a picture alone. A normal film does not exclude a compartment syndrome, a ligament injury, a tendon injury or a stress injury that has not yet changed the bone, and it does not measure how much pain a patient has. The report may state that no fracture is seen, and that sentence describes the picture rather than the patient, so the examination and the reassessment continue after the film has been filed.

The images travel with the patient to the next facility, because a receiving team that cannot see the first study may repeat it or may miss what it showed. A handover that includes the pictures, the question they were meant to answer and the answers they gave lets the next team continue a story instead of starting a new one. Where the study can be done is also a question of resources, and the honest answer differs from one country to another, since a hospital may have a radiographer at all hours, a clinic may have one on certain days, and a remote station may have no machine at all. When imaging is delayed, the limb is immobilized and the patient is kept warm while the plan is made, because an unmanaged wait becomes a complication, and the decision to move a patient in order to obtain a study belongs to the clinician in charge of the case and to the local protocol.

Referral or definitive treatment is the last step of the order, and the plain film usually decides nothing by itself while settling a great deal. A break that is displaced, a joint that has lost its fit, a fracture that reaches the joint surface, a bone broken into fragments, a fragment left within the joint and an injury in a growing child are all findings that send the patient onward to a facility with an orthopedic surgeon and an operating room. Definitive treatment includes reduction, which restores the position of the bone ends, traction, which holds them in line with a gentle continuous pull, and casting or surgical fixation, which holds the position while the bone heals, and every one of those acts is learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol. A surgeon may hold the fragments with metal devices, an operation called open reduction and internal fixation, and the images taken during and after that operation belong to the surgeon's judgment. The responder rarely decides where the patient goes, but the quality of the record and the honesty of the handover travel with the patient into that decision.

Consider a constructed illustration, which describes no real patient and no real institution. A middle aged person falls onto an outstretched hand and arrives with a painful wrist, swelling on the thumb side and tenderness over one small bone, and the films are taken in the views the local protocol uses for that region and are reported as showing no fracture. The clinician knows that this bone is one of those whose first film is often silent, so the wrist is immobilized in the position found, the person is told not to use the hand, and a further appointment is made instead of a discharge home. Another constructed illustration concerns an older adult who cannot bear weight after a fall indoors, whose first film is read as showing nothing, and who is admitted for observation and repeat imaging rather than helped back onto the feet. In both illustrations the film was not wrong but incomplete, and the examination supplied what the picture could not. That is the habit this chapter asks for, which is to treat the patient and to use the film as one witness among several.

To summarize, the plain radiograph is a shadow of a living limb and not a substitute for examining it, and it is requested after assessment and after stabilization by the clinician responsible for the patient, never by the lay first aider or the trained responder. Every injured bone is examined in at least two projections taken at right angles, the joints above and below the suspected fracture are included in the study, and the side is marked on the image, because displacement can hide in the plane of a single beam and a second injury can be missed when the picture stops at the shaft. A fracture appears as a dark line, a step in the cortex, a denser impacted band, an avulsion fragment or a break into several pieces, and a dislocation appears as a joint that has lost its fit, with overlapping outlines and broken curves along the edge of the joint. Some fractures are invisible on the first film, especially in the small bone of the wrist, the neck of the thigh bone in an older adult and the growth regions of a child, so a painful limb with a normal report is immobilized, protected and seen again instead of discharged. The neurovascular status is recorded before the patient goes to the imaging room and after the return, and the reassessment is repeated after every splint, every reduction and every journey, because the complication this course fears most is the one caused by the treatment. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, a deformed limb is never straightened in the field, an embedded object is never removed, exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, and an open wound is never explored blindly. The film does not clear a spine, does not exclude a compartment syndrome and does not replace the hands, and the patient is handed over with the images, the question they answered and the findings that came before them. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.
