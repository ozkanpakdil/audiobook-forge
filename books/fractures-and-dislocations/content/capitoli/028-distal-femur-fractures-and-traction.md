# Chapter 28 — Distal Femur Fractures and Traction

The distal femur is the widened lower end of the thigh bone, where it divides into two rounded condyles and meets the shin bone to form the knee. When it breaks, the joint is often involved, and a break between the condyles can separate them from each other and from the shaft above. The injury deserves respect, because the knee is the joint a person needs in order to walk and because the vessels and the nerves of the leg pass immediately behind the bone. Every chapter of this course follows one order, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. This chapter is educational material and it does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

The shape of the lower thigh bone explains much of what happens when it breaks. Above the knee the bone narrows into the shaft, and the place where the shaft flares into the condyles is the supracondylar region, where the bone is thinner and lies closer to the skin. The condyles roll and slide on the top of the shin bone, and between them lies a deep notch that holds the crossing ligaments of the knee. The capsule of the knee is attached above the condyles rather than at the joint line, so the condyles lie inside the joint cavity, and that is why a break here fills the knee with blood. In a child the growth region lies just above the condyles and is the busiest in the body, so an injury there can change the length and the shape of the leg for life.

The force that breaks the lower thigh bone divides these patients into two groups. In a young adult the cause is usually a high energy event, such as a vehicle collision in which the knee is driven into the dashboard, a fall from a height or a motorcycle crash. In an older adult the same break may follow a fall from standing height, because the bone has lost density, or an even smaller force when a knee replacement has already been placed. A fracture that crosses the bone around an implanted joint is a familiar companion of this injury, so the responder asks whether the patient carries such an implant. Sport, a fall from a bicycle and a direct blow each account for a share of these fractures, and in a child a simple fall is enough.

Muscle decides the direction in which the broken ends travel. The large calf muscle takes its origin from the back of the bone just above the condyles, so the lower fragment is drawn backward while the shaft is pulled forward by the muscles at the front of the thigh. The result is a limb that looks bent at the knee and a lower fragment that tilts toward the back of the leg, which is exactly where the vessels and the nerves lie. The limb may also be shortened and rotated, so that the two legs no longer match. What the responder sees is a swollen thigh and a knee that is filled and tense, and what the responder must not do is to correct that shape by pulling on the limb, because good intentions can convert a pressed vessel into a torn one.

Assessment begins before the patient is touched, because the scene and the mechanism are part of the examination. The lay first aider makes the scene safe, puts on personal protective equipment where available, counts the helpers, and notes how the injury happened and how long ago. The trained first responder then works through the primary survey in the order remembered as ABCDE, which stands for airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure. Circulation here means the search for hemorrhage, both the visible kind and the kind hidden inside the thigh, the chest, the belly and the pelvis, and the principle that orders the response is that life comes before limb. Exposure means undressing the patient enough to see the whole body, and it is followed at once by covering the patient again, because cold is a complication of trauma. Only when the patient is stable enough does the assessment turn to the limb, which is examined by looking, by feeling and by asking the patient to move it, never by forcing it.

What the responder finds at the limb is a set of observations rather than a diagnosis. The thigh may be swollen, the knee may look fuller than the other side, and the whole limb may lie in a shape the eye recognizes as wrong. A fracture that has not displaced may leave the limb looking almost normal, so the absence of deformity must never exclude this injury, and the inability to lift the leg against gravity may be the only evidence when consciousness is reduced. Pain is a finding and not merely a complaint, so the lay first aider eases it by supporting the limb, by reducing movement, by keeping the patient warm and by explaining what is happening. The trained responder may give a medicine for pain under a written protocol, while the substance, the route and the amount belong to the prescriber and the local protocol, and no dose, no route and no regimen appears here. Pain that increases despite immobilization is the finding that makes the decision urgent, because it may mean a compartment syndrome or an injury that has been missed.

Stabilization is a short list of acts performed in a deliberate order, and the first of them is restraint. A deformed limb is supported and splinted in the position in which it was found and is never straightened in the field, because the vessels and the nerves lie against the bone and because the pressure holding a torn vessel closed may be the only thing preventing further bleeding. Pulling the limb straight may also drive the sharp end of the bone through skin that was intact. The lay first aider supports the limb with the hands, with a pillow or with a rolled blanket, keeps the patient warm and still, and waits for help. Bleeding that can be controlled is controlled with a dressing, an embedded object is never removed, and exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, while wet clothing is replaced with dry covering, because a patient who is losing blood cannot afford to lose heat as well.

A knee that fills with blood deserves a word of its own. The capsule of the knee holds very little room, so even a modest amount of blood raises the pressure inside the joint and produces a knee that is tense, hot and extremely painful. Whether the joint should be aspirated is a decision for the clinician and the local protocol, because an aspiration performed in the wrong circumstances introduces infection into a joint that cannot afford it. What the responder records is the size of the swelling and whether it is increasing, because a knee that grows tighter over minutes is a different report from one that has looked the same since arrival. Nothing is bound tightly over the knee to reduce the swelling, and the limb is not elevated above the level of the heart when there is any question about the circulation.

The vessels behind the knee are the reason this fracture frightens orthopedic surgeons. The artery that supplies the leg below the knee passes through a tunnel behind the joint, tethered above by a fibrous opening in the muscles of the thigh and below by the muscle that forms the calf, so it has very little slack to give. A fragment that tilts backward, a knee that has also dislocated, and the swelling that follows either one can compress or tear that artery. The findings that suggest the artery is failing are a foot that is pale and cool, a pulse that cannot be felt and pain out of proportion to the injury, while numbness and tingling suggest that a nerve has been injured. A pulse that can still be felt does not exclude serious injury, so any of those findings means that the limb is immobilized in the position found and the patient is sent urgently to a facility where a surgeon can examine the vessels.

The neurovascular examination is performed before any intervention and repeated after every one, and its result is recorded, because a finding not written down before a splint was applied cannot be attributed afterwards. The examiner looks at the color of the foot and compares it with the other side, feels its temperature, and presses briefly on a nail to watch the color return. The pulses to be sought are the pulse on the top of the foot and the pulse behind the inner ankle, and each is compared with the uninjured limb. Sensation is tested in the space between the first and the second toe and on the outer side of the shin, and the patient is asked to pull the foot up, to push it down and to spread the toes. The trained first responder and the emergency medical technician perform this examination in the field, and the nurse and the physician repeat it in the hospital. No sign in it is perfectly reliable, because a patient who is unconscious, confused or in severe pain cannot cooperate, so the responder records what was found and says plainly what is doubtful.

Traction is a principle before it is a device. Traction means a gentle and continuous pull along the long axis of the limb, held steady for as long as it is needed, in order to overcome the pull of the muscles that have shortened the limb. Its purpose is not to set the fracture, and it is not a way of putting a dislocated joint back into place, because a pull applied to force bone ends into position can tear vessels, nerves and skin. What traction is meant to achieve is a limb held in better alignment, less movement at the fracture, less bleeding from the broken ends, less pain and a safer journey to hospital. It may be a temporary measure, a definitive treatment or a bridge to an operation, and which of those it is belongs to the clinician who plans the care.

Skin traction is the gentler of the two forms, and it works by attaching a device to the skin of the leg and letting a weight pull through it. The device may be straps fixed to the sides of the leg with adhesive, or a boot or a bandage or a manufactured splint that grips the limb, so the pull is transmitted through the skin rather than directly to bone. It is used for modest amounts of pull, for a limited period, and often while a patient waits for an operation. It is applied by a nurse, a physician or another clinician trained and authorized to apply it, according to the written protocol of the service, and never by the lay first aider. The risks are blisters, abrasions and pressure over the bony bumps on either side of the ankle, and a strap can also press on the nerve that winds around the small bone below the knee. Skin traction also slips, and a device that has slipped delivers less pull than the clinician intended while looking as though it is still working.

Skeletal traction is the more powerful form, and it places a metal pin through the bone so that the pull reaches the skeleton directly. The pin is usually placed through the lower end of the thigh bone itself or through the upper end of the shin bone below the knee, and the choice belongs to the orthopedic surgeon. It is used when a stronger and more sustained pull is needed, when skin traction has failed or would damage the skin, or when the limb must be held in line for weeks because no operation is available. Skeletal traction is applied only by a clinician trained and authorized to do it, in a clean environment and with sterile technique, and the anesthesia that makes it tolerable is a decision of the physician and the local protocol. The placement of the pin, the choice of the weight and the check of the pulses before and after belong to supervised practical training with a qualified instructor and never to a book. This course describes what the device is for and what can go wrong, and it stops there.

Because a pin passes through skin and bone, skeletal traction has complications of its own. The pin site can become infected, which is why it is cleaned, dressed and inspected according to the local protocol and why redness, discharge and new pain at the site are reported at once. The pin can loosen or migrate, the weight can be disturbed by a well meaning visitor or by a patient who slides down the bed, and the counter pull can be lost when the bed is moved. A pull that is too strong can hold the fragments apart rather than in line, can stretch a nerve, and can press on skin until it breaks down. The examination that accompanies traction is the same neurovascular examination performed at every other step, carried out before the device is applied, immediately after it is applied, and again after every adjustment of the weight. Increasing pain, a limb that changes shape, a weaker pulse, numbness, tingling, a pale or cool foot, and skin that breaks down under a strap stop the process at once, and the weight is never increased to overcome resistance, because force is not a technique.

Traction is the wrong choice in a set of situations, and in each of them the reason is a specific risk rather than a rule to be memorized. A suspected fracture of the pelvis is the clearest of them, because the device presses against the pelvis and can open an injury that was closed, and an injury to the knee or to the bones below the knee is another, because the pull and the pin pass through damaged structures. An open fracture with a wound along the line of the device, a partial amputation, and a fragment caught inside the joint are also reasons to leave the device off. A limb with a failing pulse or a numb foot is not treated with traction by a responder, because that patient needs the surgeon and the operating room. Traction is not applied when the diagnosis is unclear or when the responder has not been trained and authorized to apply it. Traction is never used to pull a deformed limb straight in the field, and it is never a substitute for immobilization or for the images the clinician needs, so in those situations the limb is immobilized in the position found and the reason is explained to the receiving team.

Immobilization means preventing movement of the injured part and of the joints above and below it, and this fracture lies close enough to the knee that the knee itself must be included. The device supports the limb from the upper thigh to the ankle, it is padded where it presses over bone, which at this level means the bony bumps at the knee, the small bone below the knee and the ankle, and it is secured firmly enough to hold without stopping the circulation. The knee is splinted in the position in which it was found and is never forced straight, because the vessels and the nerves that lie behind the joint are at risk from any movement. The material matters less than the principle, so a padded board, a pillow bound in place, a manufactured splint or the uninjured leg bound alongside with padding between the knees can each serve. The trained first responder and the emergency medical technician apply splints within the procedures their service authorizes, while the physician and the orthopedic surgeon decide what definitive immobilization is required, and casting and surgical fixation are learned under supervision.

Imaging is the step that confirms what the examination has already suggested, and it is ordered and interpreted by a clinician trained to do so. A radiograph is usually the first study, and it is taken in at least two directions so that a displacement in the third direction is not hidden, with the hip and the knee included so that a second injury at another level is not missed. Computed tomography is used for this fracture more often than for most, because the break between the condyles and the surface of the joint is frequently more comminuted than the first radiograph suggests. Magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for soft tissues, and at the knee it can show injury to the ligaments and to the cartilage that the radiograph does not reveal. In a child the growth region at the lower end of the thigh bone is difficult to assess, and the clinician may ask for images of the other leg for comparison, because an image is a picture of one moment.

Reassessment is performed after every intervention, and it is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the record, because it is the comparison that carries the information and not any single measurement. The limb is checked for color, warmth, both pulses, sensation, movement and pain, the splint or the traction device is checked for tightness, and the skin is inspected wherever a strap or a pin rests against it. Pain that increases instead of settling, numbness, tingling, weakness, a foot that becomes pale or cool, and a limb that grows tense and swollen are the findings that make the responder stop and escalate. A responder who suspects a compartment syndrome does not wait for the diagnosis to be confirmed, acts within the local protocol, which may allow a constricting bandage or splint to be released by someone authorized to do it, keeps the limb at the level of the heart rather than above it, keeps the patient warm and refers the patient urgently.

Referral or definitive treatment is the final step, and for this fracture it usually means transfer to a facility with an operating room, imaging, a supply of blood and an intensive care unit. The orthopedic surgeon with the trauma team decides what is done and when, and the patient must be stable enough to survive an operation. An open fracture at this level is an emergency of its own kind, because the wound is contaminated from the moment of injury whether it looks clean or not. The lay first aider covers such a wound with a sterile dressing and the trained responder controls the bleeding that can be controlled and immobilizes the limb in the position found, while definitive closure belongs to the operating room, because neither explores the wound blindly or pushes exposed bone back into it. A plate and screws applied to the side of the bone hold the condyles together and reattach them to the shaft, and a metal nail passed upward through the knee into the canal of the bone can hold the same fracture from within. A frame outside the limb, with pins through the skin, is used when the soft tissues are badly damaged, and in an older patient the surgeon may replace the knee joint and use the stem of the replacement to hold the fracture.

Not every fracture of the lower thigh bone is treated with an operation, and where surgery is not available or not safe, traction may be the definitive treatment rather than a bridge to something else. A fracture that has not displaced, a patient who could not survive an operation and a service without an orthopedic operating room are all situations in which a clinician may hold the limb in line with a continuous pull for weeks. That choice belongs to the orthopedic surgeon, and the pull is maintained and adjusted only by those trained and authorized to do it. The goal is a limb of the right length, in line, with a knee surface able to bear weight and a knee that does not become stiff while the bone heals. Stiffness of the knee is in fact the disability that follows this fracture most often, even when the bone has been repaired well, because the joint was filled with blood, the muscles of the thigh were injured and the patient was asked to keep the limb still. A knee that becomes hot and painful weeks later may be infected, while a swollen and painful calf may hold a clot in a deep vein, so both findings are reported to the clinician, while the rehabilitation program that restores movement is designed by the treating team with a physical therapist.

The injury behaves differently at the two ends of life. In a child the growth region at the lower end of the thigh bone is the most active in the body, and an injury that crosses it can slow the growth of the leg, stop it altogether, or produce an angulation that worsens with every year the child grows. A fracture at this level in a child is the business of an orthopedic surgeon from the first hour, even when the radiograph looks modest. A child who refuses to walk after a fall, whose knee is swollen or whose leg hurts when it is moved should be assumed to have a significant injury until a clinician decides otherwise. The older adult brings a different set of problems to the same fracture, because the bone is thinner, so a smaller force is enough and the mechanism may sound trivial. Such a patient may carry a knee replacement, may take a medicine that thins the bone or prevents clotting, and may live alone. The fracture may hurt far less than its appearance suggests, so the threshold for immobilization and for referral is lower in this group, and the decisions about how quickly an operation is performed belong to the treating team and to the local protocol.

The time of the injury, the mechanism, the position in which the limb was found, the device used, the name of the person who applied it, the pull that was set and the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention are written or spoken in a fixed order. It is worth saying aloud that the limb was not straightened and that no object was removed, because the absence of an act is as important to record as its performance. Because the energy that breaks a lower thigh bone often passes through the trunk, the spine is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has done so, and no lone manual handling technique is safe. Transport is decided by the service, and the destination is chosen for the care the patient needs rather than for the distance, because a hospital without orthopedic surgery is a stop on the way rather than a destination, and during transport the same neurovascular observations are repeated so that any change is reported before arrival.

The levels of practice named in this chapter are easy to keep in order, because every act described here has an owner. The lay first aider supports the limb in the position found, controls the bleeding that can be controlled with a dressing, keeps the patient warm and still, calls for help, and observes color, warmth, movement and feeling, but never applies traction and never straightens the limb. The trained first responder applies splints, performs the neurovascular examination and records it, and works within the equipment and the procedures the service authorizes. The emergency medical technician and the paramedic work within a broader protocol, may apply a traction device where the service trains and permits it, and may give a medicine for pain only under a written protocol. The nurse performs the neurovascular examination, checks the skin beneath straps and pins, maintains the traction the clinician has ordered and reports any change at once. The physician examines the patient, orders and interprets the images, decides about pain relief and about the referral, and performs a reduction only where training, equipment and protocol allow it, while the orthopedic surgeon decides about skeletal traction and about the operation.

To summarize, a fracture of the lower thigh bone is an injury of the knee as much as of the thigh, because the condyles lie inside the joint and because the vessels and the nerves of the leg pass immediately behind the bone. What to look for is a swollen thigh, a knee that is filling with blood, a limb that is shortened or bent, and any change in the pulses, the color, the warmth, the sensation or the movement of the foot. What to do is to follow the order every chapter of this course follows, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. What never to do is to straighten a deformed limb in the field, to remove an embedded object, to push exposed bone back into a wound, to explore an open wound blindly, or to attempt traction, reduction or casting without training and authorization. Traction is a gentle continuous pull that holds the limb in line and reduces bleeding, pain and movement, it is not a way to set a fracture, and it is avoided in a suspected pelvic fracture, in an injured knee and in a limb whose pulse is failing. A knee that tightens, a foot that pales and pain that increases instead of settling are reported at once, and who to call is decided by the service and the local protocol.
