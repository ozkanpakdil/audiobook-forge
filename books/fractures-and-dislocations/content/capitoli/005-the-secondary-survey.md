# Chapter 5 — The Secondary Survey

The secondary survey is the examination that begins once the primary survey has been completed and every problem it found has been treated, and it is the step in which the whole patient is looked at from the top of the head to the soles of the feet in a fixed order. Its purpose is to find the injuries that do not kill within minutes and would otherwise be missed, and it is where most fractures and most dislocations are found. The sequence followed in every chapter of this course is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and the secondary survey is the part of that sequence in which an injury of a limb is properly assessed. It is not performed on a patient who is still unstable, because such a patient is treated again from the beginning of the primary survey until the airway, the breathing and the circulation are controlled. This chapter is educational material for health professionals, students and trained responders, and it does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

The survey starts with the history of the event rather than with the hands, because what happened decides what the examiner looks for and how urgently the patient must be moved. The history begins with the time of the injury, which is written as a clock time rather than as an interval remembered afterwards, and with the position in which the patient was found, because a patient who has lain in one place may have a pressure injury as well as a fracture. The mechanism follows next, and a fall from a height, a collision at speed, a twisting injury of a planted foot, a direct blow and a fall from standing height suggest different patterns of injury that the examiner must consider. The patient is asked what happened in the patient's own words, and those words are repeated at the handover rather than replaced by the examiner's summary, because a patient who remembers striking the head is telling the listener something that no examination of a limb will reveal.

The medical history is gathered at the same time, and it changes what the findings mean rather than merely adding detail. A patient whose bones are weakened by disease, by a long illness or by a medicine taken over years may break a bone in a fall that would leave a healthy person unhurt. A patient who takes a medicine that thins the blood may continue to bleed from a place that looks trivial, and a patient whose heart rate is held down by a medicine may not show the rapid pulse that would otherwise be expected. The names of such medicines, the amounts and the reasons for taking them belong to the prescriber and to the local protocol, and this course never teaches a listener which substance to give or how much of it to give. The history is also imperfect in predictable ways, because a patient with an injury of the head may repeat the same answer, a patient who has been drinking may deny pain, and a frightened child may say nothing at all. The clinician weighs the history against the examination and treats an unreliable account as a reason to look more carefully rather than as a reason to relax.

The survey needs a patient who can actually be seen, so clothing is removed or cut away where the service authorizes it, and the part that has been examined is covered again before the next part is uncovered. The lay first aider does not undress a patient but keeps the patient warm, covers what is exposed and stays within the acts that need no equipment, while the trained responder, the nurse and the physician expose the body as the local protocol allows. Every part is looked at, including the back, the armpits, the groin and the skin between the buttocks, because a wound in a hidden place is a wound that is never treated and an injury that is never counted. The pull is in two directions at once, since the patient must be exposed to be examined and must be covered to be kept warm, and cold is a complication of trauma. A patient loses heat quickly on a wet road, a cold floor or a metal tailgate, so wet clothing is replaced with dry covering, the patient is insulated from the ground, and the ambulance or the room is warmed where that can be done.

The back cannot be examined while the patient is lying on it, so the patient is turned in one movement by a team, a maneuver usually called a log roll, with one person at the head holding the neck and the shoulders and directing the others. Such a turn is performed only by a team trained and equipped for it and according to the local protocol, because an injury of the spine is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has done so, and no lone manual handling technique is safe. The purpose of the turn is to look at and to feel along the back, to note any wound, bruising, swelling or step, and to record what is found before the patient is turned back. The turn is not a test of the spine, and the examiner does not press, twist or bounce the patient to see whether it hurts, because a test that moves an unstable segment can convert a survivable injury into a permanent one. A collar, a board or a vacuum mattress is applied where the service provides it and the local protocol requires it, and the same team coordinates the next move as carefully as the first. Where a service has no trained team, the patient is kept still in the position found and help is called for from one that has, and waiting is the safer choice.

The examination then follows the body from above downward, and the order is spoken as it is performed so that nothing is forgotten. The head and the face are examined for wounds, swelling, blood behind the eardrum, bruising around the eyes or behind the ear, and a nose or jaw that has moved, and the pupils are compared with each other more than once. The neck is examined for tenderness, swelling and a step, and it is examined again when the collar is removed by the clinician who is qualified to clear the spine. The chest is watched while it moves, felt for tenderness and for the crackle of air under the skin, and the abdomen is felt gently for tenderness, firmness and distension. The pelvis is pressed once and gently by a clinician, never repeatedly and never to discover how much it hurts, and the back, the buttocks and the perineum are examined during the turn. Every limb is then examined in turn, and findings are described as findings, so the record says what was seen, felt and heard rather than naming a diagnosis the examiner is not qualified to make.

A fracture announces itself through a small set of findings, and none of them is present in every patient, so the examiner gathers them together rather than trusting any one of them alone. The limb may be deformed, which means that its shape differs from the shape of the other side, and it may be shortened when the broken ends have slid over one another. Swelling and bruising appear over the injury and often spread along the limb, and tenderness is present when the bone is pressed gently at a point away from the wound, though the pressure is never applied so firmly that it tests the bone. Movement that is not possible at a joint, or movement in a direction the joint does not normally permit, is a finding that stops the examination of that part and is not reproduced deliberately. A crackling sensation under the fingers, called crepitus, may be felt when the broken ends move against each other, and it is never sought on purpose, because seeking it moves the fracture and adds pain and damage. Shortening, rotation and an open wound each carry more weight than tenderness, and the finding that most changes what happens next is a wound near the fracture, because an open injury needs a different plan.

Several of these findings are unreliable in particular patients, and the examiner who knows this will miss fewer injuries. A child may have a fracture of a bone that bends without breaking and may move the limb almost normally between bouts of crying, and a child who was alert and then becomes quiet is getting worse rather than better. An older adult may break a hip or a wrist in a fall from standing height with little pain and no visible deformity, and the absence of pain is not evidence that the bone is intact. A patient who has been drinking, a patient with an injury of the head and a patient who has received a medicine for pain may report no pain while a serious injury is present, and a frightened patient may deny pain in order to avoid being moved. For all of these reasons the threshold for immobilizing a limb and for referring a patient is lowered rather than raised when the story and the examination do not agree.

Examining a limb without moving it is a discipline rather than a technique, and it rests on three acts that are done in the same order every time. The examiner looks first at the limb as it lies, comparing it with the other side for shape, length, rotation, swelling, bruising and wounds, and at the skin beyond the injury for color and for breaks. The examiner then feels with the fingertips, keeping one hand under the limb to take its weight and sliding the other hand along the surface rather than pressing on the bone, and the patient is asked to say when the touch becomes painful. The examiner asks the patient to move the fingers or the toes and to say what can be felt on the skin, and the answer is recorded in the words the patient uses rather than in the words of the examiner. At no point is the limb straightened, pulled, rotated, lifted by the far end or tested for stability, because the vessels and the nerves of a limb run along the surface of the bone and lie exactly where the displaced end of the bone is pointing. Testing the stability of a joint by pulling on it is a clinical skill learned under supervision, and it is not a field test. The lay first aider supports the limb where it lies and reports what can be seen, while the formal examination of the joint belongs to the trained responder or to the clinician.

A wound near a fracture is examined by looking at it rather than by exploring it, and the rules about open injuries are short and absolute. The wound is covered with a sterile dressing or with the cleanest covering available, and the bleeding that can be controlled is controlled by pressure held over the dressing rather than by lifting the dressing to look underneath. An embedded object is left exactly where it lies and is stabilized in place, because it may be pressing on a torn vessel, and exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound for the same reason. A wound is never explored blindly with a finger or an instrument, since the exploration pushes contamination deeper, tears what remains intact and starts bleeding that was not there before. The neurovascular status is recorded before the dressing is applied and again afterwards, and then the limb is immobilized and the patient is referred urgently. The lay first aider covers the wound and presses on the bleeding, and the trained responder does the same within the equipment the service provides, while the cleaning, the removal of dead tissue and the definitive closure of an open fracture belong to the operating room and to the surgeon who works there.

The neurovascular status of each limb is examined during the secondary survey and examined again after every intervention, and the result is written before a splint is applied and after it is applied. The examiner compares the two sides for color and warmth, feels for a pulse beyond the injury, presses briefly on a nail to watch the color return, and asks the patient to move the fingers or the toes and to describe what can be felt. A pulse that is present does not exclude a serious injury, because the pressure inside a compartment can rise until the smallest vessels have stopped carrying blood while a large pulse at the wrist or the ankle is still felt. A pulse that is absent is an emergency, and so is a limb that has become pale, cold, painful out of proportion to the injury or unable to move. The formal examination belongs to the trained responder or to the clinician, and the lay first aider observes the color, the warmth and the ability of the patient to move and to feel and reports anything that has changed. The result is spoken at the handover as well as written, because a finding that was not recorded before an intervention cannot be attributed afterwards, and the record protects the patient and the responder alike.

Stabilization during the secondary survey means treating each problem as soon as it is found and keeping the patient in a condition in which the rest of the examination can be done at all. Bleeding that can be reached is pressed upon, a wound is covered, a limb is supported in the position in which it was found, and the patient is kept warm and quiet while the survey continues. If a problem appears that threatens the airway, the breathing or the circulation, the secondary survey stops where it is and the primary survey is repeated from the beginning, because an intervention or a new injury can change a finding that was normal a few minutes earlier. The lay first aider stabilizes by holding, by covering and by reassuring, and calls for the level of help that can do more, while the trained responder acts within the equipment, the procedures and the authorization the service provides. A fluid, a medicine for pain or an antibiotic is given only by someone the local protocol authorizes to give it, and the substance and the amount belong to the prescriber rather than to this course. Nothing in the secondary survey is worth a life, so the survey is interrupted as often as the condition of the patient requires.

Immobilization follows the examination and comes before any move of the patient, and it means preventing movement of the injured part and of the joint above it and the joint below it. A bone that is splinted on its own can still be moved by the joints at either end, so a splint reaches past both joints and holds the fracture still. The splint is padded where it presses on bone, it is applied over the position in which the limb was found, and it is secured firmly enough to hold the limb without becoming a band that stops the circulation. The material matters less than the principle, since a folded magazine, a padded board, a rolled blanket or a sling can each serve, and a device made by hand is checked more often than a manufactured one because it shifts. The trained responder and the paramedic apply splints within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, the nurse applies and checks those the ward authorizes, and the physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides what definitive immobilization is needed. Casting and surgical fixation are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and neither of them is taught from a book in this course. If the limb becomes more painful, more swollen, more pale or more numb after the splint is applied, the responder stops, seeks advice and prepares to release what the protocol allows.

Imaging comes after the examination and after immobilization, and it confirms what the examination has already suggested rather than replacing it. A radiograph is usually the first study, and it is taken in at least two directions so that a displacement in the third direction is not hidden, while the joint above the injury and the joint below it are included so that a second injury is not missed. Computed tomography shows the detail of a complex break, particularly where it enters a joint, and magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for the soft tissues, including ligaments, cartilage, tendons and muscle. Each study is requested when the answer it will give changes what is done next, and the decision belongs to the clinician who is trained to interpret the images and who is responsible for the care of the patient. The lay first aider never orders or interprets an image, and the trained responder does not decide which study is needed, although both may describe what they saw and felt before the image was taken. An image is a picture of one moment and it says nothing about how the limb is now, so the patient is examined and reassessed after every study and after every journey to and from the imaging room.

Reassessment is performed after every intervention, and it is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment rather than by the injury. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the record, and the comparison carries more information than any single measurement taken on its own. The limb is checked for color, warmth, pulse, sensation, movement and pain, the splint is checked for tightness and for the pressure it makes, and a dressing is checked for blood that has come through it. Pain that increases instead of settling, pain that is out of proportion to the injury, numbness, tingling, weakness and a limb that becomes tense, swollen and hard are the findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation. A responder who suspects that the pressure inside a compartment has risen does not wait for a confirmation that will arrive too late, because time is muscle, and the limb is not elevated above the level of the heart. The constricting bandage, splint or cast is released only by someone the local protocol authorizes to release it, and the release is followed by a further examination and by a further record. A patient whose condition has changed in any way is examined again from the beginning of the sequence, because a change is a finding in itself.

The last step of the sequence is to refer the patient or to treat the injury definitively, and it begins with the recognition that many injuries cannot be managed where the patient lies. A patient with an open fracture, a suspected compartment syndrome, a dislocation, a damaged nerve or vessel, a fracture that enters a joint, a fracture of the pelvis or a fracture in a child that crosses the growing part of the bone is referred to a facility with imaging, an operating room and an intensive care unit. Definitive treatment includes reduction, which restores the position of the bone ends, traction, which holds them in line with a gentle continuous pull, and casting or surgical fixation, which holds the position while the bone heals, and each of those acts is learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol. A reduction is not attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when the vessels or the nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, when the training or the equipment is lacking, or when an earlier attempt has failed, because in each of those situations an attempt can convert an injury that would have healed into one that cannot. The physician decides what must be done in the emergency department, the orthopedic surgeon decides what must be done in the operating room, and the nurse keeps the record and the handover in order. Force is not a technique, and a responder who meets resistance or who sees the condition of the patient change stops at once and calls for the level of care that can take over.

The record is part of the treatment rather than a formality that follows it, and the secondary survey is where most of it is written. Each finding is written before the next intervention rather than reconstructed afterwards, because the order of events is what tells the reader whether a limb was already damaged before the splint was applied. The time of the injury, the mechanism, the position in which the patient and the limb were found, the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention and any change in the condition of the patient are the facts a receiving team needs, and they are the facts most often lost. The words the patient uses about pain and about numbness are quoted rather than translated, since a patient who says that the foot has gone to sleep is describing a different thing from a patient who says that it hurts. Where a number is written down, such as a pulse of 110 beats each minute, that number is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between services, countries and editions. The same caution applies to any healing time that a service teaches, since 8 to 12 weeks in one hospital is a different estimate in another and must be checked against the guideline in use where you work. Sometimes the patient must be moved before the survey is finished, and the parts that were not examined are then named in the handover as unexamined rather than left to be understood as normal.

A short constructed illustration may help to fix the order in the mind, and it is an illustration rather than the record of any real patient. A cyclist is brought to a small hospital after striking a wall, and the primary survey has found no immediate threat, so the secondary survey begins with the history of the event and with the account of a witness. The patient says that the right shoulder hurts and that the left hand is numb, and the record notes those words as they were spoken. The right arm is looked at where it lies and supported with a hand under the elbow, and the examiner sees swelling and a change in the shape of the upper arm and feels the pulse at the wrist before and after a splint is applied by the trained responder with the equipment the service provides. The left hand is pale and cooler than the right, and the strength of the grip has changed, so the clinician is called at once and the patient is referred to a facility where the vessels can be examined and repaired. The chest is watched while the patient is undressed and kept warm, the abdomen is felt gently, the pelvis is pressed once by the clinician, and the back is examined during a turn performed by the team. Nothing was straightened, nothing was explored, and every finding was written down before the next step was taken.

To summarize, the secondary survey is the head to toe examination that follows the primary survey, and it is where the fractures and the dislocations that do not kill in the first minutes are found. It begins with the history of the event, it exposes the whole patient while keeping the patient warm, and it turns the patient with a trained team when the back must be examined, because an injury of the spine is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has done so. The limb is examined where it lies by looking, by feeling gently and by asking the patient to move and to feel, and it is never straightened, pulled, rotated or tested for stability, since the vessels and the nerves run along the surface of the bone where the displaced end is pointing. The neurovascular status is examined before any intervention and repeated after every one, and a limb that becomes pale, cold, tense or painful out of proportion to the injury stops the survey and is escalated at once, because a compartment syndrome is a clinical diagnosis and time is muscle. A wound near a fracture is covered with a sterile dressing and never explored, an embedded object is never removed, exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, and definitive closure belongs to the operating room and to nobody else. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, a reduction is not attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when the vessels or the nerves are failing, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, when the training or the equipment is lacking, or when an earlier attempt has failed, and no dose, no route and no drug regimen is given anywhere in this course. The provider level is named for every act, every number heard here is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and your local protocol, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.
