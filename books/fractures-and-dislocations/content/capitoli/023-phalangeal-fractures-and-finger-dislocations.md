# Chapter 23 — Phalangeal Fractures and Finger Dislocations

A finger that is fractured or dislocated is the injury most often called minor at the moment it is seen, and that habit is what costs hands. The phalanges are the small bones of the fingers, and the joints between them carry a wide range of movement through a very small space, so a finger left slightly out of line can leave a hand that will not close around a cup or a tool. This chapter follows the order used everywhere in this course, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. It is educational material for health professionals, students and trained responders, and it does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

The fingers are built from phalanges, and the thumb has two while each of the other four fingers has three. The bone nearest the hand is the proximal phalanx, the one beyond it is the middle phalanx, and the one at the tip is the distal phalanx, and each has a base facing the hand, a shaft, and a head that carries the joint surface. The joints are named from the bones they join, so the metacarpophalangeal joint lies between the metacarpal bone of the hand and the proximal phalanx, the proximal interphalangeal joint lies between the two phalanges nearest the hand, and the distal interphalangeal joint lies between the two furthest from it. In the thumb the corresponding joint is called simply the interphalangeal joint, and a clinician locates an injury by naming the bone and the joint in that way, so the record uses the same words and the next examiner finds the same place.

Such small bones deserve a chapter of their own because the hand heals by stiffening. Swelling collects in the loose tissue around the joints and tendons, the ligaments and the joint capsule shorten while the hand rests, and the fine gliding that lets a tendon draw a finger through its full arc is lost once that tissue becomes thick and stuck. The window in which this stiffness can still be turned back is measured in days rather than months, and a figure used in teaching, such as 3 weeks of rest for a joint, is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because services and editions differ. Early care therefore aims at protected movement rather than perfect stillness, and the clinician chooses the position that keeps the ligaments and joint surfaces under the least tension while the responder keeps the hand in the position found until that decision is made.

The mechanism of injury is the first clue and often the only clue available before anything can be seen. A direct blow crushes a finger between two hard objects, a fall onto the outstretched hand drives a finger backward at a joint, and a twisting force passed through a ball or a leash bends and rotates it at the same time. The injury patients most often dismiss is the jammed finger, which was bent backward in sport and now hurts and swells, because that same description covers a sprain that will settle and a fracture or dislocation that will not. Age matters as well, since the growing end of a phalanx in a child can be injured without the shaft breaking, so a child who will not use a hand after a fall deserves the same caution as an adult with an obvious deformity.

Assessment begins with the scene and with the patient rather than with the finger, so the responder makes sure the area is safe, notes the time of the injury, and asks what happened, what the patient felt at the moment of impact, whether the hand has been used since, and what has already been done to it. A hand injury can accompany a fall from a height, a road crash or a fight, so the primary survey is completed first, and its steps are remembered as ABCDE, meaning airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure. Only when the patient is stable enough does attention turn to the hand, because life comes before limb and a finger can wait for minutes that a brain and a heart cannot. The lay first aider observes and supports, the trained responder assesses within the procedures the service authorizes, and the diagnosis belongs to the clinician.

The first part of the local examination is looking, and the responder looks at the whole hand rather than the sore finger alone, with both hands in view at once so that the injured side can be compared with the uninjured one, because swelling crosses the spaces between the fingers quickly and a second injury is easily missed. Swelling, bruising, a wound, a torn nail, blood beneath a nail and any unnatural bend are all recorded. The most important observation in the hand is rotation, which becomes visible when the patient bends the fingers gently toward the palm, because instead of lying side by side one fingertip crosses over or under its neighbor. That observation is unreliable in a patient who cannot move the fingers because of pain, so its absence proves nothing and an examiner who cannot see it does not conclude that rotation is absent. A finger with a wound over a joint, with exposed bone, or with a nail driven into the nail bed is looked at, covered with a sterile dressing and left alone, because it is not examined by probing.

Feeling comes next, and it is done lightly along the bone and around the joints, comparing the two hands as the examination goes. The responder palpates the length of each phalanx on its sides and along its back, feeling for a step, a gap, a sharply localized tender point and the joint lines above and below the injury. A crackling sensation beneath the fingers, which is called crepitus, suggests that the bone ends are moving against one another, and it is a reason for gentleness rather than for repeated testing. Tenderness directly over a joint line suggests that the joint has been injured even when the bone feels whole, and the examiner also feels the temperature of the finger and the tightness of its skin, because a finger that becomes tense and shiny is describing pressure inside a closed space.

Movement is tested gently and by the patient, because the responder does not move an injured finger through a range that causes pain or meets resistance. The patient is asked to bend and to straighten each finger as far as comfort allows, and the difference between the two hands is noted rather than a measurement. A finger that will not straighten at all may have an injured tendon rather than an injured bone, and a finger that will not bend may have a dislocation or a fragment caught in the joint. Testing a finger against resistance, or pulling on it, is a clinical skill learned under supervision and is not a field test, so the responder does not do it. The record states what the patient could and could not do at that moment, in the patient's own words as far as possible, because the ability to move may change after any intervention.

The neurovascular examination is performed before any intervention and repeated after it, and its result is written down, because a finding that was not recorded before a splint was applied cannot be attributed afterwards. Each finger carries two digital arteries and two digital nerves, one along each side, and they lie close to the bone, so an injury that displaces the bone puts them at risk. The examiner looks at the color of the finger and of the nail bed, feels the warmth of the skin against the other hand, and presses briefly on the nail to watch the color return, which is called capillary refill. Sensation is tested on both sides of each finger, and the patient is asked to report numbness or tingling in ordinary words, since the pulse of a single finger is often too small to feel reliably and its absence alone proves nothing. The lay first aider observes color, warmth and the ability to feel and to move, the trained responder and the clinician perform the formal examination, and every result is recorded before and after each intervention without exception.

Stabilization of an injured hand is a small set of acts, and the first of them is restraint. A deformed finger is supported in the position in which it was found and is never straightened in the field, because the digital vessels and nerves lie against the bone and a movement that looks helpful can tear what remained intact. Pulling a finger straight can also turn a closed injury into an open one by pushing a sharp bone end through skin that had not yet been torn, so the lay first aider supports the hand with a pillow or a soft roll of cloth, keeps the arm against the body and waits for help. The trained responder and the paramedic may splint the hand within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, examining the neurovascular status before and after they do it. Cold is a complication of trauma rather than a detail of comfort, so the hand is insulated, wet clothing is replaced with covering, and a ring that threatens to become tight is dealt with early within the local protocol instead of by force.

Immobilization of a finger has two aims at once, which are to hold the injured part still enough to heal and to keep the joints that are not injured moving enough to avoid stiffness. Buddy strapping binds the injured finger to an uninjured neighbor with tape or with a manufactured strap, so that the healthy finger acts as a splint, and it is used for stable fractures of the middle phalanx and for some injuries of the distal phalanx. It may be applied by a trained first responder, an emergency medical technician, a paramedic or a nurse, and only to an injury assessed as stable, because strapping a rotated or unstable finger in place conceals the problem and lets it set in the wrong position. The strap is placed around the middle of the finger rather than over a joint, it leaves the tip visible so that color, warmth and sensation can be checked, and it is never wrapped so tightly that it stops the circulation. A padded splint or a custom splint may be applied by those authorized to use one, and the position chosen for a hand that must be rested is the position of function, the relaxed posture in which the wrist is slightly extended, the knuckles are bent and the fingers curve as though around a small ball, because there the ligaments along the sides of the joints are at their longest and a hand held in that posture loses less movement than a hand held flat.

Some finger injuries look small and are not small, and the open injury over a joint is the clearest example. A wound that lies over the metacarpophalangeal joint, or over any interphalangeal joint, is treated as an open injury until a clinician decides otherwise, because a joint entered by a wound is contaminated even when the wound is small. The constructed illustration is the hand of someone who has struck another person in the mouth, where a small wound over a knuckle opens the joint beneath. The responder covers the wound with a sterile dressing, controls the bleeding that can be controlled, records the neurovascular status, immobilizes the hand in the position found and arranges urgent transfer, while the wound is never explored blindly, an embedded object is never removed, and exposed bone is never pushed back into the wound. Protection against tetanus and the decision about antibiotics belong to the prescriber and to the local protocol, so no substance, no amount and no route is given in this course, and definitive cleaning and closure belong to an operating room and to nobody else.

Not every phalangeal fracture behaves the same way, and the purpose of the responder is not to classify them but to recognize which ones have stopped being minor. A fracture of the shaft that runs as a long spiral is unstable because the two ends can rotate around one another, and rotation is the displacement that a splint cannot control and that only the eye will catch. A fracture that enters a joint, which is called an intra-articular fracture, can leave a step in the joint surface that wears the cartilage away and limits movement for years. A fracture at the base of the thumb, a fracture of the distal phalanx that has a wound, and a fracture beneath a nail that has torn the nail bed are injuries that need imaging and a clinical decision rather than a bandage and reassurance. A sign that is taught as reliable may be missing in a patient whose bone is weakened by disease or by long use of a medicine that thins it, so a lower threshold for immobilization and referral takes the place of a confident diagnosis.

A dislocation of a finger is a complete loss of contact between the joint surfaces, and a subluxation is a partial loss of contact, and both tear the capsule and at least one ligament of the joint. The joint most often dislocated in the hand is the proximal interphalangeal joint, the middle joint of a finger, and it usually comes apart toward the back of the hand when the finger is bent backward. The finger looks shortened, it is bent at an angle, and the patient holds it stiffly away from the others, while the skin over the joint may be tight because the bone end is pressing against it from within. The joint at the base of the thumb is dislocated far less often but is more serious, because the ligaments that steady the thumb are large and the bone ends can become trapped between the tendon and the skin. Any dislocation of a finger is referred to a clinician, because the decision about how and when to restore the joint belongs to someone trained and equipped to make it.

The rule that a deformed limb is never straightened in the field applies to the finger as firmly as to the thigh, and it is the rule most often broken in the hand because a finger looks easy to pull straight. Force is not a technique, and a finger pulled on by someone without training can be turned from a dislocation that could be reduced gently into one that cannot be reduced at all, because a fragment of bone or a tendon can become caught between the surfaces. A reduction is not attempted when the injury is open, when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught within the joint, when the vessels or nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the diagnosis is unclear, when the responder lacks the training or the equipment, or when an earlier attempt has already failed. Each of those situations rests on the same reason, which is that the attempt can add an injury that the receiving clinician then has to repair, and the responder stops at the first sign of resistance or of a new complication. Describing what a reduction is meant to achieve and what can go wrong is the work of this chapter, while the technique itself belongs to supervised clinical training and to local protocol.

Imaging is the step that confirms what the examination has already suggested, and it is ordered and interpreted by a clinician trained to do it. A radiograph is usually the first study, and it is taken in at least two directions so that a displacement in the third direction is not hidden, with the joint above and the joint below the injury included so that a second injury is not missed. The hand is difficult to image because its bones overlap when the fingers lie together, so the clinician may ask for views taken with the fingers separated and for the request to state which finger and which joint are in question. Computed tomography shows the detail of a complex break that enters a joint and helps the surgeon decide how to treat it, while magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for soft tissues such as ligaments, tendons and cartilage, and neither study is ordered routinely. The lay first aider never orders or interprets imaging and the trained responder does not choose which study is needed, because that decision belongs to the clinician, and an image is only a picture of one moment.

Reassessment is performed after every intervention, and it is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the record, so the finger is checked for color, warmth, capillary refill, sensation, movement and pain and the strap or splint is checked for tightness. Pain that increases instead of settling, pain out of proportion to the injury, numbness, tingling, weakness, a finger that becomes cold and pale and a finger that becomes tense and shiny are the findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation. A bandage, a strap or a splint that has become constricting is released by someone authorized to do so within the limits of the local protocol, because waiting for a formal diagnosis wastes the time in which muscle and nerve can still be saved. The hand is not elevated above the level of the heart when a compartment syndrome is suspected, the patient is kept warm and referred urgently, and a ring that fitted in the morning can become a tourniquet by evening.

A compartment syndrome can occur in the hand, and although it is uncommon it is the complication this course fears most, because it is caused by the treatment as often as by the injury. The hand is divided into compartments bounded by fascia, and the small muscles within them are packed tightly between the bones, so bleeding or swelling raises the pressure against structures that have almost no room to expand. The earliest and most dependable finding is pain that is greater than the injury explains and that increases when the fingers are moved, while the later findings of pallor, coolness, an absent pulse, numbness and paralysis appear only once the damage is advanced. An intact pulse does not exclude the diagnosis, because the pressure that stops the flow through the smallest vessels is well below the pressure that would stop a pulse at the wrist. The constricting bandage, strap or splint is released within the limits of the local protocol, the hand is not elevated above the level of the heart, and the patient is kept warm and transferred urgently to a facility where the compartments can be opened if that becomes necessary.

Referral or definitive treatment is the last step of the sequence, and it begins with the recognition that some finger injuries cannot be managed where the patient is sitting. A patient is referred to a facility with imaging, an operating room and, when the injury is severe, an intensive care unit, and the reasons include an open injury, a joint that has been entered, a finger that is rotated or scissored, a dislocation that is present or suspected, an unstable or comminuted fracture, an injury of the thumb, more than one finger injured in the same hand, an injured tendon or nerve, a neurovascular status that is worsening, and a child with an injury near the growing end of the bone. A crushed fingertip, an amputation at any level and any hand in which the blood supply of a finger is in doubt are transferred as emergencies rather than booked for a later appointment. A patient whose pain is increasing, whose finger is swelling inside a splint or whose sensation is changing is referred without waiting for the next clinic day. When the responder is unsure whether an injury is minor, arranging review is safer than offering reassurance, because a finger watched for a week has often lost movement it will never regain.

Definitive treatment of a phalangeal fracture or a finger dislocation begins with a decision about what the finger will have to do once it has healed. Reduction restores the position of the bone ends or of the joint surfaces, traction holds them in line with a gentle continuous pull, and casting or splinting holds the position while healing proceeds, and each of those acts is learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol. A surgeon may hold the fragments with fine wires, with screws or with small plates, an operation called open reduction and internal fixation, and a dislocation that cannot be brought back by gentle means needs an operation to remove whatever is caught between the surfaces. The purpose of all these treatments is to restore alignment, to preserve the joint surface and to allow movement as early as the tissues permit, and the complication that threatens that purpose is stiffness rather than an imperfect line. A hand surgeon or an orthopedic surgeon decides what is needed, and none of the acts named in this paragraph is taught by this course or may be attempted by a listener who has not been trained and authorized to perform it.

The work of recovery after a hand injury belongs mostly to the patient, and the task of the clinical team is to make sure the patient knows what to do and what would mean stopping. Hand therapy, provided by a therapist trained in the rehabilitation of the hand, uses exercises, splints and advice about swelling to regain movement without pulling a healing bone out of place. Adherence is the strongest single influence on the outcome in many hand injuries, because the exercises that restore movement are uncomfortable and are easy to abandon once the pain settles. A patient who reports that the finger is becoming stiffer rather than looser, or who cannot move it as the therapist described, needs review rather than encouragement.

Two groups of patients need a lower threshold for referral than the rest. In a child the ends of the phalanges contain a growth region, and an injury there can disturb the length and shape of a finger for the rest of life even when the shaft is intact, so a child who will not use a hand after an injury is examined and imaged rather than watched at home. In an older adult the skin is thinner, the bone is more brittle and the joints stiffen faster, so a finger that would recover in a younger patient may lose its movement within days, and the fracture may be far less painful than its severity suggests. Patients whose bone is weakened by disease or by a long course of a medicine that thins it may break a finger in a fall from standing height, and where a sign is unreliable the answer is a lower threshold for immobilization, for imaging and for referral, while the decisions themselves belong to the clinician.

The record and the handover are part of the treatment rather than a formality that follows it. The time of the injury, the mechanism, the position in which the hand was found, the findings of the neurovascular examination before and after every intervention and what has been applied are written or spoken in a fixed order, so that the receiving team continues a story instead of starting a new one. Where this chapter names a time or a distance, it names an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between services, countries and editions. A hand injury in a patient who is intoxicated, confused or unable to cooperate is treated with the same care as any other, with attention to the airway while the hand is protected, and anyone whose spine may have been injured is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has done so.

To summarize, a fractured or dislocated finger is a small injury with a large consequence, because the hand heals by stiffening and a finger left out of line or left still loses the grip the patient needs. Look at both hands together for swelling, deformity and rotation, feel along the bone and around the joints, ask the patient to move the fingers and record what they can do, and examine the color, warmth, capillary refill and sensation of every finger before any intervention and again after it. The order of this course is followed in the hand as everywhere else, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and the provider level is named for every act. A deformed finger is splinted in the position found and is never straightened in the field, force is not a technique, buddy strapping is used only on an injury that has been assessed as stable, and reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol. An open wound over a joint is covered with a sterile dressing and never explored blindly, an embedded object is left where it is, and exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound. Pain that increases, pain out of proportion to the injury, numbness, tingling, weakness, and a finger that becomes cold and pale or tense and shiny are the findings that stop the responder, so a constricting bandage or splint is released within the limits of the local protocol, the hand is not elevated above the level of the heart, and the patient is kept warm and transferred urgently, because time is muscle.
