# Chapter 57 — Computed Tomography, Magnetic Resonance Imaging and Ultrasound

The radiograph is the first picture taken of an injured limb, and for most fractures it is the only one that is ever needed, but there are questions it cannot answer. A break that reaches a joint surface may be far more complex than the plain film suggests, a ligament may be torn while the bone remains whole, and a fracture may be present and invisible for days after the injury. Three further examinations are available for those questions, and this chapter is about what each of them adds, what each of them costs the patient, and where each of them belongs in the order of care. That order does not change, and it is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. Imaging never leads that order, because a picture is taken of a patient who has first been examined, protected and recorded, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

Every image begins as a question, and a study that was not ordered for a reason is a study that will not change anything. The clinician who orders it has already examined the patient, recorded the neurovascular findings and decided what the answer would do, whether that is to confirm a suspicion, to plan an operation or to exclude an injury that must not be missed. The lay first aider never orders imaging and never interprets it, the trained responder does not choose which study is needed, and the emergency medical technician or paramedic may only pass on the findings that the receiving clinician will use. Computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging and ultrasound are each requested by a physician, and the choice among them belongs to that physician together with the radiologist who will report the study. When the answer cannot change what is done, the correct decision is often to take no picture at all, because every examination has a cost and some of those costs are borne by the patient alone.

The sequence that governs this course places imaging after immobilization, and the reason is not order for its own sake. A limb that is moved for a scan is a limb that may bleed again, and a fracture that is not yet supported can shift on the trolley, in the doorway and on the table of the scanner. The neurovascular status is therefore examined and written before the patient leaves the place where the injury was assessed, and it is examined again at the destination, because a finding that was not recorded before a journey cannot be attributed after it. Immobilization before imaging also protects the picture itself, since a limb that is held still produces an image that is easier to read and easier to interpret. Nobody in this sequence is permitted to straighten a deformed limb in order to obtain a better view, and the reason is that vessels and nerves lie against the bone and a movement that looks helpful can tear what remained intact.

Computed tomography takes many radiographs from many directions around the patient and uses a computer to reconstruct them as cross sections. It shows bone with a detail that a plain film cannot match, and it can display the same information in other planes or as a three dimensional shape that a surgeon can turn over in the mind. What it adds is geometry, which means the path of a fracture line, the number of fragments, the step where a broken edge meets a joint surface, and the small piece that has come to rest inside the joint or between the bone ends. It is information of that kind that decides whether an injury can be treated in a cast or needs an operation, and that decision belongs to the orthopedic surgeon who will perform it. Computed tomography is a hospital examination, performed in a department staffed by radiographers and reported by a radiologist, and it is not a tool that is carried to the roadside.

The fractures that most often need computed tomography are those near or inside a joint, where a step of a few millimeters can change the future of the limb. Examples used in teaching include breaks of the tibial plateau, the calcaneus, the acetabulum and the vertebrae, and these are illustrations of a principle rather than a list to be memorized, because the more a fracture involves a joint surface, the more the surgeon needs to see its geometry. A scan may also find a fragment that has rotated and now blocks the movement of a joint, and that is one of the situations in which reduction must not be attempted by an untrained hand. In the spine, computed tomography shows the bone of the vertebral body and the arch with great clarity, and it is combined with other studies when a neurological deficit is present, because the question then concerns the cord and the nerve roots as well as the bone. Clearing a spine is a decision made by a clinician qualified to clear it, and no image replaces that judgment.

Radiation is the cost that computed tomography imposes on the body, and it is a real cost rather than a theoretical one. A single scan of one region delivers a dose many times that of a single radiograph, and any figure quoted for that difference must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the values change with the machine, the technique and the edition of the reference. The tissues most sensitive to radiation are those that divide quickly, so a child, a young person and a pregnant patient deserve a deliberate conversation before any scan is ordered. A pregnant patient who has been injured presents a decision that no responder can make, and the clinician weighs the injury that must be diagnosed against the exposure that the study would cause, with the radiologist and, where it is possible and appropriate, with the patient herself. A clinician may decide that the answer is worth the dose and may be right, but that decision is made with the radiologist and recorded with the reason. Alternatives are always considered first, because a radiograph may answer the question, ultrasound may answer part of it, and magnetic resonance imaging uses no ionizing radiation at all.

Computed tomography is poor at showing the soft tissues that the examination is often most concerned about. A ligament, a tendon, a piece of cartilage and the marrow inside a bone are all seen far better by other means, so a normal scan does not exclude a torn ligament or a fracture that is only a crack in the spongy bone. Metal in the field, whether a surgical implant or a fragment from the injury itself, scatters the beam and produces streaks that can hide the very thing being looked for, and the radiologist must be told what is inside the patient. A splint, a brace or a binder that stays in place during the scan may also affect what can be seen, and it is left in place unless a clinician has decided otherwise, because the limb is still injured after the picture is taken. The report is read by the clinician who ordered it, and a printed result is never handed to a patient as an explanation.

Magnetic resonance imaging places the patient inside a strong magnetic field and uses radio waves to build a picture, so it uses no ionizing radiation and shows soft tissue in extraordinary detail. Ligaments, tendons, cartilage, muscle, the fluid within a joint, the marrow of bone and the spinal cord with its nerves are all within its range, and it is the study of choice when the question concerns a structure rather than a broken cortex. It shows a fracture that a radiograph has not yet revealed, because blood and swelling inside the marrow change the signal of the bone, and that change appears long before the fracture line itself becomes visible. For the same reason it often finds the injury in an older adult whose bone has lost density, whose fracture may be present with little pain and a radiograph that looks almost normal. It shows the growth region near the end of a child's bone, and it shows the spinal cord when a patient has weakness or numbness. It is ordered by a clinician, reported by a radiologist, and performed by radiographers trained for the magnet room.

The magnet is the part of magnetic resonance imaging that every responder must respect, and the reason is that the field is always on, even when the machine is not scanning and even when the room appears empty and quiet. Any ferromagnetic object that enters the room becomes a projectile, including an oxygen cylinder, a stretcher, a pair of scissors, a tool and a key, and the list is limited only by the imagination of the people who have been hurt by it. A patient with a pacemaker, a cochlear implant, certain surgical clips, an older heart valve, a metal fragment in the eye or an implanted pump may be harmed by the field, and the decisions about those devices belong to the radiologist and the physician responsible for the patient. The screening questionnaire is completed and verified before anyone crosses the line, and the staff who enter wear personal protective equipment and follow the rules of the facility. The radiographer has both the authority and the duty to stop anyone who has not been screened, and a monitor, a pump or a cylinder that travels with the patient must be approved for the magnet room, while equipment that is not approved stays outside. The patient must also be able to lie still through a long examination, and sedation for a patient who cannot cooperate is a decision for a physician working within local protocol.

The journey to the magnet room is itself an intervention and must be treated as one. A patient with an injured limb arrives in a splint or a binder that has been applied and checked, and the limb is examined before departure and again on arrival, with the color, the warmth, the pulses, the sensation and the movement all compared with the record. The magnet room cannot receive the monitor that was used in the resuscitation room, so the patient is observed by a person who stays within sight and by equipment that is safe for the field, and any change is reported at once. Warmth is maintained throughout the journey, because a patient who is cooled in a long corridor is a patient in whom shock and clotting worsen. A splint that has become tight on the way is loosened by someone authorized to do so, and a limb that becomes tense, swollen and painful out of proportion to the injury is a reason to stop and escalate rather than to continue.

Ultrasound uses sound waves that are too high for the ear to hear, sent from a probe held against the skin, and it builds a picture as the clinician watches. It uses no ionizing radiation, the machine is small enough to be wheeled to the bedside, and the examination can be repeated as often as the question changes, which makes it the one imaging method that can be brought to a patient who must not be moved. It is used by clinicians trained and credentialed in its use, by emergency physicians, by sonographers and, in some services, by trained prehospital teams working under a protocol that authorizes it. Its greatest strength is also its greatest weakness, because the picture is produced and interpreted by the person holding the probe, so the value of the study depends on the training of that person and on knowing what to look for. A study performed by someone who is not trained in it, and interpreted by someone who was not present, is a study that can mislead.

Ultrasound answers a small number of questions about an injured limb very well, and those questions are worth naming. It shows fluid inside a joint, which tells the examiner that something within the joint has bled, and it can show a joint that has filled after a dislocation or a fracture that enters the joint surface. It shows tendons and can demonstrate a gap or a retracted end after an injury, particularly where the tendon lies close to the skin, and it can find a foreign body such as a piece of glass or wood that a wound has hidden. It can follow the flow of blood in a vessel and can help to show whether an artery has been injured or compressed, although a normal flow signal does not exclude an injury to the wall of the vessel. It can also confirm that a joint has returned to its place after a reduction performed by a qualified clinician, which is useful when that confirmation must be obtained without moving the patient to another room.

Ultrasound also guides procedures that a trained clinician performs, and the guidance is a skill learned on patients under supervision rather than from a page. Aspiration of a tense joint, drainage of a collection, the placement of a needle or a catheter and the injection of a substance into a joint are each performed only by a clinician credentialed for that act and only within the local protocol, and the substance and the amount are never taught here. The probe gives the operator a view of the needle as it advances, which reduces the number of attempts and the harm that repeated attempts can cause, but it does not make the procedure safe by itself. The person who performs it is also responsible for the sterile field, for personal protective equipment, for confirming the identity of the patient and the side of the limb, and for recording what was done. A responder who is not credentialed for the procedure assists with the comfort of the patient and with holding the limb still, and does nothing more.

What ultrasound cannot be trusted for is as important as what it can do. Sound does not pass through bone, so the interior of a bone and the joint surface behind it are hidden, and a normal examination of a limb therefore never excludes a fracture. Deep structures are poorly seen in a large or swollen limb, and the probe cannot be pressed firmly against a wound, an open injury or a very tender area without adding pain and risk. A small crack in the cortex, a fracture that does not displace and an injury that lies behind another bone are all easy to miss, and the person who reports the study may be the person who most wants it to be normal. The finding that must never be reported as a clear limb is the limb that hurts, because pain after an injury is a finding in its own right and it outranks a picture that looks reassuring.

The choice among these examinations depends on the question, the patient and the place, and it is made in a conversation between the clinician and the radiologist. A patient with a suspected break of a long bone in a well equipped hospital is assessed, splinted, examined and sent for a radiograph, and computed tomography is added only if the geometry of the fracture will change the operation. A patient with a painful knee and a normal radiograph may need magnetic resonance imaging for a ligament, while a patient with a swollen joint that must be aspirated now may need ultrasound at the bedside. In a small facility with no scanner the question changes from which study to choose to whether the patient must travel, and that decision weighs the injury against the journey. Where two studies could answer the same question, the one that is faster and safer for this patient is chosen, and the reason is written down.

The cost of imaging to a patient begins with time, and time is not an abstraction in a person who is in pain, frightened and far from home. A scan may be available at once or may wait behind other patients, and the patient who is unstable or in severe pain is given priority while the patient with a stable injury waits. Waiting is a period of risk rather than an empty pause, because a splint can tighten, a limb can swell and a patient can become cold, so the responder uses that time to keep the patient warm, to keep the limb supported in the position found and to report every change to the clinician who can act on it. Transport is the second cost, and for many patients it is the largest, because a study that is not available where the injury was assessed means a journey by road or by air, an escort trained to watch the patient and a receiving facility that has been warned. The limb is splinted before the journey and checked during it, the neurovascular status is recorded before departure and after arrival, and the record travels with the patient so that the next team continues a story instead of starting a new one. There is a third cost, which is money, because a scan may be charged for, the days away from work may cost more than the scan, and a study that will not change the treatment takes from a household that has already been injured. Pain must be treated for the patient to wait and to travel at all, and the choice and the amount of any analgesic belong to the prescriber within local protocol, because this course names no dose, no route and no frequency.

Reassessment after imaging is the step that closes the loop, and it is performed with the same observations in the same order as before. The limb is examined for color, warmth, pulses, sensation, movement and pain, and each finding is compared with the record rather than remembered, because the comparison is what has value. The splint, the cast or the binder is checked for tightness after the patient has been moved, positioned on a table and moved again, since a device that fitted in the emergency department may not fit after swelling has increased. A patient who has been lying flat and still for a long examination may be cold, may be in more pain than before and may be frightened by what is happening, and each of those findings is acted on. The complication this course is most afraid of is the one caused by the treatment, and the reassessment is the only reliable way to catch it early.

A patient who becomes worse during or after a study is a patient who stops the process, because the study serves the patient rather than the other way around. Pain that increases instead of settling, pain out of proportion to the injury, numbness, tingling, weakness, a limb that becomes pale or cool and a limb that becomes tense and swollen are the findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation. When a compartment syndrome is suspected, the diagnosis is clinical and time is muscle, so the constricting bandage, splint or cast is released within the limits of local protocol, the limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, and the patient is referred urgently. A pulse that remains present does not exclude that diagnosis, and waiting for a confirmation that will arrive too late is the mistake this course warns against. The responder records what was found, what was done and at what time, and passes that record to whoever takes over the care.

Referral or definitive treatment is the last step, and imaging exists to inform it rather than to replace it. Some injuries are managed without an operation, in a cast, a brace, a splint or a boot, and that decision belongs to the clinician responsible for the follow-up, who also decides when the next radiograph will be taken. Other injuries need an operation, which may hold the fragments with metal devices placed inside the body, an operation called open reduction and internal fixation, or with a frame that holds them from outside, and the choice between those methods is made by the surgeon after examining the patient and the images together. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and this course describes what they are meant to achieve rather than how to do them. The part of the responder ends with the safe transfer of a patient who has been assessed, stabilized, examined, immobilized, imaged and reassessed, and with a record that makes the next decision easier.

Certain things must never be done, however strong the temptation and however good the intention behind them. A deformed limb is never straightened in the field or in the imaging department in order to obtain a better picture, and the reason is the risk to the vessels and the nerves that lie against the bone. An embedded object is never removed, exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, an open wound is never explored blindly, and definitive closure belongs to the operating room and to nobody else. A patient who is unstable, who is bleeding, who is not breathing or who is in cardiac arrest is not sent to a scanner alone, because imaging must never delay resuscitation. A reduction is never attempted by an untrained hand, and never when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when the vessels or nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear or when an earlier attempt has failed, because in each of those situations the attempt can convert an injury that would have healed into one that cannot.

The spine deserves one further word in a chapter about imaging, because the studies described here are often the ones that decide whether it can be cleared. A patient with a suspected injury of the spine is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has done so, and no image and no examination by an unqualified person changes that rule. The clinician who clears a spine is trained and authorized to do it, using the criteria of the local protocol, and until that moment the patient is moved by a trained team with the precautions the protocol requires. A normal scan in a patient who cannot be examined reliably does not by itself make movement safe, and a scan that is not available does not justify an exception. Where the patient has a neurological deficit the question about the spinal cord and the nerve roots is urgent, and the referral is made without waiting for a picture that can be obtained later.

Nothing described in this chapter is learned by listening to it, and that sentence is not a formality. The scope of practice that authorizes a procedure, the credential that proves a person has been trained for it and the protocol that governs it are defined by the service, the institution and the law of the country, and they differ from place to place. A responder who has been taught to hold a probe in one service is not thereby qualified to hold it in another, and the safe course for anyone who is unsure is to support the limb, keep the patient warm, record the findings and hand the decision to a person who is qualified to make it. Computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging and ultrasound are each performed by trained professionals inside a department with rules, and each is interpreted in the light of an examination performed by a clinician.

To summarize, the radiograph remains the first and often the only picture taken of an injured limb, and the other three examinations are added when a specific question cannot be answered without them. Computed tomography shows the geometry of a complex fracture, magnetic resonance imaging shows the ligaments, the cartilage, the marrow and the spinal cord, and ultrasound shows fluid, tendons, foreign bodies and the flow of blood at the bedside, and each of them has limits the clinician must keep in mind. Every study is ordered by a physician, performed by trained professionals and reported by a radiologist, and the lay first aider never orders imaging and never interprets it. The order of care never changes, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and the neurovascular findings are recorded before and after every intervention, including the journey to the scanner. A deformed limb is never straightened to improve a picture, an embedded object is never removed, exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, an open wound is never explored blindly, and definitive closure belongs to the operating room. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and a patient who is unstable is not sent to a scanner alone, because resuscitation comes before imaging. The spine is treated as unstable until it is cleared by someone qualified to clear it, cold is treated as a complication, and every number in this course is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.
