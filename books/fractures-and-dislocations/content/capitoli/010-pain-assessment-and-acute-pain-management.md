# Chapter 10 — Pain Assessment and Acute Pain Management

Pain is the complaint that brings most injured patients to care and the symptom a responder can act on, so it belongs inside the assessment of every injury. A fracture hurts because the broken ends move, because the periosteum is torn, because muscle goes into spasm and because crushed tissue bleeds, so pain is a reading of the injury itself. Relieving pain is part of treating the injury rather than a courtesy offered afterwards, because unrelieved pain changes what the body does and a patient whose pain is controlled is safer to examine, to move, to image and to hand over. This chapter follows one order, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and it names the provider level for every act. It is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

Unrelieved acute pain is not only an experience but a set of bodily events that work against the patient. Pain raises the heart rate and the blood pressure, increases the work of the heart and raises the demand for oxygen when the circulation may already be failing. It makes breathing shallow and rapid, so the bases of the lungs are not filled and secretions are retained, which is one road to pneumonia after an injury that never touched the chest. It slows the emptying of the stomach, raises the risk of a clot in the leg because the patient will not move, and disturbs sleep when healing is most active. Pain also produces fear, and fear amplifies pain, so the two feed each other in a circle that immobilizes the patient further. Each of those effects is a reason to treat pain early rather than to wait for a diagnosis.

Pain is subjective, which means that only the patient can report it, and that report is the standard against which every other sign is judged. It is not one sensation but several at once, with a sharp part from torn tissue, a dull aching part from muscle spasm, a burning part when a nerve is involved, and an emotional part that depends on what the injury means to that person. Culture, age, an earlier experience of pain, the memory of a pain poorly treated, the fear of addiction and the wish not to be a burden all change what a patient says. A listener who hears the patient's own words learns more than a number can carry, because pain described as a tight band is a different problem from pain described as a knife. The part of the responder is to ask, to listen, to believe and to write it down.

In the sequence this course follows, pain is assessed inside the first step, which is assessment, and it never takes priority over the steps that keep a patient alive. The primary survey is remembered as ABCDE, for airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure, and pain is addressed only as far as it interferes with those, because a patient whose airway is obstructed is not soothed first. Once the primary survey is complete, the assessment turns to the limb, and there the pain is examined as carefully as the deformity. The lay first aider may ask where it hurts and report the answer, while the trained responder, the nurse and the physician read the pain in the light of the mechanism of injury and the state of the patient. The order is fixed on purpose, because each step protects the next one.

The history of the pain is taken in a fixed order so that nothing is forgotten, and the questions are simple enough for the lay first aider to ask and to repeat. The first is when the pain began and how it has changed, because pain that worsens over hours tells a different story from pain that was severe at once. The next is where it hurts and whether it travels, since pain running from the hip into the knee points toward the nerve that supplies that region. Then comes what the pain is like, whether sharp, dull, burning or cramping, and how severe it is in the patient's own words. The responder then asks what makes it worse and what makes it better, whether there is numbness, and whether breathing feels short. The mechanism of injury, the age of the patient and the medicines already taken belong in the same conversation, because a bone can break in a fall that would not have hurt a healthy person.

A responder cannot see pain, but the body says a good deal in its place, and those signs are worth reading while remembering that they mislead in both directions. A patient may guard the injured part, hold the limb still, clench the jaw, grimace, sweat, turn pale or become restless, and a child may go quiet and still, which is often worse than crying. None of those signs is a measurement and none is required, because a patient who is calm, still and pale may have severe pain while a patient who is shouting may have very little. The examiner records what was seen beside what was said, and treats the patient rather than the appearance.

Some injured patients cannot describe their pain at all, and the assessment then has to be built from other sources instead of being abandoned. A young child may lack the words and may agree with whatever is suggested, so the child is asked with a tool designed for that age, such as a row of faces, and the parent is asked what is different today. An older adult with a memory problem may be unable to report pain, so the responder watches for a new refusal to move, a new restlessness, a change in appetite or sleep, or a face that has gone blank, because such a patient may carry a fracture nobody suspected. A patient with a breathing tube, a patient who is sedated, a patient who is deaf and a patient who speaks another language all belong in the same group. The part of the lay first aider is to notice and to report rather than to decide.

Certain signs are unreliable in particular patients, and a chapter that teaches them as always present will be believed and will then mislead. A rapid pulse commonly accompanies pain, yet a patient who takes a medicine that slows the heart, such as a beta blocker, may have a normal pulse while in severe pain, and a trained athlete may be the same. A patient with an injury of the spinal cord may have pain from a fracture above the level of the injury and no sensation below it, so the two are assessed separately. Severe pain is not excluded by a score of 0 out of 10, and any such number is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol rather than a measurement to be relied on.

The single finding that must never be explained away is pain out of proportion to the injury that keeps getting worse. Muscle swelling inside a compartment that cannot expand produces pain that is deep and burning and poorly relieved by rest, and that pain is often increased when the fingers or the toes are stretched, which is a clinical test performed by an examiner trained to do it. The pain may be present while the pulse at the wrist or the ankle is still easy to feel, so an intact pulse never excludes the problem, and the skin may look normal when the muscle is already dying. Analgesia does not hide this diagnosis and must not be withheld on the excuse that it would, because a patient whose pain is controlled is easier to examine and to follow; what matters is that pain which does not settle is treated as a warning. The diagnosis is clinical and is made by a clinician, and time is muscle, so the responder who suspects it does not wait for confirmation.

Pain is recorded in the same way as the neurovascular findings, before an intervention and again after it, in the words of the patient wherever that is possible. The record carries what the patient said, where it hurt, what made it worse, what was done and what changed afterwards, together with the time of each entry, because a note without a time cannot be compared with anything. The comparison is what has value: a pain reported as 6 out of 10 that is now 3 out of 10 has improved, while the same numbers in a patient who is becoming drowsy and cold mean something else, and those numbers are an illustration to be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol. A finding that was not recorded before a splint was applied cannot be attributed afterwards, and the same rule applies to a medicine, because nobody can judge whether pain relief helped or a complication began unless the state before it was written down.

Stabilization is the second step of the sequence, and for pain it means making the patient as comfortable and as still as the situation allows while the limb is supported in the position in which it was found. A deformed limb is never straightened, because the vessels and the nerves lie against the bone and a movement that looks helpful can tear what was left intact, and that restraint holds even when pain is the reason someone wants to pull the limb straight. The lay first aider supports the limb with the hands, with a pillow or with whatever is at hand, keeps the patient warm and stays with the patient, because presence itself reduces fear and fear reduces pain. The trained responder and the paramedic may immobilize the limb within the equipment and procedures their service authorizes, and the nurse may do the same within the protocol of the facility. Cold is a complication of trauma rather than a detail of comfort, so wet clothing is removed, the patient is insulated from the ground and the injured part is covered.

The neurovascular examination belongs to the third step, it is performed before any intervention and repeated after every one, and pain is assessed in the same breath because the two are read together. The examiner looks at the color and the temperature of the skin, feels the pulse beyond the injury, presses briefly on a nail to watch the color return, and asks the patient to move the fingers or the toes and to report what is felt. Sensation is tested on both sides and compared, and numbness, tingling or the loss of a movement the patient could perform a moment earlier is recorded in the patient's own words. Pain that is increasing and pain that has changed in character belong to that examination as much as the pulse, and a pulse that is present never excludes serious injury to the vessels or to a compartment. The lay first aider observes color, warmth and the ability of the patient to move and to feel, while the formal examination belongs to the trained responder, the nurse or the clinician.

Immobilization comes fourth, and one of its purposes is to relieve pain, because a bone whose ends can move with every breath hurts more than a bone held still. The splint holds the joint above and the joint below the injury as well as the broken bone, it is padded where it presses on bone, and it is secured firmly enough to support without becoming a band that stops the circulation. The trained responder and the paramedic apply splints within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, while the physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides what definitive immobilization is required, which may be a cast, a brace or a surgical fixation. Casting and surgical fixation are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and neither is taught from a book in this course. Traction is a gentle continuous pull used to hold the ends of a bone in line in certain injuries, and it too is learned under supervision rather than improvised in the field.

Imaging is the fifth step, and it confirms what the examination has already suggested rather than deciding how the pain is treated. A radiograph is usually the first study, taken in at least two directions so that a displacement in the third direction is not hidden, and the joint above and the joint below are included so that a second injury is not missed. Computed tomography shows the detail of a complex break, and magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for soft tissues, including ligaments, cartilage, tendons and muscle. The clinician responsible for the patient orders the study and interprets it, the lay first aider never orders or interprets imaging, and the trained responder does not decide which study is needed. A patient in pain is made as comfortable as the injury allows before the journey, and any medicine given for that journey is prescribed by the authorized prescriber and given by someone whose scope permits it. Because the patient is moved to another room and often left there briefly, the limb and the pain are checked again on return, since an image is a picture of one moment.

Analgesia is the relief of pain with medicines, and in this course it is part of the treatment of the injury rather than a courtesy offered when nothing more urgent is happening. The first principle is that pain is treated as soon as it is recognized and as soon as the patient has been assessed, because a patient in severe pain cannot cooperate with the rest of the examination. The second is that the cause is treated along with the symptom, so a tight splint is loosened and a fracture is immobilized while the medicine is being arranged. The third is that different pains respond to different medicines and that combining them, which is called multimodal analgesia, usually relieves more pain with fewer effects than pushing one medicine to a high amount. The fourth is that relief is measured afterwards rather than assumed, so the patient is asked again and the answer is written down. None of those principles tells the listener what to give, because the substance, the amount, the route and the interval belong to the prescriber and to the local protocol.

The scope of practice decides who may give an analgesic, and the answer differs between services and between countries, so the listener must know the rule that applies where they work. The lay first aider gives no medicine at all, and in particular does not offer a painkiller from a household supply and does not give a drink to a patient who may need an operation unless the local protocol allows it. The trained responder and the paramedic may give what their service authorizes in writing and what their training and certification cover. The nurse gives a medicine that has been prescribed, in the amount and by the route the prescription names, and administers it according to the checks and the documentation the facility requires. The physician or another authorized prescriber decides the substance, the amount, the route, the interval and the plan for monitoring, and may delegate the giving but never the judgment. Analgesia given outside those limits is not a kindness but an unauthorized act, and it is the patient who carries the risk.

This course gives no amount, no route and no interval for any medicine, and the reason is accuracy rather than caution. The right amount for one patient can be too much for another, because weight, the work of the kidneys and the liver and the other medicines already being taken all change what a body does with a drug. Allergy, pregnancy, breastfeeding, a stomach ulcer or a bleeding tendency, a heart that beats slowly, a lung disease that makes breathing shallow and a previous bad reaction all change the choice before the choice of amount is reached. A patient who has taken an opioid regularly for a long-standing problem needs a different plan from one who has never had any, and a patient with a head injury needs a plan that will not hide a change in the level of consciousness. Because those answers differ between services, countries and editions, every threshold and time named in this book is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, and the prescriber, not the listener, holds the number.

Every class of analgesic carries effects that the person giving it must be able to recognize, and recognizing them is part of the scope of practice of whoever gives them. The opioid group relieves severe pain and can also slow the breathing, lower the blood pressure, cause vomiting and produce drowsiness that blurs the examination of the head and of the nerves, so a patient who receives it is watched rather than left alone. The nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory group reduces pain and swelling without dulling consciousness, and it can irritate the stomach, cause bleeding and strain the kidneys, particularly in an older patient or one who is short of fluid, and in some patients with asthma it provokes a severe attack. Whatever is given is recorded with the time and the effect, and a responder who sees a patient become drowsy, slow their breathing or drop their blood pressure stops, supports the airway and calls for help at once.

The route by which a medicine is given is a clinical decision that depends on the state of the patient rather than on convenience. A patient who is vomiting, a patient who may need an operation soon and a patient whose stomach is not emptying because of the injury may not be the right patient for a medicine taken by mouth, and the amount that reaches the blood after swallowing can be unpredictable when the circulation is failing. A medicine breathed in reaches the blood quickly and is used in some services for severe pain, with the patient watched throughout and with the equipment and authorization the protocol requires. A medicine given into a vein acts within minutes and can therefore be dangerous within minutes, so it belongs to a monitored setting, to a person trained and authorized to give it and to a plan for what happens if the patient stops breathing. Giving a medicine is a technique learned with a teacher and not from a page.

Local and regional analgesia work by blocking the transmission of pain along the nerve that supplies the injured part, and they can relieve pain very effectively for a period of time. A local anesthetic may be placed around a nerve by a clinician trained to do it, or injected into the space around a fracture by a clinician trained in that technique, and both acts require the training, the equipment, the authorization and the supervision the local protocol sets out. What they can achieve is a patient who can be examined, moved and imaged comfortably, while what can go wrong is a block that spreads further than intended, a toxic reaction to the medicine, an injury by the needle and bleeding where the anatomy was not as expected. Sedation belongs to a clinician working in a monitored environment with the equipment to support breathing, the staff to watch the patient continuously and a plan for an airway that has to be taken over. None of those techniques is taught from this book, none is used on a wound in the field, and the decision to use one belongs to the clinician responsible for the patient.

Certain acts are forbidden in every version of this course, and each is forbidden because it has harmed patients. No medicine is given to relieve pain from a supply prescribed for another person, from a household container whose label has faded or by a route the giver is not trained and authorized to use. A limb is never straightened in the field to make the pain better, because the movement can turn a closed fracture into an open one, can tear a vessel that was still intact and can trap a nerve that was still working. An open wound is never explored blindly and an embedded object is never removed, because the object may be holding pressure on a torn vessel, and exposed bone is never pushed back into the wound. A medicine is never given in place of an assessment, and a patient who is deteriorating is never settled with sedation instead of being reassessed. A responder who meets resistance, a limb that becomes tighter under the hand or a patient whose condition changes stops what they are doing and calls for help, because force is not a technique.

Reassessment comes after every intervention, and it is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment, whether the treatment was a splint or a medicine. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the record, because the comparison is what has value and not any single measurement. The patient is asked again about the pain in the same words as before and the answer is set beside the earlier one, the limb is checked for color, warmth, pulse, sensation, movement and pain, and the breathing and the level of consciousness are checked after any medicine that can affect them. Pain that increases instead of settling, pain out of proportion to the injury, new numbness, new weakness, a limb that becomes tense and swollen and a patient who becomes drowsy are the findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation. A responder who suspects a compartment syndrome does not wait for the diagnosis to be confirmed and acts within the local protocol, which may allow a constricting bandage or splint to be released by someone authorized to do it. The limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, the patient is kept warm and the patient is referred urgently, because the diagnosis is clinical and time is muscle.

Some patients need a lower threshold for both assessment and treatment, because the usual signs are less reliable in them. A child may be unable to say where it hurts and may become quiet rather than loud, so a parent is asked what has changed and a tool suited to the age is used, while the decision about any medicine belongs to the prescriber who knows the child's weight and history. An older adult whose bone has lost density may carry a fracture far less painful than its severity suggests and may be unable to report pain because of a memory problem, so the threshold for imaging and referral is lower rather than higher. A patient with a suspected injury of the spine is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has done so, and no lone manual handling technique is safe, so such a patient is moved by a trained team using the precautions the local protocol requires. A patient who is pregnant, a patient who has taken opioids for years and a patient who is dying each need a plan written by the clinician responsible, and the part of the responder is to observe, to report and to keep the patient safe.

Referral or definitive treatment is the last step of the sequence, and the plan for pain travels with the patient into it. An injury that needs an operating room, a damaged vessel or nerve, a suspected compartment syndrome, a dislocation, a fracture that enters a joint and a fracture in a child that crosses the growing part of the bone are all referred to a facility with imaging, an operating room and an intensive care unit. The handover names what the patient said about the pain, what was given and when, how much relief followed and what effect was seen, because the receiving team continues one story instead of starting a new one. In the operating room and afterwards, the surgeon and the anesthesia team decide what will be used for pain, and that plan is theirs to write within the protocols of their institution. Pain that is new, that returns after it had settled, that spreads or that comes with a change in the limb brings the patient back for review. Everything in this chapter is learned with a teacher and certified by an authority that also defines what may be done without it.

To summarize, pain is part of the injury and not a complaint beside it, so it is assessed in the first step of the sequence, recorded in the patient's own words before an intervention and again after it, and treated as part of the treatment. A deformed limb is splinted in the position in which it was found and is never straightened in the field, an embedded object is never removed, exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, an open wound is never explored blindly, and no medicine is given from a household supply or from another person's prescription. The lay first aider comforts, supports, keeps the patient warm and reports; the trained responder and the paramedic give only what their service authorizes; the nurse gives what has been prescribed, in the amount and by the route the prescription names; and the prescriber alone decides the substance, the amount, the route and the interval, so this course gives no dose and no regimen. Pain out of proportion to the injury, pain that keeps worsening, new numbness, new weakness and a tense swollen limb stop the responder and bring help, and analgesia is never withheld for fear that it will hide such a finding, because the diagnosis is clinical and time is muscle. Reduction, traction and casting belong to supervised training and to local protocol, and nothing in this chapter authorizes a listener to perform them. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.
