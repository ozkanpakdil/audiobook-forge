# Chapter 8 — Splinting in Practice

Splinting is not the act of tying a device to a limb but the act of deciding what must be held still and who is qualified to hold it. A splint is the simplest life-saving tool in musculoskeletal care, and it is also the tool most often applied badly, because it looks easy and the responder is in a hurry. Every chapter of this course follows one order, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

Immobilization means preventing movement of the injured part and of the joints above and below it, because a bone held alone can still be swung by the joint at either end. Its purposes are few and each is a reason: to reduce pain, to limit further displacement, to protect the vessels and the nerves that lie against the bone, to slow bleeding into the tissues, and to keep a closed injury from becoming an open one. Pain is not a side issue, because a patient in severe pain breathes badly, moves badly and is harder to assess. A splint also protects an open wound from further contamination and protects the limb from the ground and from the hands of those who move the patient. What a splint does not do is heal, and it never replaces the examination that must come before it.

Whatever material is chosen, the same principles govern its use, and they were learned long before any commercial device existed. The limb is splinted in the position in which it was found and is never straightened, because the vessels and the nerves lie against the bone and a movement that looks helpful can tear what remained intact. Padding goes where the device presses on bone, which means the ankle bones, the heel, the shin, the knee, the elbow, the wrist and the fingers. The device is secured firmly enough to hold the limb without acting as a band that stops the circulation, and a splint that is loose is as useless as one that is too tight. The joints above and below the injury are included, because a splint that stops at the fracture leaves the fracture free to move.

Assessment comes before any material is opened, and it begins with the scene, the mechanism, the time of injury and the condition of the patient rather than with the limb. The primary survey is performed first and its steps are remembered as ABCDE, meaning airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure, because life comes before limb when two correct actions compete for the same pair of hands. The lay first aider looks for danger, calls for help and does not move a patient whose spine may be injured. The trained first responder and the emergency medical technician or paramedic record the mechanism and the position in which the limb was found. The nurse and the physician add the history that changes the picture, including illness and medicine that weakens bone. A patient whose bone is fragile may break it in a fall that would leave another person unhurt, so the threshold for immobilization is lower in that patient.

Stabilization is the step between assessment and immobilization, and in the field it means support, warmth and restraint rather than a device. Bleeding that can be controlled is controlled with direct pressure, and an open wound is covered with a sterile dressing without being explored, because blind exploration adds harm and definitive closure belongs to the operating room. An embedded object is left in place and padded on either side, because it may be holding pressure on a torn vessel, and exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound. The limb is supported with the hands, with a pillow or with whatever is at hand until the splint is ready, and a patient with a suspected injury of the spine is treated as unstable until it is cleared by someone qualified to clear it, and is moved only by a trained team using the precautions the local protocol requires. Cold is a complication of trauma, so wet clothing is replaced and the patient is insulated from the ground, from metal and from the wind. Stabilization is performed by the lay first aider with the hands, by the trained responder with the equipment the service authorizes, and by the clinician with the resources of the facility.

The neurovascular examination is performed before the splint is applied and repeated after it, and this rule is the spine of the whole subject. The examiner looks at the color and warmth of the skin, feels the pulse beyond the injury, presses a nail to watch the color return, and compares both sides. Sensation is tested and described in the words of the patient as far as possible, and the patient is asked to move the fingers or the toes, whichever lies beyond the injury. Movement that was present a moment ago and is now lost is a finding of the greatest importance, and so are numbness, tingling and pain out of proportion to the injury. The result is written before the splint goes on and again after it is secured, because a finding that was not recorded before an intervention cannot be attributed afterwards. The lay first aider watches color, warmth and movement, while the formal examination belongs to the trained responder, the nurse or the clinician.

Commercially made splints are what most responders reach for first, and they fall into a few families that behave differently. A malleable padded splint is a strip of metal covered in foam that is bent to the shape of a limb and held with straps, and it suits the forearm, the wrist and the hand. A vacuum splint is a soft sleeve filled with beads that becomes rigid when the air is drawn out, and it molds to the limb and spreads pressure better than a hard device. A traction splint applies a gentle continuous pull to the leg, and it is used by the emergency medical technician or paramedic only where the service trains and authorizes it, with the technique learned under supervision and not from a book. A commercial splint is fast and familiar, but every one of them can be applied too tightly and must be checked after it is in place.

What a commercial splint does badly is worth saying as plainly as what it does well. It is sized for an average limb, so it is short on a tall patient and loose on a thin one, and a splint that does not reach beyond the joints above and below the injury allows the fracture to move. The metal strip can kink at a pressure point, and the foam can hide a fold in the skin until a blister or an ulcer has formed. The device is unavailable when the service has run out, when the vehicle is far away, or when a passerby finds the patient with nothing in hand. A traction splint demands training, a second pair of hands and attention to the skin and the nerves, and it is not used when the pelvis or the hip may be injured. A commercial splint has a place, but it is not a substitute for judgment, and the responder who has none must know what to do with folded cardboard and a strip of cloth.

A padded board is a rigid straight splint, and its value comes from that rigidity. A wooden board, a length of stiff cardboard or a folded magazine can each hold a limb against movement. The board is wrapped in cloth or foam so that it does not press directly on bone, and it is long enough to span the joints above and below the injury. It is at its best for the forearm, where a straight support along the limb is exactly what the injury needs, and at its worst where a limb must be held bent, as at the elbow or the knee. A rigid splint concentrates pressure at its ends, so the padding is thickest there and the skin is inspected when the device is removed. A board tied with a hard knot can damage the tissues beneath it, so the knot is tied over padding away from the injured part, and the tightness is checked by whoever applied it.

The sling and the swathe are the oldest tools in this subject and still among the most useful. A triangular bandage or a length of cloth supports the forearm against the chest, and a second band around the chest and the arm holds the arm against the body, and the pair together is a sling and swathe. It is at its best for the shoulder girdle, the collarbone, the upper arm and the elbow, where the chest of the patient is the splint and the cloth only keeps the limb against it. It does badly wherever the bone must be held in exact alignment, because it allows the arm to rotate and to slide, and it does badly in a patient who must be lifted, because the band can slip under the weight. The band around the chest must not press on the neck or the opposite armpit, and the hand stays visible so that its color can be watched. A ring or a watch on the injured hand is removed if it comes off easily, because swelling makes it a constriction later.

A pillow is not a splint in the strict sense, and it is one of the most useful things a responder can place under an injured limb. It supports the limb along its length, it conforms to the shape of the body instead of fighting it, and it is soft enough that it cannot cut into the skin. It is at its best for the lower leg, the ankle, the heel and the bent knee, and for the elbow when the arm is held across the body. It does badly because it is compressible and shifts as the patient is moved, so a pillow alone holds nothing still unless it is bound in place. It becomes flat and wet in the rain, and a saturated pillow no longer supports and now conducts cold. It is not a splint for a fracture of the thigh bone, where the muscle around the bone can move the fragments against any soft support.

A rolled blanket or a folded coat can be a soft bolster under a limb or a rigid body when it is rolled tightly and bound. Rolled tightly, it becomes a firm cylinder that is placed along the side of a limb and tied to it. Used as a bolster, it supports a limb in the position found and keeps it from resting on the ground. It is at its best where a rigid device is not available, where the limb needs support rather than grip, and where the pelvis or a second injured limb must be protected from pressure. It does badly because it is bulky, because it absorbs water and blood, and because it loosens as its fibers settle, so it is rechecked more often than a commercial splint. A blanket roll that has been on the ground is contaminated and is not placed against an open wound.

Binding an injured limb to its neighbor is the oldest improvisation in the field and the one that carries the most conditions. A fractured finger is strapped to the finger beside it over a thin layer of padding. The lower leg can be bound to the other leg with padding between the knees and the ankles, which turns two limbs into one rigid unit, and the padding matters because skin against skin produces chafing, sweat and a wound of its own. The method is never used when the other limb is also injured, when the pelvis or the thigh bone may be broken, or when the limbs are unequal in length. It does badly because the swelling of one limb presses on the other and because the patient cannot be moved without moving both. This method belongs to the trained first responder or to the emergency medical technician or paramedic, while the lay first aider waits for help.

Padding is not a comfort but a part of the treatment, because the tissue that dies under a splint dies quietly. The points at greatest risk are those where bone lies just beneath the skin, such as the ankle bones, the heel, the shin, the kneecap, the elbow, the wrist and the knuckles. Cloth, foam, a folded towel or a spare garment can each be used, and it is laid smooth, with no folds and no knots beneath the limb. The padding is thick enough that a finger can slide between the splint and the skin at every point, and that is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the thickness of padding and the frequency of checks differ between services. A wet or blood-soaked pad is replaced, because it loses its cushioning and holds cold against the skin. Any red mark, blister or break in the skin is recorded and reported.

Securing the splint is where most of the avoidable harm occurs, so the materials and the order in which they are used deserve attention. A bandage, a length of cloth, a strip of tape or the straps that came with the device are all acceptable, and each is applied over the padding and not over bare skin. The bands are placed above and below the injury rather than across the fracture, and they are tied firmly enough to hold without pressing the soft tissues. Knots are tied over padding and away from the injured part, with a soft pad under every knot, because a knot over a bone or a nerve can injure it. Tape is not applied directly to fragile skin, and it is never wrapped in a complete circle that could tighten as the limb swells. Once secured, the limb is not moved in order to test the splint, because the test itself can displace the fracture.

An open fracture changes the task from support to protection, and the splint is built around the wound rather than over it. The wound is covered with a sterile dressing, bleeding that can be controlled is controlled, and the neurovascular status is recorded before anything else is done. An embedded object is left where it lies and is stabilized with padding on either side so that the splint does not press it deeper, and exposed bone is never pushed back into the wound. The splint is then applied as for a closed injury, with the dressing left undisturbed and an opening kept clear if the local protocol requires one. No responder irrigates, explores or closes the wound, and no responder removes a foreign body, because definitive care belongs to the operating room and to the surgeon. The lay first aider covers the wound and supports the limb, the trained responder immobilizes and arranges urgent transfer, and the nurse prepares for the care that follows.

Reassessment after the splint is applied is not a courtesy but the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the record made before the splint, and it is the comparison rather than any single measurement that carries the information. The examiner checks the color and warmth of the skin beyond the splint, the pulse, the return of color after a nail is pressed, sensation, the ability to move, and the pain described by the patient. The splint itself is checked for tightness, for slippage and for pressure at its edges, and the straps are loosened or retied within the limits of the responder's role if they have become too tight. Findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation are pain that increases instead of settling, pain out of proportion to the injury, numbness, tingling, weakness, a limb that becomes tense and swollen, and a pulse that was present and is now absent. The lay first aider reports any change at once, the trained responder repeats the examination and documents it, and the clinician decides what the change means.

A compartment syndrome is the complication this whole subject exists to prevent, and it can be caused by the injury or by the splint that was meant to help. The limb becomes tense and swollen, the pain is severe and out of proportion to the injury, and it increases when the patient is asked to move the fingers or the toes. A pulse that is still palpable at the wrist or the ankle does not exclude the diagnosis, because pressure inside the compartment can stop the flow through the smallest vessels while the large vessel continues to beat. The diagnosis is clinical and is made by a clinician, and time is muscle, so a responder who suspects it does not wait for a confirmation that will arrive too late. The constricting bandage, splint or cast is released by whoever is authorized to release it within the local protocol, and the limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, because that lowers the pressure that keeps the tissue alive. The patient is kept warm and referred urgently, and the receiving team is told that the compartment is in question.

Imaging comes after immobilization in the sequence, and the order matters because a limb that is not yet supported will move again on the way to the imaging room. A radiograph is usually the first study, taken in at least two directions and including the joint above and the joint below so that a second injury is not missed. A splint commonly stays in place for the first image and is removed or opened by the clinician when the image is not adequate, and it is reapplied only after the neurovascular status has been recorded again. Computed tomography shows the detail of a complex break, particularly around a joint, and magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for soft tissues such as ligaments, tendons and muscle. Either study is ordered by a clinician trained to interpret it, and only when it will change what is done. The lay first aider never orders or interprets imaging, and the physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides which study is needed.

Splinting in the field is a holding action, and definitive treatment begins where the transport ends. A cast or a brace holds the position of the bone while it heals, and both are applied by a clinician or by a technician trained and authorized to do it, with the technique learned under supervision and according to local protocol. Reduction restores the position of the bone ends and traction holds them in line with a gentle continuous pull, and neither is taught from a book in this course. A surgeon may hold the fragments with metal devices, an operation called open reduction and internal fixation, and that decision belongs to the orthopedic surgeon. A reduction is not attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when the vessels or the nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, when the responder lacks training or equipment, or when an earlier attempt has failed. In each of those situations an attempt can convert an injury that would have healed into one that cannot, and force is not a technique.

The record and the handover are part of the treatment rather than a formality that follows it, and the splint has a place in both. The time and mechanism of the injury, the position in which the limb was found, the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention, and the material that was used are written or spoken in a fixed order, so that the receiving team continues a story instead of starting a new one. The record names who applied the splint, what padding was used, how tight it was felt to be, and when it was last checked. A change in the condition of the patient is reported at once, and a change in the limb is reported in the words of the patient as far as possible. The lay first aider tells the responding team what was done and what has changed, and the trained responder gives a structured handover that includes the mechanism, the findings and the treatment given. The nurse and the physician repeat the neurovascular examination before and after any change to the splint, because the record is only useful if it is used.

Some patients cannot report what they feel, and in them the splint is checked more often and more carefully. A child may not describe tingling and may cry for reasons that have nothing to do with the limb, and the bone of a child bends before it breaks, so the injury may be less obvious than the pain suggests. An older adult whose bone has lost density may break a wrist or a hip in a fall from standing height, and the fracture may be far less painful than its severity implies. A patient who is unconscious, intoxicated, sedated or living with a condition that numbs the feet cannot be asked whether the fingers are tingling, so the examiner relies on color, warmth, pulse and swelling. A patient whose nerve supply is already damaged may have no feeling to lose and no pulse to compare, which makes the first record more valuable and not less. In all of these patients the threshold for immobilization and for referral is lower, while the decisions about imaging and treatment remain with the clinician.

Splinting does not end when the last strap is tied, because the patient may wait and travel for a long time before a clinician sees the limb. The check of color, warmth, pulse, sensation, movement and pain is repeated at intervals, and the interval named in any service is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol rather than a rule fixed in this course. The patient is kept warm throughout, because cold makes the vessels narrow and the tissues less able to survive a period of poor flow, and a splint that has become wet is replaced. The limb is not elevated above the level of the heart while a compartment syndrome is suspected, and the patient is not left alone with a tight splint. If travel is long, the responders agree in advance who will check the limb and who will be told if something changes. Any change that suggests the circulation or the nerves are failing is escalated at once.

Because the same injury is met by many providers, the scope of practice deserves stating once more in the terms of this subject. The lay first aider supports the limb, keeps the patient warm, covers an open wound and calls for help, and does not straighten, pull, reduce or explore. The trained first responder and the emergency medical technician or paramedic apply the splints, the straps and the padding their service authorizes, record the neurovascular status before and after, and do nothing the protocol does not permit. The nurse checks the limb, documents the findings, prepares the materials and reports any deterioration to the physician. The physician assesses the patient, orders imaging, provides analgesia and the other treatment the local protocol allows, and refers the patient when the injury exceeds what the facility can safely treat. The orthopedic surgeon performs the reduction, the traction when it is indicated, the casting and the surgical fixation, and those acts are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol. The substance, the amount, the route and the frequency of any analgesia are decided by the prescriber under the local protocol, and this course names none of them, because a listener must not be able to take a prescription from a book. What the responder can do is describe the pain, its site, its severity, what makes it worse and what makes it better, and report a change at once.

To summarize, a splint holds an injured limb still, protects the vessels and the nerves that lie against the bone, reduces pain, and keeps a closed injury from becoming an open one, and it is chosen from what is available rather than from what is ideal. The commercial splint, the padded board, the sling and swathe, the pillow, the rolled blanket and the limb bound to its neighbor each have a place and each have a failure, and all are judged by the same rules, which are to pad the points where bone lies near the skin, to include the joints above and below the injury, and to secure the device firmly without stopping the circulation. The neurovascular status is examined and recorded before the splint is applied and again afterwards, and the examination is repeated on every journey and after every change, because a finding that was not recorded before an intervention cannot be attributed afterwards. A deformed limb is splinted in the position found and is never straightened in the field, an embedded object is never removed, exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, and definitive closure belongs to the operating room. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and no book teaches them. Cold is treated as a complication, the spine is treated as unstable until it is cleared by someone qualified to clear it, and any limb that becomes tense, painful out of proportion to the injury, numb or pale is escalated at once, because time is muscle. Whoever applies a splint acts within a named scope of practice, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.
