# Chapter 12 — Clavicle Fractures

The clavicle holds the shoulder out from the middle of the chest and is the only bony link between the arm and the rest of the skeleton, so a break in it changes the way the whole arm hangs. It lies directly under the skin, so the examiner can see and feel what a deeper bone would hide. A fracture of the clavicle is common, and teaching offers it as a little under one in twenty of all fractures in adults, an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. This chapter follows the order used everywhere in this course, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. It is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

The bone is not a straight rod, and its shape explains where it usually breaks. It curves forward in its inner half and backward in its outer half, so seen from above it looks like a shallow letter S and is narrowest at the middle. Teaching describes about four breaks in every five as falling in the middle third, an illustration to be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol. The inner end is anchored by the breastbone and by the muscles of the neck, while the outer end carries the weight of the arm through the shoulder. When the bone breaks between those anchors, the inner fragment is pulled upward and the outer fragment follows the arm downward, which is why a shortened, stepped contour appears instead of a smooth line.

What makes this small bone more than a small problem is what lies against it and beneath it. The vessels that supply the arm and the bundle of nerves called the brachial plexus pass behind the clavicle, separated from the bone by a thin layer of muscle. The apex of the lung and the first rib lie just below the outer third of the bone, so a fracture that drives fragments toward those structures can injure them without a wound on the skin. The state of the arm and of the breathing therefore matters as much as the state of the bone. The skin over the bone is thin, so a sharp fragment can stretch it until it opens, and a pale, shiny, tented area over the break changes the urgency of the case.

Assessment begins before the bone is touched, with the scene and the story of how the injury happened. The common mechanisms are a fall onto the point of the shoulder, a fall onto an outstretched hand, and a direct blow from a collision or a heavy object. The energy involved decides how much else must be suspected, so the responder asks about the height of the fall, the speed of the vehicle and whether consciousness was lost. The history also asks about illnesses and medicines that weaken bone and about smoking, which slows healing, because such a patient may break a bone in a fall that would leave another person unhurt. The time of the injury and the position in which the arm was found are written down at once.

The first task is not the shoulder but the patient, and the primary survey is remembered by the letters ABCDE, which stand for airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure. A patient who has fallen from a height may have a collapsed lung, broken ribs, a head injury or a broken neck as well, and none of those will announce itself while everyone is looking at the shoulder. Breath sounds that are absent on one side, a rising pulse, falling consciousness, or skin that has become cold and damp send the responder back to the beginning and away from the limb. Only when the patient is stable enough does the examination turn to the shoulder, and the limb is then assessed by looking, by feeling and by asking the patient to move it, never by forcing it. The principle that orders these acts is that life comes before limb, because a limb can wait for minutes that a brain and a heart cannot.

The classic picture of this fracture can be recognized from across the room, though not every patient has it. The patient often sits with the head tilted toward the injured side, the shoulder on that side lower and further forward than the other, and the arm supported across the body by the other hand. The patient may be unwilling to lift the arm above the level of the shoulder, and may guard it against any movement at all. Swelling, bruising and a visible or palpable step in the line of the bone follow within hours, and the contours of the two sides are compared from in front and from the side. A patient who is not in distress and who can move the arm freely may still have a crack in the bone, because a break that is not displaced shows only tenderness and a reluctance to lie on that side.

Gentle palpation along the bone belongs to a trained responder or to a clinician rather than to a passer-by. The fingers follow the line of the bone from the notch at the top of the breastbone to the point of the shoulder, feeling for a step, a gap and an area of greatest tenderness. Grinding the fragments together to feel or hear a crackle is not part of the examination, because moving the broken ends deliberately adds pain and can convert a closed injury into an open one. The skin is examined along the whole length of the bone, and a shiny, pale, stretched area over a prominent fragment, which is called tenting, is treated as an injury that is threatening to open. If the skin has already broken, the wound is covered with a sterile dressing, the bleeding that can be controlled is controlled, and the wound is never explored blindly, because definitive closure belongs to the operating theatre.

The neurovascular examination is performed before any intervention and repeated after it, and its result is recorded, because a finding that was not written down before a sling was applied cannot be attributed afterwards. The examiner looks at the color and the temperature of the hand, presses briefly on a nail to watch the color return, and feels the pulse at the wrist. Sensation is tested over the shoulder and along the outer side of the forearm and the hand, and the two sides are compared. The patient is asked whether there is numbness or tingling, and is then asked to move the fingers, the wrist and the elbow, and any new weakness is recorded as a change. An absent pulse is an emergency reported at once, and a pulse that is present does not exclude damage, because a pulse can survive an injury that has already reduced the flow through the smallest vessels.

Disability, the fourth letter of the primary survey, includes the neck, and a high-energy injury that breaks a clavicle can also injure the spine. An injury of the spine is treated as unstable until it is cleared by someone qualified to clear it, and no lone manual handling technique is safe, so a patient who must be moved is moved by a trained team using the precautions the local protocol requires. The broken clavicle can act as a distraction, because it is painful and obvious, while a neck injury is neither. A patient who cannot feel or move a limb, or who describes tingling in both hands or in the feet, is referred urgently. The trained responder and the paramedic work within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, and the physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides what further examination is needed.

Stabilization is a small set of acts, and the most important of them is restraint. The arm is supported in the position in which it was found, because the weight of the arm pulling on the outer fragment causes pain and displacement, and removing that weight is often the most useful thing that can be done at the scene. The lay first aider supports the limb with the hands, a pillow or a folded garment, keeps the patient warm and waits for help, while the trained responder or the paramedic may apply a sling or a splint within the procedures the service authorizes. Nothing is pushed back into a wound, no embedded object is removed, and no deformed limb is straightened in the field, because the vessels and the nerves lie against the bone and a helpful-looking movement can tear what remained intact. Cold is a complication of trauma, so the patient is insulated from the ground and wet clothing is replaced with covering.

Pain relief is part of stabilization, and the decision about it belongs to a prescriber. The substance, the amount, the route and the frequency are set by local protocol and by the clinician who prescribes, and this course deliberately names none of them, because a listener must not be able to take a prescription from a book. The paramedic may give analgesia under the protocol of the service and the authorization of its medical director, while the lay first aider gives nothing and instead supports the arm and reports the pain. Pain that increases instead of settling, or pain out of proportion to the injury, makes the decision urgent. The nurse and the physician reassess the pain after every intervention together with the limb, because a treatment that relieves the bone and leaves the patient worse is not a treatment.

The arm sling is the oldest and still the most useful device for this injury. A triangular bandage, a purpose-made sling, or a length of cloth tied behind the neck all work the same way, supporting the forearm and the elbow so that the weight of the arm is carried by the neck and the trunk instead of by the broken bone. The elbow is kept at the side and the hand higher than the elbow, so that fluid does not pool in the swollen hand, and the material is padded where it crosses the back of the neck. A lay first aider may apply a simple sling as a support while waiting for help, checking that the fingers stay warm and pink and that the bandage does not press on the neck. A sling does not put the broken ends back together, correct a shortened bone or hold the fragments still, so it relieves pain rather than treating the fracture.

The figure-of-eight bandage is the other device a listener will hear named. It passes around both shoulders and crosses at the back, and the theory is that drawing the shoulders backward brings the fragments closer together and holds them there. Studies have not shown that it reliably reduces the displacement or the shortening, and many services have stopped using it. It carries its own harms, including pressure in the armpit, damage to the skin and the nerves of the arm, swelling of the hand, and discomfort that makes the patient loosen it. Where a service still uses it, it is applied by a trained responder or a clinician within the local protocol, never by a lay first aider, and it is checked repeatedly for skin damage, swelling and the state of the fingers. A sling supports the arm comfortably while the figure-of-eight bandage attempts to hold the bone, and the attempt is not reliably successful.

Whatever device is used, the principles of immobilization are the same, and they are checked rather than assumed. The device is padded where it presses on bone or on skin, it holds the limb in the position in which it was found, and it is firm enough to support without becoming a band that stops the circulation. The fingers are examined after application for color, warmth, movement and sensation, and the same examination is repeated at intervals, because a bandage that was comfortable when applied can become tight as the limb swells. The knot of a sling is placed away from the spine and away from the side of the neck that bears the weight, and the armpit is kept dry and inspected for redness or broken skin. The physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides what definitive immobilization is required, and casting and surgical fixation are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol rather than from a book.

Imaging is the step that confirms what the examination has already suggested, and it is ordered by a clinician trained to interpret it. A radiograph is usually the first study, and it is taken in at least two directions so that a displacement in the third direction is not hidden. The joints at both ends of the bone are included in the picture, because a break at either end may be part of an injury of the joint there. Computed tomography shows the detail of a break that enters a joint, and magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for the soft tissues, including the nerves and the vessels. In a child, a view of the other side may be needed, because the growing end of the bone can look like a fracture when it is normal. The lay first aider never orders or interprets imaging, and the choice and the reading belong to the clinician responsible for the care of the patient.

What the image adds is the position of the fragments, the length of the bone, and the condition of the joints at each end. The clinician looks for shortening, which is judged by comparing the two sides rather than by trusting a single view, and for comminution, which warns that the soft tissues are more likely to have suffered. The image also shows whether a separate piece of bone has been pulled away by a ligament, and whether the shoulder blade, the ribs or the breastbone have been injured as well, because a second injury is easily missed when the first one is obvious. What the image cannot show is the state of the nerves, the vessels and the skin, so the neurovascular examination is repeated after imaging as after every other intervention.

Reassessment is performed after every intervention, and it is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the record, because the comparison is what has value rather than any single measurement. The fingers are checked for color, warmth, movement and sensation, and the pulse at the wrist is felt again. The skin under and around the device is inspected, and the neck and the armpit are checked for pressure or for the beginning of a sore. Pain that increases instead of settling, pain out of proportion to the injury, numbness, weakness and a limb that becomes tense and swollen are the findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation. A compartment syndrome is unusual after a fracture of the clavicle, but a responder who suspects it does not wait for confirmation, because the diagnosis is clinical and time is muscle. Within the limits of the local protocol an authorized responder may release a constricting bandage or sling, the limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, the patient is kept warm, and the patient is referred urgently.

Most fractures of the clavicle, in adults and in children alike, heal without an operation, and the treatment is support, time and review by a clinician. The sling is worn until the pain has settled enough for gentle movement, and the clinician then guides the return of movement and strength over the following weeks, with figures such as 6 to 12 weeks offered only as an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. The patient is warned to return if the pain increases, if the hand changes color or feeling, if the skin over the break becomes tight or shiny, or if the arm cannot be moved at all. The nurse and the physician share the work of review, and the orthopedic surgeon is consulted when healing does not follow the expected course, because practice in this area differs between countries and between editions of the same guideline.

Certain findings turn a fracture of the clavicle into an injury that needs a specialist, and they are worth learning by name. Skin tenting is the first, because skin stretched over a sharp fragment is at risk of breaking and turning a closed fracture into an open one, and an open fracture is contaminated at the moment of injury and needs an operating theatre. Shortening of the bone, marked displacement, and comminution into several pieces are the next, because they predict slower union and a shoulder that may not work as it did. A change in the neurovascular status of the arm, whether it is numbness, weakness, a cold hand or a diminished pulse, requires urgent assessment by someone able to examine the nerves and the vessels. A fracture that fails to unite, which is called a nonunion and which is more likely in a patient who smokes, is referred to an orthopedic surgeon rather than treated with more time in a sling.

Definitive treatment begins with the decision about whether the bone must be brought into a better position and held there, and that decision belongs to an orthopedic surgeon. Reduction means restoring the position of the bone ends, and it may be closed, with the fragments moved through the skin, or open, with the fracture exposed in the operating theatre. Traction means a gentle continuous pull that holds the fragments in line, and casting and bracing hold a limb still while the bone heals. Surgical fixation holds the fragments with metal devices, an operation called open reduction and internal fixation, and it is considered for an open fracture, for skin threatened by a fragment, for marked shortening or displacement, and for an injury that involves the nerves or the vessels. Every one of those acts is learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, never from a book.

Even where reduction is being considered, there are situations in which the attempt must not be made, and the reason matters as much as the rule. A suspected complex fracture-dislocation, fragments caught inside the joint, and neurovascular compromise that needs a specialist are situations in which an attempt by the wrong hands can convert an injury that would have healed into one that cannot. An open injury, an unclear diagnosis, inadequate training or equipment, and an earlier attempt that failed are the same kind of warning. The responder stops if there is resistance, if the pain suddenly changes, if the pulse or the sensation changes, or if the limb becomes harder to move, because force is not a technique. The lay first aider, the trained responder, the nurse and the physician all work within the same rule, since the bone is not straightened in the field and the technique of reduction belongs to supervised clinical training.

The child with a fracture of the clavicle deserves attention of its own, because the bone and the patient are different. The bone of a child bends before it breaks, so the injury may be a greenstick fracture, in which one side of the bone is broken and the other is only bent, and it may heal quickly with simple support. A newborn may sustain the fracture during delivery, when the infant does not move the arm, a sign called pseudoparalysis that must be distinguished from an injury of the nerves of the arm. A child is often most comfortable in a sling made from a strip of cloth, applied by a carer under the guidance of the clinician and checked for pressure and for the state of the fingers. A child who is repeatedly injured, or whose story does not match the injury, is referred and protected according to local protocol and the law.

The older adult with a fracture of the clavicle often has a bone that broke more easily than the injury suggests. Bone that has lost density breaks in a fall from standing height, and the same fall in a younger person may leave nothing but a bruise, while the fracture may be less painful than its appearance. A patient who uses a walking aid, or who needs both arms to rise from a chair, loses more independence from this injury than a young athlete does. The threshold for immobilization and for referral is therefore lower and the review is arranged sooner, while the decisions about imaging, about analgesia and about treatment remain with the clinician. The same caution applies to a patient whose bone is weakened by a tumor, by a medicine, or by a disease of the bone marrow, and such a patient is referred for assessment rather than treated with support alone.

The complications of this injury are few but worth naming, because each announces itself in a way the patient can describe. A break that fails to unite, called a nonunion, causes continuing pain and a shoulder that cannot be used, and it is more likely in a patient who smokes. A break that unites in a poor position, called a malunion, leaves a shortened and sometimes painful shoulder, and it may limit the reach of the arm above the head. Injury to the brachial plexus or to the vessels behind the bone is uncommon but serious, and it is why the neurovascular examination is repeated. A fragment that presses on the skin can cause it to break down even after treatment, which is why the skin over the break is inspected at every review. A persistent burning pain with changes in the color and the temperature of the hand may be a complex regional pain syndrome, which needs early assessment.

The record and the handover are part of the treatment rather than a formality that follows it. The time of the injury, the mechanism, the position in which the arm was found, the findings of the neurovascular examination before and after every intervention, the device that was applied, and any change in the condition of the patient are written or spoken in a fixed order. The receiving team then continues a story instead of starting a new one, and a finding that was not recorded before the sling was applied cannot be attributed afterwards, so the record also protects the responder who applied it. A spine that has not been cleared is stated as not cleared, and where this chapter names a time or a proportion, it names an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

Because the scope of practice differs between countries, every intervention in this chapter is attached to the level of provider who may perform it, and the listener must confirm that list against their own law and local protocol. The lay first aider supports the limb in the position found, applies a simple sling if trained to do so, keeps the patient warm, and calls for help. The trained first responder adds the observation of the neurovascular status, the application of a splint or a sling within the equipment the service authorizes, and a written record. The emergency medical technician and the paramedic may give analgesia and choose the position of transport under the protocol of the service and the authorization of its medical director. The nurse and the physician examine, assess and refer, and the physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides about imaging and about definitive treatment. Only the orthopedic surgeon, in an operating theatre with the right team, performs reduction, traction, casting or surgical fixation, and every one of those acts is learned with a teacher.

To summarize, a fracture of the clavicle is recognized by the arm held across the body, the shoulder lower on the injured side, swelling and a step in the line of the bone, and the findings that make it more than simple are skin tenting, a badly displaced bone, an open wound, and any change in the neurovascular status of the arm. The neurovascular examination is performed before any intervention and repeated after it, and its result is recorded, because a finding not recorded before a sling was applied cannot be attributed afterwards. The first device is usually a sling, which supports the weight of the arm without putting the bone ends back together, while the figure-of-eight bandage attempts to hold the bone and does not reliably succeed. The limb is splinted in the position found and is never straightened in the field, an embedded object is never removed, exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, an open wound is never explored blindly, and definitive closure belongs to the operating theatre and to nobody else. The patient is reassessed after every intervention, the skin under the device is inspected, the patient is kept warm, and a patient with threatened skin, a suspected injury to the nerves or the vessels, or a suspected injury of the spine is referred urgently. Most fractures of the clavicle heal with support, time and review, and the decision about reduction, traction, casting or fixation belongs to an orthopedic surgeon, because those techniques are learned under supervision and never from a book.
