# Appendix C — Quick-Reference Fracture Card

This appendix is a card to be listened to before or after an encounter rather than during one, because a responder recalling a region and a referral threshold at once gives neither enough attention. It holds the shape of each regional injury: the findings that identify it, the immediate management any responder may begin, the nerves and vessels at risk, and the urgency of referral. It names the level at which each act belongs, for the lay first aider, the trained first responder, the emergency medical technician, the paramedic, the nurse and the physician. Nothing here is a technique to be learned from a book, and nothing here replaces training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work. Practice differs between countries and between editions of the same guideline, so the protocol of your own service governs what you do. Where a figure is named, it is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

Every region is read through the same sequence, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. Assessment begins with the scene and the mechanism of injury, moves to the primary survey remembered as ABCDE, meaning airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure, and turns to the limb only when the patient is stable. The neurovascular examination is done before any intervention and repeated after it, and its result is recorded, because a finding not written down before a splint was applied cannot be attributed afterwards. Two prohibitions hold everywhere: a deformed limb is splinted in the position found and never straightened in the field, and a wound is never explored blindly, never emptied of an embedded object and never closed outside an operating room. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and they are never taught from this book. The lay first aider supports the limb, controls the bleeding that can be controlled, keeps the patient warm and calls for help, while the trained responder and the paramedic work within the equipment and procedures their service authorizes. Anyone who meets resistance, or who finds the limb more painful, tenser or less sensate after an intervention, stops and escalates, because force is not a technique.

A break of the collarbone shows as pain and swelling over the bone, tenderness at the site and an arm held still against the body. It is usually closed and is managed by supporting the arm in a sling that the trained responder or the clinician applies within local protocol, with the neurovascular status recorded before and after. The nerves and vessels of the arm pass close beneath the bone, so numbness, tingling or a weak hand changes the urgency, and a blow that breaks the shoulder blade has often injured the ribs, the lung or the head as well. A dislocated shoulder shows a squared outline, a hollow where the rounded head of the bone should sit and a patient who holds the arm away and refuses to move it. The nerve wrapping around the surgical neck and supplying the outer shoulder is the one most often injured, so numbness there or weakness in lifting the arm is recorded. A weak pulse or a pale cool hand makes the injury an emergency, and the limb is supported in the position found and transferred urgently to a facility with imaging. Restoration of the joint is not attempted from this book, nor when the diagnosis is unclear, a fragment lies in the joint, the vessels or nerves are failing, or training and equipment are lacking.

A break near the elbow is more dangerous than its size suggests, because the vessel that supplies the forearm crosses the joint in front and the nerves that move the hand lie against the bone. A dislocated elbow shows the bone prominent behind the joint, a fixed position of the arm and swelling that follows quickly. A pulse that vanishes, a hand that is pale or cool or a hand that cannot move makes it an emergency, and the lay first aider does not pull the joint, test its movement or try to straighten the arm. Both bones of the forearm show as pain, swelling, a possible deformity and a refusal to turn the palm. The forearm is a closed space bounded by bone and fascia, so bleeding inside it can cause a compartment syndrome, and a tense, swollen and disproportionately painful forearm is an emergency from the first contact. An inability to make a circle with the thumb and the index finger is a nerve finding to record and report, because the nerves of the forearm supply the muscles that bend the fingers and the thumb. A break of the upper arm bone shows as pain, swelling and a limb the patient supports with the other hand, and the nerve spiraling around it is at risk, showing as a wrist that will not lift and numbness on the back of the hand.

Injuries at the wrist show as pain, swelling, tenderness in the hollow at the base of the thumb and a hand the patient will not use. A break of the far end of the forearm bone is the commonest and may leave the wrist bent backward, shortened or displaced sideways, a finding described for the record and never corrected in the field. The nerve passing through the wrist to the hand is at risk, so numbness in the palm, the thumb or the fingers is recorded before splinting and again after. Support on a padded splint that holds the wrist as it was found, protection of the joints above and below, warmth and referral are the acts of the responder, because the final position belongs to the clinician. A small wrist bone may hurt only when the hollow at the base of the thumb is pressed, so a wrist that stays painful after a fall deserves imaging. A dislocation of those small bones is suspected when the wrist is markedly swollen, the fingers cannot be felt properly or the hand is pale, and a numb, white or cool finger is an emergency for the hand. The hand itself shows as swelling, a finger rotated or shortened and a reluctance to make a fist, and a dislocated finger is never pulled back into place from a book, nor when the skin is broken, a fragment lies in the joint or the diagnosis is unclear.

The pelvis shows as pain in the groin, the hip or the lower back after a high-energy injury, tenderness over the pelvic bones and an inability to bear weight. Bleeding into the pelvis can be massive and hidden, so a pale, cool and anxious patient with a rising pulse is treated as a patient in shock. The pelvis is not sprung apart or pressed repeatedly to test it, and a break of the socket of the hip joint or of the bone running from hip to knee shows as pain, a shortened and outwardly rotated limb and an inability to lift the leg. Support the limb as found, keep the patient flat and warm, control the bleeding that can be controlled and arrange urgent transfer with the pelvic binder or splint that local protocol authorizes. A dislocated hip shows a limb fixed in an abnormal position, severe pain and an immovable joint, and the nerve running behind the hip is at risk, so sensation and movement at the foot and ankle are recorded. Restoring a hip to its socket is done by a clinician with sedation or anesthesia in a facility with imaging, and it is urgent because the blood supply of the head of the bone is at stake. None of these injuries is manipulated, straightened or moved without immobilization, and the part of the responder is support, warmth, the neurovascular record and rapid transfer.

A break of the thigh bone shows as severe pain, swelling, a guarded deformity and a limb that is shortened and rotated, and the hidden bleeding can threaten the circulation, so a pale, restless patient with a rapid pulse is treated as a patient in shock. Immobilize the limb as found with the joints above and below controlled, using the splints or traction device the trained responder's service authorizes, and never straighten the limb to make the splint fit. The vessel running from groin to knee and the nerve supplying the front of the thigh are at risk, so the foot pulse, the color and warmth of the skin and the movement and sensation of the foot are recorded before and after immobilization. Pain out of proportion, a tense thigh, numbness and weakness raise the suspicion of a compartment syndrome there, which is uncommon but severe, and the responder acts within local protocol instead of waiting for confirmation. A break just above the knee shows as swelling, joint pain and a knee that will not straighten, and it threatens the vessels behind the knee. Traction, casting and surgical fixation are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and the responder keeps the patient warm, records what is found and transfers without delay.

The kneecap shows as swelling of the joint, a gap or a tender fragment and pain when the knee is straightened, and the skin over it is inspected because the bone lies directly beneath. A dislocated knee is a rare injury of great violence, showing an obvious deformity, an immovable joint and a knee already very swollen, and the vessel behind the knee and the nerve crossing the outer side are both at risk, so a pulse that is present does not exclude damage to the vessel. Support as found, immobilize within the equipment and procedures the service authorizes, keep the patient warm, record the neurovascular status before and after, and arrange the most urgent transfer the service can provide. A break of the upper end of the shin bone shows as swelling, pain on the inner or outer side of the knee and an inability to bear weight, and its seriousness is rarely visible from outside. A break of the shin bone in its shaft is the broken bone most likely to be open because it lies just under the skin, and it shows as pain, swelling, a visible deformity and an inability to bear weight. The compartments of the lower leg are tight, so a break there is a classic cause of an acute compartment syndrome, recognized by pain that grows instead of settling, pain when the muscles are stretched, a tense swollen limb and numbness or weakness in the foot. A pulse that can still be felt does not exclude it, so immobilize the joints above and below, keep the limb at the level of the heart and never above it, and release a constricting bandage, splint or cast only if the local protocol authorizes you to do so, because time is muscle.

The ankle shows as swelling, bruising, tenderness over one or both bony prominences and pain on weight bearing. A break of the inner or outer prominence is the common form, and the joint may be visibly displaced, which is described for the record and never corrected in the field. Support the ankle as found on a padded splint that holds the foot as it was found, include the joints above and below, protect the limb from cold and refer for imaging. The nerve behind the inner prominence and the artery beside it are at risk, and the tendon crossing the front of the ankle can be injured by the same force, so sensation over the sole, the pulse behind the inner prominence and the ability to pull the foot up are recorded before and after every intervention. A tear of the ligament binding the two bones of the lower leg together shows as tenderness above the ankle and pain when the foot is turned outward, and it is easily missed. Restoring a displaced ankle belongs to the clinician, is often urgent because the skin over the bone can fail, and the responder never tests the joint by forcing it.

The heel bone shows as swelling of the heel, bruising of the sole, pain that prevents weight bearing and a history of a fall from a height, and the injury is often worse than the swelling suggests. Support the foot as found, immobilize the ankle and the foot together within the procedures the service authorizes, keep the patient warm and refer urgently for imaging. A break of the small bone below the ankle joint that carries the weight of the body shows as pain and swelling around the ankle and tenderness deep to the bony prominences, and it is serious because the blood supply of that bone is fragile. The middle of the foot shows as swelling, bruising across the sole and pain when it is squeezed, and a dislocation there can be subtle while the injury is severe. The nerves of the foot are at risk, so numbness, a loss of light touch and a foot that is pale or cool are recorded and reported at once. Breaks of the long bones of the foot and of the toes show as swelling, tenderness and pain on weight bearing, and their urgency is set by the skin and the circulation, while dislocations of the foot are never restored by the responder and definitive care belongs to the clinician.

A suspected spinal injury shows as pain in the neck or back after a fall, a collision or a blow, tenderness over the bones and any numbness, tingling or weakness in the limbs, and the absence of pain does not exclude a serious injury. Such a patient is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear the spine has done so, and no lone manual handling technique is safe, so the patient is moved by a trained team using the precautions the local protocol requires. Keep the head and trunk as found, maintain the airway and the breathing while protecting the neck, keep the patient warm and transfer to a facility able to assess the spine. An injury of the spinal cord is suggested by weakness or paralysis, loss of sensation, loss of bladder control and a changed pattern of breathing, and the level of the injury decides what the patient has lost. Some patients break bones more easily and heal them less predictably, so the card is used with a lower threshold in each of them. The bone of a child bends before it breaks, and a growth region near the end of a bone can be injured without a break, while an older adult whose bone has lost density may break a hip or a wrist in a fall from standing height with far less pain than the injury deserves. Pain relief, antibiotics and tetanus protection belong to the prescriber and the local protocol, and the substance, the amount and the way it is given are never taken from this course.

An open fracture shows a wound near the bone, blood on the skin or bone that is visible, and it is contaminated even when it looks clean, which makes it an emergency at every level. Cover the wound with a sterile dressing, control the bleeding that can be controlled, immobilize the limb as found, record the neurovascular status before and after, protect against hypothermia and transfer to a facility with an operating room. The person who cleans, explores or closes such a wound outside an operating room is doing harm, and no listener to this card should be that person. Urgency can be read from a few findings rather than from a diagnosis: an absent or diminished pulse, a pale or cool limb, a part that is numb or weak, a wound over the bone, a deformity that threatens the skin, a suspected compartment syndrome, a suspected spinal injury or an unstable patient means emergency transfer with the receiving team warned in advance. A closed injury that is neurovascularly intact and comfortable in its splint is transferred promptly but without the same alarm, and every figure in this card is an illustration, because a teaching value for the time within which an open injury should reach surgery differs between services and countries. Gloves, eye protection and a barrier for the face are worn for every contact with blood or body fluid, and the record and the handover carry the time of injury, the mechanism, the position in which the limb was found and the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention, so that the receiving team continues a story rather than starting a new one.

To summarize, this card is a memory aid for the road and the bedside rather than a manual of procedures, and it is listened to before or after an encounter and never in place of the protocol of the service. In every region the order is the same: assess, stabilize, examine the neurovascular status, immobilize, image, reassess, and then refer or treat definitively, with the neurovascular findings recorded before every intervention and repeated after it. The findings that make an injury an emergency are a pulse that is absent or weak, a limb that is pale, cool, numb or weak, a wound over or near the bone, a deformity that threatens the skin, pain that grows instead of settling, a limb that is tense and swollen, and a patient who is unstable. A deformed limb is splinted in the position found and never straightened in the field, a wound is never explored blindly, exposed bone is never pushed back, an embedded object is never removed, and definitive closure belongs to the operating room. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and they are not attempted when the diagnosis is unclear, when a fragment lies in the joint, when the vessels or nerves are failing or when training and equipment are lacking. Cold is treated as a complication, the spine is treated as unstable until someone qualified clears it, and the patient is kept warm, comforted and watched until the handover. The lay first aider supports, warms and calls for help, the trained responder works within what the service authorizes, and the physician or the surgeon decides what definitive treatment is required. Where this card names a time, a distance or a threshold, the figure is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol before it is used.
