# Chapter 14 — Shoulder Dislocation

The shoulder trades stability for movement, and it pays for that freedom with the highest rate of dislocation of any joint in the body. A dislocation means the joint surfaces are no longer in contact at all, and in the shoulder the head of the upper arm bone leaves its socket and usually travels forward and downward, which is why this injury is called an anterior dislocation. The patient is almost always in pain, guards the arm and refuses to let anyone move it, and the shape of the shoulder can be unmistakable once the examiner knows what to look for. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work. Every injury in this course is taught through one order, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and the step that protects the patient most is the neurovascular examination recorded before anything is done.

The commonest mechanism is a fall onto the outstretched hand while the arm is lifted away from the body and turned outward, because that position levers the head of the bone against the front and lower edge of the socket until it tears through the capsule and slips out. A direct blow from behind the shoulder, a tackle that forces the arm backward and a violent wrench of the arm in sports can produce the same displacement in other ways. The head usually comes to rest below a bony prominence at the front of the shoulder called the coracoid process, and the socket behind it is left empty. Whatever the mechanism, the energy does not stop at the joint, so the examiner thinks about the whole limb and about the rest of the patient, and the shape of the shoulder is only one part of the picture. Ask what happened, when it happened and whether the shoulder has come out before, and write the answers down, because each of them changes what should be done next.

What the responder sees is described as a squared-off outline, and the phrase is worth unpacking because it is the key to recognition. The normal shoulder has a rounded curve formed by the deltoid muscle lying over the head of the bone, and when the head leaves the socket that curve is lost, so the outer edge of the shoulder falls into a straighter line and the acromion stands out with a hollow beneath it. The arm is held away from the body rather than against it, in a position of slight abduction with the hand turned outward, and the patient supports the forearm with the other hand to ease the pull on the joint. An attempt to bring the elbow across the chest is resisted, and no one should try to prove the diagnosis by forcing that movement. Swelling and bruising appear with time and may be modest at first, so the shape of the shoulder is more useful early than the amount of swelling.

Assessment begins with the scene and with the mechanism, then moves to the primary survey, whose steps are remembered as ABCDE, meaning airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure. Life comes before limb, so a patient who fell from a height or who is confused after a blow to the head is stabilized before anyone turns attention to the shoulder. The shoulder may be the loudest injury and not the most dangerous one, and a distracting deformity can hide a head injury, a chest injury, an injury of the spine or bleeding elsewhere. Once the patient is stable enough, the history continues with the time of the injury, the position of the arm at impact, whether the shoulder has dislocated before, and whether the patient takes a medicine or has an illness that weakens bone. A patient whose shoulder has dislocated several times may have put it back without help, but that history does not remove the need for examination and imaging, because a repeated dislocation can still carry a fracture or an injury of a nerve. Ask whether the patient can move the fingers and whether the hand feels normal, and record the answer in the words the patient uses.

Stabilization means keeping the patient warm, controlling the bleeding that can be controlled, and supporting the injured part in the position in which it was found. The arm is not pulled, twisted, lifted or straightened, and reduction is not attempted outside a setting where it belongs, because the head of the bone lies against a network of nerves and against the artery that supplies the arm, and a movement that looks helpful can tear a vessel or a nerve that was only stretched. The lay first aider supports the arm with the hands, with a pillow or with a sling improvised from clothing, keeps the patient warm and waits for help. The trained first responder and the paramedic may apply a support within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, without testing the joint by moving it. Cold is a complication of trauma rather than a detail of comfort, so the patient is insulated from the ground and wet clothing is replaced with covering. Nothing is placed under the arm that levers the elbow away from the body, because the position found is the position protected.

The neurovascular examination is performed before any intervention and repeated after it, and its result is recorded, because a finding that was not written down before a support was applied cannot be attributed afterwards. Begin by looking at the hand for color, swelling and the way the fingers sit, then feel the temperature of the skin and the pulse at the wrist. The pulse is felt at the wrist and, by a trained examiner, at the elbow, and a finger is pressed to watch the color return. Sensation is tested on both sides, and the area to test with particular care is the skin over the outer and upper part of the shoulder, which is supplied by the axillary nerve. That nerve winds around the surgical neck of the bone and is the nerve most often injured when the shoulder dislocates, so its territory is examined before anything is done and again afterwards. Movement is tested by asking the patient to make a fist, to straighten the fingers, to spread them and to bend the wrist. A responder who finds numbness, tingling, weakness or a lost movement does not repeat the test to be sure, but records it, protects the limb and escalates.

An examination that is painful and incomplete is still worth doing, and the record must say what was possible rather than what was assumed. A patient in severe pain may not cooperate with testing of the deltoid muscle, and swelling over the shoulder can blunt the sensation of a light touch, so apparently normal sensation in the first minutes does not exclude an injury of the nerve. The examiner therefore writes down the words the patient used and the movements the patient could perform, and states plainly when a test could not be done, because the next clinician needs to know the difference between a nerve that was working and a nerve that was never tested. The strength of the shoulder itself is not tested by the responder, because asking the patient to lift the arm against resistance moves the dislocated joint and can add harm. The lay first aider observes color, warmth and the ability to move and to feel, and reports any change at once, while the formal examination belongs to the trained responder, the nurse or the physician.

The axillary nerve and the axillary artery both pass close to the lower part of the shoulder joint, and that anatomy is why movement is feared in this injury. Injury of the nerve is common after a shoulder dislocation and is usually a stretch rather than a tear, so it often recovers, but the recovery begins with a finding that somebody wrote down at the right moment. Injury of the artery is uncommon and dangerous, and it is more likely in an older patient whose vessels are stiffened by disease and in a shoulder that has been out of place for many hours. The findings that suggest damage to a vessel are a hand that is pale or cool, a pulse that is weak or absent, pain out of proportion to the injury, and a shoulder that swells rapidly or bruises widely, and those findings make the injury an emergency. A large collection of blood in the shoulder is not a reason to press on the shoulder, to wrap it tightly or to delay transfer, because pressure applied from outside does not stop bleeding from a torn artery inside.

Immobilization means preventing movement of the injured part and of the joints above and below it, and in the shoulder that means supporting the arm so that its weight does not pull on the joint. A sling that holds the forearm in front of the body, with the arm as it was found, is the usual support, and a bandage that encircles the chest and the arm may be added by someone working within a protocol that authorizes it. The sling is padded where it presses, secured firmly enough to hold and not so firmly that it becomes a band, and checked after it is applied and again after the patient has been moved. The material matters less than the principle, since a triangular bandage, a strip of cloth or a pinned shirt can each serve. In an anterior dislocation the arm is not forced across the chest and the elbow is not pulled down toward the opposite hip, because that movement levers the head of the bone and can increase the damage. The lay first aider may improvise a sling, the trained responder and the paramedic apply and check support within their procedures, and the nurse or the physician decides what support is used once the patient arrives. Immobilization is a step on the way to imaging and to definitive care, not a treatment that settles the diagnosis.

Imaging is the step that confirms what the examination has already suggested, and it is ordered by a clinician trained to interpret it. A radiograph is the first study, taken in at least two directions, because a dislocation that is obvious from one view may be invisible from another, and the joints above and below are included so that a second injury is not missed. The clinician looks for the empty socket, for the position of the head of the bone, and for a fracture of the rim of the socket or of the neck of the bone, because any of those changes what can safely be done next. Computed tomography shows the detail of a complex break around the joint and is requested when the plain pictures leave the diagnosis in doubt, especially when a backward dislocation is suspected. Magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for soft tissues, including ligaments, cartilage, tendons and muscle, and it is rarely the first study in an acute dislocation. The lay first aider never orders or interprets imaging, the trained responder does not choose the study, and the decision belongs to the physician or the orthopedic surgeon responsible for the care of the patient. An image is a picture of one moment, and the patient must still be examined afterwards.

Reassessment is performed after every intervention, and it is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the record, and it is the comparison that carries the value rather than any single measurement. The hand and the forearm are checked again for color, warmth, pulse, sensation, movement and pain, and the support is checked for tightness and for pressure points. Pain that increases instead of settling, pain out of proportion to the injury, numbness, tingling, weakness, pallor, coolness, and a limb that becomes tense and swollen are the findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation. A compartment syndrome is uncommon in the shoulder itself, but it can follow a severe injury of the arm or a bandage that is too tight, and because the diagnosis is clinical a responder who suspects it does not wait for confirmation. The constricting bandage or support is released within the limits of local protocol, the limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, the patient is kept warm, and the patient is referred urgently, because time is muscle.

Referral or definitive treatment is the final step of the sequence, and a dislocated shoulder is almost always referred, because the joint must be put back into place and the patient must be examined afterwards by someone qualified to do it. Reduction means restoring the surfaces of the joint to their normal contact, and its purposes are to relieve the pull on the nerves and the vessels and to ease pain. A shoulder that has been out of place for a long time is harder to reduce, and any figure offered for a safe interval before reduction is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between services and editions. The reduction is learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol by a physician or an orthopedic surgeon who is trained and authorized to do it, and the technique belongs to supervised clinical training, not to a book. Whether the patient is given pain relief or sedation, and which substance in which amount, is decided by the prescriber within the local protocol, and no dose, route or regimen may be taken from this course. A dislocation is not reduced in the field or in an ambulance by an untrained hand, because the nerves and the vessels lie against the bone.

There are situations in which an attempt at reduction is not made at all, or is deferred to a place with an operating room and a specialist, and each one has a reason worth saying aloud. A suspected complex fracture-dislocation is not reduced, because the attempt can drive a fragment further into the joint or into a vessel. Entrapped fracture fragments are not worked free by force, because force converts a joint that could be repaired into one that cannot. A limb whose vessels or nerves are failing is not manipulated, because the specialist who will repair them needs to see the injury before it is disturbed. An open injury is not reduced in the field, because the wound must be covered, the contamination must be managed in an operating room, and an embedded object is never removed. An unclear diagnosis is not treated by an attempt, because a backward dislocation handled as an ordinary forward dislocation can be made much worse. Inadequate training or equipment, and an earlier failed attempt, are also reasons to stop, and a failed attempt is not repeated by the same person, because repetition multiplies the damage without improving the chance of success.

After the joint has been put back, the neurovascular examination is repeated and recorded again, and a finding that has changed is reported at once rather than at the end of the shift. The clinician repeats the imaging to confirm that the joint surfaces are in contact and that no fracture has moved, and the limb is supported in the position the clinician chooses. The length of time a shoulder is supported after a first dislocation depends on the age of the patient, on whether the injury is a first or a repeated one and on the practice of the treating service, so any number of weeks offered in teaching is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. A patient who is at risk of a further dislocation is taught the movements to avoid and the exercises that maintain movement, and that instruction is given by the clinician or by a physiotherapist within the treating service. The task of the responder does not end at the door of the emergency department, because reassessment after every intervention continues through every movement of the patient.

Several complications are worth naming, because each of them is looked for rather than waited for. A shoulder that has dislocated once is more likely to dislocate again, and the risk is greater in a younger patient and in an injury that tore the front of the socket, so a young patient with a first dislocation deserves a clear plan rather than reassurance alone. A fracture of the greater tuberosity, of the rim of the socket or of the neck of the bone may accompany the dislocation, and it may appear only on a later radiograph or on a computed tomography study. A tear of the rotator cuff is more common in an older patient, and its sign, weakness of the shoulder, may be hidden by pain at first, so a shoulder that stays weak after the pain settles is not a failure of effort. An injury of a nerve that was not recorded before a reduction cannot be attributed afterwards, which is one reason the first examination is written down and kept. Stiffness and lasting loss of movement are the quiet complications, and they are limited by the treatment and by the exercises the treating clinician prescribes.

Some patients need a lower threshold for concern rather than a different sequence. A child who will not use an arm after a fall may have an injury of the growing region near the end of the bone rather than a dislocation, and the examination of a child is limited by fear and by the difficulty a child has in describing what is felt, so the limb is protected and the child is referred. An older adult whose bone has lost density may dislocate a shoulder and break it at the same time, and the fracture may be the part that is missed because the dislocation is so obvious. A patient who has been injured during a seizure, an electric shock or a period of unconsciousness may have dislocated the shoulder backward, and a backward dislocation is easily missed because the arm lies across the body and the shoulder may look almost normal. In every one of these patients the sequence does not change, and what changes is how quickly the patient is passed to a clinician.

The record and the handover are part of the treatment rather than a formality that follows it. The time of the injury, the mechanism, the position in which the arm was found, the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention, what was applied and when, and any change in the condition of the patient are written or spoken in a fixed order, so that the receiving team continues a story instead of starting a new one. The words the patient used about numbness, tingling or weakness are more valuable than any paraphrase, and so are the words the examiner used about what could not be tested. A handover states plainly that the shoulder is dislocated, that the arm was supported in the position found, that no reduction was attempted, and what the neurovascular findings were at the last assessment. A clear handover also names what the responder is worried about, such as a pulse that has become harder to feel or pain that is climbing, because that is what decides how quickly the patient is seen.

Scope of practice decides who performs each act, and the whole list is worth gathering in one place. The lay first aider supports the arm, keeps the patient warm, observes color, warmth, movement and feeling, and calls for help, and those acts need no equipment and no authorization. The trained first responder and the paramedic may apply and check a support, monitor the limb and record the findings within the procedures their service authorizes, and they do not reduce a dislocation, do not test the joint by moving it and do not give medication outside a protocol that names the substance and the circumstances. The nurse assesses, records, reassesses, positions, supports and escalates, and administers medication only as it has been prescribed and permitted. The physician examines, orders imaging, decides on pain relief within the local protocol, and either reduces the joint or refers the patient. The orthopedic surgeon manages the reduction when it is complex, treats the associated fracture, and decides on rehabilitation and on the return to activity.

Pain in a dislocated shoulder is severe, and the response to it is limited by scope of practice rather than by indifference. The lay first aider does not give medication, and the trained responder or paramedic gives pain relief only where a protocol authorizes it, because a substance given by the wrong route or in the wrong circumstance turns one injury into two emergencies. The findings that make pain relief urgent are pain that prevents the patient from cooperating with the examination, pain that is rising, and pain accompanied by numbness, tingling or a change in the pulse, and that combination is reported at once. The substance, the amount, the route and the frequency are decided by the prescriber within the local protocol, and none of them may be taken from this course. The nurse who administers what has been prescribed reassesses the patient afterwards, watching for drowsiness, for a change in breathing and for any change in the limb, because sedation can mask the very findings that matter. Comfort is a clinical objective, and treating it well protects the limb.

The fall that dislocated the shoulder may also have injured the neck or the back, and an injury of the spine is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has done so. No lone manual handling technique is safe, so the patient is moved by a trained team using the precautions the local protocol requires, and the arm is supported during every part of that move. A patient who is confused, who has lost consciousness or who cannot describe the pain may have injuries that are not yet known, and the shoulder is then only one part of a larger problem. The responder keeps the patient warm, keeps the limb supported, records what changes, and hands the patient on with the story intact.

Talking to the patient is a clinical act rather than a courtesy. Explain that the shoulder is out of its socket, that a clinician will put it back, that the arm will not be moved until then, and that the responder will stay with the patient and watch the hand. Ask the patient to report numbness, tingling, a change in the temperature of the hand or pain that is getting worse, and say why that report matters, because a patient who understands that movement may add damage is far easier to keep still. Consent for the examination and for any intervention is part of the same conversation, and a refusal is respected and recorded.

Force is not a technique, and the shoulder is the joint where that sentence is easiest to forget. Pulling on an arm to see whether the shoulder slips back is an attempt at reduction, and it is forbidden to anyone without the training, the authorization and the setting to do it properly. When a movement meets resistance, when pain suddenly increases, when the hand becomes pale or cold, or when a pulse disappears, the intervention stops and the patient is escalated rather than persuaded. An open injury of the shoulder changes the priority rather than the sequence, so the wound is covered with a sterile dressing, the bleeding that can be controlled is controlled, the neurovascular status is recorded, the limb is immobilized in the position found, the patient is kept warm, and the patient is transferred urgently to a facility with an operating room. The wound is never explored blindly, exposed bone is never pushed back into it, and an embedded object is never removed, because the object may be holding pressure on a torn vessel. Definitive cleaning and closure belong to the operating room and to nobody else, and a dislocation with an open wound is not reduced by the responder.

To summarize, a dislocated shoulder is an injury of the whole limb, and the squared-off outline, the arm held away from the body and the pain that resists movement are the findings that identify it. The neurovascular examination, and in particular the sensation over the outer part of the shoulder and the movement of the fingers, is performed and recorded before any intervention and repeated after every one, because a finding that was not written down cannot be attributed afterwards. The limb is supported in the position found, the patient is kept warm, the spine is treated as unstable until someone qualified has cleared it, and nothing is pulled, straightened or tested by movement. Reduction and its techniques are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol by a clinician trained and authorized to do it, and a reduction is not attempted in the field, when the diagnosis is unclear, when the injury is open, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when a vessel or a nerve is failing, when the equipment or the training is inadequate, or after an earlier failed attempt. Pain relief and every medicine belong to the prescriber and to the local protocol, and no dose, route or regimen may be taken from this course. The patient is reassessed after every intervention, the findings are handed over in a fixed order, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.
