# Chapter 19 — Forearm Fractures

The forearm is two bones rather than one, and that fact decides almost everything about how its fractures behave. The radius lies on the thumb side and the ulna on the little finger side, and the two are joined along most of their length by a strong sheet of fibrous tissue called the interosseous membrane. Together the bones, that membrane and the joints at each end form a ring, and a ring cannot fail in only one place. A fracture of one bone is therefore treated as an injury of the whole forearm until someone qualified has shown otherwise, and the examination and the imaging must reach the elbow above and the wrist below. This chapter follows the order used in every chapter of this course, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively.

The reason the forearm has two bones is rotation. When the radius turns around the ulna the palm turns from facing forward to facing backward, and that movement places the hand where the eye and the work need it. A fracture that heals with the bones in the wrong rotational relationship, even one that looks straight on a picture, costs the patient the ability to turn the palm, and that loss is felt in eating, in dressing and in every task that asks the hand to be turned. The examiner compares the two sides, notes which way the palm and the thumb are pointing, and records that before anything is applied to the limb.

The ulna is easy to feel along its whole length because its border lies just under the skin, and that same lack of covering is why a break of the ulna is more likely than most fractures to open through the skin. The nerves of the forearm travel in the spaces around the bones, with the median nerve on the palm side, the ulnar nerve along the little finger side of the ulna, and the radial nerve winding around the upper radius. The arteries follow a similar path, one on the thumb side and one on the little finger side, and the hand is fed by both of them. A pulse at the wrist may therefore survive the loss of one artery while the other is already compromised, which is one reason the examination looks for more than a pulse.

Forearm fractures arrive by a small number of routes, and the mechanism tells the responder what else to suspect. A fall onto an outstretched hand drives force up the radius from the wrist and is the commonest route to a break of the lower forearm. A direct blow, a fall from a height, a road collision or a crush sends energy across both bones at once and is the setting in which the two are broken together. A raised forearm used to shield the head can produce a break of the ulna near the elbow together with a dislocation at the elbow, and a fall onto a fixed hand can produce the mirror image at the wrist. The responder records the mechanism in the words of the patient or of a witness, because the pattern of injury is often written in that account.

Assessment begins before any hand is laid on the limb. The responder looks at the position of the arm, the shape of the forearm, the direction in which the palm and the thumb are facing, and whether the skin is broken. A forearm that is bent, shortened or twisted is left exactly as it is found, because the broken ends may be lying against a vessel or a nerve, so a well meaning attempt to make the limb look straight can tear what is still intact. The responder asks about pain, about numbness or tingling in the hand and about whether the fingers can be moved, and compares what is said with the other side. Tenderness is sought along both bones and at the elbow and the wrist, because pain in either joint points to a second injury that a break of one bone alone would not explain. The findings are said aloud and written down with the time, because a finding that was not recorded before a splint was applied cannot be attributed afterwards.

The rule that governs this region is that a break of one bone is presumed to have injured the other bone or the membrane between them until that has been excluded. A single break can displace only if the ring has failed somewhere else, so the examiner looks for tenderness over the other bone, for a step in its surface, and for pain when the forearm is turned. The membrane can tear without a second fracture, and that injury alone can allow the two bones to come together and to block rotation. When any part of this picture is present, the responder stops testing, immobilizes the limb in the position found, and refers the patient to a clinician who can examine and image the whole forearm.

The findings of a both bone injury are usually plain, and they are worth describing in the order in which a listener would notice them. The forearm looks shorter than the other one, it is bent, and the hand hangs or points in a direction the patient cannot correct. The smooth ridge of the ulna, which should run as one continuous line under the skin, is interrupted by a step, and the width of the arm is increased by swelling that is already present at the first examination. The skin over that ridge is examined with particular care, because a sharp end of bone lying just beneath it can break through during handling, and a wound of any size means an open fracture. The palm may lie face down when the patient is sure it was face up before the injury, and that single observation tells the examiner that the bones have rotated.

Two patterns of injury show how closely the elbow, the forearm and the wrist are bound together. In the first, the ulna breaks near the elbow and the head of the radius slips out of its joint at the same time, and the injury at the elbow is easily missed because attention is drawn to the obvious break below it. In the second, the radius breaks near the wrist and the joint between the two lower ends of the bones is disrupted, so the ulna rides out of line at the wrist while the radius alone appears broken on the picture. Both patterns carry the names of the clinicians who first described them, and both show that the ring has failed in two places. The treatment of both belongs to an orthopedic surgeon, and neither is corrected in the field.

Stabilization of a forearm injury is a small set of acts performed in a fixed order, and the first of them is restraint. The limb is supported in the position in which it was found, with the hands, with a pillow, or with whatever padding is at hand, and it is never straightened, because force applied to a deformed forearm can convert a closed injury into an open one and can release the pressure that was holding a torn vessel closed. Bleeding from an open wound is controlled by the pressure that can be applied, without blind exploration of the wound and without removing anything embedded in it, and exposed bone is covered and left where it lies. The patient is kept warm, wet clothing is replaced with covering, and the limb is protected from the cold ground, because cold is a complication of trauma and not a detail of comfort. The lay first aider performs the acts that need no equipment and calls for help, while the trained responder and the paramedic act within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes.

Transport is part of stabilization and not a separate errand. A patient with a forearm injury is moved in the position of greatest comfort, usually sitting if there is no other reason to lie flat, with the forearm supported across the chest and never allowed to hang by the side, because a dependent hand swells quickly. The limb is supported by the hands of the responder and by the splint together, and the movement is planned aloud before it is made so that no one pulls on the injured arm. Where the mechanism could have loaded the spine, the spine is treated as unstable until it is cleared by someone qualified to clear it, and such a patient is moved by a trained team using the precautions the local protocol requires.

The neurovascular examination is performed before any intervention and repeated after it, and in the forearm it is the step that decides how urgently the patient must move. The examiner looks at the color of the hand and at whether the fingers are pale, dusky or mottled, and feels the temperature of the hand against the other side. The pulse is felt at the wrist on the thumb side and on the little finger side, and the time taken for color to return after a nail is pressed is noted. Sensation is tested in the web between the thumb and the index finger, along the little finger and over the back of the hand, and the patient is asked to spread the fingers, to make a fist, to bend the wrist and to straighten the thumb, because each of those movements depends on a different nerve. A pulse that is present does not exclude injury to an artery or to a compartment, and a pulse that is absent is an emergency that overrides everything else.

The forearm holds several closed compartments, and the one on the palm side is the one most often threatened by bleeding and swelling after a fracture of both bones. Because the fascia does not stretch, the pressure inside rises rather than dispersing, and the smallest vessels fail first, so muscle and nerve are starved while the pulse at the wrist may still be easily felt. The earliest and most reliable finding is pain that is out of proportion to the injury and that increases rather than settles, particularly when the fingers are straightened by someone else, and it is followed by a limb that feels tense and hard to the touch. Numbness and tingling in the fingers, weakness, and a hand that feels cool or looks pale come later, and a responder who waits for them has waited too long. The diagnosis is clinical and is made by a clinician, and time is muscle, so the responder acts on suspicion within the limits of the local protocol and does not wait for a confirmation that will arrive too late. The arm is not elevated above the level of the heart, because that lowers the pressure that is keeping what flow remains, and the patient is referred urgently.

Immobilization means preventing movement of the injured bones and of the joints above and below them, and in the forearm that means the support must span the elbow and the wrist. A support that holds only the bones between the two joints allows the broken ends to move every time the elbow or the wrist is used, and that movement is exactly what it was meant to prevent. The splint is padded where it presses on a bony point, it is applied over the position in which the limb was found, and it is secured firmly enough to hold without becoming a band that stops the circulation. A sling supports the weight of the forearm and is adjusted so that the hand is not left hanging below the elbow, since a dependent hand swells. Casting, traction and definitive immobilization are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and none of them is taught from a book in this course.

The trained responder and the paramedic may apply a splint within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, while the nurse may do so within the standing orders of the ward or the clinic, and the physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides what definitive immobilization the injury requires. The moment the splint is secured, the neurovascular examination is repeated in the same order and compared with what was recorded before, because a splint that is too tight shows itself first in the fingers and not in the forearm. The responder checks that the fingers are warm, that their color is normal, that they can be moved and that sensation has not changed, and asks whether the pain has changed. If the pain is worse, if the fingers tingle or feel numb, or if the hand has become pale or cool, the splint is loosened at once by whoever is authorized to do it and the patient is escalated. An intervention that is followed by a worsening examination has failed, and the correct response is not to wait and see.

Imaging confirms what the examination has already suggested, and it is ordered by a clinician trained to interpret it. A radiograph is usually the first study, and for a forearm injury it must show the whole length of both bones together with the elbow above and the wrist below, since a picture of the shaft alone hides the dislocation that so often accompanies the break. Computed tomography is used when the pattern near a joint is complex or when the relationship of the fragments is unclear, and magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for the soft tissues, including the membrane between the bones and the ligaments around the joints. Each study is requested only when the answer it gives will change what is done, and the choice belongs to the clinician responsible for the patient. The lay first aider never orders or interprets an image, and the trained responder does not decide which study is needed.

An image is a picture of one moment, and it does not replace the examination or the reassessment. A fracture can be present when the first picture looks normal, particularly in a child, and a fracture can displace after the picture was taken, so a limb that is more painful or more swollen than the image suggests is treated according to the limb and not according to the picture. The same caution applies at the other end of life, where a bone that has lost density can break with little force and where the injury may hurt less than its severity suggests. Where this chapter names a time, a size or a distance, it names an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between services, countries and editions.

The reassessment that follows immobilization is repeated at intervals, and its purpose is to catch the complication caused by the treatment rather than the one caused by the injury. Swelling inside a splint or a cast has nowhere to go, so the limb is checked for the pain, the tightness and the loss of movement that mark a rising pressure, and the fingers are checked for warmth, color, movement and sensation. A bandage that encircles the limb, a cast applied over an arm that later swelled, or a splint secured too tightly can each produce the harm, and each is released within the limits of the local protocol by someone authorized to do so. The pain of a forearm fracture should settle with support and with the measures the local protocol provides for comfort, and pain that climbs despite them is the finding that stops the process and sends the patient for urgent review.

Referral or definitive treatment is the last step of the sequence, and in the forearm the decision turns on how many parts of the ring have failed and how well the soft tissues have tolerated it. A fracture of both bones in an adult is treated as an unstable injury and is referred to an orthopedic surgeon, because the two bones cannot hold each other in place and the position a splint achieves is lost as the swelling changes. A break of one bone with tenderness or instability at the other bone, at the elbow or at the wrist is referred for the same reason, since the second injury is the one that will limit the arm if it is missed. An open fracture, a limb with a failing pulse, a hand that is numb or weak, and any suspicion of a compartment syndrome are referred urgently to a facility with imaging, an operating room and an intensive care unit. A fracture that enters a joint, a fracture in a child that crosses the growth region, and a fracture in a patient whose bone is weakened by disease or by long use of a medicine are also referred, because each needs a judgment that cannot be made where the patient lies.

Definitive treatment of a forearm fracture aims at three things, which are to restore the length of the bones, to correct the rotation so that the palm can turn again, and to hold both until the bone heals. A clinician may achieve this by reduction, which restores the position of the broken ends, followed by a cast or a brace in the injuries that will hold their position, and by an operation in those that will not. In an operation the surgeon may expose the bones and hold the fragments with metal plates and screws, an act called open reduction and internal fixation, or may pass a rod inside the canal of each bone, a method used particularly in children so that the growth regions are not crossed. Traction may be used as a temporary measure to hold the limb in line with a gentle continuous pull while the patient waits, and it is applied only by those trained and authorized to apply it. Every one of these acts is learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and the technique itself belongs to supervised clinical training and never to a book.

Pain is treated, and the listener will not find a substance, an amount, a route or a frequency in this course. The decision about which medicine to give, how much, and by which path belongs to a prescriber working within the local protocol, and the responder's part is to report the pain, to support and immobilize the limb, to keep the patient warm, and to ask for help early rather than late. A finding that makes the decision urgent is pain that is severe, rising or out of proportion to the injury, because that finding may mark a compartment syndrome, and pain that is masked is not pain that has been treated. The lay first aider gives nothing by mouth and nothing by any other route, and the trained responder, the nurse and the paramedic give only what the protocol of their service authorizes them to give. Sedation, tetanus protection and antibiotics are likewise decisions for a clinician, and where the injury is open the finding that makes them urgent is the wound itself.

Swelling deserves its own attention because it is the complication that a well applied splint can cause. The forearm has little room to spare, and fluid that collects after a both bone fracture can raise the pressure inside the compartments within hours, so the limb is watched rather than merely supported. A hand that is allowed to hang below the elbow will swell, which is why a sling is adjusted so that the forearm lies level or slightly raised, and why the fingers are left visible at all times. Cooling may be part of the local protocol, but it is never applied so as to numb the limb, never left in place without checking the skin beneath it, and never allowed to delay the referral of a patient whose pain is climbing.

Children break the bones of the forearm more often than adults do, and their injuries differ in ways that change the response. The bone of a child is more elastic, so it can bend without breaking, and it can break on one side only, with the other side intact and the bone bowed rather than separated. None of that is a reason to be casual, because the growth region near each end of the bone can be injured without an obvious fracture, and an injury there can shorten or deform the arm for life. A child who will not use the arm, who cries when the forearm is turned, or whose hand looks pale or feels cool is referred without debate, and the decisions about reduction and about casting belong to a clinician trained in the care of children.

The elbow and the wrist lose their movement quickly when they are held still, and the rotation of the forearm is the hardest of all to regain once it has been lost. This is why the fingers, the thumb and the shoulder are moved as soon as the treating clinician permits, even while the forearm itself is held, because a shoulder that is not moved while the arm is in a sling can become painful and stiff in its own right. Rehabilitation is prescribed and supervised by the treating clinician together with a physical therapist or an occupational therapist, and its pace is set by the surgeon and by the local protocol rather than by the impatience of the patient. The responder's part is to explain why the fingers must keep moving, to keep the arm supported and warm, and to pass on any increase in pain, swelling or numbness instead of dismissing it as part of recovery.

The record is part of the treatment rather than a formality that follows it. The time and the mechanism of the injury, the position in which the forearm was found, the state of the skin, the findings of the neurovascular examination before and after every intervention, and every change in the condition of the patient are written or spoken in a fixed order, so that the receiving team continues a story instead of starting a new one. A handover that says only that the arm is broken leaves the next clinician to guess what the arm was like before the splint went on. Every technique named in this chapter is learned with a teacher, practiced on models and on each other, and certified by an authority that also defines what may be done without supervision, and a constructed example used in teaching is an illustration rather than a real patient.

A deformed forearm is never straightened in the field, because the broken ends may be lying against the vessels and the nerves, and the movement that makes the limb look better can take away the hand. An embedded object is never removed and exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, since the object may be holding pressure on a torn vessel and the bone carries contamination inward. An open wound is never explored blindly; it is covered with a sterile dressing, the bleeding that can be controlled is controlled, the neurovascular status is recorded, the limb is immobilized in the position found, and the patient is referred urgently, with definitive closure left to the operating room. The stability of the two bones is never tested by pulling or twisting them, because that examination is a clinical skill learned under supervision and not a field test, and a test performed in the field can turn a stable injury into an unstable one. Nothing is given by mouth to a patient who may need an operation, no medicine is given outside the local protocol, and no transfer is delayed for the sake of a tidier dressing or a straighter limb.

To summarize, the forearm is a ring formed by the radius, the ulna, the membrane between them and the joints at each end, so a fracture of one bone is presumed to have injured the other or the membrane until that is excluded, and the examination and the images must include the elbow above and the wrist below. What to look for is a forearm that is bent, shortened or twisted, a palm that faces the wrong way, a break in the smooth ridge of the ulna, a wound, tenderness over the other bone, and pain in either joint, and what to record is the color, warmth, pulse, sensation and movement of the hand before any intervention and again after every one. What to do is to support the limb in the position found, to control the bleeding that can be controlled, to cover a wound and an embedded object without removing or exploring anything, to immobilize the forearm and both joints, to keep the patient warm, to reassess after every act, and to refer a both bone injury, an open injury, a failing pulse, a numb or weak hand and any suspected compartment syndrome urgently to a facility with imaging, an operating room and an intensive care unit. What never to do is to straighten a deformed limb, to push exposed bone back into a wound, to test the stability of the bones by pulling or twisting them, to give any medicine outside the local protocol, or to delay a transfer for a tidier appearance. The neurovascular examination is never skipped and never assumed, reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and the person to call is the clinician responsible for the patient and the receiving facility that can treat the injury definitively. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.
