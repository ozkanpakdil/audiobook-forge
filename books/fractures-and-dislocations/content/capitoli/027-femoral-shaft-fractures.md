# Chapter 27 — Femoral Shaft Fractures

A fracture of the thigh bone is one of the few fractures that can kill a patient before anyone decides whether the leg needs an operation. The femur is the longest and strongest bone in the body, and the force that breaks it usually injures the trunk as well. The thigh then fills with blood that nobody can see, and the limb shortens as the muscles around it pull. Every chapter of this course follows one order, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. This chapter is educational material and it does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

The femur has three parts, and the difference between them decides how each injury is managed. The proximal femur ends in the head that sits in the socket of the pelvis, and the distal femur widens to form the surface of the knee joint. Between them lies the shaft, a long tube of thick bone with a narrow cavity in the middle that holds marrow. The shaft is surrounded on all sides by the muscles of the thigh, which are wrapped in fascia and confined by the deep fascia of the limb. Those muscles form a closed space in which a large volume of blood can collect unseen, and they are strong enough to move the broken ends a long way.

The mechanism tells the responder how much energy passed through the patient, and in the thigh it is usually a great deal. A head on collision, a fall from a height, a struck pedestrian and a motorcycle crash are the usual ways a healthy adult breaks a femoral shaft. In a child the same bone can break in a fall from a bicycle, and in an older adult it can break in a fall from standing height when the bone is already weak. A thigh that breaks under a force that seems trivial is a finding worth reporting rather than a reason to relax. The mechanism is the first piece of the assessment, because it predicts what else may be injured and how quickly the patient may deteriorate.

The reason this fracture is treated as a life-threatening injury is hemorrhage, and the hemorrhage is invisible. A closed fracture of the femoral shaft can release 1 to 2 liters of blood into the muscles and tissues of the thigh. Any such number is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because estimates differ between services, countries and editions. In a person of small body size that loss can cause shock before the skin shows more than swelling. Injured muscle also loses the ability to hold fluid inside its vessels, so the loss continues after the injury has happened.

Shortening is the sign that follows from the anatomy, and it is the one most often seen. The muscles pull the lower fragment upward while the upper fragment is held near the hip, so the broken ends override one another. The same pull rotates the lower limb outward, so the foot of the injured side may lie turned away from the other foot. Angulation adds a bend at the fracture, and the thigh takes on a shape the eye can recognize from the foot of the bed. The responder compares the two limbs without lifting them and records the difference instead of trying to correct it.

What the responder sees is a thigh that is swollen, tense and changed in shape, with the knee and the foot lying in an unusual direction. What the responder feels is warmth, firmness and often exquisite tenderness over the fracture. What the patient reports is severe pain in the whole thigh, pain made worse by movement, and an inability to bear weight on that leg. A patient whose level of consciousness is reduced by a head injury, by alcohol or by another substance may report nothing at all. In that patient the swelling and the posture of the limb are the only evidence available.

Because the force is great, the thigh must never be allowed to take all of the attention. The same impact can tear the aorta, collapse a lung, lacerate the spleen or the liver, and break the pelvis, the ribs, the spine or the skull. The principle that orders the response is that life comes before limb, because a limb can wait minutes that a brain and a heart cannot. Bleeding inside the chest, the belly or the pelvis may be larger than the bleeding in the thigh, and it may be the reason the patient is deteriorating. The responder therefore completes the primary survey before turning to the limb, and treats the patient rather than the radiograph.

Assessment begins before the patient is touched. The scene is made safe, personal protective equipment is put on, and the mechanism is established from the patient and from witnesses. The primary survey then proceeds in the order remembered as ABCDE, which stands for airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure. Circulation here means the search for hemorrhage, both the visible kind and the kind hidden inside the thigh, the chest, the belly and the pelvis. Exposure means undressing the patient enough to see the whole body, and it is followed at once by covering the patient again, because cold is a complication of trauma.

The history is gathered while the examination proceeds, and it has a fixed content. The time of the injury matters because the condition of the patient changes with time and because the receiving team will ask for it. The list of medicines matters because a patient whose blood clots slowly loses more blood and reaches shock sooner. The illnesses matter because a bone weakened by a tumor or by a long illness may have broken before the fall rather than after it. Whether the patient is pregnant, and whether the patient carries a pacemaker or an artificial joint, are part of the handover as well.

Stabilization of the patient with a thigh fracture is a small set of acts performed in a deliberate order, and the first of them is restraint. A deformed limb is supported and splinted in the position in which it was found and is never straightened in the field. The vessels and the nerves of the thigh lie close against the bone, so a movement that looks helpful can tear what remained intact. Pulling the limb straight may also convert a closed fracture into an open one, and it may release the pressure that was holding a torn vessel closed. The lay first aider supports the limb with the hands, with a pillow or with a rolled blanket, keeps the patient warm and waits for help.

Bleeding that can be controlled is controlled at once, which usually means a dressing over a wound if one exists. An embedded object is never removed, because it may be pressing on a torn vessel and its removal may release the bleeding. Exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, because that carries contamination deeper without restoring the bone to a useful position. The patient is kept flat or in the position that best protects the airway, and the injured limb is supported on a padded surface. Wet clothing is replaced with dry covering, because a patient who is losing blood cannot afford to lose heat as well.

Traction is the act of applying a gentle and continuous pull along the long axis of the limb. In the thigh it is used to overcome the pull of the muscles that have shortened the limb, and to hold the fragments in better alignment. Its purpose is not to set the fracture, but to limit further movement and further bleeding, to ease pain and to make the limb safer to transport. Traction is applied through a device fixed to the leg and to the body, and it transmits a steady pull while the patient lies on a stretcher. A well applied device can leave a patient calm on the way to hospital, while a badly applied one can injure skin, press on the perineum or damage vessels and nerves.

Traction of the femur is applied only by those who are trained and authorized to apply it, and the authority comes from the service and the protocol rather than from the possession of a device. In most services the people who apply it are the emergency medical technician and the paramedic who have completed the training and whose service permits it. In the hospital it is applied by the orthopedic team, and the lay first aider never applies traction at all. The technique itself, with its straps, its counter-pressure and its check of the pulses before and after, belongs to supervised practical training with a qualified instructor. What this chapter can do is describe what traction is meant to achieve and what can go wrong.

A device that causes increasing pain, that changes the shape of the limb, that reduces the pulse, or that produces numbness or tingling is adjusted or removed at once by someone authorized to do so. Force is not a technique, and stopping is a decision that a trained responder is expected to make. Over-traction can pull the fragments apart and can injure the vessels and the nerves. Pressure from straps can damage skin over the ankle, the knee or the pelvis, so the device is checked for tightness and for pressure over bony points after it is applied. Where the trained responder cannot apply traction safely, immobilization alone is the better answer.

Several situations make traction the wrong choice, and the reason is always a specific risk. A suspected fracture of the pelvis is the clearest of them, because the device presses against the pelvis and can open a pelvic injury and increase the bleeding hidden there. An injury to the knee or to the bones below the knee is another, because the device pulls through those injured structures, and a partial amputation is a third. An open fracture with a wound along the line of the device, a fragment caught in the joint, and a limb with a failing pulse or a numb foot are also reasons to leave the device off. In each of those situations the responder immobilizes instead and explains the reason to the receiving team.

Where no traction device is available, the trained responder may immobilize the injured limb by binding it to the uninjured limb, with padding between the knees and between the ankles. This is an improvised measure, and it is checked more often than a manufactured device because it shifts with movement and can tighten during transfer. The binding must be firm enough to prevent movement and loose enough to permit the circulation, so the toes and the foot are examined after it is applied. The lay first aider supports the limb with the hands, but the decision to bind one limb to the other belongs to the trained responder working within the local protocol.

The neurovascular examination is performed before any intervention and repeated after every one, and its result is recorded. A finding that was not recorded before a splint was applied cannot be attributed afterwards, which is why the record is part of the examination. The examiner looks at the color of the foot, feels its temperature, and compares the injured side with the uninjured side. The pulses sought are the pulse in the groin, behind the knee, behind the inner ankle and on the top of the foot. Sensation is tested on the top and the outer side of the foot, and the patient is asked to move the ankle and to spread the toes.

A pulse that is present does not exclude a serious injury to the vessels, because a large vessel can be torn while the smaller ones below it still produce a pulse. A pulse that is absent is an emergency, and a limb that is pale, cool or painful out of proportion to the injury is an emergency for the same reason. Numbness, tingling, weakness and the loss of a movement the patient could perform a moment earlier suggest damage to a nerve. These signs are not equally reliable in every patient, because a patient with a head injury, a patient who is confused or intoxicated, and a frightened child may not cooperate. Where a finding is uncertain, the responder says so rather than recording a normal result that was never obtained.

Compartment syndrome of the thigh is uncommon when the femur alone is broken, but it becomes a real risk after a crush or a long period in shock. The findings are a thigh that is tense and swollen far beyond what the fracture explains, and pain that increases rather than settling. Pain that is made worse by stretching the muscles of the thigh is another warning finding. The diagnosis is clinical, and the examiner must not wait for a measurement that will arrive too late, because time is muscle and muscle that has died cannot be replaced. The responder who suspects it releases any constricting bandage or splint within the limits of the local protocol, keeps the limb at the level of the heart rather than above it, keeps the patient warm and refers the patient urgently.

Immobilization means preventing movement of the injured part and of the joints above and below it, so a fracture of the femoral shaft is immobilized in a way that also controls the hip and the knee. The device is padded where it presses over bone, which in the thigh means the prominences of the pelvis, the knee and the ankle. It is secured firmly enough to hold without becoming a band that stops the circulation. The material matters less than the principle, since a traction device, a padded board, a pillow bound in place or the uninjured leg itself can each serve. Casting, skeletal traction and surgical fixation are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and none of them is taught from a book.

Because the energy that breaks a femur often passes through the spine, the spine is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has done so, and no lone manual handling technique is safe. The neck is stabilized with the device the service uses, and the patient is rolled as a unit by a trained team using the precautions the local protocol requires. A patient whose level of consciousness is reduced, whose neck or back hurts, or who has been in a high energy crash is presumed to have a spinal injury until a qualified examiner decides otherwise. Immobilizing the thigh must never become a reason to drag the patient or to roll the patient alone. Where the two needs compete, the airway and the spine take precedence over the appearance of the leg.

Imaging is the step that confirms what the examination has already suggested, and it is ordered and interpreted by a clinician trained to do so. A radiograph is usually the first study, and it is taken in at least two directions so that a displacement in the third direction is not hidden. The hip and the knee are included because a break of the neck of the femur is a recognized companion of a break of the shaft. Computed tomography shows the detail of a complex break and is also used to examine the chest, the abdomen and the pelvis. Magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for soft tissues, and the lay first aider neither orders nor interprets any study.

Reassessment is performed after every intervention, and it is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the record, because the comparison is what has value rather than any single measurement. The limb is checked for color, warmth, the pulses, sensation, movement and pain, and the device is checked for tightness and for pressure over bony points. Pain that increases instead of settling, pain out of proportion to the injury, numbness, tingling and a foot that has become pale or cool are the findings that stop the responder. A patient whose pulse rises, whose skin becomes cold and clammy or whose level of consciousness falls is bleeding somewhere, and the thigh may not be the place.

One complication deserves separate mention, because it appears after the patient reaches the hospital and is often mistaken for something else. Fat embolism happens when fat from the marrow of the broken bone enters the blood and travels to the lungs and the small vessels of the body. It typically appears a day or so after the injury, though the timing varies and any number given for it is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. The picture is a patient who becomes short of breath, restless, confused or drowsy, with a rapid pulse and a rash of tiny red spots across the chest and neck. The diagnosis belongs to the hospital team, while the responder supports the airway, keeps the patient warm and calls for urgent help.

Referral or definitive treatment is the final step of the sequence, and for a fracture of the femoral shaft it almost always means transfer to a facility with an operating room, imaging, a supply of blood for transfusion and an intensive care unit. The decision about where the patient goes belongs to the service and the local protocol, and a hospital without orthopedic surgery is a stop on the way rather than a destination. The orthopedic surgeon and the trauma team decide what operation is performed and when, and the guiding rule is that the patient must be stable enough to survive it. In a patient who is still bleeding, an operation on the thigh may be delayed while a temporary measure holds the bone. The timing differs between services, and any figure offered for it is an illustration to be confirmed against the local protocol.

Several operations are used, and a listener should be able to recognize their names without being able to perform them. The most common permanent treatment is a metal rod passed down the canal inside the bone, which is called intramedullary fixation, and it holds the fragments in line while the bone heals without a cast. An external frame with pins that pass through the skin is used when the soft tissues are badly damaged or when the patient is too unstable for a long operation, and it is often temporary. Skeletal traction, in which a pin is placed through the bone near the knee and a weight pulls the limb, remains a definitive method where surgery is not available. Open reduction and internal fixation, in which the surgeon exposes the fracture and holds the pieces with plates and screws, is used for fractures that extend into the knee or the hip.

An open fracture of the femoral shaft is an emergency at a different level from a closed one. The bone or the deep tissues have communicated with the outside, and the wound is contaminated at the moment of injury whether it looks clean or not. The part played by the responder is limited and precise, which is to cover the wound with a sterile dressing, control the bleeding that can be controlled, examine the neurovascular status, immobilize the limb in the position found, keep the patient warm and arrange urgent transfer. Tetanus protection and antibiotics are decisions for the prescriber and the local protocol, and the substance, the amount, the route and the frequency belong to them and not to this course. Definitive closure belongs to the operating room, and a responder who explores the wound or pushes exposed bone back into it adds harm.

The same injury behaves differently at the two ends of life. A child's bone bends before it breaks, so a child who refuses to walk after a fall may have a fracture that no one can see. The growth region near each end of the femur can be injured without the shaft breaking, and that can affect the length of the leg for life. A fracture of the femoral shaft in a young child is often treated without an operation, by holding the limb in line in a device or in an enclosure that includes the trunk and both thighs, and a clinician trained to do it applies it. An older adult whose bone has lost density or contains a tumor can break the thigh in a fall from standing height, or even while turning in bed, and the pain may be far less than the injury suggests. In both patients the response is the same sequence with a lower threshold for immobilization and for referral.

Pain is a finding and not merely a complaint, and it is part of what the responder records. The lay first aider eases pain by supporting the limb in the position found, by reducing movement, by keeping the patient warm and by explaining what is happening. The trained responder may be authorized to give a medicine for pain under a written protocol, and which medicine is used and in what amount are matters for the service and the prescriber. No dose, no route and no regimen appears in this course, and a listener must not be able to take a prescription from it. The finding that makes the decision urgent is severe pain that is not eased by immobilization, because that may mean a compartment syndrome or an injury that has been missed.

The record and the handover are part of the treatment rather than a formality that follows it. The time of the injury, the mechanism, the position in which the limb was found, the device used, the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention, and any change in the condition of the patient are written or spoken in a fixed order. It is worth saying aloud that the limb was not straightened and that no object was removed, because the absence of an act is as important to record as its performance. Speaking to the patient is part of the treatment as well, so the responder explains what has been found and what will happen next, in plain words. A receiving team that knows which findings are firm can repeat the examination and trust the record second.

Transport is decided by the service, and the destination is chosen for the care the patient needs rather than for the distance. Reassessment continues during transport, and any change in the pulse, the breathing, the skin or the level of consciousness is reported before arrival. The levels of practice named in this chapter are easy to keep in order. The lay first aider supports the limb, keeps the patient warm and calls for help, while the trained first responder and the emergency medical technician immobilize with the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes. The paramedic works within a broader protocol, and the nurse, the physician and the orthopedic surgeon receive the patient, repeat the examination, order the images and plan the definitive operation.

Several acts are forbidden in every chapter of this course, and they are forbidden here together with their reasons. A deformed limb is never straightened in the field, because the vessels and the nerves lie against the bone and because the pressure holding a torn vessel closed may be the only thing preventing more bleeding. An embedded object is never removed and exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound. An open wound is never explored blindly, because the exploration adds injury and does not change what must happen next. Traction is never applied by someone who has not been trained and authorized to apply it, and reduction, skeletal traction and casting are never learned from a book.

Numbers in this course are illustrations and never rules. A volume of blood lost, a pressure inside a compartment and a time to the operating room are figures that differ between services, countries and editions. Each of them is confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol before it is used to make a decision about a patient. The fracture of the femoral shaft is treated as a life-threatening injury because it bleeds into a space nobody can see, because the force that broke it often injured the trunk as well, and because the patient may change from stable to critical while the limb is being splinted. And it happens to people who were healthy that morning.

To summarize, a fracture of the femoral shaft is a limb injury that is also a threat to life, because the thigh can hide a large hemorrhage and because the force that broke it often injured the trunk as well. An embedded object is never removed, exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, an open wound is never explored blindly, and definitive closure belongs to the operating room.
