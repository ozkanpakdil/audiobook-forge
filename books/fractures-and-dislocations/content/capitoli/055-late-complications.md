# Chapter 55 — Late Complications

A fracture or a dislocation is often called healed long before the patient has recovered, and the weeks and months that follow are when the slow complications appear. The wound has closed, the splint has come off and everyone is relieved, and that is exactly when a bone that has stopped uniting, a bone that has united crooked, a joint that has stiffened, a bone that has lost its blood supply and a child's growth region that has been injured begin to decide the rest of that patient's life. Every chapter of this course follows one order, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and this chapter follows it while naming the provider level for every act. These complications are missed because they arrive without an audience: in the first hour the injury is loud, while in the sixth week the patient is at home and the only witness is a limb slowly losing alignment or movement. The follow-up appointment exists for that reason and is part of the treatment rather than a courtesy at the end of it, so a responder supports it instead of replacing it. This is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

Bone heals through a sequence worth describing in words, because the words explain why some fractures take so much longer than others. The first step is the bleeding between the broken ends, which forms a collection called a hematoma, and the next is the arrival of cells that build a soft bridge of cartilage and fibrous tissue across the gap. The third is the replacement of that bridge by woven bone, called callus, which appears on a radiograph as a cloud around the fracture, and the fourth is the slow remodeling that reshapes the rough bone into the column it was before. The blood supply is the most important factor at every stage, since no stage happens without vessels reaching the fracture, and the mechanical environment is the second, since a fracture that still moves is one whose bridge keeps breaking. As an illustration, most breaks in the shaft of a long bone have joined by 3 to 6 months, a span that differs between bones, ages and editions and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

A fracture that heals slowly is not the same as a fracture that has failed to heal, and the distinction belongs to a clinician working with time and with imaging. What the patient reports is consistent: the fracture site still aches after a long day, it hurts when weight goes through the limb and it hurts when the limb is twisted in a way a united bone would not allow. What the examiner finds is tenderness that has never left the site of the break and sometimes a hinge of movement where there should be none. A responder does not test for movement at a fracture site by pulling on it, because that examination belongs to a clinician and the test itself can disturb a bridge that is trying to form. When the ends have still not joined by the time the clinician expects, the condition is called a nonunion, and the gap may stay open and filled with scar or the ends may grow rounded and sealed over. The causes are few: the blood supply to a fragment may have been destroyed at the moment of injury, an infection may be present in bone that looks healed from the outside, the ends may be held apart by muscle caught between them, and the fracture may have been left free to move. Illness, poor nutrition, smoking and medicines that suppress healing all work against the patient, and the diagnosis is made by the orthopedic surgeon with imaging rather than from the outside by a responder.

A bone that unites in the wrong position is described as malunited, and the position is judged against the normal anatomy of that bone and against the other side of the same patient. An arm or a leg that is visibly bent, a limb that has become shorter and a limb whose shape differs from the one opposite are all signs, and rotation is the one most often missed, because a bend is easy to see and a twist is not. A forearm that can no longer turn the palm upward, a wrist that sits at an angle so that grip feels weak, a foot that points inward or outward so the shoe wears unevenly, a finger that crosses its neighbor when the hand closes and an elbow that can no longer reach the mouth are the complaints that follow. Pain often joins them, because the joint above or below a malunited bone is loaded in a direction it was not built for.

Whether a malunion is accepted or corrected is a decision of the orthopedic surgeon, and it turns on how much the deformity costs the patient, how old the patient is, how much the joint has suffered and what an operation would risk. A small bend in a bone that does not cross a joint may cause no trouble at all, while the same bend in a forearm stops the hand from doing its work, so function matters more than the size of the bend. When a limb has shortened by 2 centimeters or more the patient may limp, and the effect of that difference on the joints above and below is assessed by the clinician, since such figures differ between patients and services and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. The lay first aider, the trained responder and the nurse do not straighten a healed limb, do not force a joint through a deformity and do not tell the patient that nothing can be done. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and no part of that learning comes from a book.

Stiffness of a joint is the late complication patients notice most and resent longest, and it is the one most often accepted as inevitable when it is not. A joint held still loses the looseness of its capsule and ligaments, the cartilage that lines it is nourished by movement and suffers without it, and the muscles that cross it lose length and strength, so a joint immobilized for the safety of the bone pays a price that appears weeks later. The price is a range of movement that no longer reaches where it did, first in the direction the device restricted most and later in every direction. The patient reports difficulty with the small acts of daily life, and those acts are the true measures of a joint: combing the hair, reaching a back pocket, fastening a shoe, kneeling and rising from a low chair. The older patient and the patient whose limb stayed swollen for a long time lose movement fastest, and a joint guarded through fear loses it as surely as one that is splinted.

The management of a stiff joint begins with finding out whether the stiffness comes from the capsule, from scar tissue, from a block of bone, from a displaced fragment, from an infection or from pain, because each answer leads somewhere different. A joint that is simply tight after immobilization is treated with graded movement, with therapy and with patience, and that program is set by the physical therapist together with the surgeon for that particular patient, and a joint that is left in one position for weeks can stiffen into a fixed contracture that is far harder to undo than simple tightness. Where the joint does not respond, the orthopedic surgeon may consider an operation to release the tightened tissue or to remove a block, and that decision belongs to the surgeon and to the operating room. Nothing in this chapter teaches a technique for stretching, because a joint forced past its limit tears and bleeds, and blood inside a joint produces more scar and less movement than before. A responder never pulls a stiff joint straight and never tells a patient to push through the pain, and the nurse reports a joint that becomes hot and swollen after exercise rather than increasing the exercise.

The pain that persists deserves attention of its own, because pain brings the patient back and is the finding most often explained away. Pain is expected while a fracture heals and while a joint regains its movement, but the pain of a late complication has a different character. Pain that increases over weeks instead of easing, pain that wakes the patient at night, pain far worse than the injury appears to explain and pain in a limb that has changed color, temperature or sweating all point away from ordinary healing. One condition in which those changes come together is the complex regional pain syndrome, in which severe pain, altered skin color and temperature, altered sweating and extreme sensitivity to a light touch follow an injury that may have seemed minor. Its signs overlap with those of infection, of a blood clot and of a nerve injury, so the diagnosis is clinical and belongs to a clinician. Assessment begins with the account of the patient, who is asked where the pain is, when it began, what eases it, what worsens it, what it prevents and whether it disturbs sleep, and the answers are written down so that the next visit has something to compare. Relief of pain is part of good care and is also the step that can hide a complication, so any relief given is recorded, the examination is performed anyway, and the choice of substance, amount and route belongs to the prescriber and to the local protocol.

Some bones break in a way that destroys their blood supply, and the death of that bone is a late complication that unfolds over months. The head of the thigh bone, the small bone at the base of the thumb side of the wrist, the bone that forms the lower part of the ankle joint and the head of the upper arm bone are the sites where this is most feared, because each depends on a vessel that enters through a narrow place and can be torn by a fracture or a dislocation. The bone dies quietly, and for a long time the patient notices nothing, and then the dead segment loses its strength and collapses under the load of the joint. The findings are pain in the groin, in the wrist or in the ankle that worsens with use, stiffness of the joint and pain that continues long after the fracture should have settled. A radiograph may look almost normal at the beginning, and magnetic resonance imaging can show the change much earlier, which is one reason it is used when pain does not match the picture. A dislocation left unreduced, a bone already weakened by illness, a heavy use of alcohol and long treatment with medicines that thin the bone or suppress inflammation all make the death of bone more likely, and when the dead segment collapses the joint becomes arthritic, so the choice among the operations that may help belongs to the orthopedic surgeon.

A child's bone is not a small adult bone, and the late complications of injury in a child follow from that difference. Near each end of a growing bone lies a region called the growth plate, where cartilage is produced and then turned into bone, and it is that region which makes the bone longer rather than merely thicker. The plate is softer than the shaft and it sits close to the joint, so an injury that looks like a sprain may have crossed the plate, while an injury that breaks the shaft may spare the plate altogether. When the plate is injured, part of it may stop growing while the rest continues, and the result is a bone that grows crooked, or a limb that ends up shorter than the other, or a joint whose surfaces tilt as the child grows. Sometimes the injured plate grows faster for a time instead, so the injured limb becomes longer rather than shorter, which is the opposite of the expected direction and is no less of a disturbance. The damage depends on the pattern of the injury, on the age of the child and on how much growth remained, and those are estimates that differ between children and are confirmed by the specialist.

The difficult part of a growth disturbance is that nobody sees it at the moment it begins. A child whose limb length changes by a little each year may have no pain at all, and the family notices that one shoe is worn differently, that one sleeve is short or that the child limps when tired. A child who stops using an arm after a fall, who cries when the arm is handled or who refuses to bear weight deserves an examination and an image rather than reassurance, even when the skin is unmarked, because the growth plate is injured more often than it is seen. Follow-up after such an injury continues for as long as the bone grows, which means years rather than weeks, and the family has to be told why in words they can keep, or the appointments will be dropped exactly when they are doing their work. Comparison with the opposite limb, measured in the same way at each visit, is the simple observation the clinic repeats, and the interpretation belongs to the pediatric orthopedic surgeon. Nothing done at the roadside prevents a growth disturbance, and only the follow-up finds it in time, which is why the appointment belongs to the treatment rather than to the paperwork.

Assessment in the late phase is a comparison rather than a single examination, and the sequence is the same one this course follows everywhere. The history comes first and it begins with the injury, the time it happened, how it was treated and what has happened since, including every operation, every device and every missed appointment. The patient is then asked what the limb prevents and what it permits, because the function of the limb is the outcome that matters and it is heard rather than seen. The examination repeats the order used in the first hours and compares the two sides: the shape of the limb, its length, the alignment of its joints, the movement at each joint, the strength, the sensation and the pulses. The general condition of the patient belongs in the same examination, since a late infection, a clot or another illness can present at a follow-up visit as easily as at any other time. The record carried from the earlier visits is what turns today's finding into a change.

Stabilization in the late phase looks modest, because the emergencies of the first hours lie behind the patient, and it still has content. It means keeping the patient warm, protecting a limb that is painful or unstable from a load it cannot carry, supporting the limb in the position the treating team has chosen rather than in the position that feels easiest, and controlling any bleeding from a wound that has begun to discharge. A patient whose fracture is not united needs support for walking, and the device that provides it is chosen, fitted and adjusted by the clinician, the orthopedic practitioner or the physical therapist according to the local protocol, never improvised by a responder. A patient who arrives at a clinic with unrelenting pain is not stabilized by being sent home with reassurance, and a patient who is short of breath, confused or feverish after an injury is stabilized as any other seriously ill patient is, with attention to the airway, to the breathing and to the circulation. Life still comes before limb when two correct actions compete for the same pair of hands, and here the competition is usually between the general illness of the patient and the limb that brought them in. Any relief of pain and any medicine belong to the treating clinician and to the local protocol.

The neurovascular examination is performed before any intervention and repeated after it, and in the late phase it is repeated because the limb itself has changed since the last visit. The patient is asked whether the limb feels normal, numb, tingling or weaker than it did, and the answer is recorded in the words of the patient wherever that is possible. Color and temperature are compared side to side, the pulses beyond the injury are felt and described rather than merely noted as present, and the movement of each finger or each toe is tested against the other side. Sensation is tested through the territory of the nerves that cross the injured region, because a nerve can be pressed months later by scar or by a bone that healed crooked and the loss can be partial and easy to miss. A pulse that is present does not exclude a problem, and a change in the neurovascular findings is an emergency until a clinician says otherwise. The lay first aider reports color, warmth and whether the fingers or the toes still move, while the trained responder, the nurse and the physician perform the formal examination.

Immobilization in the late phase is mostly a matter of continuing a plan and noticing when it no longer fits the limb. A cast or a brace that was correct in the first week may be wrong in the sixth, because the swelling has fallen and the limb has changed shape, and a device that has become loose lets the bone move while a device that has become tight presses on the skin. Whoever applied the device, whether a trained responder, a paramedic or a physician, checks the edges, the padding and the fit, and the nurse checks the skin wherever it can be seen, especially over the heel, the ankle, the elbow and the back of the wrist. Only someone the local protocol authorizes removes or replaces a cast, splits it to relieve pressure or alters a brace, and the orthopedic surgeon decides what definitive immobilization the injury needs once the late phase has begun. Casting, reduction and traction are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and nothing in this course teaches a listener to perform them. A responder never tightens a device to make it feel secure and never scratches an itch beneath a cast with any object.

Imaging in the late phase answers questions the examination has already framed, and it is ordered by a clinician trained to interpret it. A radiograph is the first study in most clinics, and it is taken in at least two directions so that a change in the third direction is not hidden, with the joint above and the joint below included so that a second problem is not overlooked. Computed tomography shows the detail of a bone that has healed crooked or failed to unite, especially near a joint, and magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for soft tissues and for bone that may have lost its blood supply, since it can show that change before a radiograph does. An image taken today is compared with the images taken before, and that comparison is the reason the earlier pictures are kept and carried with the patient. Imaging is never ordered or interpreted by a lay first aider, and the trained responder does not choose the study, because the decision belongs to the clinician responsible for the patient. An image is a picture of one moment, and a patient whose pain has changed must still be examined.

Reassessment is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment, and in the late phase it catches the complication caused by time as well. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the record, because the value lies in the comparison rather than in any single measurement. The findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation are pain that increases instead of settling, pain that wakes the patient at night, a new deformity in a limb that had none, a joint that is becoming stiffer rather than looser, a wound that has begun to discharge, a limb that has become hot and swollen, a new numbness or weakness and a child who has stopped using the limb. A patient who says that something feels wrong, even when the examination is unremarkable, is reporting a change that deserves a second look rather than an explanation. A responder also says aloud what would make the situation unbearable, which here means a limb losing function week by week, a wound that is spreading, a patient who can no longer walk or a patient who is becoming unwell for a reason nobody has explained. The intervals at which these observations are repeated differ between services and injuries and are confirmed against the local protocol.

Referral or definitive treatment is the final step of the sequence, and the late complications are almost all reasons to refer rather than to watch at home. A suspected nonunion, a malunion that costs the patient function, a bone that may have lost its blood supply, a growth disturbance in a child, a joint that has become arthritic and a bone that may be infected each belong to a facility with imaging, an operating room and an intensive care unit. The referral is made by the clinician responsible for the patient, while the lay first aider and the trained responder call for help early rather than deciding what treatment is needed. The orthopedic surgeon decides what the bone needs, which may be a different device, a graft of bone, an operation to correct a deformity, an operation to restore a blood supply or the replacement of a joint, and every one of those decisions belongs in that surgeon's hands and in the local protocol. The record and the handover travel with the patient, so the time of the injury, the treatment given, the position of the limb and the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention are written in a fixed order. A patient with an injury of the spine is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has done so.

The follow-up appointment is part of the treatment because these complications cannot be prevented by anything done in the first hour and cannot be found by anyone who is not looking. A bone that is failing to unite does not announce itself with a fever, a joint that is stiffening does not hurt while it stiffens and the growth plate of a child does not complain. What finds them is a patient who returns, a clinician who compares today with last time and a record that carries the earlier findings forward. The system that makes that possible is made of ordinary things: a clear appointment, a written plan, a copy of the images, a telephone number to call when something changes and a patient who understands why any of it matters. Where the patient cannot return, the responder and the community health worker who see the limb between visits become the eyes of the team, and their report of a new deformity, a new pain or a new weakness is worth more than a perfect examination performed a year later.

To summarize, the complications of the late phase are quieter than the injury that caused them, and they are found by looking for them rather than by waiting to be told. The findings to watch for are pain that increases instead of settling, pain that wakes the patient at night, tenderness that never leaves the fracture site, movement where a healed bone should be solid, a limb that is bent, twisted or shorter than the other, a joint that is becoming stiffer rather than looser, a wound or a sinus that discharges, a new numbness or weakness, and a child who has stopped using a limb. A fracture that has not united, one that has united crooked, a bone that has lost its blood supply, a joint that has become arthritic and an injured growth plate are all conditions the clinician assesses with the history, the examination and the images, and none is diagnosed from the outside by a responder. The acts of the responder are to support the limb in the position found, to keep the patient warm, to protect the skin under any device, to record the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention, to arrange the appointment and the transport and to report a change early rather than to explain it away. The acts that must never be performed are these: a deformed limb is never straightened in the field, a healed deformity is never forced or remanipulated, a stiff joint is never pulled straight, a cast is never tightened or split by anyone the local protocol does not authorize, an embedded object is never removed, exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, an open wound is never explored blindly, and no responder takes a prescription from this book. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and a reduction is not attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when the vessels or the nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear or when an earlier attempt has failed. Who to call is decided before the emergency rather than during it: the clinician responsible for the patient, the orthopedic surgeon, the emergency service and the facility with imaging, an operating room and an intensive care unit, and for a patient who collapses, the emergency number with CPR started at once.
