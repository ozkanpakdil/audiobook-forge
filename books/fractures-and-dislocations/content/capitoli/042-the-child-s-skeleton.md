# Chapter 42 — The Child's Skeleton

A child is not a small adult, and nowhere is that plainer than in the skeleton, which is still growing and behaves differently from the moment of injury onward. The same energy that breaks an adult's bone may bend a child's bone, buckle it, split it along a plane an image cannot show, or injure the region that makes the bone grow. That difference changes what the responder looks for, what the responder may do, and what the family must be told about the years ahead. This chapter follows the order used throughout this course, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and it names the provider level for every act. It is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

Every long bone of a child lengthens at two regions, one near each end, and each is built in layers that explain what follows. The shaft is the diaphysis, separated from the end of the bone by a flared zone called the metaphysis, which is thin-walled and blood-rich. Between the metaphysis and the end lies the growth plate, also called the physis, a layer of cartilage whose cells divide, mature and become bone in an ordered sequence that lengthens the bone, and beyond it lies the epiphysis, which carries the joint surface. Because cartilage does not absorb x-rays the way bone does, the plate appears on a radiograph as a gap and the centers of bone in the epiphysis appear as separate pieces, so the growing skeleton can look like one that has been broken in several places. Only a clinician should read that appearance.

The growth plate is the weakest link when a force arrives as tension or as shear, which is why an injury that would tear a ligament in an adult can separate the plate in a child. The cartilage cells stand in columns with little to resist a sideways pull, and the zone where those columns are replaced by bone is the most fragile part of the structure. A force along the length of the bone is therefore transmitted to the plate rather than to the joint, so the joint may survive while the region that produces growth is disturbed. A child who cannot move a joint after a twisting injury is therefore not merely a child with a sprain. Tenderness that lies exactly over the plate rather than over the joint line must be recorded, because the responder is often the only person who can describe where it was before a splint covered it.

Clinicians describe injuries of the growth plate in several patterns, and the names differ between editions and countries, so the reason for a pattern matters more than its label. In the mildest pattern the fragment separates through the plate itself, with no break in the bone on either side, and in the next the break runs through part of the plate and out through a corner of the metaphysis, taking a small triangle of bone. A third crosses the plate and exits through the epiphysis, so the joint surface is involved, a fourth combines those, and the last is a crush of the plate in which nothing appears displaced. That last pattern is often recognized only in hindsight, when the bone stops growing, so tenderness over a plate deserves review even when the first image is reassuring.

An injury to the growth plate can leave a bridge of bone across the cartilage, and that bridge acts as a staple, tethering one side of the bone while the rest lengthens. The result is an angular deformity that worsens with time, or a limb shorter than its partner, and the amount depends on which plate was injured, how much growth remained and how much of the plate was damaged. A plate near the knee or the ankle can change the length of a whole limb, and the head of the thigh bone has a blood supply that is easily disturbed in a child, so an injury of the hip is an emergency even when the child seems comfortable. The decisions that follow belong to the orthopedic surgeon, and the part played by everyone else is recognition, protection, accurate recording and prompt referral.

The periosteum is the tough fibrous sleeve that wraps the bone except where a joint surface is exposed, and in a child it is thicker, stronger and far more active than in an adult. It carries vessels that feed the outer bone, it holds the cells that can make new bone, and it can bend without tearing when the bone beneath gives way, which is why a child's bone often breaks on one side only while the other side merely bends. Such an injury is called a greenstick fracture because it resembles what happens to a young green stick of wood, and because the sleeve stays intact it is usually stable and may hold the ends so close that the limb looks almost straight. The same sleeve can bend without any break at all, leaving the bone permanently curved in a pattern called plastic deformation, which is easy to dismiss as a bruise until the curve is compared with the other side.

The activity of that sleeve explains why children heal so quickly and why their healing is not always a comfort. New bone forms along the periosteum within days, so a repeat image taken about 2 weeks later can show a fracture the first image did not, a timing that differs between services and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. Speed of healing also means that a fracture which is going to heal in a poor position will do so quickly, so a deformity that needs correcting has to be recognized early. The periosteum can slip between the broken ends, a finding called interposition, and it is one reason a reduction may fail no matter how carefully a qualified clinician attempts it. Interposition is not diagnosed or treated in the field, because that examination belongs to the orthopedic surgeon, while the responder records that the bone is not straight and that the plate is tender.

The bone of a child is softer and more porous than the bone of an adult, because it contains more water and more protein and less mineral, and that changes the way it fails. It absorbs energy by bending before it breaks, so the same force may produce an incomplete injury, a bulge in the surface, or a curved bone rather than a snapped one. A fall on an outstretched hand that would snap an adult's wrist may instead compress one side of the child's bone into a small ridge, called a buckle or torus fracture, which is tender but rarely displaced. A child with such an injury may still move the limb, may still use the hand and may have no swelling, so the absence of an obvious deformity proves nothing. The softer bone is also more forgiving of small errors of alignment, but only up to a point, and no responder decides how much to accept or corrects anything.

Because the bone is softer and the plate is weaker, the tissues that give way first in a child often differ from those in an adult, which has a practical consequence for recognition. A ligament that would tear in an adult may stay intact while the bone to which it is attached is pulled away, an injury called an avulsion fracture, and the fragment may be small enough to be missed. A similar pull near a joint may separate a growth plate instead of tearing a ligament, and the two look alike from outside because both produce swelling and pain in the same place. The responder records what the child will and will not do, where it hurts, and what the limb looked like before and after immobilization, and does not test the joint by pulling on it, because that skill is learned under supervision.

Remodeling is the process by which a growing bone corrects the shape it has been left in, and it is why a child's fracture is judged by different standards than an adult's. It works in three ways at once, because new bone is laid down on the side under strain, old bone is removed from the side that is not, and the growth plate itself can realign as it lengthens, so the bone slowly straightens. How much correction is possible depends on the age of the child and the growth that remains, on how close the fracture lies to a plate, and on whether the deformity lies in the plane in which the nearby joint moves. A bend in the plane of the elbow or the knee remodels far better than one that is not, and a limb left rotated or shortened does not remodel at all, because growth cannot untwist a bone or lengthen one that has healed too short.

Remodeling is measured in months and years rather than days, and it is not a promise that every deformity will disappear. A fracture that is to be corrected by growth must lie in a position the plate can correct, the family must understand that reviews will continue long after the pain has gone, and someone must watch for the deformity that does not improve, because a bone that has healed bent may need an operation later. A child's fracture also sometimes overgrows, because healing stimulates the plate near the break. Growth continues for years, so a damaged plate may produce a deformity only after a growth spurt, and a limb left short may change the way a child walks, while some of those outcomes are unavoidable and some are the price of delay.

Recognition of a fracture in a child depends less on what can be seen than on what the child will and will not do. A child who will not use an arm, who will not bear weight on a leg, who holds a limb still and refuses to let it be moved, or who cries when it is handled is describing an injury even when there is no swelling and no bruise. A very young child may show nothing except irritability, a refusal to feed, or a cry that changes when the limb is touched, while a trivial mechanism such as a fall from a low height can produce a real fracture because a child is light and the forces are small. Comparing the two sides is a habit worth keeping, and so is examining the whole child, because a child who has fallen often has more than one injury.

Assessment begins with the scene, the mechanism, the age of the child and the time since the injury, and the history includes illness that affects bone, medicines that thin it, and anything that has made the child's bones fragile. The primary survey comes next, and its steps are remembered as ABCDE, meaning airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure, with the whole child rather than the injured limb as its subject. A child compensates for blood loss by raising the heart rate and tightening the vessels and may look well until that compensation fails, so a child who becomes quiet, pale or drowsy after an injury is more worrying than the same behavior in an adult. Life comes before limb whenever two correct actions compete for the same pair of hands, and exposure is followed at once by covering the child again, because cold is a complication of trauma and not a detail of comfort. The limb is assessed only when the child is stable enough, and only by looking, by feeling gently and by asking.

Among the injuries a child may bring to a responder are those that were inflicted, and the features that raise that possibility are worth knowing even though the judgment belongs to others. Concern is raised when the explanation does not fit the injury, when a child who cannot yet walk has a fracture, when injuries of different ages are found together, or when help has been delayed without good reason. The correct acts are to keep the child safe and warm, to avoid interrogation or accusation, to record what was seen and said in the words used, and to pass the concern to the clinician in charge. The duty to report is set by local law and local protocol, and a responder who suspects such an injury does not send the child home to be brought back later.

Stabilization of an injured limb in a child is the same small set of acts as in an adult, and the first of them is restraint. The limb is supported and splinted in the position in which it was found and is never straightened in the field, because vessels and nerves lie against the bone and a movement that looks helpful can tear what remained intact or turn a closed fracture into an open one. A deformed limb is supported by hand, by a pillow or by whatever is at hand, and the child is kept warm while help is arranged. An open wound is covered with a sterile dressing, the bleeding that can be controlled is controlled, exposed bone is never pushed back into the wound, and an embedded object is never removed, because it may be holding pressure on a torn vessel. The lay first aider performs only the acts that need no equipment, the trained responder and the paramedic work within the equipment and procedures their service authorizes, and nobody in the field explores an open wound blindly, because definitive closure belongs to the operating room.

Cold deserves its own place in the care of an injured child, because a child loses heat faster than an adult and has less reserve to replace it. The surface of a child is large in relation to the weight, the skin is thin, and the layer of fat beneath it is small, so a limb left uncovered on cold ground loses warmth quickly. Wet clothing is replaced with dry covering, the child is insulated from the ground, and a hat or a blanket over the head helps, because much heat is lost there. A young child who is cold may not shiver but may instead become quiet and sleepy, which can be mistaken for calm. Because cold worsens bleeding and complicates every injury, warmth is part of the treatment of the fracture, and any temperature, time or distance named in this chapter is an illustration to be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, since such figures differ between services and countries.

The neurovascular examination is performed before any intervention and repeated after it, and its result is written down, because a finding that was not recorded before a splint was applied cannot be attributed afterwards. The examiner looks at the color and temperature of the skin, feels the pulse beyond the injury, presses briefly on a nail to watch the color return, and asks the child to move the fingers or the toes. Sensation is tested on both sides and compared, and in a child the testing may have to be turned into a game or done while the caregiver distracts the child, because numbness and tingling are reported by the child and not by the examiner. The lay first aider observes color, warmth and the child's ability to move and feel, while the formal examination of pulses and sensation belongs to the trained responder or the clinician. A pulse that is present does not exclude serious injury to the vessels or to a compartment, and a pulse that is absent is an emergency.

A compartment syndrome is harder to recognize in a child, because a frightened child may cry for other reasons and a quiet child may be assumed to be comfortable. The limb that is tense, swollen and painful out of proportion to the injury is an emergency at any age, and pain that increases instead of settling, pain not relieved by the measures available, and a child who cannot be comforted are the findings that should stop the responder. Numbness, tingling, weakness, pallor and coolness come later, and waiting for them is waiting too long, because the diagnosis is clinical and time is muscle. A responder who suspects it does not wait for confirmation, does not elevate the limb above the level of the heart, releases a constricting bandage or splint only within the local protocol and only when authorized, keeps the child warm, and refers urgently. The substance and amount of any medicine for the pain belong to the prescriber and the local protocol, and no dose, route or frequency may be taken from this course.

Immobilization means preventing movement of the injured part and of the joints above and below it, because a bone that is splinted alone can still be moved by the joint at either end. A splint is padded where it presses on bone, is applied over the position found, and is secured firmly enough to hold without becoming a band that stops the circulation. In a child these principles demand more care, because the limb is small and the child may wriggle against whatever is applied. The material matters less than the principle, since a folded magazine, a padded board, a sling or the body of the caregiver can each serve, and an improvised device must be checked more often because it shifts, while a sling holding the arm against the chest is often the whole of the immobilization a child's upper limb needs before transfer. The trained responder and the paramedic apply splints within the equipment and procedures their service authorizes, while the physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides what definitive immobilization is required, and casting, traction and surgical fixation are learned under supervision rather than taught from a book.

Whatever is applied must be checked, and in a child the checking is repeated more often than in an adult because the limb is small and the margin between firm and too tight is narrow. The fingers or toes beyond the splint are watched for color, warmth, swelling and the ability to move, and a digit that becomes pale, cool or painful is a signal to loosen the device within the local protocol and to seek advice. A child who becomes distressed after a splint is applied is telling the responder something, and the answer is not to wait and see whether the distress passes. Because a child's fracture may swell for hours, a device that fits at one moment may be too tight later, so the family must be told what change should bring them back, and instructions given on transfer are part of the immobilization.

Imaging confirms what the examination has suggested, and it is ordered and interpreted by a clinician trained to do so, never by a responder in the field. A radiograph is usually the first study, and it is taken in at least two directions so that a displacement in the third direction is not hidden, while the joint above and the joint below are included so that a second injury is not missed. In a child the reading is more difficult, because the growth plate appears as a gap and the bony centers at the ends of the bones appear as separate pieces that can be mistaken for fragments. A fracture through the plate may not be visible at all on the first image, so a clinic may review the child after about 1 to 2 weeks, when new bone begins to show, though that interval differs between services and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. Children are more sensitive to radiation than adults, so the clinician weighs the benefit of every study.

Reassessment is performed after every intervention, and it is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the record, because the comparison is what has value rather than any single measurement, and the limb is checked for color, warmth, pulse, sensation, movement and pain while the splint is checked for tightness. Pain that increases instead of settling, pain out of proportion to the injury, numbness, tingling, weakness, and a limb that becomes tense and swollen are the findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation, and in a child the same findings may appear as distress that cannot be soothed. A responder who suspects a compartment syndrome acts within the local protocol rather than waiting for confirmation, and the child is kept warm, the limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, the receiving team is warned, and the transfer is not delayed for a test that will be repeated at the hospital. Medicines for pain are chosen and given only by those the local protocol authorizes, and a responder whose scope of practice forbids giving a medicine says who will.

Referral or definitive treatment is the final step, and it begins with the recognition that some injuries cannot be managed where the child lies. A child with a suspected injury of the growth plate, a displaced fracture, a fracture that enters a joint, an open fracture, a damaged nerve or vessel, a suspected compartment syndrome, a fracture of the thigh bone, a joint that will not move, or an injury that does not match the explanation given is referred to a facility with imaging, an operating room and an intensive care unit. Transport is arranged with the limb immobilized and the neurovascular findings handed over. Definitive treatment includes reduction, which restores the position of the bone ends, traction, which holds them in line with a gentle continuous pull, and casting or surgical fixation, which holds the position while the bone heals, and every one of those acts is learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol. When the growth plate is involved, timing matters more than in an adult, because the longer a displaced plate waits the greater the risk to the cartilage that is trying to grow. A reduction must not be attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when the vessels or nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the injury is open or the diagnosis unclear, when the training or equipment is lacking, or when an earlier attempt has failed, because an attempt can convert an injury that would have healed into one that cannot. Force is not a technique, and in a child it can damage the plate and change the length of the limb for life.

The record and the handover are part of the treatment rather than a formality that follows it, and in a child they carry information that no later examiner can recover. The age of the child, the time of the injury, the mechanism, the position in which the limb was found, the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention, and any change in the condition are written or spoken in a fixed order, so the receiving team continues a story rather than starting a new one. The fact that a child cried when the limb was touched belongs in the record in those words. An injury of the spine is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has done so, and no lone manual handling technique is safe, so a child with a suspected spinal injury is moved by a trained team using the precautions the local protocol requires. Everything described in this chapter is learned with a teacher, practiced on models and on each other, and certified by an authority that also defines what may be done without supervision.

To summarize, a child's skeleton is not a small adult's skeleton, and the growth plate, the thicker and more active periosteum, the softer bone and the greater remodeling explain why the same force produces a different injury, a different recovery and a different future. A child who will not use a limb, will not bear weight, or cries when the limb is handled has described a fracture even when nothing can be seen, and tenderness over the growth plate or a joint that will not move makes the transfer urgent. The course follows one order in every chapter, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and it names the provider level for every act. A deformed limb is splinted in the position found and is never straightened in the field, an embedded object is never removed, exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, an open wound is never explored blindly, and a constricting splint is released only within the local protocol by someone authorized to do it. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and a reduction is not attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when the vessels or the nerves are failing, when the injury is open or the diagnosis unclear, when the training and the equipment are missing, or when an earlier attempt has failed. A child is kept warm, a suspected spinal injury is treated as unstable until it is cleared by someone qualified to clear it, an injury that does not fit its explanation is reported through the proper channel, and the child is reassessed after every intervention.
