# Chapter 41 — Spinal Neurological Assessment

A suspected injury of the spine is the one injury in which the broken bone matters less than the question of whether the nervous system inside it still works, because a vertebra will heal and a spinal cord will not. A patient may arrive walking and talking and lose the use of a hand an hour later, and only an examination repeated in the same order will notice that change. This chapter follows the sequence this course uses everywhere, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. The examination is a clinical skill, so this chapter says what is looked for, why it matters and who may perform each act, and it does not teach a technique that belongs to supervised training. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, or the law of the country in which you work.

The spinal cord lies inside a canal formed by the vertebrae, and the column of bone and the cord within it must be thought of and examined together. In an adult the cord ends near the first or the second lumbar vertebra, and below that point the canal carries a bundle of nerve roots called the cauda equina. Each segment of the cord gives rise to roots that leave between the vertebrae, and each root supplies a strip of skin and a group of muscles, so the bone that is broken and the cord that has suffered are not always at the same level. The cord can also be injured while the bone remains whole, and in a child it may be hurt with nothing abnormal on a radiograph, a pattern called spinal cord injury without radiographic abnormality.

The decision to treat a spine as unstable begins with the mechanism of injury and with what the patient cannot tell you. A fall from a height, a dive into shallow water, a vehicle collision, a crushing blow to the trunk and a fall in sport all carry enough energy to break the spine in a patient who feels comfortable. In an older adult a fall from standing height is enough, particularly when the head is thrown back and the neck is forced into extension. The patient who is intoxicated, confused, unconscious or distracted by a painful injury elsewhere cannot report pain or weakness reliably, and is treated as having an unstable spine until someone qualified clears it. The lay first aider does not make this judgment alone but suspects the injury, keeps the head and trunk still, and calls for a team.

Assessment begins before the spine is touched, and it begins with the primary survey, whose steps are remembered as ABCDE, meaning airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure. Life comes before limb, because a blocked airway and a failing circulation kill in minutes while a damaged cord cannot be repaired by any hurry at the roadside. The disability step is the first neurological observation, and it records the level of consciousness, the movement of the limbs, and the size and the reaction of the pupils. Only then does the examination turn to the spine, and it turns to it with the neck already supported, because an assessment that moves an unstable spine is part of the injury rather than part of the care. The lay first aider performs no formal examination of the spine but keeps the patient still and warm and calls for help.

Stabilization means that the head, the neck and the trunk are kept in the position in which they were found and are moved as one unit. No traction is applied to the neck and no attempt is made to straighten it, because a pull on the head is a force delivered to an injured column, and that technique belongs to supervised clinical practice. The trained first responder and the paramedic hold the head with both hands, or apply the collar, the blocks and the straps their service authorizes, saying aloud what they are doing so that the team moves together. A patient on the ground is turned by a team using the method the local protocol describes, and no single responder performs a lone manual handling technique, because one pair of hands cannot control a spine. The patient is then insulated from the ground and kept warm, since cold is a complication of trauma and not a detail of comfort.

The neurovascular examination is performed before any intervention and repeated after it, and its result is recorded, because a finding that was not written before a collar was applied cannot be attributed afterwards. In a suspected spinal injury the first examination is whatever the patient can do at the moment of first contact, taken where the patient lies rather than after the patient has been moved. Numbness, tingling, weakness and the loss of a movement the patient could perform a moment earlier are the findings that matter, recorded in the words the patient used. Each finding is written with the time at which it was made, since a weakness noticed at one moment and recorded later has lost the interval that would have shown how fast the injury is moving. The physician interprets the record, while the responder at every level contributes to it by repeating the observations and reporting them unchanged.

The examination is carried out with the patient lying still, the examiner working from the side so that nothing is leaned across the face or the chest. Gloves, eye protection and the other items of personal protective equipment are worn whenever there may be blood or body fluid, because a rescuer who becomes a patient helps no one. The patient is told what will be done before it is done, consent is asked for each part, a chaperone is present for the genital or the anal region, and the patient is covered apart from the area being tested. The limbs are then examined in a fixed order, with the arms before the legs in most protocols, and the examiner compares the two sides at every step, because a limb is judged against its partner rather than against a memory of what is normal.

Sensation is tested with a light touch and with a pinprick, and the two are not the same test, because one travels in the pathways that carry position and vibration and the other in those that carry pain and temperature. The skin is mapped into strips called dermatomes, each supplied mainly by the root of one spinal segment, so a strip of altered sensation points toward the segment that is injured. That map is drawn from averages rather than sharp borders, because the strips overlap and the arrangement differs between one person and another. The patient's eyes are closed and the answers are given in words, such as sharp or dull, which even a patient who cannot use the word pain can supply. The sharp end is never pressed hard enough to break the skin, and the examiner records what the patient feels rather than what the map predicts.

A few landmarks let a listener place a level without a chart. The top of the shoulder marks the fourth cervical segment, the outer upper arm the fifth, the thumb and the index finger the sixth, the middle finger the seventh, and the little finger the eighth. The inner side of the arm at the armpit marks the second thoracic segment, the nipple line the fourth thoracic, the umbilicus the tenth thoracic, and the groin the first lumbar. On the leg, the front of the thigh marks the third lumbar segment, the inner leg above the knee the fourth, the top of the foot the fifth, and the outer foot and the little toe the first sacral. The skin around the anus is supplied by the lowest sacral roots, and these landmarks are approximate in every patient and never replace testing the person in front of you.

The sensory level is the lowest segment at which sensation is normal on both sides, and it is an estimate rather than a measurement, written for the right side and for the left side separately because the two may differ. The word level is used in three senses, since the level of the bone injury is what imaging shows, the sensory level is the lowest segment with normal sensation and the motor level is the lowest segment with normal power, and the three do not always agree. A level that has moved upward since the last examination is one of the most urgent observations in this course, because it means the injury is extending toward the segments that supply the diaphragm. A level that has moved downward often signals recovery, but it is not a reason to relax the observation.

Movement is examined segment by segment, and the examiner tests the key movement each segment supplies rather than every muscle in the limb. Strength is described on a scale that runs from 0, which is no movement at all, to 5, which is movement against full resistance, and the useful middle of that scale is described in words. The words are movement that can be seen but cannot lift the part against gravity, movement that can lift against gravity but not against resistance, and movement that acts against resistance but is weaker than the other side. The examiner asks the patient to perform the movement and then resists it gently, and never forces a joint through a range the patient cannot achieve. A painful limb may be moved weakly for that reason alone, and the record should say so, because pain and weakness are different findings with different meanings.

In the arm, bending the elbow is supplied mainly by the fifth and the sixth cervical segments, straightening the wrist by the sixth, straightening the elbow by the seventh, bending the fingers by the eighth, and spreading the fingers by the first thoracic segment. In the leg, bending the hip is supplied mainly by the second lumbar segment, straightening the knee by the third, lifting the foot upward by the fourth, lifting the great toe by the fifth, and pushing the foot downward by the first sacral segment. The movement of the diaphragm belongs to the third, the fourth and the fifth cervical segments, so an injury high in the neck threatens breathing, and such a patient is watched for a weak cough and a quiet voice. The trained responder supports ventilation within the procedures the service authorizes, while the physician decides whether a definitive airway is needed.

The single most useful question in the whole examination is whether the lowest sacral segments still work, because their function separates a complete injury from an incomplete one. Those segments are tested in three ways: by sensation around the anus, by the pressure the patient feels when a finger presses deeply in the anal canal, and by a voluntary contraction of the anal sphincter. Any one of the three that is present means the injury is incomplete, and an incomplete injury may recover useful function far more often than a complete one. When all three are absent the injury is described as complete at that moment, and the word complete describes the examination rather than the future. The finding is written with the time and the name of the examiner, because prognosis and the choice of treatment will turn on it, and a test that is omitted is not a negative test.

The examination that gives this information is a rectal examination, and it belongs to a clinician or a trained examiner acting within the local protocol, not to a first aider and not to a responder at the roadside. It is performed with gloves, with a lubricant permitted by the service, with a chaperone present, and only after the patient has been told what will be done and has agreed. The examiner notes whether sensation is present, whether the patient feels deep pressure and whether the sphincter contracts around the finger, and each observation is recorded separately. A patient may refuse, and a refusal is respected and written down rather than argued with. The absence of the examination is recorded as an omission and never as a normal finding, and the local protocol decides whether it is done at the scene or deferred to the receiving facility.

Immediately after a severe injury the cord may stop working without being destroyed, a state called spinal shock, in which the limbs are floppy and the reflexes are absent below the level of the injury. In that state the examination cannot tell a complete injury from an incomplete one, because the functions that would answer the question are temporarily silent. The end of the state is marked by the return of the reflex that contracts the anal sphincter, and that test belongs to a clinician. Until the reflex returns, repeated examinations are recorded in the same words so that a comparison remains possible, and no conclusion about completeness is drawn from an absent reflex alone. A patient in spinal shock may lose control of the bladder, which a clinician manages according to the local protocol. The responder's part is to notice the flaccid limbs, keep the patient warm, record what is seen, and say plainly that the picture may change.

A cord injury high in the neck or in the upper part of the thoracic spine may remove the nerve supply that keeps the blood vessels narrowed, and the patient then has a low blood pressure with a pulse that is slow rather than fast. That pattern is called neurogenic shock, and it differs from the shock caused by bleeding, in which the pulse is usually rapid and the skin is cold and clammy. The two may exist in the same patient, and the responder must never assume that the cord injury explains a low blood pressure. The physician decides whether fluid or a medicine to support the blood pressure is needed, and that decision belongs to the prescriber and to the local protocol. This course names no substance and gives no amount, no route and no frequency, because a listener must not be able to take a prescription from it.

Several patterns of incomplete injury are recognized, and the picture at the bedside differs with each. In central cord syndrome the arms are weaker than the legs and the loss of sensation is patchy, and the pattern is seen most often in an older person whose canal is already narrowed after the neck is forced backward. In anterior cord syndrome the injury spares the back of the cord, so the patient loses movement and the sensation of pain and temperature below the level while still feeling vibration and the position of a limb. In the pattern named after Brown-Sequard one half of the cord is injured, and the patient has weakness and loss of position sense on the same side with loss of pain and temperature on the opposite side. When the roots below the end of the cord are injured instead, the patient has floppy weakness of both legs, numbness in the saddle area and retention of urine, and that is a surgical emergency referred urgently for decompression.

Nothing in the field examination of the neck requires the patient to move it, and the responder never asks for that movement. The patient is not asked to touch the chin to the chest, to turn the head or to lift the head from the stretcher, because each of those movements is a test of an unstable column performed with the cord as the instrument. The absence of neck pain does not clear the neck, and the presence of pain does not prove that the spine is broken. The clearing of the cervical spine is a decision made by a clinician who has the examination, the imaging and the authority the local protocol requires, and the decision is recorded. The lay first aider holds the head still if trained and waits, because waiting is the safest act available at the roadside.

The examination is repeated after every intervention and at intervals set by the local protocol, because a spinal injury can change in either direction while the patient lies still. The change this course fears most is the one caused by the treatment rather than by the injury, which is why the examination is repeated after a collar is applied, after the patient is rolled and after the patient is lifted. The times named for those repetitions differ between services and between editions of the same guideline, so a figure such as every 15 minutes is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the previous record, and the comparison is what has value rather than any single result.

Deterioration announces itself in a small number of ways, and the examiner listens for all of them. The sensory level moves upward, or a limb that could be lifted a moment ago cannot be lifted now. The patient reports new numbness in the hands, new tingling in the feet, or a change in the way the chest feels when breathing. Breathing becomes shallow or the cough becomes weak, the voice changes, or the patient becomes restless and then drowsy, and the bladder may become distended with no urge to pass urine at all. Any one of those findings stops the responder, prompts a repeat of the full examination and leads to an urgent escalation, because an injury rising toward the segments that supply the diaphragm threatens life rather than walking.

The record is part of the treatment, and in a spinal injury it is the only way a later change can be proved. It carries the time of the injury, the mechanism, the position in which the patient was found, the observations of the primary survey, and the neurological findings on each side and in each limb. It carries the time of each examination and the name of the examiner, and the exact words the patient used to describe what was felt. It also carries what was not examined and why, since an omission recorded is a gap that can be filled while an omission unrecorded looks like a normal result. The handover to the receiving team follows the same order, so that the next clinician continues a story instead of starting a new one.

Immobilization and examination are performed together rather than one after the other, because the collar that protects the neck also hides it. A wound, a swelling and a piece of glass in the hair are all found by looking before the collar is placed, and the skin beneath the collar is checked afterward by trained staff according to the local protocol. The collar is applied by the trained responder or the paramedic in the size and manner the service authorizes, and a collar that is too large can pull the neck while one that is too tight can press on the vessels. The examination is repeated after the collar, the blocks or the straps are applied, because the device itself can change what the patient is able to do. Nothing in this chapter authorizes a responder to remove a collar that another clinician has placed, and a collar removed for any reason is replaced and the fact recorded.

Each act in this sequence belongs to a named level of practice, and the sequence fails when those levels are blurred. The lay first aider recognizes the mechanism, keeps the head and trunk still with the hands if trained, keeps the patient warm and calls for help, and performs no examination of the spine. The trained first responder performs the observations of the primary survey, applies the spinal motion restriction the service authorizes, and reports the findings without interpreting them. The emergency medical technician and the paramedic may perform a limited neurological check within their own procedures and repeat the observations during transport. The nurse repeats the observations, records them and reports any change to the physician, who performs the complete examination, orders the imaging and decides on the destination. The orthopedic surgeon or the spinal surgeon decides whether the column must be stabilized or the cord decompressed, and the classification of the injury on the impairment scale belongs to an examiner trained and certified in it.

Imaging is ordered by a clinician, it answers a question the examination has raised, and it never replaces the examination. A radiograph of the spine is often the first study, and computed tomography gives far better detail of the bone, the alignment and the canal. A study of the blood vessels of the neck may be added when the injury is high in the neck, because an artery there can be torn by the same force that broke the vertebra and the tear can cause a stroke. Magnetic resonance imaging shows the cord, the disks, the ligaments and the blood within the canal, and it is used when the examination has found a deficit the bone does not explain. The images are read by a clinician trained to read them, and the responder neither orders nor interprets them, because a normal study at one moment does not clear a spine the examination has not cleared.

Reassessment follows the patient through every move, since each move is an intervention. It is repeated after the roll onto the board, after the lift onto the stretcher, after the collar is adjusted, on arrival at the receiving facility and at every handover, and each set is compared with the one before it. Referral or definitive treatment is the final step, and for a spinal injury it means transfer to a facility with imaging, a spinal surgeon, an operating room and an intensive care unit. An operation may relieve the pressure on the cord and stabilize the column with metal, and the technique, the timing and the choice of operation belong to the surgeon and to the local protocol. What the responder contributes is the accurate record of the examination, because the surgeon's decision rests on how the patient has changed since the injury.

The spine of a child bends farther than the spine of an adult while the cord tolerates less stretching, so a child may injure the cord with no break in the bone. The child may be too young to describe numbness, may be frightened into silence and may answer a question to please the examiner, so the examination is performed with a parent present and by a clinician whenever possible. An older adult with a narrow spinal canal or a spine stiffened by arthritis is a different patient with the same sequence, since the neck may be forced backward in a fall from standing height while the radiograph looks ordinary. In both groups the pain may be less than the injury suggests, and the record then says which parts of the examination were completed and which were not. The threshold for immobilization and for referral is lower in both groups, and the decisions about imaging and treatment remain with the clinician.

Nothing in this chapter can be learned from listening alone, and the listener should hear that as a limitation rather than as a formality. The examination of the spine, the testing of sensation, the grading of movement, the clearing of the neck and the classification of a cord injury are learned with a teacher, practiced on models and on each other, and certified by an authority that also defines what may be done without supervision. The numbers and the intervals named in this chapter differ between services, between countries and between editions, and each one must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. A responder who is uncertain about the level of practice that authorizes an act asks before performing it, and asking is a sign of competence rather than of doubt.

To summarize, a suspected injury of the spine is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has done so, and the examination of movement and sensation in each limb is performed before any intervention and repeated after every one, because the change that matters is a comparison and not a single observation. The sequence is the one this course follows everywhere, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and each step belongs to a named level of practice. Assessment begins with the primary survey remembered as ABCDE and with the principle that life comes before limb, stabilization keeps the head, the neck and the trunk in the position found, and cold is treated as a complication. The examination records sensation and movement on both sides, the level of any loss and the sacral sparing that separates a complete injury from an incomplete one, and the testing of the lowest sacral segments belongs to a clinician or to a trained examiner within the local protocol. A rising level, new weakness, new numbness or a change in breathing is a reason to stop and to escalate at once, and any person with a suspected spinal injury and any neurological finding is referred urgently to a facility with imaging, a spinal surgeon and an intensive care unit. A deformed limb is never straightened in the field, an embedded object is never removed, an open wound is never explored, and reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol.
