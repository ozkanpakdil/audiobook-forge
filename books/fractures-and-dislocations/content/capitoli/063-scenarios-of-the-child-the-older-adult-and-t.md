# Chapter 63 — Scenarios of the Child, the Older Adult and the Open Injury

Three patients in this course are frightening for the same reason, and none of them looks frightening at the door. The child whose elbow is swollen and who will not use the arm, the frailer older adult whose hip hurts although nobody saw a fall, and the patient whose open fracture looked like a scratch each carry an injury larger than the finding that announces it. What follows are constructed illustrations rather than real patients, and they are educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work. Every one of them is carried through the same order, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and every act along the way is named with the level of provider who may perform it.

The bone of a child behaves differently from the bone of an adult, and that difference explains both why these injuries are missed and why they matter. The bone is more elastic, its outer sleeve is thicker and stronger, and near each end of a long bone there is a growth region where cartilage is turning into bone. Because that sleeve resists tearing, a child's bone may bend, buckle or break on one side only and then spring back toward its usual shape, so that a limb which appears almost straight still contains an injury. Because the growth region is softer than the shaft, force may damage it without cracking the hard bone at all, and an injury there can disturb the future length and shape of the limb. A child also loses heat quickly through a large surface and small reserves, so warmth matters from the first minute.

Consider a constructed illustration of a child of 7, who is carried into an urgent care room by a parent after falling from a low garden wall, and the parent says the child cried at once, held the arm against the body, has refused to use it since, and now has a swollen elbow. Assessment begins before the elbow is examined, because the scene is made safe, the mechanism and the time are established, and the child is taken through the primary survey whose steps are remembered as ABCDE, meaning airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure. The principle that decides the order when two correct actions compete for the same pair of hands is that life comes before limb, because a limb can wait for minutes that a brain and a heart cannot. The history is taken from the carer as well as from the child, and it includes what the child was doing, whether the arm was pulled by the hand, whether the child has been unwell with a fever and how the child normally uses that arm, while the judgment about the severity of the injury belongs to the clinician.

The swollen elbow of a child has many possible explanations, and telling them apart is clinical work rather than a field skill. Among the possibilities are a pulled elbow, in which a ligament slips over the head of one of the forearm bones after a sharp pull on the hand, a break just above the elbow, a break of the upper end of one of the forearm bones, bleeding into the joint from a torn structure, and an infection of the joint or of the bone. That is why the responder protects the elbow, records what is seen and leaves the testing of movement, of stability and of tenderness to a clinician trained and equipped for it. Nobody in the field should pull on the hand of a child with a swollen elbow in the hope of making it better, because that movement may convert an injury that needed protection into one that needs a surgeon.

Stabilization of the child is a short list of acts, and the most important of them is restraint. The limb is supported in the position in which it was found with the hands, with a pillow or with a folded garment, and a deformed limb is never straightened in the field, because vessels and nerves lie against the bone and a movement that looks helpful can tear what remained intact. A child loses heat quickly, so wet clothing is replaced with dry covering, the child is insulated from the ground and the head is covered, since cold is a complication of trauma and not a detail of comfort. Any medicine is chosen and given by a prescriber working within the local protocol, with the substance and the amount belonging to that prescriber rather than to this course, while the lay first aider performs the acts that need no equipment and the trained responder works within the procedures the service authorizes.

The neurovascular examination is performed before the limb is supported or splinted and repeated after every intervention, and its result is written down, because a finding that was not recorded before a splint was applied cannot be attributed afterwards. The examiner looks at the color and the temperature of the hand and compares it with the other hand, presses briefly on a nail to watch the color return, feels the pulse at the wrist, and asks the child to move the fingers or watches whether the child grasps a toy. Sensation is tested lightly on both sides and compared, and in a child the answer may come as a word, as a grimace or as nothing at all, so the absence of a complaint is not evidence that sensation is intact. The vessels and the nerves at the elbow run directly against the bone, so a break just above the elbow can injure the vessel that supplies the forearm and the nerve that moves the thumb, and the pulse may remain palpable while that vessel is damaged, so the trained responder or the clinician repeats and records the examination after every intervention.

Immobilization means preventing movement of the injured part and of the joints above and below it, because a bone that is splinted alone can still be moved by the joint at either end. The splint is padded where it presses on bone, it is applied over the position found, and it is secured firmly enough to hold without becoming a band that stops the circulation, and it is checked at intervals because an improvised device shifts. The trained responder and the paramedic apply splints within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, while the physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides what definitive immobilization is required, which may be a cast, a brace or an operation. Casting, reduction and traction are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and the technique of each of them belongs to supervised clinical training and never to a book. A reduction is not attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught inside the joint, when the vessels or the nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, when the responder lacks the training or the equipment, or when an earlier attempt has failed, and in each of those situations the attempt can turn an injury that would have healed into one that cannot.

Imaging is the step that confirms what the examination has already suggested, and it is ordered by a clinician trained to interpret it. A radiograph is usually the first study, and in a child the growth regions and the cartilage that has not yet turned to bone make the picture harder to read, so more than one view may be needed and a break that is invisible on the first film may show itself when the limb is imaged again a little later. Computed tomography shows the detail of a complex break around a joint, and magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for soft tissue and for the growth region when the clinical question demands that detail, and the choice belongs to the clinician responsible for the child's care. A child with a swollen elbow, an obvious deformity or any change in the neurovascular status is referred urgently to a facility with imaging, an operating room and an orthopedic surgeon, and the timing of any operation differs between services and is confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

Reassessment of the child is performed after every intervention, and it is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the record, and the comparison is what carries the value rather than any single measurement. The findings that stop a responder and prompt escalation are pain that increases instead of settling, a forearm that becomes tense and swollen, numbness or tingling in the fingers, weakness, pallor, coolness and a pulse that has changed or disappeared. A responder who suspects a compartment syndrome does not wait for the diagnosis to be confirmed, because the diagnosis is clinical and time is muscle, and the constricting bandage or splint is released by someone the local protocol authorizes to release it. The limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, the child is kept warm, and the child is referred urgently with the times and the findings written into the handover.

The second patient is an older adult, and here the constructed illustration is a woman of 84 found sitting on the floor of her living room, complaining of pain in the groin and the front of the thigh, unable to stand, and with no fall remembered by her or by the carer who found her. The absence of a fall is the detail that misleads, because the bone may have given way first and the fall, if there was one, may have been the consequence rather than the cause. A break of the hip can also follow a twist in bed or a turning movement that nobody would call a fall, and it can happen in a patient whose bone has lost density over years without any symptom at all. The responder therefore treats the mechanism as a whole and refuses to be reassured by the story that there was no accident, while the clinician takes the fuller history, including the illnesses and the medicines that affect bone and bleeding, from the patient and the carer.

The examination of an older adult carries traps of its own. A limb that is shortened and turned outward is a well known sign of a broken hip, yet the bone ends may be impacted into one another so that the limb looks almost normal, the patient may be able to take a few steps with support, and the pain may be felt in the knee rather than in the hip. A sign that is treated as always present will be believed even when it is absent, so no single finding excludes the injury, and the threshold for referral is set by the mechanism, by the age of the patient and by the inability to bear weight rather than by the shape of the leg. The responder does not ask the patient to stand or to walk in order to settle the question, does not pull on the leg and does not turn the limb to see how far it moves, because each of those acts can displace an impacted break and add to the bleeding. Injury of the spine is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has done so, and some injuries of the pelvis and of the spine present with pain in the hip or the groin.

Stabilization of the older adult begins with leaving the patient where she was found and keeping her still, while the neck and the back are protected if the mechanism is unknown. No lone manual handling technique is safe, so moving the patient is the work of a trained team using the equipment and the precautions the local protocol requires, and the lay first aider waits with the patient and supports the limb as it lies. A leg that is turned outward is never straightened and never pulled, and the trained responder immobilizes the limb within the equipment and the procedures the service authorizes, while the physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides what definitive immobilization is required. Warmth is a priority in this patient, because a thin older adult on a cold floor loses heat quickly, so the patient is insulated from the ground, wet clothing is replaced and the head and the feet are covered. Pressure injury can begin within a short time on a hard surface when the skin is thin and the patient cannot move.

The neurovascular examination of the older adult follows the same order and answers a different baseline question. The examiner looks at the color and the warmth of the foot, presses a toenail to watch the color return, feels the pulses at the ankle, tests sensation on the top of the foot and on the sole, and asks the patient to move the ankle and the toes, comparing the injured limb with the other one. Many older patients have narrowing of the vessels that has developed over years, so the pulse may be weak and the skin cool on both sides before any injury occurred, and what has value is the difference between the two limbs and the change from the record rather than an absolute standard of health. A break of the thigh bone can release enough blood to threaten the circulation, with a figure offered in teaching as an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between services and editions. A rapid pulse, pale and cool skin and a falling level of consciousness are findings of shock, so this examination is performed by the trained responder or by the clinician and the findings are written before any intervention and repeated after each one.

Imaging and definitive treatment for the older adult are decided by clinicians, and the part played by the responder is to make those decisions possible quickly. The physician orders the radiographs, and a break of the hip may be invisible on the first film, in which case the clinician may request another view, a computed tomography scan or a magnetic resonance imaging scan, because the examination says the injury is there although the picture does not show it. Definitive treatment for most of these injuries is surgical and belongs to the orthopedic surgeon, who may hold the bone with metal devices or replace the worn joint, and the choice depends on the patient, on the pattern of the break and on what the local service can offer. Reduction, traction and casting are part of that work, they are learned under supervision, and they are performed according to local protocol rather than from anything a listener has heard or read. The transfer of an older adult with a suspected broken hip is urgent, and the record must say plainly that the patient cannot bear weight, when the pain began and what the neurovascular status was at each stage.

The third patient is the one whose open fracture looked like a scratch, and the constructed illustration is a man of middle age who came off a bicycle at low speed a short while ago. He is standing, he is speaking in full sentences, there is a small wound on the front of his shin with a spot of blood on the trouser leg, and he would like to go home. Everything about the scene suggests a trivial injury, and that is precisely why this patient is in the chapter, because an open fracture is defined by communication between the broken bone and the outside world and not by how large the opening is. The wound may be a puncture, a scratch or a small hole, and it may lie some distance from the broken bone because the skin slides over the bone when the limb moves, so a wound at the knee can belong to a break in the middle of the shin, and bone may not be visible at all. Bleeding may be slight or absent because the wound is small or because the broken end has moved back inside the limb, so the question that decides the management is not how the wound looks but whether anyone suspects that it reaches the bone, and that question is settled by a clinician and never by the size of a stain.

Recognition of the open injury therefore requires a deliberate search rather than an impression. The whole limb is inspected in good light, front and back, including the back of the thigh and the calf and the spaces between the fingers and the toes, and the patient is asked directly whether there was blood at the scene, on the clothing or in the shoe. Nothing is inserted into the wound to see how deep it goes, and no probe is passed along it, because that act carries contamination deeper and can start bleeding that was not there before. An open fracture is contaminated from the moment it happens, since the skin carries organisms that reach the broken bone during the injury itself, and it remains contaminated even when it looks clean.

Stabilization of the open injury is a short and disciplined set of acts, and it begins with the safety of the responder. Gloves and protection for the eyes, which are part of personal protective equipment, are put on before the wound is touched, because blood carries infection and a responder cannot help anyone after becoming a patient. The wound is covered with a sterile dressing, and bleeding that can be controlled is controlled by pressing on the tissue around the wound rather than on any exposed bone or protruding fragment. An embedded object is never removed, because it may be holding pressure on a torn vessel, and exposed bone is never pushed back into the wound, because the bone is contaminated and the limb will not tolerate the attempt. A wound is never explored blindly, and the part played by the responder is to cover the wound, to control the bleeding that can be controlled, to record the neurovascular status, to immobilize the limb in the position found, to keep the patient warm and to arrange urgent transfer, while definitive closure belongs to the operating room and to nobody else.

The neurovascular examination of an open injury is performed before the dressing is applied and repeated after every intervention. Color, warmth, the pulse beyond the injury, the return of color to a pressed nail, sensation on both sides and movement are all recorded in words, together with the pain and the time of the observation. A pulse that is present does not exclude damage to a vessel or the beginning of a compartment syndrome, and an open wound does not protect the limb from swelling inside the closed compartments that lie deeper. The dressing applied in the field is not lifted in order to look again at the wound, because looking adds contamination and bleeding and changes nothing, whereas a change in the neurovascular status or in the general condition of the patient changes everything. Pain that increases, a limb that becomes tense and swollen, numbness, tingling and weakness are the findings that stop the responder, and the response within the local protocol is to release a constricting bandage or splint, to keep the limb level rather than raised above the heart, to keep the patient warm and to refer urgently, while the lay first aider reports only what is seen and felt.

Two questions about medicine always arise with an open injury, and this course answers both by naming the decision maker rather than the medicine. The first is whether the protection of the patient against tetanus is up to date, and the responder asks when the last protection was given and passes the answer on, while the decision about what is given and how belongs to a prescriber working to the local protocol. The second is whether an antibiotic is needed, and here the finding that makes the decision urgent is the open fracture itself, together with a wound that has been in contact with soil, manure or standing water. No substance, no amount, no frequency and no route appears anywhere in this course, because a listener must not be able to take a prescription from a book. The scope of practice forbids the responder to give any medicine outside a specific authorization, and those authorizations differ between services and between countries.

Imaging of the open injury is ordered by the clinician, and the radiographs are taken together with an assessment of the wound, so that the injury of the bone and the injury of the soft tissue are understood as one problem. In the operating room the surgeon removes the contaminated and the dead tissue, washes the wound out, stabilizes the bone and decides whether and when the skin can be closed, and every part of that work is learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, with the timing differing between services and between countries. The duty of the responder is finished before that point, and it is to cover the wound, to control the bleeding that can be controlled, to record the neurovascular findings, to immobilize the limb in the position found, to keep the patient warm and to move the patient urgently to a facility with an operating room. An open fracture is an emergency even when the patient feels well and even when the wound has stopped bleeding, and the record must say that the wound was found, where it was, how it looked and what was done to it.

The three patients in this chapter share one property, and it is the property that makes them worth remembering. In each of them the injury is larger than the finding, which is a swollen elbow in a child whose bone bent instead of breaking cleanly, a hip that hurts in a patient who cannot recall falling, and a small wound over a bone that has communicated with the outside world. A listener who waits for the textbook picture waits past the moment when the outcome could still be changed, and the response is a lower threshold for immobilization and for referral rather than a different sequence. The sequence itself does not change, and it is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. The record carries the time of the injury, the mechanism, the position in which the limb was found, the measures taken to keep the patient warm and any change in the condition of the patient, so that the receiving team continues a story instead of starting a new one.

None of this licenses a listener to act outside the law, the training or the equipment that applies where the listener works. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, a deformed limb is splinted in the position found and never straightened in the field, an embedded object is never removed, exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, and an open wound is never explored blindly. The injury of a child, the injury of an older adult and the open injury are all referred to a facility with the people and the equipment to treat them, and the decisions about imaging, about medicine and about operation belong to those people and to no one else. Every time, volume, distance and threshold named in this chapter is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between services, between countries and between editions.

To summarize, three patients in this course mislead by appearing less injured than they are, and they are the child with a swollen elbow who will not use the arm, the frailer older adult with pain in the hip and no remembered fall, and the patient whose open fracture looked like a scratch. In each of them the sequence is the same, and it is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, with the neurovascular findings recorded before every intervention and repeated after it. The bone of a child bends and buckles and can injure its growth region, so a quiet swelling and a refusal to use the limb are taken seriously, and nobody pulls on the hand of the child to put the elbow back. An older adult may break a hip without falling, may feel the pain in the knee and may still take a few steps, so the leg is not straightened, not pulled and not tested by walking, and the patient is kept flat, still and warm. An open fracture is contaminated however small the wound, so the wound is covered with a sterile dressing, bleeding is controlled around it, an embedded object is never removed, exposed bone is never pushed back, the wound is never explored, and the patient is moved urgently toward an operating room. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, no dose, no route and no regimen appears in this course, and the spine is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has done so.
