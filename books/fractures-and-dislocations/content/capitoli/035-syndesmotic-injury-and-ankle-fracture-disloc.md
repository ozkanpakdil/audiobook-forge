# Chapter 35 — Syndesmotic Injury and Ankle Fracture-Dislocation

Two injuries of the ankle are taught together in this chapter because one of them is quiet and the other is loud, and both are missed for the same reason. The first is an injury of the strong bands between the two bones of the lower leg, a structure called the syndesmosis, which is easy to overlook because the ankle may look almost normal and the patient may still walk. The second is the ankle fracture-dislocation, in which the joint is out of place and the skin stretched over the bones is at risk of tearing from the inside, and it is one of the few injuries in this course in which a delay of hours changes what the patient keeps. Both are taught through the order this course follows everywhere, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. This chapter is educational material, and it does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

The lower leg is built from two bones, a thick one on the inner side called the tibia and a slender one on the outer side called the fibula, and at the ankle they form a bracket rather than a simple hinge. The bracket holds the lower ends of both bones together above a bone of the foot called the talus, so the talus sits in a socket and the foot can bend up and down within it. The two bones are not fused, because the socket must widen a little when the foot bends up, and that movement is possible only because the bones are bound to each other by strong fibrous bands. Those bands together are the syndesmosis, and the most important of them lie at the front of the ankle, at the back of the ankle, and along the two bones as a sheet called the interosseous membrane. The sheet runs far up the leg, and its work is to keep the two bones moving as one unit while holding the talus centered. When the bands are torn or a bone is broken, the socket can open wider than it should and the talus then shifts or turns inside it, which is the beginning of both of the injuries in this chapter.

The usual mechanism is a twisting force applied to a planted foot while the leg turns the other way, which is why the injury appears in sport, in a fall on a step and whenever one part of the body stops and the rest keeps moving. The energy that tears the ligaments on the outer side of the ankle may instead travel between the two bones, and it may also break the fibula at the ankle or, less often, break it far above the ankle near the knee. A foot driven upward into the socket with great force can injure the syndesmosis and the front of the lower end of the tibia at the same time, and a fall from a height delivers exactly that energy. A patient who describes a crack heard at the moment of the injury and who cannot bear weight deserves the same urgency as a patient whose ankle is visibly out of place. The mechanism is therefore recorded in the words of the patient or of a witness, because it tells the receiving team what to look for.

A syndesmotic injury is missed because its tenderness lies above the joint line, in the space between the two bones, rather than over the prominent bone on the outer side of the ankle where a sprain is expected. The ankle may be only slightly swollen, the skin may show no bruise at the beginning, and the patient may take several steps before the pain becomes sharp, which is how the injury comes to be called a sprain and sent home. Several examinations are taught for it, among them pressing the two bones of the lower leg together above the ankle and turning the foot outward against resistance. Those tests are not perfectly reliable, and their meaning differs in a patient with an old injury, with a nerve condition, or who cannot cooperate because of pain, so a negative test never takes the injury off the list. What the responder can do is simpler and safer, which is to look for the swelling, ask where it hurts most, record that the pain is above the joint line, protect the limb, and pass that description to the clinician who will examine it.

A ligament on the outer side of the ankle heals in a few weeks in most patients, while an injured syndesmosis heals more slowly and less predictably, and the reason lies in how the two bones are loaded. Every time weight passes through the leg the talus presses outward on the socket and tries to widen it, so the torn bands are pulled apart with each step instead of being brought together, and healing tissue that is stretched again and again may lengthen rather than rejoin at its original length. The blood supply of the region also arrives from the ends of the bones rather than through the middle of the bands, so a tear lying between the two bones has less to work with. A period of healing offered in teaching for this injury is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the true time differs with how much was torn and with the treatment chosen. The practical point that follows is simple, because an injury allowed to bear weight before it is assessed can become worse while the patient believes it is improving.

An ankle fracture-dislocation is defined by the position of the bones rather than by the number of them that are broken. The talus is no longer sitting in the socket, and the bones that form the socket have broken, torn away from their ligaments, or both. The injury therefore includes the displacement of the joint, a break of the bone on the inner side or the outer side or both, and often a tear of the syndesmosis, and it may also include a break of the back of the lower end of the tibia. The foot may lie turned outward or inward, or it may be pushed backward or forward, and the whole limb looks wrong in a way that even an untrained bystander recognizes. Because the joint surfaces are no longer in contact, the injury is unstable by definition, and no splint makes it stable.

The emergency in this injury is not the broken bone but the skin, because bones that have moved can press the skin from the inside until it tears. The skin over the inner side of the ankle is thin, and it lies directly over the lower end of the tibia, so a foot displaced outward can drive that bone against it with the force of the whole injury. What the responder looks for is skin that has become pale, white, shiny or tight, a crease or a dimple that was not there before, blisters filled with blood, or a small wound that may look like an abrasion but that leads down to the bone beneath it. A wound of that kind turns a closed injury into an open one, and from that moment the deep tissues are contaminated. Skin can survive under such pressure only for a limited time, and a figure taught for that window is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, which is why the patient is moved to a place where a clinician can act instead of waiting for a routine appointment.

Assessment begins before the limb is touched, because a patient with a deformed ankle may have a more dangerous injury elsewhere. The responder considers the scene, the mechanism, the time of the injury and the age of the patient, and then performs the primary survey whose steps are remembered as ABCDE, which stands for airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure. The limb is examined only when the patient is stable enough, because a life comes before a limb. Both legs are uncovered and compared, and a shoe or a boot is cut away rather than pulled off, since pulling can move the bones and can hide the deformity from the next examiner. The responder does not ask the patient to stand on the injured limb to prove the injury, because weight bearing loads the syndesmosis and can widen a socket that is already unstable. The lay first aider looks, keeps the limb still and calls for help, while the history, the examination and the decision about what a finding means belong to the trained responder and to the clinician.

Stabilization means keeping the patient alive, warm and still while the limb is supported in the position in which it was found. A deformed ankle is never straightened in the field, and the reason is anatomy rather than caution, because the vessels and the nerves lie against the bones of the ankle and a limb that is pulled straight can tear what remained intact and can turn a closed injury into an open one. Bleeding from a wound is controlled by pressure around the wound and never by pressing on exposed bone, and an embedded object is left where it lies because it may be holding a torn vessel closed. Exposed bone is never pushed back into the wound, and a wound is never explored with a finger or an instrument, because blind exploration adds injury and contamination while adding nothing to the plan. The limb is supported on a pillow or on whatever is at hand, the toes are left visible, and the patient is insulated from the ground, since cold is a complication of trauma and not a detail of comfort. Wet clothing is replaced with dry covering, and the patient is not left on a cold surface while the responder waits for transport.

The neurovascular examination is performed before any splint is applied and repeated after every intervention, and its result is written down, because a finding that was not recorded before a splint was applied cannot be attributed afterwards. The examiner feels for the pulse behind the inner ankle, which is the posterior tibial artery, and for the pulse on the top of the foot, which is the dorsalis pedis artery, and compares each with the other side, since either may be faint or absent in a healthy person. Color, warmth and the time it takes for color to return after a nail is pressed are noted, and the toes are checked for sensation on the sole and in the web space between the first and second toes. The patient is asked to move the toes and the ankle, and any movement weaker than on the other side is recorded. A pulse that can be felt does not exclude serious injury to the vessels, to the nerves or to a compartment, while a pulse that cannot be found is an emergency to be reported at once. The trained responder or the paramedic may perform this examination and record it, while the interpretation of an abnormal finding belongs to a clinician.

Compartment syndrome is the complication this course fears most in the lower leg, and it can follow an injury that looked modest at the beginning. The compartments of the lower leg and of the foot are closed spaces bounded by bone and by a tough sheet of fibrous tissue, and bleeding or swelling inside one of them raises the pressure until the flow through the smallest vessels fails. The earliest and most reliable finding is pain that is greater than the injury appears to explain and that grows instead of settling, together with pain when the muscles of the compartment are gently stretched, an act performed by a clinician. A limb that is tense and swollen, a foot that is numb over the sole or the toes, and toes that cannot be moved are late findings, and a pulse that can still be felt does not exclude the diagnosis. When a responder suspects it, the constricting bandage or splint is released within the limits of the local protocol by someone authorized to do it, the limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, the patient is kept warm, and the patient is referred urgently, because the diagnosis is clinical and time is muscle.

Immobilization means preventing movement of the injured part and of the joints above and below it, so an ankle injury is held together with the foot and with the leg and not with the ankle alone. A splint is padded where it presses on bone, it is applied over the position in which the limb was found, and it is secured firmly enough to hold without becoming a band that stops the circulation. The material matters less than the principle, and a commercial splint, a folded blanket, a padded board or a pillow bound in place can each serve, although an improvised device must be checked more often because it shifts. The lay first aider supports the limb with the hands and with whatever is at hand, while the trained responder and the paramedic may apply a splint within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes. The splint is checked after it is applied, the toes are left visible so that their color can be watched, and any ring or tight band below the injury is removed before swelling makes that impossible. A cast is not a splint, a cast is applied by a clinician, and no field responder decides that one is needed.

Imaging is the step that confirms what the examination has already suggested, and it is ordered by a clinician trained to interpret it. A radiograph is usually the first study, taken in at least two directions so that a displacement in the third direction is not hidden, with the joint above and the joint below included so that a second injury is not missed. For the ankle that means the whole length of the fibula is part of the picture, including the part just below the knee, because a tear of the syndesmosis can accompany a break far above the joint. A radiograph may appear nearly normal when the syndesmosis is torn, since the widening of the socket may show itself only when the foot is loaded, so a normal film does not clear the patient. The clinician may then need a view taken while the foot is stressed, a computed tomography study or a magnetic resonance imaging study, because computed tomography shows the detail of a complex break while magnetic resonance imaging shows the bands themselves. Either study is requested when the answer will change what is done, and neither the lay first aider nor the trained responder decides which study is needed.

Reassessment is performed after every intervention, and it is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the record, because the comparison carries the value rather than any single measurement. The limb is checked for color, warmth, pulse, sensation, movement and pain, the skin is checked for new tightness, blistering or a wound, and the splint is checked for tightness, for slipping and for pressure over bone. Pain that grows instead of settling, pain out of proportion to the injury, numbness, tingling, weakness, and a limb that becomes tense and swollen are the findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation. A responder who suspects a compartment syndrome does not wait for the diagnosis to be confirmed and acts within the local protocol, and a responder who finds that a pulse has disappeared or that the toes have gone pale reports it at once rather than adjusting the splint alone. The nurse who receives the patient repeats the same examination, records it, and keeps the limb visible so that a change is noticed early.

Referral or definitive treatment is the last step, and for both of the injuries in this chapter it begins with the recognition that the decision belongs to someone else. A patient with an unstable syndesmosis, a fracture-dislocation, a wound over the joint, a damaged nerve or vessel, or a tense swollen leg is referred to a facility that has radiography, an operating room and an intensive care unit, and the referral is urgent rather than routine. The orthopedic surgeon decides whether the two bones of the lower leg must be held together while the bands heal, a judgment that depends on how far the socket widens under load. When the joint is judged unstable, the hold may be provided by a screw or by a flexible device placed across the two bones, and the surgeon may also use plates and screws to hold a broken bone, an operation called open reduction and internal fixation. Some ankles are treated without an operation, in a cast or in a walking support with restricted weight bearing, and which route is taken differs between countries and between surgeons. Recovery is then measured in weeks and months, and the plan is adjusted by the treating team, while the responders have no part in that choice and only have to deliver the patient in the best possible condition for it.

Reduction is the word for restoring the position of the joint and of the bone ends, and in an ankle fracture-dislocation it is done to take the pressure of the bone off the skin before the skin dies. That pressure is the reason the injury is treated as an emergency, and the act itself is a clinical procedure performed with sedation, with monitoring and with imaging available, by an emergency physician or an orthopedic surgeon according to the local protocol. The responder never performs it, never pulls on the foot to improve the look of the limb, and never allows a bystander to try, because force applied by an untrained hand can tear the skin further, break a bone that was intact, or injure the vessels behind the inner ankle. The technique, the amount of force and the way the limb is held afterwards are learned under supervision on real patients and are not taught from a book, and no chapter of this course can take the place of that training. What the field responder gives instead is time saved, which is the one thing that reliably protects the skin.

There are situations in which even a qualified clinician does not attempt a reduction, and the reason is always that the attempt can make the injury worse rather than better. It is not attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment of bone is caught between them, when the vessels or the nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, or when the person present lacks the training or the equipment. An earlier failed attempt is also a reason to stop, because repetition adds injury without adding position, and resistance felt during any attempt is a signal to stop rather than to push harder. The listener should hear the pattern rather than the list, which is that whenever the harm of trying is greater than the harm of waiting for the right hands, the patient is moved instead. Traction, which holds the bone ends in line with a gentle continuous pull, and casting, which holds a position while the bone heals, are learned in the same supervised way, and both can cause skin damage, loss of position, stiffness or a compartment syndrome when they are done without training or follow-up.

Some acts are set aside in every chapter of this course because they turn an injury that would have healed into one that cannot. A deformed limb is splinted in the position found and is never straightened in the field, an embedded object is never removed, exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, and an open wound is never explored blindly. The wound is covered with a sterile dressing, the bleeding that can be controlled is controlled, the neurovascular status is recorded, the limb is immobilized, and the patient is referred urgently, while definitive closure belongs to the operating room and to nobody else. Antibiotics and protection against tetanus are decisions for the prescriber and the local protocol, and no dose, no route and no regimen are given in this course, because a listener must not be able to take a prescription from these words. Pain relief follows the same rule, and the responder who covers the wound well, splints the limb in the position found and reports the findings has done the part that belongs to the responder.

The same injury behaves differently in different patients, and a course that teaches one picture will be believed. An older adult whose bone has lost density can break an ankle in a fall from standing height, and the skin over that bone may be thin and slow to heal, so a pressure sore from a splint is a real complication of the treatment. A patient with diabetes may have lost the sensation of the feet long before the injury and may report little pain while the joint is badly displaced, so the absence of pain is not evidence that the injury is minor. A child has a growth region near the ends of the tibia and the fibula, and a twist that tears a ligament in an adult can injure that region or pull a small piece of bone away with the band in a child, which changes both the treatment and the follow-up. A patient whose bone is weakened by disease also breaks more easily, and the mechanism may look trivial in the history. In all of these patients the order of the steps does not change, but the threshold for immobilization and for referral is lower, and the decisions about imaging and about treatment belong to the clinician.

Everything the responder has noticed is written down or spoken in a fixed order, because the receiving team continues a story rather than starting a new one. The time of the injury, the mechanism, the position in which the limb was found, the state of the skin, the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention, the splint that was applied and by whom, and any change in the condition of the patient are recorded and handed over in that order. An ankle injury caused by a fall from a height or by a heavy blow is treated as part of a whole patient, and an injury of the spine is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has done so, with no single manual handling technique regarded as safe. The patient who must wait is kept warm, covered and observed, and the limb is left visible so that a change in color or in swelling is noticed by whoever is nearest. Where this chapter names a time, a size or a period of healing, it names an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between services, countries and editions.

The two injuries of this chapter can be brought together in a constructed illustration. A middle-aged person hurries down a step, plants the foot and turns the body, and feels a sharp pain above the outer ankle rather than over the prominent bone, while the ankle is only slightly swollen and the person walks to the car. By that evening the leg is tense, the sole of the foot is numb and the pain is severe, and the clinician finds a widening of the socket that the first radiograph did not show. In another constructed illustration an older person slips on ice, the foot turns outward, and the ankle is visibly out of place with pale, tight skin over the inner side of the joint. The responder supports that limb in the position found, keeps the patient warm, records the pulses and the sensation, sends the patient to a facility with an operating room, and does not touch the joint. Neither example is a real patient and neither carries any institutional detail, and they are written only to show that the loudness of an ankle injury is no measure of its severity.

To summarize, the syndesmosis is the set of strong bands between the two bones of the lower leg, and an injury of it is missed because the tenderness lies above the joint line. An ankle fracture-dislocation is an unstable injury in which the joint is out of place and the skin over the bones may tear from the inside, which makes it urgent rather than routine. The order is always the same, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, with the neurovascular findings recorded before and after every intervention. A deformed limb is splinted in the position found and is never straightened in the field, an embedded object is never removed, exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, and an open wound is never explored blindly, while definitive closure belongs to the operating room. Reduction, traction and casting are performed by qualified clinicians, learned under supervision and carried out according to the local protocol, and a reduction is not attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when the vessels or the nerves are failing, when the injury is open, or when training and equipment are lacking. The skin of the ankle sets the clock, so the patient with a fracture-dislocation goes to a facility with an operating room without delay, and every patient is kept warm, reassessed after each intervention and handed over with a clear record.
