# Chapter 43 — Common Pediatric Fractures

A child who has fallen and now holds one arm against the chest is a common sight in pediatric emergency care, and the injury is seldom the one an adult would suffer from an equal fall. A child's bone is softer and more elastic, and the sleeve around it is thicker than the sleeve around adult bone, so a bone may bend, buckle or crack on one side instead of breaking in two. This chapter follows the order used in every chapter of this course, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and it names the level of practice for each act. It is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

The differences begin in the tissue rather than in the size of the bone. The periosteum is the tough sleeve that wraps the bone, and in a child it is thick and firmly attached at the ends, so it often stays intact on one side of a break and holds the broken ends near each other. A crack on one side with a bend on the other is called a greenstick fracture, and a bone that is compressed and bulges without a clear crack has a buckle fracture, which is common in early childhood. Because a child's bone remodels as the child grows, a bend may straighten over months while a twist about the long axis does not, which is one reason a forearm injury in a child is never dismissed as minor.

Near each end of a long bone in a child lies a growth region, a band of cartilage in which new bone is made and from which the bone lengthens. An injury that crosses that band may disturb the length or the shape of the bone for the rest of childhood, and the damage may not show at the first examination, because cartilage does not appear on a radiograph as bone does. A growth region can be mistaken for a fracture line, and a fracture through it can be mistaken for the ordinary appearance of a growing bone, so the image is read by a clinician who knows the appearances of childhood. Tenderness over the end of the bone, swelling, and a child who will not use the limb raise suspicion even when the radiograph is normal, and the responder records them, immobilizes the limb and refers.

Assessment begins with the scene, the mechanism and the child, and only then turns to the limb. The time of the injury, the height of the fall, the surface struck and whether a vehicle was involved all belong to the story, because the same fracture means different things after a stumble and after a road crash. The primary survey comes first, and its steps are remembered as ABCDE, meaning airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure, because a child who is drowsy or bleeding elsewhere is treated before an arm is examined. A child may be unable to say where it hurts, may be too frightened to answer, or may cry whenever the limb is touched, so the parent is the historian and the child's behavior is the finding. The limb is examined by looking, by feeling and by asking the child to move it, never by forcing a movement the child resists, and a movement the child will not make is itself a finding.

Sometimes the story does not fit the injury, and that mismatch is a clinical finding rather than a moral judgment. A child who is not yet mobile cannot break a thigh bone by rolling off a sofa, a bruise in the shape of a hand is not caused by a fall, and injuries of different ages suggest more than one event. When the mechanism does not explain the injury, or when the caregiver delays seeking help for reasons that do not hold, the responder records what was said and what was seen and reports it along the path that the local protocol and the law require. That duty belongs to every health professional and every trained responder, and it is carried out without accusation, while the child is immobilized and referred exactly as any other child would be.

Stabilization of the injured limb in a child is a small set of acts, and the most important of them is restraint. A deformed arm or leg is supported in the position in which it was found and is never straightened in the field, because the vessels and the nerves lie against the bone and a movement that looks helpful can tear what was left intact. The limb is supported by the hands of the first aider, by a pillow or by whatever is at hand, and the child is kept warm, because cold is a complication of trauma. A child who may need sedation or an anesthetic is kept from food and drink, a decision that belongs to the treating clinician. The lay first aider supports and warms the child, the trained responder and the paramedic apply the splints and slings their service authorizes, and both keep the parent with the child whenever it is safe.

The neurovascular examination is performed before any intervention and repeated after it, and in a child it takes patience rather than force. The examiner looks at the color and warmth of the hand or foot, compares the two sides, presses briefly on a nail to watch the color return, and feels the pulse beyond the injury. A pulse may be hard to find in a plump or frightened child, and the examiner's own pulse may be mistaken for the pulse of the child, so the finding is checked on the other side and recorded with its uncertainty. Sensation is tested lightly on both sides, and the child is asked to move the fingers or the toes, because a movement that has disappeared is the clearest sign of a nerve that is no longer working. The record made before the splint is applied is the only thing that can be compared with the record made afterwards.

Immobilization means preventing movement of the injured part and of the joints above and below it, because a bone that is splinted alone can still be moved by the joint at either end. In the upper limb this usually means a support that holds the elbow and the wrist, and in the lower limb a splint that holds the knee and the ankle, applied over padding that protects the skin and the bony points. The device is secured firmly enough to hold the limb without becoming a band that stops the circulation, and the fingers or the toes are left visible so that color, warmth and movement can be checked. The trained responder and the paramedic apply these devices within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, while casting and surgical fixation are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol.

A radiograph is usually the first study in a child with a suspected fracture, and it is taken in at least two directions, with the joint above and the joint below included so that a second injury is not missed. The appearances of growth make a child's image harder to read, so the clinician may ask for a view of the other side or for a repeat image after an interval, and any interval chosen is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. Computed tomography is used when the detail of a complex break around the elbow will change what is done, and magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for the soft tissues and for the growth region. The lay first aider never orders or interprets an image, and a normal radiograph does not exclude a fracture in a child who will not use the limb.

Reassessment is performed after every intervention, and in a child it rests more on observation than on words. The limb is checked again for color, warmth, pulse, sensation, movement and pain, the support is checked for tightness, and the findings are compared with the record made before it was applied. A child who becomes quieter is not necessarily better, because a limb that is losing its blood supply becomes painful and then numb, and the child may simply stop crying about it. Where a compartment syndrome is suspected, the constricting bandage, splint or cast is released within the limits of the local protocol by someone authorized to do it, the limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, and the child is referred urgently, because the diagnosis is clinical and time is muscle.

Referral or definitive treatment is the last step of the sequence, and it begins with the recognition that a child's fracture is not always treated where the child lies. A child is referred to a facility with imaging and with a clinician trained in the care of children when the injury is open, when the pulse or the sensation has changed, when a compartment syndrome is suspected, when the fracture crosses a growth region, or when treatment will need sedation or an operating room. The receiving facility is told what was found, what was done and what changed, in a fixed order, so that the next team continues a story instead of starting a new one. The level of practice is named with each act, since the lay first aider supports and warms, the trained responder and the paramedic examine and splint, the nurse monitors and reassesses, and the physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides about imaging, reduction, casting or operation.

The fracture that matters most in this chapter is the break of the bone just above the elbow, called the supracondylar fracture. It is the commonest fracture of the elbow in childhood, and it happens most often when a child falls onto an outstretched hand with the elbow partly straight, so that the force travels up the forearm and out through the thin part of the bone above the joint. The child holds the elbow bent and refuses to use the arm at all, while the elbow swells quickly and a deformity may appear, though in a plump arm the change in shape can be hard to see. This is not a sprain and it is not a pulled elbow, and a child who will not use an arm after a fall is treated as having this injury until a clinician has said otherwise.

The vessel that supplies the forearm and the hand passes in front of the bone at the elbow, and the median, radial and ulnar nerves pass close to the same place, so a displaced break can kink the vessel, tear it or trap a nerve. The forearm is also divided into tight compartments by fascia, and the swelling that follows the injury raises the pressure inside them, which can shut off the blood supply to muscle and nerve even while a pulse can still be felt at the wrist. A pulse that is present therefore does not exclude a hand that is at risk, and the findings that matter are a hand that is pale, cool and slow to refill, a child who will not move the fingers, and pain that grows instead of settling. This is the injury above all others in this chapter that can cost the limb, and the same anatomy explains why straightening a displaced supracondylar fracture is a hospital procedure and never a field procedure.

The response to a suspected supracondylar fracture is made of restraint, warmth, a careful record and speed. The limb is supported and splinted in the position in which it was found, the neurovascular status is recorded before and after the splint is applied, and the arm is never straightened, pulled or reduced outside a hospital, because the fragments lie against the vessel and the nerves. The child is kept warm and is given nothing by mouth, since sedation or an anesthetic may be needed, and is transferred urgently to a facility where an orthopedic surgeon and a clinician experienced in the anesthesia of children are available. The definitive treatment of a displaced supracondylar fracture is decided by the orthopedic surgeon and may include a reduction under anesthesia and the holding of the fragments with wires, and every one of those acts is learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol.

Fractures of the forearm are among the commonest fractures of childhood, and they often involve both bones, the radius and the ulna. The mechanism is usually a fall onto the outstretched hand, and the result may be a greenstick crack on one side of the bone, a buckle at the end of the bone, or a complete break with displacement. The child guards the forearm, holds it against the body and will not turn the palm up or down, and there is swelling, tenderness along the bone and sometimes a visible bend. The forearm is a tight space in which muscle, vessels and nerves are packed against the bone, so the swelling that follows a fracture of both bones is the finding to watch most closely, and the limb is supported and the child referred for imaging and for the decision about treatment.

Two things decide how a fractured forearm will work months later, and both belong to the clinician rather than to the responder. The first is the alignment of the bones in rotation, because a forearm that has healed with a twist loses the turn of the palm, and that loss disables a child far more than a small bend that growth would have straightened. The second is the condition of the soft tissues, because a forearm that has been swollen and tight can leave a hand that is stiff and weak even when the bone has healed. The part of the responder is to immobilize the limb as a whole, including the elbow and the wrist in the support that is applied, and to check the fingers before and after that support is applied. The trained responder and the paramedic do so within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, and if the pain increases or the fingers become pale, the child is reassessed at once and referred urgently.

A fracture of the clavicle is one of the commonest injuries of childhood, and it is usually caused by a fall onto the shoulder or onto the outstretched hand. The child holds the arm still against the body and is unwilling to lift it, the shoulder on the injured side sits lower and further forward than the other one, and the head may be tilted toward that side. There is tenderness over the bone, and sometimes a visible step in its outline that will later become the firm bump of new bone. The vessels and nerves that supply the arm pass behind the clavicle, so the neurovascular status of the hand is recorded before anything is applied and again afterwards. Most fractures of the clavicle in a child heal well with support and time, and the choice of support belongs to the clinician, while the trained responder applies the sling the local protocol authorizes. A fracture of the inner part of the clavicle, where the great vessels lie, a fracture that opens the skin, a pale or numb hand, and a child who is unwell all make the injury urgent and require immediate referral, and no one pulls on the arm or attempts to straighten it.

The clavicle can also break at birth, and that injury is seen in the nursery rather than in the field. A newborn with a fractured clavicle does not move the arm on that side, a finding sometimes called pseudoparalysis, and the arm lies still while the other arm and both legs move normally. The injury may be mistaken for a stretch of the nerves of the arm that happens during a difficult delivery, and the two can occur together, so the infant is examined by a clinician who can distinguish them. A firm bump that appears over the fracture some weeks later is new bone forming as it heals rather than a tumor, and the family is told that it is expected, though the judgment about any swelling belongs to the clinician.

The pulled elbow, also called the nursemaid's elbow, is not a fracture at all, and it is one of the few injuries in this chapter that a clinician can usually settle quickly. The head of the radius, one of the bones of the forearm, is held against the bone of the upper arm by a ring of ligament, and in a young child that ring is slack enough that a sudden pull on the hand lets the head slip part of the way out of it. It happens most often between the first and the fourth year of life, a range that must be checked against the version of the guideline and the protocol that govern your service, because such figures differ between services and countries. The child stops using the arm, holds it slightly bent with the palm turned down and cries when the arm is moved, yet there is no swelling, no deformity and no bruising, which is exactly why the injury is sometimes dismissed as a refusal to use the arm.

The treatment of a pulled elbow is a specific, gentle maneuver that returns the head of the radius into its ring, and the maneuver is learned in supervised clinical training and performed according to local protocol rather than read about in a book. It may be performed by a physician or by a trained provider whom the local protocol authorizes, and it is not an act for a lay first aider, for a bystander, or for anyone who has not been taught it with a teacher. A radiograph is often normal in a pulled elbow, so a normal image does not exclude it, and the maneuver is not attempted when there is swelling, bruising, tenderness over the bone, a deformity, a wound, or any change in the pulse, the sensation or the movement of the hand. It is not repeated, because repeated attempts turn a simple injury into a swollen elbow that can no longer be assessed. The aim of the maneuver is that the child uses the arm again, and where the child does not, the clinician reassesses and images the elbow, while a fever or a hot and swollen joint in a child who is unwell points to an infection that belongs to the clinician at once.

The toddler's fracture is a crack in the bone of the lower leg that is easy to miss and easy to remember once it has been seen. It is usually a fine spiral or oblique crack in the tibia, the larger bone of the lower leg, and it happens in a young child who twists the leg while learning to walk, steps into a small hole or falls while running. The child refuses to put weight on the leg, crawls or asks to be carried, and may be perfectly content while sitting still, while the leg shows no swelling, no deformity and no bruise. The tenderness is found only by pressing carefully along the bone, and a missed toddler's fracture leaves a child in pain and hides the possibility that the history is not the whole story.

Radiographs in a toddler's fracture are often normal at the first visit, and that is what makes the injury dangerous to the careless. A clinician may therefore ask for the leg to be imaged again after an interval, or may use another study, and the choice of study and the length of the interval belong to the clinician who knows the local protocol. The age of the child is usually between the first and the third year, and that range is another illustration, to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because practice differs between services and editions. The part of the responder is to think of the injury in a child who will not walk, to immobilize the leg as the local protocol allows, to keep the child warm, and to refer with the record of what was found.

Of the injuries in this chapter, some threaten the limb within hours and others threaten the child's comfort for days, and knowing which is which is the first skill of the responder. The supracondylar fracture of the elbow is the clearest emergency, because the vessel and the nerves of the forearm lie against the broken bone and the swelling inside the forearm can close the circulation to the hand. An open fracture of any bone is an emergency, and the wound is covered with a sterile dressing while the bleeding that can be controlled is controlled and the child is referred for an operating room. A fracture of both bones of the forearm with a tense, swollen compartment is an emergency too, and so is any limb with a pulse that is fading or a hand that is pale and cold. The fracture of the clavicle, the pulled elbow and the toddler's fracture seldom threaten the limb, but each of them can be missed, and where the responder is uncertain, the child is treated as if the worse injury were present.

Comfort is part of the treatment of a child and it is never an afterthought. Pain makes a child move, and movement makes the injury worse, so a supported limb and a child who is kept warm and near a parent will often settle before anything else is done. Where a medicine for pain is needed, the substance, the amount and the way it is given are decided by a prescriber authorized to decide them and are recorded according to the local protocol. For an open fracture, protection against tetanus and the use of an antibiotic are decisions for the prescriber, and this course names no dose, no route and no regimen, so no prescription can be taken from it.

A child loses heat faster than an adult, because the surface of a small body is large in relation to its weight, so warmth is a treatment rather than a courtesy. Wet clothing is replaced with dry covering, the child is insulated from the ground, and a hat and a blanket are used whenever they are available, while the injured limb is left visible enough to be checked. The record and the handover carry the rest of the care, and the time of the injury, the mechanism, the position in which the limb was found and the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention are written or spoken in a fixed order. An injury of the spine in a child is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has done so, and no single manual handling technique is safe on its own, so a child who has fallen from a height is moved by a trained team using the precautions the local protocol requires.

To summarize, a child's bone bends before it breaks, so a fracture that looks minor may still involve a growth region, a nerve or a vessel, and the child may be unable to say where it hurts. A child who will not use an arm or a leg is assessed, stabilized, examined for the neurovascular status, immobilized in the position found, imaged, reassessed, and then referred or treated definitively, and the neurovascular findings are recorded before and after every intervention, because a finding that was not recorded cannot be attributed afterwards. The supracondylar fracture of the elbow is the injury that most often threatens the limb, an open fracture and a tense swollen forearm are emergencies, and the fractured clavicle, the pulled elbow and the toddler's fracture are the injuries most often missed. A deformed limb is never straightened in the field, an embedded object is never removed, exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, and no one attempts a reduction when the diagnosis is unclear, when the injury is open, when the vessels or the nerves are failing, or when the person lacks the training and the equipment. The maneuver for a pulled elbow, the reduction of a fracture, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and this course teaches only what they are for, what can go wrong and who is qualified to do them. Cold is treated as a complication, the spine is treated as unstable until it is cleared by someone qualified to clear it, no dose and no regimen is given here, and the child who is getting worse is escalated at once.
