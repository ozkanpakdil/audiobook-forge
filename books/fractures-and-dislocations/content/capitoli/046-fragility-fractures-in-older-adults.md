# Chapter 46 — Fragility Fractures in Older Adults

A fragility fracture is a break in a bone that has lost its strength, and the force behind it is often no more than a fall from standing height. The bone gives way before anything violent has happened to the body, so an injury a young person would walk away from leaves an older patient on the floor. The difference between this patient and the patient of the earlier chapters is not the fracture pattern but the body around it. The bone has thinned, the skin has grown thin and dry and tears where it would once only bruise, the heart and lungs have little reserve, and the reflexes that answer a stumble are slower. This chapter is educational material, and it does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work. Every chapter of this course follows one order, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and it names who may perform each act.

Assessment begins before the patient is touched, with the scene and with the story it tells. The lay first aider notes what the patient fell onto, how the limb lies, and whether the patient is awake and breathing, and nothing is moved until that much is known. The next questions decide the course of care. When was the patient last seen well and walking, where do they live, who is with them, and can they get up. The hours spent on the floor matter more than the minutes in the ambulance, because cold, pressure, thirst and fear accumulate while the patient waits, so a patient found after a long night arrives weaker than the fracture alone explains. The fall itself is often the symptom of something that is not an injury, so the responder notes where the patient was, what they were doing, and whether the fall is remembered. The history is taken from the patient when the patient can give it, and from the family or the caregiver when the patient cannot.

The primary survey comes before the limb, and its steps are remembered as ABCDE, meaning airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure. Life comes before limb, because a brain and a heart cannot wait. In the older patient this survey is quick but never superficial, because a fall may be the first sign of an illness that is not an injury at all. The airway is checked and kept clear, the chest is watched for equal movement, and the ribs are felt for tenderness, because a rib broken in a fall can lead to an infection of the chest in the days that follow. The pulse and the skin are felt for shock, the level of consciousness is recorded, and the body is exposed enough to find the second injury that the first one hides. Only then does the assessment turn to the limb, which is looked at, felt and asked to move, and never forced.

Confusion is the finding that makes this chapter necessary, because in an older patient a change in the mind may be the only sign of a fracture. A patient who was lucid at breakfast may be drowsy, restless, frightened or unnaturally quiet after a fall, and that change is treated as an injury until someone qualified has shown that it is not. The confusion may come from the fracture itself, through pain, blood loss and fear, or from a medicine, an infection, a low blood sugar, a stroke or a blow to the head, and it may also be the reason the patient fell in the first place. A patient who repeats a question, who cannot say where they are, or who has stopped speaking needs to be examined rather than reassured, and the lay first aider never attributes confusion to old age. The responder reports what has changed, when it changed, and what the patient was like before, because that comparison tells the clinician whether the mind or the injury came first.

Pain in the older adult is real, and it is far more often underreported than absent. The patient may say discomfort rather than pain, or may say nothing at all and simply stop moving it. A patient with a memory problem may show the pain as agitation or as a face that tightens when the limb is moved, and that expression is a finding worth recording. Pain is measured with the tool the local service uses, at rest and on moving, and it is measured again after every intervention. Relief of pain belongs to the prescriber, because the substance, the amount, the route and the interval belong to the local protocol and to the clinician who knows the patient's kidneys, stomach and heart. The responder measures and records the pain, supports and immobilizes the limb, and reports pain that is not controlled, while the lay first aider gives nothing by mouth unless a protocol authorizes it, and nobody gives a medicine taken from the patient's own supply.

The medicines an older patient takes change both the chance of the injury and the response to it. A medicine that reduces the clotting of the blood means that a fall which would leave a bruise can instead bleed, and the blood may collect in the thigh, in the pelvis, in the wall of the abdomen, or inside the skull, where it is dangerous and cannot be seen. A medicine that slows the heart can hide the rapid pulse that would otherwise announce blood loss. A medicine that lowers the blood pressure, a sedative, or a medicine taken for sleep can make the patient unsteady, and some medicines weaken the bone and thin the skin over years of use. The responder asks what is taken, writes the names down or brings the boxes, and never stops, changes or advises a medicine, because that decision belongs to the prescriber and to the local protocol.

The skin of the frail patient is part of the injury rather than a covering over it. It is thin, it has lost the padding beneath it, and it tears under shearing forces that a younger person would not notice, so a wound over a fracture may have been made by the fall, by a rescuer's hand, or by the tape that held a dressing. Any break in the skin near a broken bone is treated as an open fracture until a clinician has shown otherwise, because the deep tissues may have communicated with the outside even when the wound is small. The responder covers the wound with a sterile dressing, controls the bleeding that can be controlled, records the neurovascular status, immobilizes the limb in the position found, keeps the patient warm, and refers the patient urgently. No embedded object is ever removed, no exposed bone is ever pushed back into a wound, and no wound is ever explored blindly. Cleaning and closure belong to the operating room, and the nurse or the physician who dresses such a wound before that acts within the protocol of the local service.

The neurovascular examination is performed before any intervention and repeated after it, and its result is written down, because a finding that was not recorded before a splint was applied cannot be attributed afterwards. In the older patient this examination is harder, and it is never skipped for that reason. The examiner looks at the color and the warmth of the skin, compares the two sides, presses briefly on a nail to watch the color return, feels the pulse beyond the injury, and asks the patient to move the fingers or the toes. Sensation is tested on both sides, and in a patient who cannot answer, the response to a light touch and the change from the previous record carry the information. Sensation may already have been reduced before the injury by diabetes or by disease of the nerves, so the comparison with the other limb is worth more than any single test. A pulse that is present does not exclude a threatened compartment, and a pulse that is absent is an emergency. The lay first aider observes color, warmth and movement, and the formal examination belongs to the trained responder or to the clinician.

The hip is the fracture that most often decides whether an older patient walks again, and it deserves the most careful assessment. The patient usually cannot put weight on the leg, and the leg may lie shorter than the other and turned outward, but a fracture whose ends are still packed together can allow the patient to stand and even to take a step, so the ability to walk does not exclude the injury. Pain is felt in the groin or the thigh and is worse when the hip is moved or when the heel is pressed upward with the leg straight. The general condition matters as much as the limb, because a fracture of the hip can bleed a large volume into the thigh and the pelvis, and the older heart may answer with little more than a rising pulse when the patient sits up. The neurovascular examination of the leg is recorded before anything is applied, and the skin over the sacrum, the heels and the elbows is inspected.

Management of the suspected hip fracture in the field is a study in restraint. The limb is supported and immobilized in the position in which it is found and is never straightened, because the vessels and the nerves lie against the bone and a movement that looks tidy can tear what remained intact. A pillow between the knees keeps the legs from crossing, and the patient is lifted by a team with a device the local protocol authorizes, never by one pair of arms. Traction applied to the leg is not a field treatment for this fracture, and the reduction of a displaced hip belongs to the orthopedic surgeon in an operating room. The definitive decision, whether the bone is held with metal or the joint is replaced, belongs to the surgeon and to the patient, and the part played by the responder is to deliver a warm, comfortable and documented patient early. Any target for the time to an operation, such as a period counted in hours, is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the protocol of the local service.

The vertebra is the fragility fracture most often missed, because it may follow no fall at all. A patient who bends to lift a bag, who sits down heavily, or who stumbles onto the buttocks may break one of the blocks of the spine, and the pain is felt in the middle or the lower part of the back, worse on standing and easier on lying flat. Such a fracture is often called a strain for weeks, and the patient simply becomes less willing to go out. The loss of height and a posture that has become bent forward are signs that appear with time, and they speak of fractures that have already happened. A broken vertebra is also a warning about the future, because bone that has broken once is likely to break again, so the treating team arranges the assessment and the prevention that follow. The responder's part is to suspect the injury from the story and the posture, to keep the spine still, and to describe the mechanism in words rather than in a diagnosis.

The spine is treated as unstable until it has been cleared by someone qualified to clear it, and no lone manual handling technique is safe in a patient with a possible injury of the spine. The examination belongs to a clinician, or to a trained responder working within a protocol, and it includes the sensation of the trunk and the limbs, the strength of the legs, the ability to pass urine, and the feeling around the saddle area, where the nerves that serve the bladder and the bowel lie. Numbness there, weakness of both legs, or the inability to pass urine after a fall are findings that stop the responder and send the patient to a facility with imaging and a surgeon without delay. A rigid collar or a board is applied by those trained and authorized to apply it, and it is removed as soon as the clinician who clears the spine permits, because a hard board under a frail patient becomes a pressure injury within a short time. The patient is kept warm, the head is kept in line with the body, and the transfer is made by a team that has agreed on the plan before the lift begins.

The wrist is the commonest fragility fracture of all, and its danger is the nerve that runs through the canal on the palmar side of the joint. A fall onto the outstretched hand leaves the wrist swollen, tender and sometimes deformed, and the deformity may be obvious or almost absent. The skin over the wrist is thin and a small tear is easily missed under the swelling, so it is looked for deliberately. The examiner asks about numbness or tingling in the thumb and the first fingers, tests the sensation of the fingertips, feels the pulse at the wrist and watches the color of the hand, because that nerve can be compressed by bleeding and swelling rather than cut by bone. The fingers and the thumb are moved and the movement is recorded, because a tendon can be injured even when the bone is only slightly out of place. The trained responder supports the position found with a splint or a sling within the local protocol, the hand is left at the level of the heart rather than raised above the head, and the patient is referred for imaging and for the decision about an operation.

The upper end of the humerus is broken by a fall onto the shoulder or by a direct blow, and it is the fracture in which the examination most easily misleads. The patient has pain and swelling around the shoulder and often cannot lift the arm, yet a patient with a fracture may still raise it a little, and a patient whose tendon is torn may not raise it at all, so the loss of movement alone does not separate the two. The examiner looks for bruising that spreads down the arm, feels the bone gently, and tests the sensation over the outer part of the shoulder and the strength of the muscle that lifts the arm away from the body, because the nerve that supplies it runs close to the bone. The trained responder supports the arm in a sling within the local protocol, and a pillow under the elbow keeps the weight of the arm from pulling on the fracture. The decision about an operation belongs to the orthopedic surgeon, and the responder does not test the shoulder by moving it or by lifting the arm.

The pelvis of an older patient can break with the same small force, and the fracture may be described by the patient as pain in the hip, in the groin or in the lower back. A pelvic fracture from a fall in a frail patient is usually a break of the ring rather than the massive disruption seen after a high-energy crash, and it may be stable, but it can still bleed and it makes every movement painful. The responder does not press on the pelvis to see whether it moves, because that test causes pain and adds nothing the field can use. The patient is kept flat and still, the legs are supported, and the warmth and the pressure areas are attended to while help is arranged. A pelvic binder is applied by trained responders to the patient with suspected bleeding after a high-energy injury, and whether it helps in the low-energy fracture of a frail patient is a clinical decision for the treating team. Signs of shock are watched for continuously, because a pelvic fracture can bleed into the space behind the abdomen with no swelling that can be seen.

Immobilization means preventing movement of the injured part and of the joints above and below it, and in the older patient it is done with padding and with patience. The splint is applied over the position found, padded where it presses on bone, and secured firmly enough to hold without becoming a band that stops the circulation. Devices that are rigid and narrow are the ones most likely to injure thin skin, so the skin is inspected before the device is applied, the straps are kept away from bony prominences, and the device is checked for tightness each time the patient is moved. The patient is turned at the interval the local protocol sets, and the skin under every strap and edge is inspected, because a sore that was not recorded on arrival will be attributed to whoever touched the patient last. Casting, the application of plaster or a synthetic shell to hold a bone while it heals, is learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and it is never taught from a book, because a cast that is too tight around a swelling limb can cost the limb. The trained responder and the paramedic apply splints and cover the patient against cold within the procedures their service authorizes, while the physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides what definitive immobilization is required, and cold is treated as a complication of trauma rather than a detail of comfort.

Early mobilization and good control of pain are the two measures that most often decide whether the patient walks again. The complications of lying still gather quickly in a frail body, and they include pressure injuries, infection of the chest, clots in the veins of the legs, loss of muscle, constipation and confusion. Each of them is easier to prevent than to treat, and each of them begins in the hours before admission rather than in the days after it. Pain that is not controlled keeps the patient from breathing deeply, from coughing, from moving and from sleeping, so it feeds the very complications that immobility creates. The plan for getting the patient up, for taking weight through the limb and for returning home belongs to the treating team of physician, surgeon, nurse and physiotherapist, and it is decided together with the patient and the family. The part that belongs to the responder is to relieve what can be relieved within the scope of practice, to immobilize well, to move the patient without leaving them on a hard surface, and to hand over a patient who is warm and comfortable.

Imaging confirms what the examination has already suggested, and it is ordered by a clinician trained to interpret it. A radiograph is usually the first study, taken in at least two directions so that displacement in the third direction is not hidden, and it includes the joint above and the joint below so that a second injury is not missed. The hip is a special case, because a fracture there can be invisible on the first radiograph, and a patient who cannot bear weight with a negative image is kept for further study rather than sent home, a decision that belongs to the physician. Computed tomography shows the detail of a complex break around a joint, and magnetic resonance imaging shows bone and soft tissue together and can tell an old fracture of the spine from a new one. The clinician orders a study when its answer will change what is done, and the first aider never orders or interprets an image. A picture is a record of one moment, so the patient is examined and reassessed after it has been taken, and an ordinary image never cancels an abnormal examination.

Reassessment is performed after every intervention and compared with the record, because the comparison is what carries the value rather than any single measurement. The limb is checked for color, warmth, pulse, sensation, movement and pain, and the splint, the bandage and the cast are checked for tightness and for pressure. The whole patient is checked as well, because a frail patient changes for reasons that have nothing to do with the limb, and a new confusion, a faster rate of breathing, a falling blood pressure or a temperature that has appeared belong in the handover. The findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation are pain that increases instead of settling, pain out of proportion to the injury, numbness, tingling, weakness, a pulse that cannot be felt, and a limb that becomes tense and swollen. When a compartment syndrome is suspected, the constricting bandage, splint or cast is released within the limits of the local protocol by someone authorized to do it, the limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, the patient is kept warm, and the patient is referred urgently, because the diagnosis is clinical and time is muscle. Force is not a technique, and a responder who meets resistance stops and asks for help.

Referral or definitive treatment is the last step of the sequence, and in this population it often decides the rest of the patient's life. A patient with a suspected fracture of the hip, a fracture that has opened the skin, a suspected injury of vessels or nerves, a suspected compartment syndrome, a fracture that enters a joint, or a fracture of the spine is referred to a facility with imaging, an operating room and an intensive care unit by the clinician or by the responder within the local protocol. The handover names the age of the patient, the time of the fall, the ability to walk before it, the medicines, the neurovascular findings before and after each intervention, and the treatment already given. Definitive treatment of the hip may be an operation to hold the bone with metal or to replace the joint, and that choice belongs to the orthopedic surgeon together with the patient and the family, while the assessment of bone strength and the plan that may prevent the next fracture belong to the clinic that follows afterwards. Reduction, which restores the position of the bone ends, traction, which holds them in line with a gentle continuous pull, and casting, which holds the position while the bone heals, are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and the technique of each belongs to supervised clinical training rather than to a book. A reduction is not attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when the vessels or the nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, when the responder lacks the training or the equipment, or when an earlier attempt has failed.

More than one injury is common, and the second one is easily missed when attention has fixed on the obvious fracture. The head, the ribs, the hands, the feet and the other hip are examined, and the patient is asked about every region rather than only the painful one, because a frail patient may not volunteer a second source of pain. A pulse that looks ordinary does not exclude bleeding. The record and the handover are part of the treatment rather than a formality, and the time of the fall, the last time the patient was seen well, the position in which the limb was found, the state of the skin on arrival, the medicines, the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention, and any change in the condition are written and spoken in a fixed order. Everything described in this chapter is learned with a teacher, practiced on models and on one another, and certified by an authority that also defines what may be done without supervision. Where this chapter names a time, a distance or a figure, it names an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the protocol of the service in which you work, because such figures differ between services, countries and editions.

To summarize, a fragility fracture is a break caused by little force in a bone that has lost its strength, and in an older patient it may announce itself as confusion, as a refusal to stand, or as pain in the back that is called a strain. Look for the hip that cannot bear weight even when the patient can still take a step, for the wrist that has swollen after a fall onto the hand, for the shoulder that will not lift, for the pelvis that hurts in the groin, and for the spine that hurts on standing and eases on lying. Assess the whole patient before the limb, keep the patient warm, treat the spine as unstable until someone qualified has cleared it, examine the neurovascular status before and after every intervention and write down what you found, immobilize in the position found with padding over the skin, and reassess after everything you do. Never straighten a deformed limb in the field, never remove an embedded object, never push exposed bone back into a wound, never explore a wound blindly, never move a patient with a possible injury of the spine alone, and never give a medicine outside your authorization. Pain is relieved by the prescriber within the local protocol, and reduction, traction and casting belong to supervised clinical training rather than to a book. Call for help early and refer a suspected fracture of the hip, an open fracture, a failing limb or a suspected compartment syndrome to a facility with imaging, an operating room and an intensive care unit, and say who the patient was before the fall, because the ability to walk yesterday is part of the picture today.
