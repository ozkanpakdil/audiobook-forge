# Chapter 18 — Radial Head and Olecranon Fractures

The elbow holds two fractures that behave like opposites, and the difference between them is the difference between rotation and a pull. A radial head fracture is recognized when the patient cannot turn the palm up and down without pain, while an olecranon fracture is recognized by a gap at the back of the elbow that widens because the triceps pulls the broken piece away. Both injuries arrive at the same joint after a fall, and both are missed by a responder who looks only at the swelling. This chapter follows the order this course keeps everywhere, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. It names the provider level for every act, and it is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

The elbow is where three bones meet, and each of these two fractures belongs to a different one of them. The humerus ends in two rounded columns with a spool between them, and that spool is the hinge on which the forearm swings. The ulna runs to the wrist on the side of the little finger, and its upper end carries the olecranon, which is the point of the elbow and the lever on which the triceps pulls. The radius runs beside the ulna on the side of the thumb, and its upper end is a disc called the radial head, which turns against the rounded part of the humerus while the forearm rotates. The radial head is therefore a pivot and the olecranon is a lever, so the examination of one fracture tests rotation and the examination of the other tests straightening against resistance.

A radial head fracture usually follows a fall onto an outstretched hand with the elbow slightly bent, so that the force travels up the radius and drives the head against the humerus. The same fracture appears when the elbow is forced sideways, because the head is then crushed against the bone by the angulation of the joint. The energy may continue along the forearm and injure the wrist, which is why the wrist is examined in every patient whose elbow has been hurt. An illustration, offered as a constructed example and not as a description of any real patient, is a cyclist who lands on one hand and feels the pain on the outside of the elbow rather than in the wrist where the force entered. The mechanism is the first clue, and it is obtained before the limb is touched, because a patient in pain will guard the arm once the examination begins.

An olecranon fracture usually follows a direct blow to the point of the elbow, as when a person falls backward onto it, or a sudden violent contraction of the triceps that tears the bone apart where the tendon joins. Because the olecranon lies directly under the skin with almost no muscle over it, a blow there often breaks the skin as well as the bone, and an open injury is more common at this site than at most others in the body. The weight of the falling body, the hardness of the ground and the thinness of the covering are all part of the reason. A wound over the point of the elbow after a fall is therefore treated as an open fracture of the olecranon until a clinician has shown otherwise, and the wound is covered rather than inspected.

Neither fracture is obliged to arrive alone, and the examiner who finds one looks for the others. The elbow may have come apart at the moment of injury and gone back into place by itself, leaving a radial head fracture and a fracture of the coronoid process behind, and that combination is unstable even when the joint appears to be sitting in place. A radial head fracture may also travel with a torn membrane between the two forearm bones and an injury at the wrist, so that the whole forearm rather than the elbow alone has lost its length. When the diagnosis is in doubt, an elbow that has just been dislocated is treated as unstable and is referred. The responder does not test the joint to discover which of these is true.

Recognition of the radial head fracture begins with the patient's account and with a careful look from a step back. The elbow is held slightly bent, often with the forearm resting halfway between supination and pronation because any attempt to turn it further hurts. Swelling appears on the outside of the elbow, over the soft spot below the small bony prominence on the outer side of the humerus, and the point of greatest tenderness is found there with one finger rather than the whole hand. The examiner compares the two sides and may notice that the injured elbow has lost the last part of its straightening. The finding that gives this fracture its personality is a block to rotation, and the way to look for it is to ask rather than to push. When a piece of the head has been caught between the bone ends, rotation stops suddenly against a hard stop that will not clear with waiting, and deciding whether a stop is mechanical or simply produced by pain belongs to a clinician.

Recognition of the olecranon fracture is usually easier and sometimes too easy to trust. The back of the elbow is swollen, bruised and tender, and the examiner may feel a gap between the broken piece and the shaft of the ulna, with the piece riding higher than it should when the arm is bent. A scrape or a small wound over the point of the elbow is looked for in every case, and the patient is asked whether bone was visible at any moment. The ability to straighten the elbow is the sign most often taught for this fracture and the sign most often misleading. A fibrous expansion of the triceps may still transmit the pull across a widely separated break, and a painful elbow whose fracture has not moved at all may also refuse to straighten. The test is performed by asking and never by forcing the arm against the patient's resistance, because a sign that can be present in the wrong patient is a clue and not a test that decides.

Assessment follows the same order in this chapter as in every other, and the order exists because each step protects the next. The responder begins with the scene, checking for danger, noting the number of patients and the time of the injury, and considering the mechanism. The primary survey follows, remembered as ABCDE, which stands for airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure, and the limb is not examined in detail until the patient is stable enough to tolerate it. Life comes before limb, because a limb can wait for minutes that a brain and a heart cannot. The history then carries as much weight as the examination, so the age of the patient, the delay since the injury, and any illness or medicine that weakens bone or thins the blood are asked and recorded, because each answer raises the chance of a complication.

Examination of the limb is done by looking, by feeling and by asking the patient to move, and never by forcing movement. The responder looks at both arms from the front and from the side, comparing the position of the two elbows, the amount of swelling and the state of the skin from the shoulder to the fingers. Feeling is done gently, beginning away from the injured area and finishing with one finger over the point of greatest tenderness, so that the patient does not guard before the examination is complete. The joints above and below are checked, which here means the shoulder, the wrist and the small joints of the hand, because the force that broke the elbow may have injured them as well. Clothing is cut away rather than pulled over the broken limb, because a sleeve dragged over a fractured elbow moves the joint and may displace a fragment that was still in place.

Stabilization of an injured elbow in the field is a small set of acts, and the most important of them is restraint. The arm is supported in the position in which it was found, with a sling, a pillow, a folded garment or the hands of the first aider, and it is never straightened, because the vessels and nerves lie against the bone around the elbow and a movement that looks helpful can tear what stayed intact. Straightening a deformed elbow may also convert a closed fracture into an open one, or release the pressure that a collection of blood was exerting on a torn vessel. The patient is kept warm, because cold is a complication of trauma rather than a detail of comfort, and wet clothing around the arm is replaced with dry covering. Nothing is given by mouth to a patient who may need an operation.

Who may do what is stated plainly, because scope of practice is not a formality. The lay first aider supports the arm with the hands or with whatever is at hand, keeps the patient warm and still, and calls for help, and nothing more is required of that level or permitted to it without training. The trained first responder and the emergency medical technician or paramedic may apply a sling or a splint within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, and they record the neurovascular findings before and after doing so. The nurse and the physician in an emergency department may assess the limb, arrange imaging and give treatment within the protocol of the facility. The orthopedic surgeon decides what definitive treatment the fracture needs, and reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol rather than from any book.

The neurovascular examination is done before any intervention and repeated after it, and its result is written down in words. The examiner looks at the color and the temperature of the hand, presses briefly on a nail to watch the color return, and feels the pulse at the wrist on both the thumb side and the little finger side. Sensation is tested on both hands for comparison, and movement is tested by asking the patient to spread the fingers, bend the wrist and touch the thumb to each finger in turn. The pulse is never used alone to decide that the vessels are safe, because the elbow carries a rich network of smaller vessels that can keep the hand supplied even when one of the two main branches below the elbow has been torn. A pulse may therefore be present while a vessel is injured, and a hand that is pale, cool or slow to refill is a warning even when the pulse can be felt.

Three nerves pass around the elbow within a short distance of the bone, and each has a recognizable territory in the hand. The nerve that winds around the back of the humerus supplies the muscles that lift the wrist and straighten the fingers, so an injury to it shows as a wrist that droops and fingers that cannot be held out against gravity. The nerve that passes behind the bony ridge on the inner side of the elbow supplies most of the small muscles of the hand, so an injury to it shows as a hand that cannot spread the fingers or grip firmly, with numbness along the little finger. The nerve that passes through the front of the elbow supplies the muscles that turn the palm down and the deep muscles that bend the fingertips, so an injury to it may show only as an inability to pinch the tips of the thumb and the index finger together. Testing all three is quick, and it is repeated after every intervention.

Recording the findings is part of the treatment rather than a task that follows it. The time of the injury, the mechanism, the position in which the arm was found, the color, warmth, pulse, sensation and movement before the splint, the same observations after the splint, and any change in the patient are written or spoken in a fixed order. The receiving team then continues a story instead of starting a new one, and a change is recognized because there is something recorded to compare it with. The first question the surgeon will ask is not what the swelling looks like but what the arm could do before the splint was applied.

Immobilization means preventing movement of the injured part and of the joints above and below it, and at the elbow that means supporting the shoulder as well as the wrist. The arm is placed in a sling that carries the weight of the forearm, usually with the elbow bent at a right angle, and the sling is adjusted so that the hand sits higher than the elbow and the fingers remain visible. A collar and cuff, a broad arm sling or a padded splint may each be used, depending on what the local service authorizes. The exact angle at which the elbow is supported is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because services differ, and the position of comfort is often the safest choice in the field. A patient who is more comfortable with the arm hanging is allowed to keep it there while the sling is prepared around that position.

A point of mechanics helps the responder understand why the arm is not forced into a tight bend when the olecranon is broken. The triceps pulls the fragment toward the shoulder, and bending the elbow increases the gap, so an olecranon fracture held tightly flexed may separate further while it waits. A radial head fracture behaves differently, because the joint surfaces are pressed together by the same movement and its fragments are not under the pull of a strong tendon. A splint or sling around a swollen elbow can also become a band, so padding is placed where the device presses on bone and the device is secured firmly enough to hold the arm without compressing the swelling. Every device is checked again after a short interval, and after any movement of the patient or change of position, because an improvised support shifts as the patient is moved.

An open injury at the elbow changes the priority of everything that follows, and the olecranon is one of the places where an open injury is most often seen. A wound over the point of the elbow is covered with a sterile dressing, and bleeding that can be controlled is controlled by pressure around the wound rather than by probing inside it. An embedded object is never removed and exposed bone is never pushed back into the wound, because the object may be holding pressure on a torn vessel and the bone carries contamination deeper when it is moved. The wound is never explored blindly, and definitive cleaning, repair and closure belong to the operating room and to nobody else. Tetanus protection and antibiotics are decided by a clinician according to the local protocol, and the substance, the amount, the timing and the route are not described in this course.

Pain is treated as a finding to be recorded and as a symptom to be relieved within the limits of each provider's scope. The lay first aider offers reassurance, stillness and warmth and gives no medicine at all. The trained responder or paramedic may give analgesia only when the service protocol authorizes it and only after the neurovascular examination has been recorded, because a medicine that dulls the pain may also hide the change that matters most. The nurse and the physician decide what is appropriate, and the substance, the amount and the route belong to the local protocol and the prescriber rather than to this course. The finding that makes the decision urgent is pain out of proportion to the injury, or pain that appears when the fingers are gently straightened, because those findings suggest a compartment syndrome rather than ordinary fracture pain.

Imaging confirms what the examination has already suggested, and it is ordered by a clinician trained to interpret it. A radiograph of the elbow is taken in at least two directions, and the side view is taken with the elbow truly sideways, because a view that is slightly rotated hides the fracture line and misleads the reader. Views taken with the forearm turned into different positions are often needed to show the radial head, and a fracture there may appear only as a faint line. Blood inside the joint pushes the pad of fat at the front of the elbow away from the bone, and that shadow on the side view means that something inside the joint is injured without saying what it is. Computed tomography shows the shape of the pieces when the fracture is comminuted, magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for the soft tissues including the ligaments and the cartilage, and both are ordered by the clinician responsible for the patient. A radiograph that looks normal in a patient with tenderness exactly over the radial head does not exclude a fracture.

Children deserve a separate word, because the ends of their bones are not yet bone. The upper end of the radius is largely cartilage in a young child, and cartilage does not show on a radiograph, so a fracture through it may be invisible even when the child will not use the arm. The age at which the bony center of the radial head appears is given in teaching as about 4 years, an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the range among healthy children is wide. The examiner therefore relies on tenderness, swelling, the refusal to use the arm and the mechanism, and refers a child whose elbow is tender over the radial head even when the radiograph is reported as normal.

Reassessment is performed after every intervention, and it is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with what was recorded before, because the comparison is what carries the information rather than any single measurement. The examiner checks the color, warmth, pulse, sensation and movement of the hand, asks about the pain, and looks at the fit of the sling or splint for tightness or a crease that presses on the skin. Pain that increases instead of settling, numbness, tingling, weakness, and a forearm that becomes tense and swollen are the findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation. The front of the forearm is the compartment most often affected after an elbow injury, and the smallest vessels there fail while a pulse at the wrist can still be felt, so the diagnosis is clinical, time is muscle, and the responder releases whatever constricts the limb within local protocol, keeps the limb at the level of the heart rather than above it, and refers urgently.

Referral or definitive treatment is the last step of the sequence, and for these two fractures the decision turns on what the joint can still do. A crack of the radial head that does not block rotation and has not moved is usually treated without an operation, with a short period of support followed by gentle movement, because the elbow stiffens faster than almost any other joint. A radial head fracture that blocks rotation, or that has lost its shape, or that travels with instability of the whole forearm, is treated by an orthopedic surgeon, and the choice among removing a fragment, holding it with metal or replacing the head belongs to that surgeon. An olecranon fracture that has separated is pulled apart by the triceps, so it is treated by an operation that holds the pieces while the bone heals, and one of these two fractures punishes stiffness while the other punishes delay. All of those acts are learned under supervision, and none of them is taught from a book.

Reduction is the act of restoring the position of the bone ends, traction is a gentle and continuous pull that holds them in line, and casting or surgical fixation holds the position while the bone heals. Each of them is described here so that the listener understands what is being done and why, and none of them is taught here as a technique, because the feel of a joint that is about to yield and the moment to stop are learned with a teacher, under supervision, and certified by an authority. A reduction must not be attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when the vessels or the nerves need a specialist, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, when the responder lacks training or equipment, or when an earlier attempt has already failed. Each of those situations can turn an injury that would have healed into one that cannot. For these two fractures the elbow is not manipulated, not straightened, not pulled and not rotated to see whether it moves better afterwards, and force is not a technique.

One pattern deserves special attention, because it is easy to treat as an elbow problem when it is really a problem of the whole forearm. When the force of a fall travels up the radius, it can tear the membrane that connects the two forearm bones and injure the joint at the wrist, so that the radius slides toward the hand and the forearm loses its length. The finding to record is pain at the wrist and tenderness along the space between the two bones, and the decision about how to manage it belongs to the orthopedic surgeon. Older adults deserve the same attention for a different reason, since bone that has lost density breaks with little energy and the fracture may be far less painful than its severity suggests. An older adult who has fallen and now has pain on the outside of the elbow is treated as having a fracture until a clinician decides otherwise.

The choice of where to send the patient should be made early rather than at the moment of departure. A facility that can take radiographs, examine a limb and call upon an orthopedic surgeon is needed when the elbow is deformed, when a wound over the point of the elbow is present, when the pulse or sensation has changed, when the forearm is tense, or when the patient is a child with tenderness over the radial head. When the distance is long, the responder says plainly what has been found and what is feared, so that the receiving team can prepare. The care is shared, and each level reports what it has done and what it cannot do, using the same order every time. Everything in this chapter is learned with a teacher and certified by an authority that also defines what may be done without supervision.

A constructed illustration, offered as a teaching example and not as a description of any real patient, may help to fix the sequence in mind. A woman of middle age trips on a step and falls onto her outstretched hand, and afterward holds her right arm bent with the palm facing neither up nor down. Her wrist is not tender, and the only tender spot she can identify is on the outside of the elbow over the radial head. The first aider supports the arm as it lies and keeps her warm, while the responder records that her fingers are warm and pink and that turning her palm upward reproduces the pain. A splint is applied in the position found, the hand is left visible for later checks, and the findings are recorded again after the splint. At the hospital a clinician orders radiographs and decides the definitive treatment.

To summarize, the elbow holds two fractures that behave like opposites, and the radial head fracture is recognized by tenderness on the outside of the elbow with pain or a hard block when the palm is turned, while the olecranon fracture is recognized by swelling over the point of the elbow, a gap that widens because the triceps pulls the fragment, and often a wound over the bone. The ability to straighten the elbow is unreliable in both directions, and a normal radiograph in a patient with tenderness exactly over the radial head does not exclude a fracture, so the arm is splinted in the position found and never straightened, because the vessels and nerves lie against the bone. The neurovascular status is examined and recorded before any splint and repeated after every intervention, the fingers are left visible so that the hand can be watched, and the limb is kept warm. Pain out of proportion to the injury, a tense forearm, a changing pulse or new numbness stop the responder and prompt urgent referral, because a compartment syndrome is diagnosed clinically and time is muscle. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, an open wound is covered and never explored, an embedded object is never removed, and exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound. Reduction is not attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when the vessels or nerves need a specialist, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, when training or equipment is lacking, or when an earlier attempt has failed.
