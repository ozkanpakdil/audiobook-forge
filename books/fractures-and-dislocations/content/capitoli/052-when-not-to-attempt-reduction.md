# Chapter 52 — When Not to Attempt Reduction

Reduction is the act of restoring a dislocated joint or a displaced fracture to its proper position, and it is one of the few acts in musculoskeletal care that can heal and harm in the same movement. There are situations in which the correct decision is to leave the deformity alone, to support it as it was found, and to hand the patient to someone who has everything the attempt requires. This course follows one order in every chapter, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and reduction belongs at the end of it. A rule without a reason is forgotten and then broken, so every situation in this chapter is named with the reason it exists. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

A reduction forced through fragments that cannot be guided will drive those fragments through the vessels and the nerves that lie against the bone. One attempted on an open injury pushes organisms from the skin into the deep tissues, and one tried again after an earlier failure strips the periosteum, grinds the articular cartilage and leaves the joint more firmly locked than it was before. Each of these harms comes from the treatment rather than from the fall, and each of them is the kind of complication this course is most afraid of. Force is not a technique, so a deformity left alone in the field is not a failure of care but a decision that protects the patient until the right hands and the right equipment are available.

The contraindications this chapter takes one by one are a suspected complex fracture-dislocation, entrapped fracture fragments, significant neurovascular compromise that needs a specialist, an open injury, an unclear diagnosis, inadequate training or equipment, and a previous failed attempt. They overlap, because a limb with an open wound and an absent pulse carries several of them at once. What they share is one consequence, which is that in each of them the attempt is more likely to add an injury than to remove one. The task of the responder is to recognize that one of these conditions is present, to say which one, and to act on the alternative pathway rather than on the reduction, while the judgment that a reduction is contraindicated remains a clinical decision. The lay first aider needs only the shape of the rule, which is that a deformed limb is left as it is found and help is called.

Assessment decides whether the contraindication exists at all, and it begins before the limb is touched. The responder establishes the mechanism and the time of the injury, whether the patient is awake and breathing normally, and whether there is more than one injury. The limb is examined by looking at it, by feeling it gently and by asking the patient what can be moved, never by forcing it through a range of motion. A deformed limb that is not moved can still be examined, by inspecting the skin for a wound, comparing the shape with the other side and writing down the position in which the limb lies. Where the patient cannot describe the injury because of a head injury, intoxication, shock or the pain of another wound, the history is incomplete and the diagnosis is uncertain, which is itself one of the contraindications. Life comes before limb, so the primary survey, whose steps are remembered as ABCDE, meaning airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure, is completed first.

Stabilization is what happens while the assessment is completed and the patient waits, and it is a small set of acts performed with the hands and with whatever is at hand. The limb is supported as found, the patient is kept warm, the bleeding that can be controlled is controlled, and the patient is protected from further injury. The lay first aider does those things and nothing more, because they need no equipment and no authorization, while the trained first responder and the emergency medical technician work within the procedures their service authorizes, apply dressings, use personal protective equipment where it is available and move the patient no more than is necessary. Nothing in stabilization is a reduction, and no step of stabilization requires the deformity to be corrected.

The neurovascular examination is performed before any intervention and repeated after every one, and its result is recorded, because a finding not written down before a splint was applied cannot be attributed afterwards. The examiner looks at the color and the temperature of the hand or the foot, feels for the pulse beyond the injury, and asks the patient to move the fingers and the toes. Sensation is tested on both sides and compared, and the words the patient uses are recorded as closely as possible. Numbness, tingling, weakness and the loss of a movement the patient could perform a moment earlier suggest injury to a nerve, while pallor, coolness, a diminished pulse and pain out of proportion to the injury suggest injury to a vessel. A pulse that is present does not exclude serious injury to the vessels or to a compartment, and a pulse that is absent is an emergency. This examination is the most important reason not to attempt a reduction in the field, because a limb that has lost its nerve or its supply cannot afford what a forced maneuver costs.

The first contraindication is a suspected complex fracture-dislocation, which means a joint that has come out of place at the same time as the bone around it has broken into several pieces. The fracture may run into the joint surface, the rim of the joint may be broken off with the capsule still attached, or the shards may sit where no hand can feel them through the skin. In such an injury the fragments cannot be guided, and a pull that would put a simple dislocation back will instead drive a sharp fragment into the vessels behind the joint and the nerves around it. The reduction is performed by an orthopedic surgeon in an operating room, with imaging during the procedure and with the means to fix the bone once the joint is restored. The responder keeps the limb as found, records the neurovascular status, immobilizes and refers urgently, and the word suspected carries the rule, because the field diagnosis does not have to be certain.

The second contraindication is an entrapped fracture fragment, which means that something has been caught between the two surfaces of the joint and is holding them apart. The picture is a joint that looks as though it should go back into place and simply does not, and the examiner may feel a soft or a hard block that stops the movement before it is complete. Traction can tighten that trap rather than open it, because the interposed tissue is drawn more firmly across the joint as the surfaces are separated, and a sustained pull can strangulate the tissue that blocks the way. The longer the block is worked on, the more the tissue swells, and a joint that might have been closed with a small procedure becomes one that needs an open operation. The finding that a joint will not reduce is a report to be made rather than an obstacle to be overcome, so every level of responder stops, immobilizes in the position found and refers without delay.

The third contraindication is significant neurovascular compromise that needs a specialist, and it is the one in which the temptation to act is strongest and the harm from acting is greatest. The findings are an absent or barely palpable pulse beyond the injury, a hand or a foot that is pale or blue, skin cool to the touch, a limb that has become tense, pain out of proportion to the visible injury, and a numbness or a weakness that is progressing rather than fading. A brief tingling that settles when the limb is supported may be positional and is watched, but a deficit that is growing is a surgical emergency. A vessel may be torn, crushed, in spasm or compressed by a fragment, and the treatment is a vascular and orthopedic assessment with imaging, an operating room and an intensive care unit, none of which a pull in the field can replace. The responder records what was found and when, keeps the limb as found, keeps the patient warm and refers as a priority, naming in the referral the level of care that is needed.

The fourth contraindication is an open injury, and the rule is broader than the wound suggests, because any wound in the same region as a broken bone makes that fracture an open fracture, however small the wound. The organisms that live on the skin enter the deep tissues in the moment of the injury, so those tissues are contaminated whether or not bone can be seen. A reduction on such a limb pushes that contamination deeper, tears the damaged soft tissue envelope and can enlarge the communication between the outside and the bone. Exposed bone is never pushed back into the wound, because it may be the one thing holding pressure on a torn vessel, and an embedded object is never removed for the same reason. A wound is never explored blindly, because a finger in a wound finds tissue it cannot identify and displaces what was tamponading the bleeding. The responder covers the wound with a sterile dressing, controls the bleeding that can be controlled, records the neurovascular status, immobilizes the limb as found, keeps the patient warm and refers urgently, because definitive cleaning and closure belong to the operating room and to nobody else.

The fifth contraindication is an unclear diagnosis, and it is the quietest of the seven because it is the state of the responder's own knowledge rather than a finding in the limb. The diagnosis is unclear when the mechanism does not fit the deformity, when the patient cannot give a history, when the limb cannot be examined because of pain or because the patient is not awake, or when the injury could be a fracture, a dislocation, an injury of the growth region of a child or a combination of them. A joint that is out of place is not the same injury as a broken bone, and either can produce the same outward shape, so an attempt made on a guess may complete a fracture that has not been recognized. In a child an injury near the end of a bone may involve the growing region, which can be damaged with no fracture visible at all and with consequences that last for years. Until the diagnosis is clear the limb is splinted as found and the patient is referred, because waiting costs hours while a wrong reduction costs a lifetime.

The sixth contraindication is inadequate training or equipment, and this one is about the person and the place rather than about the injury. Reduction is a procedure learned under supervision, practiced on models and in the operating room and performed according to local protocol, and it is not a technique that can be learned from a book or from a recording. The lay first aider supports the limb and calls for help, the trained first responder and the emergency medical technician immobilize and transport, the paramedic works within the procedures the service authorizes, the nurse and the physician act within their licenses and their protocols, and the orthopedic surgeon performs the reduction where imaging, anesthesia, assistance and an operating room are available. Equipment matters as much as training, because a reduction performed without monitoring, without a means to control pain, without imaging and without a plan for failure is an experiment on a patient. When in doubt, the answer is to keep the limb as found, to record, to immobilize, to keep the patient warm and to refer.

The seventh contraindication is a previous failed attempt, and it includes the attempt just made as well as any attempt made before the responder arrived. Every pass at a joint or a fracture costs tissue, because the periosteum is stripped from the bone, the articular cartilage is scuffed and ground, the capsule is stretched further, and bleeding and swelling increase inside a space that has no room for them. The tissues lose their elasticity as they are worked, so the second attempt meets more resistance than the first and needs more force, which is exactly the direction in which harm lies. A joint or a fracture that has been tried and has not moved has already answered the question, and the answer is that something about this injury is beyond what the field can do. Repeating the attempt also adds pain, and pain brings muscle spasm that makes the limb even harder to move. The rule is to stop, to immobilize, to document the attempt and who made it, and to refer, and that documentation is not an accusation but clinical information the receiving team needs.

Running through all seven situations is one instruction a responder can carry in the hands, which is to stop if there is resistance or a complication. Resistance may be felt as a hard block that does not give, as a limb that will not follow the direction in which it is guided, or as a muscle that grips and holds. The correct response is not a firmer pull but an immediate stop, a return of the limb to the position in which it was found, a fresh neurovascular examination and a report of what changed. Restraint after resistance is the whole of the technique this chapter teaches, and no target seen on an image justifies the loss of a nerve or a vessel.

One situation deserves to be described because it is often mistaken for an exception, and that is the limb that is obviously dislocated and has no pulse. A knee that has been dislocated may also have reduced itself before the responder arrived, so that it looks almost normal while the vessel behind the knee has been torn, and this is one of the few injuries in which a normal appearance is a warning rather than a reassurance. Some services authorize a gentle positioning of the limb under a specific protocol and by a named level of provider, and where that authorization exists it is followed exactly as written, with the neurovascular status recorded before and after. This course does not teach that maneuver and does not describe how it is performed, because it belongs to supervised clinical training in the service that authorizes it. Where the authorization does not exist the limb is splinted in the position found and the transfer is made as urgently as the service allows.

What, then, does a responder do when reduction is contraindicated? The answer is the rest of the sequence, performed in order and performed well. The limb is supported and splinted in the position in which it was found, with the joints above and the joints below included, and the splint is padded where it presses on bone. The neurovascular status is examined and written down before the splint is applied and again after it is in place, and any change is acted on. Bleeding that can be controlled is controlled with pressure and dressings, an embedded object is stabilized in place, and an open wound is covered rather than explored. The patient is kept warm, is given nothing by mouth without an instruction that belongs to the local protocol, and is moved no more than is necessary, and the receiving facility is told what is coming, what was found and what was done.

Pain is a finding and a harm in itself, and in this setting it must be addressed without any of the details belonging to this book. The decision to give a medicine, the choice of the substance, the amount, the way it reaches the patient and how often it is repeated belong to a prescriber working within the local protocol, and they are never taken from a recording. What the responder can do is recognize the findings that make the decision urgent, which are pain out of proportion to the injury, pain that increases instead of settling, a limb that becomes tense and swollen, and a patient who cannot be made comfortable. Those findings are reported at once, because a compartment under pressure produces pain of that kind rather than ordinary pain, and the treatment for it is not a stronger medicine but a surgical assessment. The lay first aider offers comfort, position, warmth and reassurance and gives no unauthorized substance, while the trained responder may assist with an ordered medicine within the scope and the protocol of the service.

Immobilization after a refusal to reduce is not a compromise and it is not a delay, because it is the act that protects the vessels and the nerves that lie against the bone. A deformed limb is splinted in the position in which it was found and is never straightened in the field, because the tissues a movement would tear are the same tissues the reduction was declined to protect. The splint holds the joints above and below the injury, is padded where it presses over a bony point, and is secured firmly enough to hold the limb without stopping the circulation. Improvised materials serve well, and a rolled magazine or a padded board can do the work, but an improvised device shifts, so it is checked more often than a manufactured one. A splint is not a place to correct angulation, and a splint tightened to straighten a limb has become a tourniquet. The trained responder and the paramedic apply splints within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, while the orthopedic surgeon decides what definitive immobilization is required, and casting and surgical fixation are learned under supervision rather than from a book.

Cold is a complication of trauma and not a detail of comfort, and a patient whose reduction has been declined often waits longer than one who has been treated. Wet clothing is replaced with dry covering, the patient is insulated from the ground, and the head and the limbs are covered, because heat is lost fastest where the surface is largest and the covering is thinnest. A cold limb is also harder to examine, since cool skin, a faint pulse and a sluggish return of color can all be produced by cold alone. Where a number such as a target temperature is named in the guidance of a service, it is an illustration confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

A word belongs here about the spine, because a spine that has been injured is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has done so, and no chapter in this course may describe a lone manual handling technique as safe. A dislocated vertebra, a fracture through the body of a vertebra or a fracture through the bony arch can each press on the spinal cord or on the nerves that leave it, and a movement made in the field can turn a patient who could walk into a patient who cannot. Reduction of an injury of the spine is performed in a hospital, with imaging, with monitoring and with the means to act if the neurological picture changes. The responder keeps the head and the body in line, uses the precautions and the equipment the local protocol requires, moves the patient only with a trained team, and keeps the patient warm and still until the transfer is made.

Imaging converts a suspicion into a diagnosis, and it is ordered and interpreted by a clinician, never by the responder. A radiograph is usually the first study, taken in at least two directions so that a displacement is not hidden, and it includes the joint above and the joint below so that a second injury is not missed. Computed tomography shows the detail of a complex break, particularly where the fracture runs into a joint surface, and magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for the soft tissues, including ligaments, cartilage, tendons and muscle. An image is a picture of one moment, so the patient is examined again after it has been taken, and a limb that deteriorates while waiting for a study is reported at once. Where reduction is contraindicated the image is still needed, because it tells the surgeon what the injury is and how it must be approached.

Reassessment follows every intervention and every wait, and it is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the record, because the comparison is what has value rather than any single measurement, and they are color, warmth, pulse, sensation, movement, pain and the tension of the limb. Pain that increases instead of settling, pain out of proportion to the injury, a limb that becomes tense and swollen, numbness and tingling, weakness, pallor, coolness and a pulse that fades are the findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation. A compartment syndrome is suspected on those findings alone, and no pulse is required to be absent before the suspicion is raised, because the flow through the smallest vessels can fail while a large pulse is still felt. Where one is suspected, the constricting bandage, splint or cast is released within the limits of the local protocol by someone authorized to do it, the limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, and the patient is referred urgently, because the diagnosis is clinical and time is muscle. A responder not authorized to release a splint asks someone who is, without waiting for a confirmation that would arrive too late.

Referral is the final step of the sequence, and when reduction is contraindicated it becomes the most important one. A patient with a suspected complex fracture-dislocation, an entrapped fragment, a failing pulse or a progressive nerve deficit, an open injury, an unclear diagnosis, an attempt that has already failed, or a limb the responder is not trained or equipped to touch is referred to a facility with imaging, an operating room and an intensive care unit, and where possible to one with the specialists that injury needs. The referral is a story told in a fixed order, which is the time of the injury, the mechanism, the position in which the limb was found, the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention, what was done and by whom, and what has changed since. The receiving team continues that story instead of starting a new one, and the difference is measured in minutes. The level of care is named in the referral, so that a patient who needs a vascular specialist is not received by a team expecting a simple splint.

A responder who writes down the neurovascular status before a splint, the position in which the limb was found, the attempt that was declined and the reason for the decision is protecting the patient from a later mistake and protecting the record from a gap. Everything in this chapter is learned with a teacher and certified by an authority that also defines what may be done without supervision, and the recording of every finding is part of the clinical act rather than a formality. Where this chapter names a time, a distance or a threshold, it names an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between services, countries and editions. The course repeats its order in every chapter, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

To summarize, reduction can restore a joint or a fracture and can also destroy the tissues that surround it, so there are situations in which the only defensible act is to leave the deformity alone. Those situations are a suspected complex fracture-dislocation, an entrapped fragment, significant neurovascular compromise that needs a specialist, an open injury, an unclear diagnosis, inadequate training or equipment, and a previous failed attempt, and in each of them the attempt is more likely to add an injury than to remove one. What the responder does instead is to support and splint the limb in the position in which it was found, never to straighten a deformed limb in the field, never to remove an embedded object, never to push exposed bone back into a wound, and never to explore a wound blindly. The neurovascular status is examined and recorded before every intervention and repeated after every one, the patient is kept warm, the spine is treated as unstable until it is cleared by someone qualified to clear it, and a compartment syndrome is suspected clinically, released within the local protocol and referred urgently. Pain is treated within the scope and the protocol of the service and by a prescriber, and reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol. Stop if there is resistance or a complication, refer the patient to a facility with imaging, an operating room and an intensive care unit, and tell the receiving team the story in its fixed order.
