# Chapter 65 — The Clinical Decision-Making Framework

Every injury in this course is met with the same questions in the same order, and this chapter gathers those questions into one framework that a responder can run in the head at the roadside, in a corridor, or at the foot of a bed. The framework does not replace the sequence that every chapter of this course follows, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively; it is that sequence spoken as questions a person can remember when nothing can be looked up. The first few questions are usually answered in seconds, while the rest may take minutes, and several of them are answered again after every act of care. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

The reason the questions are fixed rather than left to the moment is that a frightened memory does not sort priorities by itself. Under pressure the eye goes to the obvious deformity, to the blood on the jacket and to the limb that is bent the wrong way, while the quiet problem that will kill first may be a chest that does not move or a pulse that is fading. If the order is fixed, the responder does not have to decide what to think about first, and the decision that matters most, which is that life comes before limb, is made before the patient is even reached. A fixed shape also makes the assessment repeatable, so that the second person to arrive continues the same examination instead of beginning a different one.

The framework asks thirteen questions, and it asks them in a fixed order: what happened, what is injured, whether the patient is stable, whether there is a life-threatening problem, whether there is a limb-threatening problem, whether there is a neurological injury, whether the fracture is open, whether the injury is unstable, whether it requires reduction, how it should be immobilized, what imaging is required, where the patient should go, and what needs to be reassessed. The first two belong to assessment, the next two decide how quickly everything else must happen, and the questions that follow move through the examination of the limb, its immobilization, its imaging and its destination. In practice the framework is not walked from the first question to the thirteenth and then put away, because an unstable patient sends the responder back to the beginning, and an intervention sends the responder forward only as far as the next reassessment. A useful way to hold it is as a loop with a gate, where the gate is the stability of the patient and everything about the limb waits behind it.

Question one is what happened, and it is answered from the scene before it is answered from the patient. The responder notes the position of the patient, the height of a fall, the nature of a collision, the object that struck or the surface that was struck, the time of the injury, and whether the patient has been moved since. The mechanism of injury is the best early guide to which tissues took the energy, and it predicts which injuries may be present even when the patient cannot tell the story. The environment belongs to this question as well, because traffic, machinery, fire, water, electricity and the risk of a further collapse decide whether anyone may approach at all. The lay first aider stays out of an unsafe scene and calls for help, while the trained responder enters only with the authorization of the service, and personal protective equipment is put on before contact whenever blood or other body fluids may be present.

The mechanism also tells the responder how much energy was involved, and energy is the part of the injury that the skin conceals. A fall from a low height and a fall from a roof can produce the same swollen ankle and a very different set of hidden injuries, so the height, the landing surface and the part of the body that landed first are all recorded. As a constructed illustration, a patient who strikes a knee against a dashboard may have a break of the thigh bone and a dislocation of the hip on the same side, and the hip may be noticed only when the whole body is examined. Any threshold for a dangerous height or a dangerous speed is also an illustration, and the current guideline and the local protocol define the figure your service uses, because services and countries set them differently. The age of the patient and the illnesses and medicines that have weakened the bone belong here too, since a fall that leaves one person unhurt can break the hip of another.

Question two is what is injured, and it is answered by the whole-body examination that follows the primary survey rather than by the part the patient complains about. The head, the neck, the chest, the abdomen, the back, the pelvis and each limb are examined in turn, and each limb is compared with its fellow on the other side. The limb is examined where it lies, by looking, by feeling and by asking the patient to move it if the patient is able, and never by forcing a movement the patient cannot make. Swelling, deformity, bruising, an open wound and tenderness over a bone point toward a fracture, while a joint that is empty, locked or sitting in the wrong direction points toward a dislocation.

No single finding proves that a bone is broken and no single finding excludes it, because a fracture may be present in a limb that looks straight, that moves without grating and that is not badly swollen, particularly in a child and in an older adult whose bone has lost density. Grating, which is the sensation when two broken ends move against each other, is reliable when it is present and dangerous to seek, because the movement that produces it can injure the vessels and the nerves that lie against the bone. A patient who is distracted by a more painful injury, intoxicated, confused or unable to speak may not report a second injury at all, so the examination is completed whatever the patient says. The responder records what was seen and felt instead of what it is called, because a description can be judged later and a diagnosis made in the field cannot.

Question three is whether the patient is stable, and it is the gate through which every other question must pass. Stability is judged by the primary survey, whose steps are remembered as ABCDE, meaning airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure, and it is judged again whenever the patient changes. An airway that is not open, a breathing that is not adequate and a circulation that is failing are treated before the limb is examined, because a limb can wait for minutes that a brain and a heart cannot. The trained responder and the paramedic perform the primary survey and the interventions their service authorizes for it, while the nurse repeats the observations that show whether the patient is holding and the physician directs the resuscitation. When two correct actions compete for the same pair of hands, the principle that decides between them is that life comes before limb. A falling level of consciousness, a changed pattern of breathing, a weak and rapid pulse and skin that has become cold and pale send the responder back to the beginning without apology.

Question four is whether there is a life-threatening problem, and at the roadside it is most often bleeding, both the bleeding that can be seen and the bleeding that cannot. Blood pouring from a wound is controlled by direct pressure, and the trained responder may use a tourniquet or another device that the local protocol authorizes, applied by someone trained and certified to apply it, while the lay first aider presses on the wound and calls for help. Blood collecting inside the chest, the abdomen, the pelvis or the thigh cannot be seen, and it is suspected from the mechanism, from a rising pulse and from skin that is cold and clammy. A pelvic binder is applied by a trained responder or a clinician within the local protocol when the mechanism and the examination suggest an unstable pelvis, and it is part of stabilization rather than of diagnosis. An operating room and a blood bank, and not the roadside, are what finally treat these problems, so the task of the responder is to recognize them and to shorten the time until the patient reaches them. No drug, no dose and no route is given from this book, because prescribing belongs to a prescriber who works within the local protocol.

Stabilization is the second step of the course sequence, and in the field it is a small set of acts performed in the right order. The patient is kept warm, wet clothing is replaced with covering, the body is insulated from the ground and the head is covered, because cold is a complication of trauma and not a detail of comfort. Bleeding that can be controlled is controlled, the injured limb is supported in the position in which it was found, and nothing is straightened. A patient with a suspected injury of the spine is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has done so, and the patient is moved only by a trained team using the precautions the local protocol requires, because no single manual handling technique is safe on its own. The lay first aider supports, warms and waits, the trained responder, the emergency medical technician and the paramedic act within their equipment and their procedures, and the nurse and the physician continue the same work with the resources of the facility. Stabilization is never allowed to delay the transport of a patient who cannot be made stable where the patient lies.

Question five is whether there is a limb-threatening problem, and it is answered by the neurovascular examination together with the look of the limb. A limb whose pulse has weakened or disappeared, that is pale and cool beyond the injury, that is painful out of proportion to what is seen, or that has lost sensation or movement is a limb at risk, and each of those findings is recorded before anything is done and repeated afterward. A compartment that is tense, swollen and painful when it is stretched is an emergency even when a pulse is still present, because an intact pulse does not exclude a compartment syndrome and the diagnosis is clinical. An open fracture, a limb that has been crushed or partly amputated, and a joint that remains dislocated are the other limb-threatening problems a responder must recognize. A limb-threatening finding means urgent transfer to a facility with an operating room and a surgeon, and it means that nothing is done on the way that could add to the injury. Diagnosis and treatment belong to the clinician and to the orthopedic surgeon, while recognition, recording, immobilization and movement belong to the responder.

Question six is whether there is a neurological injury, and it is asked about the limb and about the spine at the same time. In the limb, the question is whether the patient can feel a light touch and can move the part that the nerve beyond the injury supplies, and the answer is compared with the other side and written down. In the spine, the question is whether there is weakness, numbness or a loss of bladder or bowel control, because those findings change the urgency of the transfer. A patient who is unconscious, confused, intoxicated or sedated cannot give a reliable answer, and the absence of a complaint is not the absence of an injury. A neurological finding that appears or worsens after a splint, a move or a journey is reported at once, because it may mean that the treatment caused it.

The neurovascular examination is the step this course turns on, and it is performed before any intervention and repeated after every one. The examiner looks at the color and the temperature of the skin, feels the pulse beyond the injury, presses briefly on a nail to watch the color return, and asks the patient to move the fingers or the toes. Sensation is tested on both sides and compared, and the words the patient uses are recorded as far as possible rather than translated into a term the patient did not say. The result is written before the splint is applied and after it is applied, and it is written where the next person will find it, because a finding that was not recorded before an intervention cannot be attributed afterward. The lay first aider observes color, warmth and the ability of the patient to move and to feel, the trained responder and the paramedic perform the formal examination, and the nurse and the physician repeat it and interpret it.

Question seven is whether the fracture is open, and the answer changes the destination, the urgency and the precautions of the whole plan. A fracture is open when the bone or the deep tissues have communicated with the outside, whether the wound is large or a puncture the size of a fingertip, and an open fracture is contaminated even when it looks clean. The responder covers the wound with a sterile dressing, controls the bleeding that can be controlled, records the neurovascular status, immobilizes the limb in the position found, keeps the patient warm, and arranges urgent transfer. An embedded object is never removed, because it may be holding pressure on a torn vessel, and exposed bone is never pushed back into the wound, because the movement can carry contamination inward and tear what remains intact. The wound is never explored blindly with a finger or with an instrument, and definitive cleaning and closure belong to the operating room and to nobody else. Antibiotics, tetanus protection and pain relief are decided by a prescriber according to the local protocol, and this book gives no substance, no amount and no route, because those belong to the prescriber and to the guideline current where you work.

Question eight is whether the injury is unstable, and the word unstable describes behavior rather than appearance. A fracture is unstable when the broken ends will not hold their position without support, as they often will not when the bone is broken into several pieces. A joint is unstable when its surfaces no longer stay in contact, which is what a dislocation is, and the same joint may remain unstable after it has been put back if a ligament or the rim of the joint has been torn. Instability matters to the responder because an unstable injury can move during handling, during a transfer and during a journey over a bad road, and each movement can injure a vessel or a nerve that lies against the bone. The response of the responder is not to test the instability but to immobilize the limb in the position found, to handle the patient gently, and to say in the handover what was seen. Judging how unstable an injury is and what will hold it belongs to the clinician and to the orthopedic surgeon.

Question nine is whether the injury requires reduction, and this is the question in which the framework most needs its discipline. A reduction is the act of restoring broken ends or dislocated surfaces to their proper position, and it is done to relieve pressure on vessels and nerves, to restore the length and the alignment of the limb, and to make the injury holdable while it heals. Whether it is needed, when it is done, who does it and where it is done are decisions for a clinician, most often an orthopedic surgeon in a facility with imaging and an operating room. A deformed limb is never straightened in the field, because the vessels and the nerves lie against the bone, and a movement that looks helpful can tear what remained intact or turn a closed fracture into an open one. The technique of reduction is learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and it is not taught from a book, because the hands that perform it must have felt resistance and known when to stop. Traction, which holds broken ends in line with a gentle continuous pull, and casting, which holds the position while the bone heals, are learned in the same way and belong to the same supervised training.

A reduction is not attempted when the source lists a reason to leave the injury alone, and those reasons are worth naming because a rule is followed only when the reason behind it is understood. The attempt is not made when the injury is a suspected complex fracture and dislocation, when a fragment of bone is caught within the joint, when the vessels or the nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, when the responder lacks the training or the equipment, or when an earlier attempt has already failed. In each of those situations the attempt can convert an injury that might have healed into one that cannot, and force is not a technique. Resistance met during any maneuver is a signal to stop, to support the limb where it now lies, to record what has changed and to escalate. For the lay first aider, the trained responder and the paramedic, the answer to this question in the field is almost always the same, which is to immobilize the limb as found and to leave reduction to the people and the place equipped for it.

Question ten is how the injury should be immobilized, and the principle is that movement is prevented at the fracture and at the joints above and below it. The limb is splinted in the position in which it was found, the splint is padded where it presses over bone, and it is secured firmly enough to hold without becoming a band that stops the circulation. The material matters less than the principle, because a folded magazine, a padded board, a pillow and the body of the patient can each serve, and an improvised device is checked more often because it shifts. The trained responder and the paramedic apply splints and the authorized traction devices within the equipment and the procedures their service permits, and they recheck the neurovascular status after the splint is on. The lay first aider supports the limb with the hands or with whatever is at hand and applies nothing that presses on the injury, while the physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides what definitive immobilization is needed, which may be a cast, a brace or a surgical fixation. A splint is a treatment that can cause its own complication, which is why the limb is examined again after it is applied and why a tight splint is a reason to stop and escalate.

Question eleven is what imaging is required, and the word required carries the weight of that question. A radiograph is usually the first study, and it is taken in at least two directions so that a displacement in the third direction is not hidden, while the joint above and the joint below the fracture are included so that a second injury is not missed. Computed tomography, which is often called CT, shows the detail of a complex break, particularly where it enters a joint, and magnetic resonance imaging, which is often called MRI, is used mainly for soft tissues, including ligaments, cartilage, tendons and muscle. Imaging is ordered by a clinician trained to interpret it, never by the lay first aider, while the trained responder and the paramedic report the findings and the mechanism that may make a study necessary. Either study is requested when the answer it gives will change what is done, and it is performed after the limb has been immobilized so that the journey to the imaging room does not move the injury. An image is a picture of one moment, so the patient is examined and reassessed after the picture is taken, and the image never replaces the hands.

Question twelve is where the patient should go, and the answer follows from the answers to the questions before it. A patient with an open fracture, a suspected compartment syndrome, an injured vessel or nerve, a dislocation, a fracture that enters a joint, or a fracture in a child that crosses the growing part of the bone is referred to a facility with imaging, an operating room and an intensive care unit. A patient with a suspected injury of the spine is taken to a facility able to assess and to stabilize the spine, and the transfer is made with the precautions the local protocol requires. The choice also depends on distance, weather, the resources of the nearest hospital and the hour of the day, and those belong to the dispatcher, the receiving clinician and the local protocol rather than to the preference of the responder. The trained responder and the paramedic communicate the mechanism, the findings, the interventions already made and the neurovascular status before and after each one, because the receiving team continues a story rather than starting a new one. The lay first aider keeps the patient warm and still and keeps the time of the injury, because those three things change what the hospital can do.

Question thirteen is what needs to be reassessed, and the honest answer is almost everything, in the same order, after every intervention and at every change in the patient. The observations that matter are the same ones throughout, which are the airway, the breathing, the circulation, the level of consciousness, the color and the temperature of the limb, the pulse beyond the injury, the sensation, the movement and the pain. They are compared with the record rather than judged alone, because the meaning lies in the change and not in any single measurement. Pain that increases instead of settling, pain out of proportion to the injury, numbness, tingling, weakness and a limb that becomes tense and swollen are the findings that make the responder stop, and they are also the findings that make the responder escalate. A responder who suspects a compartment syndrome does not wait for the diagnosis to be confirmed, and within the local protocol the constricting bandage, splint or cast is released by someone authorized to release it, the limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, the patient is kept warm, and the patient is referred urgently, because the diagnosis is clinical and time is muscle. The framework loops back at this point rather than ending, since every intervention changes the answers to the questions that came before it.

The complication this framework most fears is the one caused by the treatment, which is why reassessment is a question in its own right and not a habit left to goodwill. A splint applied too tightly, a bandage that has become a band, a patient left too long in one position and a patient who has become cold while waiting are complications that follow care rather than injury. Each of them is found by looking at the patient again with the same questions in mind, and each is corrected within the limits of the local protocol by a person authorized to correct it. Some patients need the questions asked with a lower threshold for action, although the framework itself does not change: a child's bone bends before it breaks, an older adult's bone may break in a fall from standing height with little pain, and a patient who is intoxicated, confused or distracted may report nothing at all. The record and the handover are part of the treatment rather than a formality after it, so the time of the injury, the mechanism, the position in which the limb was found and the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention are written or spoken in a fixed order.

To summarize, the framework is thirteen questions asked in a fixed order, and it follows the sequence this course uses everywhere, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. Look for the mechanism and the energy behind it, for the injuries the patient does not mention, for bleeding that cannot be seen, for a limb that has lost its pulse, its color, its warmth, its feeling or its movement, for a wound over a fracture, and for a nerve or a vessel that has changed. Stabilize by warming the patient, controlling the bleeding that can be controlled, supporting the limb in the position found and treating the spine as unstable until someone qualified clears it, and record the neurovascular status before every intervention and after every one. Never straighten a deformed limb in the field, never remove an embedded object, never push exposed bone back into a wound, never explore an open wound blindly, and never attempt reduction, traction or casting unless you are trained, authorized and supervised to do it. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and a reduction is not attempted when the joint is shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when the vessels or the nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, or when an earlier attempt has failed. Reassess after every intervention, hand the patient over with the same story in the same order, and call the receiving facility and the specialist that the local protocol names.
