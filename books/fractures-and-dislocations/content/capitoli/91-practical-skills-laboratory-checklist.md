# Appendix B — Practical Skills Laboratory Checklist

The practical skills laboratory is the place where this course stops describing and begins to check, and this appendix is the checklist a learner and an assessor work through together. Every item is spoken as a sentence or a run of sentences rather than as a mark on a form, and the whole of it is meant to be used with a teacher present. The skills below cannot be learned from a book, from a recording or from this course, because a hand learns a position by being corrected and not by being told. The learner practices on a manikin first and on a healthy colleague afterwards, with consent, and never on an injured patient until a qualified supervisor has signed the skill off and the service has authorized it. The assessor is a course instructor, a clinical educator, or a qualified practitioner designated by the service, and that assessor's judgment about safety overrides every other item on the list. This appendix, like the rest of the course, is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

The general standard by which every skill is judged is the same, and it is worth stating before the individual items. The learner must name the step being performed, must perform it within the scope of practice of the level being trained, and must say what would make the step stop. A skill is demonstrated when it is performed in the right order, slowly enough to be watched, on the right patient, with the reason attached to it, and when the learner can describe what would count as harm. It is unsafe when the learner forces a limb, works outside the level they are authorized for, works alone where the protocol requires a team, or cannot say what they would do if the patient became worse. The sequence every skill in this appendix belongs to is the sequence of the whole course, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. An assessor who sees a step skipped marks the skill as not yet achieved, however neat the finished work looks.

The examination of an injured limb begins before the limb is touched, and the learner must demonstrate that order. The first part is the scene and the mechanism of injury, because the energy that broke a bone also passed through everything in its path. The second part is the primary survey, whose steps are remembered as ABCDE, meaning airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure, and the learner states aloud that life comes before limb. Then the limb is exposed enough to be seen, with the patient covered everywhere else and kept warm, because cold is a complication of trauma and not a detail of comfort. It is unsafe to walk to the injured limb first, to move the patient before the airway and the breathing have been assessed, or to remove clothing in a way that twists a limb. The lay first aider may check the airway and the breathing and summon help, while the trained responder and the clinician carry the survey further.

The limb itself is examined by looking, by feeling and by asking the patient to move it, and never by forcing it. Looking means the whole limb and both sides of the body for comparison, because a limb compared with its partner is read far better than one seen alone. Feeling means the temperature of the skin, the tenderness over a bone, the swelling, and the places where the skin has broken, and it is done gently enough that the patient does not pull away in pain. Asking means inviting the patient to move the fingers or the toes and to report what they feel, and the words of the patient are worth more than the interpretation of the examiner. What the learner then produces is a description rather than a diagnosis, naming the findings with the shared anatomical words and recording the position in which the limb was found. A sign that is reliable in one patient may be absent in another, so the learner says which findings are strong and which are weak in the patient in front of them. It is unsafe to test the stability of a joint by pulling on it, to push the ends of a suspected fracture against one another, to probe a wound with a finger or an instrument, and to try to straighten a limb that is deformed. Each of those acts belongs to a clinician trained to perform it, and each of them can convert an injury that would have healed into one that cannot.

Application of a splint is assessed as a way of protecting the limb and not as a way of making it look straight. The learner must demonstrate that the limb is held in the position in which it was found, that the splint is padded where it presses against a bone or a joint, and that it holds the joint above the injury and the joint below it, because a bone splinted alone can still be moved by the joints at either end. The splint is secured firmly enough to prevent movement and loosely enough that the circulation is not compromised, and the fingers or the toes are left visible so that they can be checked afterwards. The material matters less than the principle, and an improvised device is checked more often because it shifts. The trained first responder, the paramedic and the nurse apply splints within the procedures their service authorizes, while the lay first aider supports the limb with the hands and waits for help. The physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides what definitive immobilization is required, and casting and surgical fixation are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol rather than from any page of this course. It is unsafe to straighten a deformed limb in order to fit a splint to it, to apply a splint so tightly that firmness is mistaken for safety, and to apply a splint over an open wound that has not first been covered.

Every splint is checked after it is applied, and that check is part of the skill being assessed rather than a separate item. The learner examines the color and the temperature of the skin beyond the splint, feels for a pulse that was felt before, presses briefly on a nail to watch the color return, tests sensation on both sides, and asks the patient to move the fingers or the toes. The findings are compared with those recorded before the splint was applied, and any new numbness, tingling, weakness, pallor, coolness or pain out of proportion to the injury is reported at once. Adjusting or releasing a splint is done within the local protocol by a responder authorized to do it, and the learner must say plainly what would make them stop and escalate rather than continue to adjust. The same rule governs the constricting bandage or splint released when a compartment syndrome is suspected, the limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, and the patient is referred urgently because the diagnosis is clinical and time is muscle. It is unsafe to apply a splint and never look at the limb again, to attribute new pain to the injury alone, and to hide uncertainty from the receiving team.

The sling and the swathe are assessed as one skill, because together they rest the upper limb and stop it from swinging. The learner must show that the hand is left visible below the sling so that its color and warmth can be watched, that the part of the sling crossing the neck is padded so that it does not cut into the skin or the vessels, and that the swathe crosses the chest without restricting the chest wall. The sling is set so that the elbow is supported and the hand rests higher than the elbow, because a hand that hangs down becomes swollen and painful. Any responder trained to apply a sling may do so, and the physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides how long the limb is rested and what definitive treatment follows. It is unsafe to secure a sling so tightly that the fingers change color or the patient cannot breathe comfortably, to cover the hand so that it cannot be seen, and to use a sling as a substitute for the examination that should have come before it.

The log roll is the skill with the highest penalty for failure, and it is assessed with the seriousness it deserves. The learner must demonstrate the team, the leader who gives the count, the hands placed where the protocol requires them, the head and the trunk moved together as one unit, and the airway watched by a member of the team whose only task is that. The learner must state aloud that an injury of the spine is treated as unstable until it has been cleared by someone qualified to clear it, and that no lone manual handling technique is safe. The roll is used to examine the back, to place a board beneath the patient, or to move a patient when the protocol requires it, and never simply because it is a skill that has been learned. The number of rescuers a roll requires is settled by the local protocol and by the size of the patient, and any number offered in teaching is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol of your service. It is unsafe to roll a patient alone, to roll without a count, to let one member pull on an injured arm, and to take hold of the head in order to steer it. A failed roll is a failure of the team, and the learner must say when the roll should not be attempted at all.

Transfer from the ground to the stretcher is assessed as its own skill, because a limb that survived the injury and the splint can still be lost during the lift. The learner must demonstrate the briefing before the lift, the position of each member of the team, the leader who gives the word, the hands that support the injured limb, the straps that hold the patient to the carrying device, and the check of those straps before the patient is raised. The patient is covered and insulated from the ground and from the wind during the transfer, and the receiving surface is padded where the injured part will rest. The paramedic and the nurse usually lead the transfer within the procedures their service authorizes, while the lay first aider keeps the head steady and the patient warm and does not lift alone. It is unsafe to lift a patient by the clothing, to let a limb hang unsupported, to leave a strap over the injured part, and to finish the transfer without repeating the neurovascular examination afterwards.

The reassessment after the transfer repeats the same observations in the same order, and the comparison with the earlier record is what has value rather than any single measurement. The learner must demonstrate the examination of color, warmth, pulse, sensation, movement and pain, and must say what has changed since the roadside, while the trained responder and the clinician decide what that change means. Where a pelvic injury is suspected, the learner states that the pelvis is stabilized with the device the local protocol provides, that the patient is not rolled onto the injured side for convenience, and that the receiving team is told the pelvis has not been cleared. Where a spinal injury is suspected, the learner states that the patient is moved by a trained team using the precautions the protocol requires, and that the spine is not declared clear by anyone not qualified to clear it. It is unsafe to move a patient with a suspected pelvic or spinal injury on the strength of a single examination, and unsafe to treat a normal pulse as proof that nothing is wrong.

The neurovascular examination is assessed twice for every intervention, once before it and once after it, and the learner who performs it only afterwards has not achieved the skill. The learner must demonstrate the color and the temperature of the skin, the pulse felt beyond the injury and compared with the other limb, the color of a nail as it refills after brief pressure, the sensation tested on both sides, the movement of the fingers or the toes, and the pain the patient reports. The findings are spoken in a fixed order, written down before the intervention and written again after it so that the two records can be compared, and the words of the patient are recorded as far as possible rather than translated. A pulse that is present does not exclude serious injury to the vessels or to a compartment, and a pulse that is absent is an emergency, and the learner must be able to say both of those sentences. The lay first aider observes the color, the warmth and the ability of the patient to move and to feel, while the formal examination belongs to the trained responder or to the clinician. It is unsafe to skip the examination because the limb looks well, to assume findings from an earlier patient, to record a normal finding that was never tested, and to write the record after the splint rather than before it, because a finding that was not recorded before an intervention cannot be attributed afterwards.

What the learner must also demonstrate is the meaning of those findings and the action they demand. Numbness, tingling, weakness, pallor, coolness and pain that grows rather than settles are stated aloud as findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation, and the learner names the person who must be told and the urgency with which the patient must move. The learner must describe what a compartment syndrome looks like at the bedside, that it is diagnosed clinically, that the limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, and that a constricting bandage or splint is released within the local protocol by a responder authorized to do it. That item is assessed in words and through a constructed scenario, because a manikin cannot reproduce the pain of a compartment that is filling, and the scenario is an illustration rather than a patient. It is unsafe to wait for a confirmation that will arrive after the muscle has died, to elevate the limb in the belief that swelling will settle, and to apply something cold in a way that hides the skin from the examiner.

The record and the handover are assessed as clinical skills in their own right, because a finding that is not passed on has not been made. The record is completed by the responder who made the observation, and the handover is given to the receiving clinician or nurse. The learner must demonstrate a handover in a fixed order, naming the time and the mechanism of the injury, the position in which the limb was found, the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention, the treatment given, and what has changed since the last assessment. The record must be legible to someone who was not present, must distinguish what was observed from what was concluded, and must carry the time at which each observation was made. It is unsafe to hand over a patient without the neurovascular findings, to describe a splint as applied without saying when it was checked, and to leave a change in the condition of the patient out of the story because it seemed unimportant.

Practicing the skills of this appendix has limits that the learner must be able to state, and the assessor marks those limits as part of the skill. Reduction, which restores the position of the bone ends, traction, which holds them in line with a gentle continuous pull, and casting, which holds the position while the bone heals, are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and none of them is taught as a technique by this course. A reduction is not attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when the vessels or the nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the injury is open or the diagnosis unclear, when the responder lacks the training or the equipment, or when an earlier attempt has failed, and the learner must give the reason rather than only the rule. The learner must also state that no dose, no route and no regimen is given anywhere in this course, that the substance and the amount belong to the local protocol and to the prescriber, and that the decision to give any medicine is made by someone authorized to make it. Practice on each other requires consent, personal protective equipment appropriate to the task, and a supervisor present, and a learner who cannot state the scope of practice for a step has not yet earned the step.

To summarize, this appendix is a checklist for supervised practice and not a substitute for it, and every item in it names the provider level that may perform the step. The examination of an injured limb is assessed as looking, feeling and asking the patient to move, always comparing both sides, never forcing a joint, never probing a wound and never straightening a deformed limb, which is splinted in the position found. The application of a splint is assessed as padding the bony points, holding the joints above and below, leaving the fingers or the toes visible and checking the limb afterwards, and the sling and the swathe are assessed as supporting the arm without hiding the hand or restricting the chest. The log roll and the transfer are assessed as team skills with a leader and a count, with the spine treated as unstable until it is cleared by someone qualified to clear it, and with no lone manual handling technique ever taught as safe. The neurovascular examination is assessed before every intervention and again after it, its findings are recorded in a fixed order, and a present pulse is never taken as proof that the limb is safe. Reduction, traction and casting belong to supervised clinical training and to local protocol, no dose and no route appears anywhere in this course, and any figure named here is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol of your service. This appendix is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.
