# Chapter 17 — Elbow Dislocation

The elbow is a joint a person uses all day, and injuring it changes ordinary life quickly. A dislocation means the joint surfaces have lost contact, and most often the forearm moves backward. It is one of the dislocations in which delay damages the arm, because the artery and the three nerves lie close against the bone. This chapter follows the order used throughout this course, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. It is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

Three bones meet at the elbow, and their arrangement explains much of what follows. The humerus widens at its lower end into two columns, with an epicondyle on each side. The ulna lies on the side of the little finger and carries the olecranon, the point felt at the back of the elbow. The radius lies on the side of the thumb, and its flat upper end, the radial head, turns when the palm is turned over. A capsule surrounds the joint and is reinforced by ligaments on each side, so the joint is stable in health and unforgiving when it is disrupted.

Four structures lie close to the elbow, and each can be injured with it. The brachial artery runs down the inner side of the arm and passes in front of the joint, where it divides into the vessels of the forearm and the hand. The median nerve travels with that artery, directly in front of the joint surface from which the ulna moves away. The ulnar nerve runs behind the inner epicondyle in a shallow groove, and it tingles when the elbow is struck. The radial nerve winds around the outer humerus, supplies the muscles that straighten the wrist and fingers, and carries feeling from the back of the hand.

The usual mechanism is a fall onto an outstretched hand, with the arm taking the weight of the body and the elbow partly bent. The force travels up the forearm and drives the ulna and the radius backward while the humerus stays, levering the olecranon over the lower humerus. The energy of the injury predicts the damage, so a young person with a violent mechanism is more likely to have broken bone as well. In a constructed illustration of the kind used in teaching, a young adult lands on one hand during a game, and the arm is found bent the wrong way with the point of the elbow standing out behind.

What the responder sees is a limb whose outline has changed. The elbow is held partly bent and cannot be moved further, because the joint surfaces are no longer engaged. The point of the elbow sits too far back and too high, with a hollow where it should be, and the forearm looks shorter than the other one. A dislocation with a wound over it is treated as an open injury, and the responder does not test how far the joint moves or try to put it back.

One injury is easily mistaken for the other, and the difference matters. A break of the humerus just above the joint can push the lower fragment backward and produce much the same shape. That break may also injure the artery and the median nerve, and it can be the more dangerous of the two. No field test separates them reliably, and the responder is not expected to settle the question at the scene. The safe position for both is the position found, supported and immobilized without any attempt to straighten it.

The reason this dislocation is urgent is the narrow space through which the artery and the nerves must pass. When the surfaces separate, the artery in front of the joint is stretched, and it may be torn, kinked or bruised. A joint left out of place adds a second problem, because swelling inside the closed sleeve of the arm raises the pressure in the compartments of the forearm. Time is muscle, and delay also makes the reduction harder, because swelling and early scar make the joint resist every attempt to return it.

Assessment begins before the limb is touched. The primary survey is performed in the order remembered as ABCDE, which stands for airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure, and the limb waits until the patient is stable enough. A patient who fell from a height may have a head injury, a spinal injury or blood inside the abdomen, and any of those comes first. The history includes the time, the height of the fall, the medicines taken and whether this joint has dislocated before. The lay first aider observes and reports, while the structured examination belongs to the trained responder, the paramedic, the nurse or the physician under the procedures their service authorizes.

The neurovascular examination decides how urgent the case is, and it is done before and after every intervention. The pulse is felt at the wrist on the thumb side, and in the elbow crease or the upper arm if the wrist pulse cannot be found. Capillary filling is tested by pressing a fingernail until it blanches and watching how quickly the color returns. Sensation is tested in the area of each of the three nerves, and movement is tested by asking the patient to spread the fingers, bring the thumb across the palm and straighten the wrist against gravity. Every finding is spoken aloud and written with the time, and the lay first aider reports only what the patient says and what can be seen.

The record is written before any intervention, because a finding cannot be attributed afterwards if it was never noted before. If the patient could not feel the little finger before the splint was applied and still cannot feel it after, the splint is not the cause. The examination is repeated in the same order after every intervention, and the two records are compared rather than each being judged on its own. A change in any finding is an emergency, and the patient is referred without further delay.

An intact pulse at the wrist is reassuring but not a guarantee, and treating it as one is the commonest error made with this injury. The artery can be torn while the pulse remains palpable, because blood reaches the hand through smaller vessels around the elbow. The vessel may also be bruised rather than divided, so a clot forms slowly and a hand that was pink on arrival is pale an hour later. Pain that grows rather than settles, a hand that becomes cold or loses its color, and a forearm that becomes tight and hard matter more than the pulse. The examiner therefore repeats the whole examination, and a responder who finds any of those changes stops and escalates.

Two findings point directly at a torn artery, and both require immediate action. The first is an expanding swelling at the elbow, especially if it is tense and enlarging while it is watched. The second is a mass that beats in time with the pulse, which is called a pulsatile hematoma, and it is handled by covering it and supporting the limb. A wound that keeps bleeding despite firm direct pressure, or that bleeds in spurts, is treated the same way, with a sterile dressing and urgent transfer to a facility with an operating room.

Each nerve gives its own pattern of loss, and the pattern tells the clinician where to look. Injury to the ulnar nerve weakens the spread of the fingers and the pinch between thumb and index finger, and it removes feeling from the little finger. Injury to the median nerve weakens the bending of the thumb, the index finger and the middle finger, and the movement that brings the thumb across the palm. Injury to the radial nerve weakens the straightening of the wrist and the fingers, so the hand hangs down when the arm is lifted. None of these tests is perfect, and the absence of a clear loss does not exclude damage.

One complication changes the whole plan of care. The median nerve can be caught within the dislocated joint, trapped between the humerus and the ulna. A joint held open by a trapped nerve will not return to its place, and this is one reason a reduction must never be forced. A reduction is also not attempted when a fragment of bone is caught in the joint, when the joint surfaces are shattered, when the vessels or nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, when the team lacks training or equipment, or when an earlier attempt has failed. When the clinician meets resistance, the attempt is stopped and the patient goes to an operating room.

Not every dislocated elbow arrives dislocated, because the joint sometimes slips back into place by itself. The shape of the limb may then look almost normal, and the examiner sees only swelling, bruising and tenderness. The patient still cannot use the arm comfortably, and the joint is still unstable. A swollen elbow after a fall that cannot be moved through a full range deserves the same immobilization and referral as an obvious deformity. The absence of a deformity is a finding to interpret with care, not a reason to relax.

Stabilization in the field is a short list of acts, and the most important of them is restraint. The limb is supported in the position in which it was found and is never straightened, because straightening pulls on the artery and the nerves. The lay first aider supports the arm with a pillow, a folded garment or both hands, keeps the patient still and warm, and waits for help. The trained responder or the paramedic may apply a splint within the equipment and procedures the service authorizes, holding the elbow comfortably. Nothing is given by mouth, because a patient who may need an operation should arrive with an empty stomach, and covering is added against cold.

An improvised splint is padded where it presses on bone and firm enough to hold without stopping the circulation. The material matters less than the principle, since a rolled magazine, a padded board or a folded blanket can each serve. The trained responder checks the pulse, color, warmth and sensation in the hand after the splint is applied. If the hand becomes pale, cold or painful, the splint is loosened within the limits of the local protocol and the patient is referred urgently. Nothing is ever pushed into the joint, an embedded object is left where it lies because it may be holding pressure on a torn vessel, and a dislocated joint is not manipulated by anyone.

Pain relief is part of humane care, and a book that teaches a dose is a dangerous book. The responder needs to know who decides, what makes the decision urgent and what the scope of practice forbids. The substance, the amount, the route and the timing belong to the prescriber and to the local protocol, and they differ between services, countries and editions. Relief becomes urgent when the patient cannot be moved or examined because of pain, and when the pain is out of proportion to the injury. The physician or the advanced practice nurse authorized to prescribe decides what is given, and the paramedic gives only what the service protocol permits.

Reassessment is performed after every intervention, and it exists to catch the complication caused by the treatment. The hand is checked for color, warmth, pulse, capillary filling, sensation and movement, and the splint for tightness. The findings that stop the responder are pain that increases instead of settling, numbness or tingling that is new, a movement the patient has lost, and a forearm that becomes tense, swollen and hard. A responder who suspects a compartment syndrome acts without waiting for confirmation, because the diagnosis is clinical and time is muscle. The constricting bandage, splint or cast is released by someone the local protocol authorizes, the limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, and the patient is referred urgently.

Imaging is ordered by a clinician trained to interpret it, and it answers questions the examination has already raised. A radiograph is taken in at least two directions, and the joint above and the joint below are included so that a second injury is not hidden. The clinician looks for a break of the radial head, of the coronoid process of the ulna, of the medial epicondyle and of the lower humerus, because each changes what must be done. Computed tomography is used when a fragment is unexplained, and magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for ligaments, cartilage and tendons. An image is a picture of one moment, so the limb and the neurovascular status are checked again after imaging.

Reduction means restoring the joint surfaces to their proper contact, and it relieves the stretch on the artery and the nerves. It is performed by a physician or an orthopedic surgeon trained and certified to do it. The technique itself belongs to supervised clinical training and to the local protocol, and no book can teach it safely. Before the attempt the neurovascular status is recorded, and after it is recorded again, because the value lies in the comparison. A reduction is not attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment or the median nerve is caught in the joint, when the vessels or nerves are failing, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, when the team lacks training or equipment, or when an earlier attempt has failed. In each of those situations the attempt can turn an injury that would have healed into one that cannot, and the correct act is to stop and escalate.

The complications of this injury are the reason it is watched for so long. The joint stiffens easily, because the capsule thickens and the muscle tightens, and a joint held still for a long time may never regain its range. New bone can form in the soft tissues around the joint, a process called heterotopic ossification, and it can block movement permanently. The artery can clot after the joint has been returned to its place, and the swelling that follows any reduction can raise the pressure in the compartments of the forearm. The median nerve may be injured at the moment of injury or during the reduction, and the ulnar nerve may suffer if the elbow is kept bent for a long time. Infection follows an open injury that is not treated properly, and instability follows a joint whose ligaments and bone have not healed in a way that holds.

Definitive treatment depends on what the injury turns out to be, and the decision belongs to the orthopedic surgeon. An unstable joint may need a hinge that allows controlled movement, and a joint with a trapped fragment or a shattered surface may need reconstruction. An open injury needs thorough cleaning in an operating room, and definitive closure of the wound belongs there and nowhere else. A patient whose arm remains pulseless after reduction needs a surgeon trained in the repair of blood vessels. Traction, casting and the surgical holding of fragments are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and this course describes what they achieve rather than how to carry them out.

Referral is a decision made early and reviewed often, not a formality at the end of the sequence. Every patient with a dislocated elbow, a suspected dislocation that has reduced itself, a break around the elbow, a wound near the joint, a lost pulse, a numb hand or a tense forearm is referred to a facility with imaging, an orthopedic surgeon and an operating room. A simple dislocation treated where the right skills and follow-up exist may be managed there, but the decision to keep the patient belongs to the clinician who accepts responsibility for the outcome. The responder states plainly what was found, what was done, what changed and what is still unknown, and gives the times. The receiving team should be able to continue a story rather than to start a new one.

The elbow of a child behaves differently, and one difference matters more than any other. The ends of a child's bones are made of cartilage that does not appear on a radiograph in the same way as bone, so an injury that has moved the growing part can be hard to see. The elbow of a child is also the site of an injury in which the lower humerus breaks just above the joint, which can look like a dislocation while endangering the blood supply of the forearm. A child who will not use an arm, holds it still or cries when it is moved deserves radiographs and a clinician's examination. The scope of practice does not change because the patient is a child, but the threshold for imaging and referral is lower.

Age changes the elbow in ways that alter both the injury and the recovery. An older adult whose bone has lost density may break the radial head or the lower humerus in a fall from standing height. The same patient often has less muscle to protect the joint and more difficulty regaining movement, so rehabilitation matters as much as the operation. A patient who has dislocated an elbow before may do so again with less force, because the capsule and the ligaments have been stretched. In each of these patients an examination that relies on a single sign will mislead, and the response is a lower threshold for imaging and referral.

Some of the signs taught for this injury are less reliable than they sound. The pulse at the wrist may be present with a torn artery, and it may be absent for a moment in a patient who is cold or shocked and then return. The apparent shortening of the forearm is a comparison between two limbs, so it can be missed if only one arm is examined. The point of the elbow cannot always be felt clearly once the joint has swollen, and a wound may be small enough to hide under a sleeve. Sensation testing depends on the ability of the patient to describe what they feel, and age, fear and other injuries all affect that.

The record and the handover are part of the treatment rather than a formality that follows it. The time of the injury, the mechanism, the position in which the limb was found and the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention are written or spoken in a fixed order. A delay of 6 hours is a common threshold for concern in teaching, and that figure is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. The record travels with the patient, because the comparison is worthless if the earlier finding cannot be produced. A patient whose condition changes during transport is reassessed, and the change is reported before the handover.

A constructed illustration may hold the sequence in mind. A middle-aged person trips on a step and falls onto one outstretched hand, and the elbow is found bent with the point standing out behind. The arm is supported in that position with a pillow, the hand is examined and the findings are written, and no one straightens the limb. The receiving clinician records the neurovascular status again, orders radiographs and checks for a break of the radial head and the coronoid process before any attempt to put the joint back. If the joint returns smoothly and the hand remains well, the patient is protected briefly and then begins supervised movement, and if it does not, the patient goes to the operating room. The example is an illustration and not a real patient, and every decision in it belongs to the clinician and to the local protocol.

The order is worth walking through once more. The first step is to assess, which means the scene, the mechanism, the primary survey in the order remembered as ABCDE and then the limb. The next is to stabilize, which means keeping the patient warm, controlling the bleeding that can be controlled and supporting the limb in the position found. Only then comes the neurovascular examination, performed before any intervention, recorded and repeated after every one. Immobilization follows, applied by the trained responder within the authorized equipment, and imaging comes after that, ordered by the clinician who will interpret it. Reassessment follows every intervention, and the last step is referral or definitive treatment, which belongs to the physician, the orthopedic surgeon and the vascular surgeon.

Transport adds its own hazards. The splinted limb is secured so that it cannot swing against the stretcher or the door, and the patient is positioned so that the whole patient can still be assessed. An injury of the spine is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has done so, and no lone manual handling technique is safe. A patient with this injury and a mechanism that could have hurt the spine is moved by a trained team using the precautions the local protocol requires. The patient is covered against cold throughout the journey, and the hand is checked repeatedly, because a hand that was pink at the scene can become pale in a cold ambulance.

Rehabilitation begins when the joint has been reduced and held safely, and it is supervised rather than self-taught. The aim is a joint that moves again without becoming unstable, and the balance between movement and protection changes with the injury. A program that starts too late leaves a stiff elbow, and one that forces movement through pain can dislodge a fragment or stretch a ligament that is trying to heal. The patient is taught what to watch for at home, including increasing pain, numbness, a cold hand and any change in the shape of the limb. The number of weeks named for any stage of this recovery must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

Two situations demand that the responder move faster than usual. The first is the arm that remains pulseless after the joint has been returned to its proper position, because the hand has a limited tolerance for a lack of blood. The second is the forearm that becomes tense, swollen and painful out of proportion to the injury, especially when the pain increases on stretching the fingers. In both situations the constricting device is released within the limits of the local protocol, the limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, and the patient is kept warm. The patient is transferred urgently to a facility with a vascular surgeon. The responder does not explore the wound, does not remove an embedded object and does not push exposed bone back into the limb, because what to do with the vessel belongs to the surgeon.

An open dislocation, in which the joint has communicated with the outside, is treated as a contaminated wound from the first moment. The responder covers it with a sterile dressing, controls the bleeding that can be controlled, records the neurovascular status, immobilizes the limb in the position found and arranges urgent transfer. The wound is not irrigated with anything improvised, not packed tightly and not explored, because the wound will be cleaned properly in an operating room. Protection against tetanus and the use of antibiotics are decisions made by the prescriber under the local protocol, and the wound itself makes the decision urgent. What the responder must not do is take a dose, a drug name or a schedule from a course that is listened to rather than read.

To summarize, a dislocated elbow is a joint whose surfaces have lost contact, most often with the forearm driven backward, and it is one of the dislocations in which delay damages the arm, because the brachial artery and the median, ulnar and radial nerves lie against the bone that has moved. The course follows one order in every chapter, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and it names the provider level for every act. A deformed limb is splinted in the position found and is never straightened in the field, an embedded object is never removed, exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, and an open wound is never explored blindly, because definitive closure belongs to the operating room. An intact pulse does not exclude a torn artery and does not exclude a compartment syndrome, so the neurovascular examination is recorded before every intervention and repeated after every one. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and a reduction is not attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment or the median nerve is caught in the joint, when the vessels or the nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, when the team lacks training or equipment, or when an earlier attempt has failed. Cold is treated as a complication, the spine is treated as unstable until it is cleared by someone qualified to clear it, and the patient is reassessed after every intervention and watched until the handover. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.
