# Chapter 16 — Humeral Shaft and Distal Humerus Fractures

The upper arm bone is called the humerus, and this chapter deals with the two parts of it that lie below the shoulder: the shaft, which is the long middle section, and the distal end, which widens into the elbow. A break of the shaft is a break of a bone surrounded on every side by muscle, and its most famous complication is a nerve that stops working for a time. A break of the distal end enters the elbow itself, and what is at risk there is the blood supply to the forearm and the future movement of the joint. The order used in every part of this course is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

The shaft of the humerus is nearly cylindrical in its middle and becomes triangular as it approaches the elbow, and it is wrapped in a thick sleeve of muscle that gives the arm its shape. The biceps lies in front of it and bends the elbow, the triceps lies behind it and straightens the elbow, and the muscles of the shoulder attach near its upper end. Because those muscles surround the bone on every side, a break of the shaft bleeds into a closed space and the arm swells, which is one reason this is not the small injury it can appear to be at first. The bone also carries the weight of the arm below the break, and that weight, with the pull of the muscles, produces the deformity and the shortening that the responder sees and feels. Muscle also explains why the broken ends so often settle into a position in which the arm lies across the chest rather than straight down at the side.

Two important structures travel along the shaft rather than through the middle of the arm, and both lie close against the bone. The radial nerve passes from the back of the upper arm around the bone in a shallow channel called the radial groove, or spiral groove, and there it rests directly on the surface of the shaft, while below the groove it pierces a sheet of fibrous tissue near the outer side of the elbow, which is a second place where it can be caught. On the inner side of the arm the brachial artery and the median nerve run together toward the front of the elbow, and the ulnar nerve passes around the back of the inner bump of the elbow, called the medial epicondyle. This arrangement is efficient in health and dangerous in injury, because a bone that breaks and shifts moves exactly where these structures lie, so a break of the shaft can injure a nerve without any wound on the skin.

The distal humerus widens into two columns of bone that end in the rounded surface of the elbow joint, divided into a part that hinges with the ulna and a part that turns with the head of the radius. Above the joint the bone thins into two narrow columns separated by a hollow, and this is where the bone most often gives way, because it is the weakest link between the strong shaft and the strong joint surface. The ulnar nerve lies in a groove behind the medial epicondyle, and the median nerve and the brachial artery cross the front of the joint, both close to a displaced fragment from the distal end. A break that splits the joint surface therefore has consequences beyond the broken bone, because a joint that is damaged and then held still becomes stiff, and the elbow tolerates stiffness badly, so the distal injury is a different problem with a different urgency.

The two injuries reach the patient by different routes, and the history is worth listening to carefully. A break of the shaft often follows a direct blow to the arm, a fall onto the side, or a twisting force such as a throw or a struggle, and it can follow a fall from a height in which the energy is greater. A break of the distal end usually follows a fall onto the outstretched hand with the elbow bent or partly bent, and in a child the same fall is the most common cause of a break just above the elbow. The mechanism matters because it predicts how much energy passed through the limb, and greater energy means a greater chance that the skin, the muscle, the vessels and the nerves have been injured as well.

A patient with a break of the shaft usually arrives supporting the injured arm with the other hand, because the limb is too painful and too unstable to be left unsupported. The arm may be visibly bent, it may be shorter than the other one, and the elbow may point in a direction that does not match the other side. That shortening means the broken ends have slid past one another, because the muscles pull the lower fragment upward while the weight of the arm pulls it downward. Swelling and bruising appear over the hours that follow rather than at once, so an arm that looks almost normal in the first minutes can never be dismissed. The patient may be unable to lift the arm, to bend the elbow or to turn the palm, and each finding is recorded rather than interpreted, because the responder looks, compares the two sides and does not move the limb to find out more.

The distal injury announces itself at the elbow rather than in the middle of the arm. The joint swells quickly, the arm is held at a fixed angle that the patient will not change, and any attempt to move it produces pain. The point of the elbow may look more prominent than usual, and bruising appears around the joint and along the forearm within a short time. A distal injury can look very much like a dislocation of the elbow, and the two cannot be told apart by looking and feeling, so the difference belongs to the radiograph and to the clinician. In a child the same injury may produce almost no visible deformity, and the only sign may be a child who will not use the arm and who cries when it is moved. The responder records the position of the arm, the swelling, the wound if there is one and the neurovascular findings, and then supports what is there.

An open injury deserves separate attention, because the humerus lies just under the skin for much of its length, and a wound over the fracture site means that the bone or the deep tissues have communicated with the outside. Such a fracture is contaminated even when the wound looks clean. The wound is covered with a sterile dressing, the bleeding that can be controlled is controlled by pressure around the wound and not inside it, and anything embedded is left exactly where it is. Bone that has come through the skin is never pushed back, because it may be holding pressure on a torn vessel and because pushing it back carries contamination deeper into the arm. The wound is never explored blindly, and definitive cleaning and closure belong to the operating room and to nobody else. The patient is kept warm, the arm is supported as it lies, and transfer is arranged urgently.

Assessment begins before the arm, and it begins with the scene and with the patient rather than with the fracture. The primary survey follows the steps remembered as ABCDE, which are airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure, and no limb injury is treated before those steps are complete. A patient who has fallen from a height may have a broken arm and an injured spine, a torn vessel in the chest or bleeding inside the abdomen, and the arm is then the most visible injury rather than the most dangerous one. When two correct actions compete for the same pair of hands, life comes before limb, because a limb can wait for minutes that a brain and a heart cannot. The history includes the time, the mechanism, the age of the patient and any illness or medicine that has weakened the bone, and a break through diseased bone is passed on rather than decided in the field.

Stabilization in the field is a small set of acts, and the most important of them is restraint. The arm is supported in the position in which it was found, with the hands, a pillow or whatever the responder has, and it is never straightened in order to make it look better. Pulling the arm straight can tear a nerve or a vessel that was still intact, can turn a closed fracture into an open one, and can release the pressure that was holding a torn vessel closed. The lay first aider gives support and warmth, while the trained responder, the emergency medical technician and the paramedic work within the equipment and procedures their service authorizes. Bleeding is controlled with a sterile dressing and direct pressure, the wound is covered, and the patient is kept warm because cold is a complication of trauma. Nothing is given by mouth when an operation may be needed, and the decision about that belongs to the clinician who will arrange the operation.

The neurovascular examination is performed before any intervention and repeated after it, and its result is recorded, because a finding not written down before a splint was applied cannot be attributed afterwards. The examiner looks at the color of the fingers, feels their warmth and compares the two arms. The pulse is felt at the inner side of the elbow and at the wrist, and a finger is pressed on a nail to watch the color return. Sensation is tested in the territory of each of the three nerves of the arm, in the web space between the thumb and the index finger, in the pulp of the index finger and in the little finger. Movement is tested by asking the patient to lift the wrist against gravity, to spread the fingers and to touch the thumb to the little finger, and each is compared with the other hand. The result is written in the words of the patient as far as possible, so that the receiving team can compare it with the next examination.

The radial nerve is the structure this chapter fears most in the shaft injury, and when it stops working the patient cannot lift the wrist or straighten the fingers, a finding called wrist drop, while the back of the hand between the thumb and the index finger feels numb. The deficit may be there from the moment of the injury, or it may appear after the arm has been moved, splinted or examined by someone. A nerve that was working before an intervention and is not working afterwards must be reported at once, because the intervention may have caused it and the plan may need to change. The finding is documented in the same words each time, so that the next examiner can see not only that the wrist does not lift but also when it stopped lifting. Testing the nerve by asking the patient to push hard against painful resistance is not a field test, because pain can imitate weakness. Only a clinician can judge the meaning of the deficit, and it is the surgeon who decides whether the nerve must be inspected.

Some findings are unreliable in particular patients, and a responder who relies on a single sign will miss the injury. An intact pulse does not exclude severe damage to the vessels or to the compartments of the arm, because the flow through the smallest vessels can fail while a pulse at the wrist remains strong. A patient who has been drinking, who has an injury to the brain, who is in severe pain or who is a frightened child may be unable to cooperate with a test of sensation or movement, and in an older adult the pain may be less than its severity suggests. The examiner therefore compares the two sides, repeats the examination after every intervention and records what could not be tested as clearly as what could. It is never correct to write that the neurovascular status is intact when the examination was not performed.

Immobilization means preventing movement at the fracture and at the joints above and below it, because a bone held alone can still be moved by the shoulder and by the elbow. For the shaft injury the arm is carried across the body in a sling that takes the weight of the forearm, with a swathe that binds the arm to the chest as far as the injury allows. The support is applied over the position found, padded where it presses on bone, and fastened firmly enough to hold yet loosely enough to let the fingers stay warm and pink. When the chest has also been injured, the band around the chest is left loose and the arm rests against a pillow instead. A trained responder, a paramedic or a nurse authorized by protocol may apply a padded splint along the sides of the arm, and a splint from the shoulder to the hand belongs to that training and to the local protocol rather than to this book. The purpose is not to make the arm look straight but to keep it from moving, and a support that straightens a bent arm has failed.

The distal injury is immobilized with the elbow at the angle in which it was found, and that angle is never forced open or forced closed. A splint applied along the back of the arm and the forearm, padded and shaped to the position found, is the support a trained responder or a clinician may use within the local protocol, and it holds the elbow still while the patient is moved. The arm is then carried across the body in a sling, with the hand supported and the fingers visible so that the circulation can be watched. Nothing is wrapped around the elbow tightly enough to compress it, because the swelling of this injury is rapid and a band that was comfortable when it was applied may be dangerous an hour later. An improvised support from a padded board, a rolled blanket or a folded magazine can serve while help is coming. The check is the same at every level: color, warmth, pulse, sensation, movement and pain, before the support is applied and after it is applied.

A deformed arm is not straightened in the field, and no listener may learn from a book how to reduce, to apply traction or to cast this injury. Reduction means restoring the broken ends to their proper position, traction means holding them in line with a gentle continuous pull, and casting means holding the position while the bone heals, and all three are skills learned on real patients under supervision and performed according to local protocol. The reason for that restraint is anatomy, because the radial nerve, the median nerve and the brachial artery lie against the bone where a movement of the broken ends can tear them. A reduction is not attempted, even by a clinician, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, when a fragment or the nerve is caught between the ends, when the vessels or the nerves need a specialist, when the equipment or the training is inadequate, or when an earlier attempt has failed. In each of those situations an attempt can convert an injury that would have healed into one that cannot, and force is never a technique.

Imaging confirms what the hands have already suggested, and it is ordered and interpreted by a clinician trained to do it. The first study is usually a radiograph, taken in at least two directions so that a displacement in the third direction is not hidden, and it must show the whole bone with the joints above and below, because a second break or a dislocation may sit at either end. The elbow needs particular care, because a break of the distal end and a dislocation can look alike and an angled view is often needed to see the joint surface. Computed tomography shows the detail of a complex break that enters the joint, and magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for the soft tissues, including ligaments, tendons, cartilage and muscle. A break of the shaft together with a break of the forearm on the same side, called a floating elbow, is a serious combination that needs a surgeon and an operating room. The trained responder does not decide which study is needed, the lay first aider neither orders nor interprets one, and because an image is a picture of one moment the patient is examined again after every journey to the imaging room.

Reassessment is performed after every intervention, and it is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with what was recorded before, and the comparison has value rather than any single measurement. The limb is checked for color, warmth, pulse, sensation, movement and pain, and the support is checked for tightness and pressure points. Pain that increases instead of settling, pain out of proportion to the injury, numbness, tingling, weakness and a forearm that becomes tense and swollen are the findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation. The most feared of these is a compartment syndrome, in which pressure inside the closed space of the arm or the forearm rises until muscle and then nerve are starved of blood, while a pulse at the wrist can still be felt. A responder who suspects it does not wait for confirmation, because the diagnosis is clinical and time is muscle, so within the limits of local protocol a constricting bandage or splint is released by a nurse or a clinician authorized to do it, the limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, and the patient is kept warm and referred urgently.

Referral or definitive treatment is the last step of the sequence, and it begins with the recognition that some injuries cannot be managed where the patient lies. Any patient with an open injury, an absent pulse, a new nerve deficit, a suspected compartment syndrome, a break that enters the elbow joint, a displaced break at the distal end, or a break of the shaft together with a break of the forearm is referred to a facility with imaging, an operating room and an intensive care unit. A child with a break just above the elbow is referred urgently, because the bone there is thin, the displacement can be great and the blood supply to the forearm can be threatened. A patient whose arm cannot be supported comfortably, whose pain is not controlled or whose neurovascular findings change in any way is also referred without delay. The referral is a spoken and written handover, and it carries the time, the mechanism, the position in which the arm was found, the support applied and the neurovascular findings before and after each step. When the responder is uncertain whether a patient needs a specialist, the answer is to ask the receiving clinician rather than to wait and see.

Definitive care differs between the two injuries, and in both cases it belongs to the orthopedic surgeon and to that surgeon's team. Many breaks of the shaft heal well in a splint or a brace applied by the treating clinician, and some need an operation in which the fragments are held with a metal plate or with a rod placed inside the canal of the bone. The choice depends on the break, the soft tissues, whether a nerve or a vessel is injured and on the patient, and it is not a decision for the responder or for this book. A nerve that has stopped working is usually watched for a period decided by the surgeon, because most such injuries recover on their own, while a trapped nerve or one that fails to recover may be explored and repaired in the operating room. A displaced break of the distal end in an adult is usually treated with an operation, because the joint surface must be put back together accurately and the elbow must be moved early rather than held still. In every case the technique belongs to supervised clinical training, and no part of this course teaches a listener to perform it.

Some patients deserve a lower threshold for imaging and referral rather than a different sequence, and complications explain why the reassessment is never skipped. The bone of a child is more elastic than the bone of an adult and has a growth region near each end, so a child's break may look minor and still disturb growth, while an older adult with thin bone may break the shaft in a fall from standing height. A patient treated for cancer elsewhere may break the arm through a deposit of tumor, called a pathological fracture, and in such a patient the pain may be far less than the injury suggests. A break of this bone may fail to unite, which is called a nonunion, or unite in a position that leaves the arm shortened or bent, which is called a malunion, and the elbow may stiffen after a distal injury even when the repair was good. The radial nerve may fail to recover, the median and ulnar nerves may be injured, and a compartment syndrome not recognized in time can leave a clawed hand and a useless wrist. Some of these complications are caused by treatment, and that is why this course asks the same questions after every intervention.

The record and the handover are part of the treatment rather than a formality that follows it. The time of the injury, the mechanism, the position in which the arm was found, the wound if there was one, the support applied and the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention are written in a fixed order, so that the receiving team continues a story instead of starting a new one. Any change in consciousness, breathing, pulse or skin color sends the responder back to the beginning, because the limb is never the priority over the airway, and a patient with this injury may also have an injured spine, which is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has done so. Comfort belongs to the treatment as well, so the responder records the pain in the same way each time, because a patient in severe pain cannot cooperate with an examination, and medicine for pain is given only by a clinician or a paramedic authorized to give it, while the substance, the amount and the route belong to the local protocol and to the prescriber. Warmth is provided throughout, because cold worsens pain and hides the color of the skin, and everything here is learned with a teacher and certified by an authority that defines what may be done unsupervised.

Where this chapter names a time or a distance, it names an illustration rather than a standard, because such figures differ between services, countries and editions, and they must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. Every figure in this chapter is therefore a teaching example and not a threshold to apply without checking it. Practice also differs in where the line is drawn between a break treated in a brace and one that needs an operation. What can be learned from a book, and what this course teaches, is what to look for, what to record, what to do with the hands, and who to call.

To summarize, the upper arm bone can break in its shaft or at its distal end, and the two injuries look alike on the outside while carrying very different dangers. A break of the shaft produces a bent and shortened arm, and its particular risk is the radial nerve that runs in the groove against the bone, so a patient who cannot lift the wrist or who feels numb on the back of the hand has a finding to be recorded before anything is applied and again afterwards. A break of the distal end enters the elbow itself, threatens the brachial artery, the median nerve and the ulnar nerve, and can leave the joint permanently stiff, so it is always referred. The sequence is to assess with the primary survey first, to stabilize by supporting the arm as it lies, to examine and record the neurovascular status before and after every intervention, to immobilize with a sling and a swathe or with a padded splint applied within the local protocol, to image through a clinician, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. A deformed arm is never straightened in the field, an embedded object is never removed, exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, an open wound is never explored blindly, and definitive closure belongs to the operating room. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and a reduction is not attempted when the injury is open, when a fragment or the nerve is caught between the ends, when the vessels or nerves need a specialist, or when the training and the equipment are not there. A suspected compartment syndrome is acted on at once by releasing the constricting bandage within the local protocol, without elevating the limb above the level of the heart, and by referring the patient urgently, because time is muscle. Everyone with a suspected break of this bone is referred to a clinician, the arm is supported and the patient kept warm throughout, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.
