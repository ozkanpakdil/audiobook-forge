# Chapter 20 — Wrist Fractures

The wrist is the part of the arm that people break most often, and the bone that breaks is usually the lower end of the radius, the bone on the thumb side of the forearm that carries most of the weight of the hand. Beside it lies the ulna, thinner and ending a little lower, joined to the radius in a way that lets the palm turn from facing forward to facing backward. An injury here can look dramatic and still heal well, or look modest and still threaten the fingers, so the responder trusts the examination rather than the appearance. This chapter follows the order used throughout this course, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. What is written here is educational material, and it does not replace training, certification, supervision, the protocol of your service, or the law of the country in which you work.

The lower end of the radius is broad and flat where it meets the small bones of the hand, and that broad surface carries most of the load when a person catches a fall. The ulna carries less of that load, but it provides the pivot that lets the forearm rotate, and the joint between the two lower ends is part of the injury whenever the radius is displaced. On the little finger side of the wrist a person can feel a small point of bone called the ulnar styloid, which is where the ligaments that steady the arrangement attach, and it is often broken at the same time. Because the two bones and their joints work as one unit, an injury of one is treated as an injury of both until somebody qualified has shown otherwise.

The median nerve is what makes this fracture more than a broken bone. It passes through a fibrous tunnel at the front of the wrist together with the tendons that bend the fingers, and the tunnel has no room to give. The nerve lies just under the skin across the front of the joint, and the lower end of the radius lies directly behind it, so a bone that tilts backward presses the nerve against the roof of the tunnel. That nerve carries sensation from the thumb, the index finger, the middle finger and the thumb side of the ring finger, and it carries the movement that turns the thumb across the palm. The ulnar nerve runs along the little finger side and supplies the little finger and the adjacent part of the ring finger, while a branch of the radial nerve supplies the back of the hand between the thumb and the index finger.

The commonest mechanism is a fall onto an outstretched hand, in which the weight of the body travels up the arm while the hand is fixed against the ground. The force bends the lower end of the radius backward, and the more it tilts, the more the shape of the wrist changes. A fall from a height, a road collision, a direct blow from a heavy object, and a fall in which the hand is trapped all injure the same region with more energy behind them, so the soft tissues suffer more. A fall onto the back of the hand, or a blow that drives the hand forward instead of backward, produces the opposite tilt, and the names used for the two shapes differ between countries and between editions of the same text. The mechanism tells the responder what else to suspect, because the same fall that broke the wrist may have injured the elbow, the shoulder, the neck or the head.

Most listeners have seen the shape called the dinner-fork deformity, named after the curve of a fork that lies on a table with its handle raised. The lower end of the radius tilts backward, the hand follows it, and the back of the wrist takes on a step that a hand can feel before the eye is certain. The responder does not need the name in order to act, because the shape is only a sign, and a wrist that looks almost straight may still be broken. What matters is that the deformity is seen, described in plain words and left exactly as it was found. Straightening it is not a field act, however tempting the shape looks, and the reason is the nerve and the vessels that lie against the front of the bone.

A wrist is never treated on its own, and the assessment begins with the scene and with the patient rather than with the arm. The primary survey comes first, and its steps are remembered as ABCDE, which stands for airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure. A patient who fell hard enough to break a wrist may also have a head injury, a chest injury or bleeding elsewhere, and a patient who is confused after a fall cannot be considered as a wrist alone. When two correct actions compete for the same pair of hands, the principle that decides the order is that life comes before limb, because a limb can wait minutes that a brain and a heart cannot. Only when the patient is stable enough does the examination turn to the wrist, and the wrist is then examined by looking, by feeling and by asking, never by forcing.

Assessment of the wrist itself begins with questions, and the answers shape everything that follows. The responder asks when the injury happened, how it happened, which hand the patient uses for writing and for work, and what the patient was doing in the moment before the fall. The age of the patient matters, because the bone of a child bends where the bone of an adult snaps, and the bone of an older adult may break with very little force. Illnesses and medicines that weaken bone belong in the history, as does an earlier injury or operation on the same wrist, since an old injury can make a new one harder to read. The responder asks whether the fingers can be moved, whether they feel normal, and whether the pain is spreading up the arm or into the hand. A patient who cannot remember the fall, or who was found on the floor with no account of it, is assessed for a medical cause of the fall as well as for the fracture.

The first look is taken before anything is touched, and it is compared with the other side. The responder notes the position of the hand, the shape of the wrist, the direction in which the palm and the thumb are turned, and whether the skin is broken. Swelling can hide a deformity and can also disguise how far the bone has moved, so a wrist that swells quickly is watched rather than squeezed. Skin that is stretched and shiny over a bone pressing from beneath is a warning that the cover may give way, and such a finding is reported at once even while the skin is still whole. A wound near the wrist makes the injury open until a clinician has shown otherwise, and even a small puncture is treated as a communication with the deep tissues.

Feeling and asking come next, and both are done gently. The examiner feels along the bones for tenderness and for a step in the surface, and compares the two wrists without pressing hard on the injured one. The hollow at the base of the thumb on the back of the hand is felt for tenderness, because the small bone inside it, called the scaphoid, is the bone most often missed when a wrist is injured, and it may not be visible on the first radiograph for some days. The examiner asks the patient to move the fingers and the thumb, and never pulls or pushes the wrist to test how stable it is, because that test belongs to a clinician and can convert a closed injury into an open one. Sensation is tested on the pads of the fingers of both hands, and the patient is asked whether the two sides feel the same.

Stabilization of the wrist is a small set of acts whose most important member is restraint. The limb is supported in the position in which it was found, with a pillow, a rolled blanket or the patient's own body, and it is never straightened in the field. An object embedded in the wrist is never removed, and bone that has come through the skin is never pushed back into the wound, because both may be holding pressure on a torn vessel and both carry contamination deeper. A wound is covered with a sterile dressing, the bleeding that can be controlled is controlled, and the wound is never explored blindly. The lay first aider does these acts with whatever is at hand and calls for help, while the trained responder works within the equipment and the procedures the service authorizes. Definitive cleaning and closure of an open injury belong to the operating room and to nobody else.

Stabilization also belongs to the whole patient, and it continues while the limb is being handled. The patient is kept warm, because cold is a complication of trauma rather than a detail of comfort, and wet clothing is replaced with covering while the ground beneath the patient is insulated. If the fall was from a height, or the patient struck the head or the neck, the spine is treated as unstable until somebody qualified to clear it has done so, and no lone manual handling technique is safe. Whether the patient may eat or drink before definitive care is decided by the receiving team, not by the responder and not by this course. The handover carries the time of the injury, the mechanism, the position in which the limb was found, and the neurovascular findings with the times at which they were taken.

The neurovascular examination is the step that must never be skipped, and it is performed before any intervention and again afterwards. The examiner looks at the color of the hand and fingers and at the warmth of the skin, and compares one side with the other. The pulse is felt at the wrist on the thumb side, and the examiner presses briefly on a fingernail and watches the color return, a test that is quick and that can be repeated without hurting the patient. Sensation is tested in each finger, with the median territory of the thumb, the index finger, the middle finger and the thumb side of the ring finger given particular attention, because that is the nerve this fracture threatens most often. The patient is asked to bend and straighten the fingers and to spread them, and any movement that has become weaker than before is recorded as a change. Pain is treated as a finding rather than as a complaint, and the patient is asked to describe it, because pain out of proportion to the injury is the earliest signal of a compartment that is filling.

The result of that examination is written before the limb is immobilized and written again after, and it is written so that another person can compare the two. A finding that was not recorded before a splint was applied cannot be attributed afterwards, and a numbness discovered after splinting will be blamed on the splint when it may have been present from the moment of the fall. The patient's own words are kept where possible, since a patient who says that the fingers feel like wood is describing a change that no measurement in this course can replace. A second examiner repeats the observations in the same order, and the value lies in the comparison rather than in any single finding. The record travels with the patient, and it is part of the treatment rather than a formality that follows it.

Some patients cannot give a reliable examination, and the responder must know that before trusting a reassuring answer. A young child may be too frightened to say what can be felt, an older adult who is confused may answer at random, and a patient who has been drinking or who has a head injury may not report numbness at all. In such a patient the examination is repeated at intervals, the limb is compared with the other side, and the threshold for immobilization and for referral is lowered rather than raised. A pulse that can be felt does not exclude serious damage to the vessels or to a compartment, and a pulse that cannot be felt is an emergency. Consider an illustration, and only an illustration: an older patient with a broken wrist who says the hand is fine, whose answers grow vague as the hours pass, and whose fingers are found to be cooler and duller than those of the other side is a patient who must be seen again and moved urgently. A sign that is absent in one patient is not a sign that is absent in the injury.

Immobilization means preventing movement of the injured part and of the joints above and below it, because a bone that is held alone can still be moved from either end. The material matters less than the principle, and a padded board, a folded magazine, a pillow or a sling can each serve, so long as it is padded where it presses on bone and secured firmly enough to hold without becoming a band. The lay first aider supports the limb and does not apply a formal splint, while the trained responder and the paramedic apply splints and slings within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes. The physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides what definitive immobilization is required, which may be a cast, a brace or an operation, and that decision is made with the images in front of them. Whatever is applied, the fingers are left visible and able to be examined, because the hand is the part that will show the complication first.

A splint that is too tight is the complication this course fears most, and it can happen with the best material and the kindest intention. The responder checks that the fingers have not become swollen, pale, cool or numb since the splint was applied, and that the skin at the edges of the material is not being rubbed or pinched. An improvised device shifts more than a manufactured one, so it is checked more often, and a sling that holds the arm too high or too tightly can press in the wrong place. If any of those findings appears, the responder does not adjust the limb or remove the device unless the local protocol authorizes it, and instead reports the change and gets the patient seen. Cold is part of the problem as well, so the hand is covered and not left exposed on a cold surface.

The neurovascular examination is repeated after the splint is applied, and this second examination has the same standing as the first. The examiner checks the color and the warmth of the fingers, the pulse, the return of color after pressure on a nail, the sensation in each finger and the ability to move the fingers. The two records are then compared, and any deterioration is reported as an emergency rather than as a detail. Numbness that appears only after a splint has been applied suggests that the splint is the cause, and the constricting material is released within the limits of the local protocol by somebody authorized to do it. The limb is not elevated above the level of the heart when a compartment syndrome is suspected, and the patient is not left waiting in a corridor for a radiograph while the hand is changing. The purpose of the examination is to notice the complication while it can still be reversed.

Imaging confirms what the examination has already suggested, and it is ordered by a clinician trained to interpret it. A radiograph is usually the first study, and it is taken in at least two directions so that displacement in the third direction is not hidden, with the joint above and the joint below included so that a second injury is not missed. The images of the wrist are read with particular attention to the joint surface of the radius, to the ulnar styloid, and to the small bones of the hand, including the scaphoid at the base of the thumb. That bone may look normal on the first radiograph and still be broken, which is why tenderness in the hollow at the base of the thumb is treated as a fracture until it is shown not to be, a practice whose details differ between services. Computed tomography shows the detail of a break that reaches into the joint, and magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for the soft tissues and for the bone that the radiograph cannot settle. The lay first aider never orders or interprets an image, and the trained responder does not decide which study is needed.

A second radiograph is often taken after a splint has been applied or after a reduction has been performed, because what matters is where the bone lies inside the device and not only how it looked before. The image is a picture of one moment, and the limb must still be examined after the picture has been taken, since a film cannot report pain, sensation or the tightness of a bandage. Some wrists are imaged again during the following days and weeks, because a break can move inside a cast as the swelling settles, and a break of the scaphoid may become visible only on a later study. The responder's part is to keep the limb supported, to keep the patient warm, and to pass on what has changed since the last observation.

Pain in a broken wrist is treated by a prescriber, and the responder must not be able to take a prescription from this course. The name of the substance, the amount, the route and the frequency belong to the local protocol and to the clinician who knows the patient, and no dose appears here on purpose. What the responder contributes is the finding that makes the decision urgent, which is pain out of proportion to the injury, pain that increases while the limb is at rest, or a patient whose pain is not controlled by the plan already in place. A nerve block at the wrist is a procedure for a clinician trained and authorized to perform it, and a reduction performed under sedation belongs to a clinician working with monitoring and help in a setting that can manage the airway. Whether tetanus protection is needed after a wound is a decision for the prescriber, who weighs the wound, the history of the patient and the local protocol, and the responder's part is to report the wound and the time it happened. Anything given by a responder is given only under a protocol that authorizes that responder to give it.

Reassessment after every intervention is the step that catches the complication caused by treatment, and in the wrist there are two complications to watch. The first is compression of the median nerve inside its tunnel, in which the numbness present at the start spreads or deepens, the thumb weakens, and the hand becomes clumsy. The second is a compartment syndrome of the forearm, in which the pressure inside a closed space of muscle rises, the pain becomes severe and out of proportion, the forearm is tense and swollen, and the fingers hurt when they are moved by the examiner. The diagnosis is clinical and belongs to a clinician, and time is muscle, so a responder who suspects either of them does not wait for confirmation that will arrive too late. The constricting bandage, splint or cast is released within the limits of the local protocol by somebody authorized to do it, the limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, and the patient is referred urgently. A limb that becomes worse after a splint has been applied is a limb whose splint must be examined again, not a limb to be tolerated.

Referral or definitive treatment is the final step, and it begins with the recognition that some wrists cannot be managed where the patient lies. A patient with an open injury, a damaged nerve or vessel, a suspected compartment syndrome, a break that reaches into the joint, a break in a child that crosses the growing part of the bone, a suspected break of the scaphoid, or a wrist that has dislocated as well as broken is referred to a facility with imaging, an operating room and an intensive care unit. The interval that makes an open injury urgent is measured in hours rather than days, and that figure is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because it differs between services and countries. Definitive treatment includes reduction, which restores the position of the bone ends, traction, which holds them in line with a gentle continuous pull, casting, which holds the position while the bone knits, and surgical fixation, in which a surgeon holds the fragments with metal devices in an operation called open reduction and internal fixation. Each of those acts is learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and the technique does not come from a book, this one included.

A reduction is not attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when the vessels or the nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, when the responder lacks the training or the equipment, or when an earlier attempt has failed. In each of those situations the attempt can turn an injury that would have healed into one that cannot, and the reason is that force applied to the wrong thing tears what is still intact. A responder who meets resistance stops, because resistance is information and force is not a technique. Traction is sometimes used in a fracture clinic as a temporary way of holding a wrist while other decisions are made, and it is applied there by people trained to do it, never in the field and never as a substitute for the transfer of the patient. The listener is not being taught to reduce a wrist, and no chapter in this course teaches reduction, traction or casting as a skill to be learned from a book.

After the wrist has been treated the work is not finished, and the hand must be observed as carefully as before. The cast or splint is checked for tightness and for pressure over the bony points, the neurovascular status is recorded again, and the patient is told in plain words what change should bring them back. Stiffness of the fingers is the outcome that costs the most in the long run, so the fingers are kept moving when the treating clinician allows it, and how soon and how much is decided by that clinician and by the therapist rather than by the responder. In a child the injury may cross the growing part of the bone, and healing there needs follow-up for a longer period than the fracture alone suggests, while in an older adult the same fall may be the first sign that the bone is weak, and that question belongs to the treating team. Every interval named in this chapter, including the weeks that a bone takes to knit, is an illustration that must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, since such numbers differ between services, countries and editions.

To summarize, the wrist is the part of the arm that people break most often, and the break is usually at the lower end of the radius, with the ulna and its styloid often involved at the same time because the two bones work as one unit. The median nerve crosses the front of the joint inside a narrow tunnel, so a backward tilt of the bone, swelling or a tight splint can take away the feeling in the thumb, the index finger, the middle finger and the thumb side of the ring finger, and the same nerve can be compressed into a clumsy, weak hand. The dinner-fork shape and the tenderness in the hollow at the base of the thumb are signs to be recognized and recorded, not corrected, and a limb is splinted in the position in which it is found and is never straightened in the field. The neurovascular status is examined before any intervention and after every one, with the color, the warmth, the pulse, the sensation in each finger and the movement of the fingers written down with the time, because a finding that was not recorded before a splint was applied cannot be attributed afterwards. An embedded object is never removed, exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, an open wound is never explored blindly, and definitive closure belongs to the operating room. Cold is a complication of trauma, the spine is treated as unstable until somebody qualified has cleared it, and pain, antibiotics and tetanus protection are decisions for the prescriber, with the substance and the amount left to the local protocol. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and a reduction is not attempted when the joint is shattered, when a fragment is trapped, when the nerves or vessels are failing, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, when the training or equipment is lacking, or when an earlier attempt has failed. This chapter is educational material, and it does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.
