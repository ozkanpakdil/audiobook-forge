# Chapter 37 — Talar and Midfoot Injuries

The talus and the midfoot are small structures that carry a large share of the weight of the body, and injuries to them are among the most easily missed in the whole skeleton. The talus passes the load of the body from the leg into the foot, and it is almost entirely covered with joint cartilage and has no muscle attached to it. The midfoot is the group of small bones and joints that holds the arch and passes force from the heel to the toes. When the talus breaks, the danger is that it loses the blood supply that keeps it alive, and when the midfoot is disrupted, the danger is that joints which must line up exactly heal out of line. Every chapter of this course follows one order, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. What follows is educational material, and it does not replace supervised training, certification, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

The talus sits like a keystone between the two bones of the lower leg above it and the calcaneus below. The body is the block that forms the ankle joint with the tibia and the fibula, the neck is the narrow bridge that turns forward and inward, and the head is the rounded end that meets the navicular bone. Almost the whole surface of the talus is covered with cartilage and no tendon or muscle attaches to it, so its nourishment arrives only through the soft tissue around it. Blood reaches it at a few small points, mostly through the neck and through a canal between the talus and the calcaneus, and from there it flows backward to fill the body of the bone. In most bones a break does not threaten the life of the bone, because the supply is rich, but in the talus it enters through the very part that most often breaks, so a fracture of the neck can leave the body with no blood at all.

Talar injuries are produced by force driven up through the foot rather than by a blow that arrives from the side. The ordinary causes are a fall from a height, a fall down stairs, a motor vehicle crash in which the foot is pressed against a pedal, and any landing in which the full weight of the body arrives on a planted foot. The position of the foot at the instant of impact decides which part of the bone fails. When the foot is forced upward toward the shin while the leg is fixed, the neck of the talus breaks, and that is the injury which carries the greatest risk to the blood supply. When the force arrives with the ankle held in a neutral position, the body of the talus may be driven between the two bones of the lower leg and split, while a small fracture of the lateral process on the outer side follows a combination of upward and inward twisting.

The difficulty of these injuries is that the foot often looks far better than it feels. Swelling fills the hollows around the ankle and the heel within a short time, and a swollen foot hides the bony landmarks an examiner would otherwise use. A fracture of the talar neck may produce no visible deformity until the broken ends move apart, and the patient may describe only a deep ache in the middle of the foot. A fracture of the lateral process is easily mistaken for a sprain of the ankle, because the tenderness lies on the outer side of the joint and the ligaments that are usually injured lie immediately beside it, so the examiner who finds tenderness there also presses on the talus itself. A fracture of the posterior process causes pain at the back of the ankle, and bruising that appears on the sole should raise the question of a deeper injury, because blood has traveled from a structure the examiner cannot see.

The midfoot is the region between the hindfoot, which is the talus and the calcaneus, and the forefoot, which is the metatarsals and the toes. It contains the navicular, the cuboid and three cuneiform bones, together with the joints among them and with the bones behind and in front, two of which let the foot twist as the heel strikes and lock the arch as the foot pushes off. The most important row of joints in the midfoot is the one where the midfoot meets the metatarsals, and its keystone is the base of the second metatarsal, which is set back into a notch between the cuneiform bones and held there by a strong ligament running from the medial cuneiform. That ligament is the last structure to give way when force drives the front of the foot away from the back, and when it tears the arch loses its shape. A displacement of a few millimeters that would be unimportant in the shaft of a long bone is a serious injury here.

Midfoot injuries are produced by the same axial load that breaks the talus, usually with a twist added, and the classic description is a foot that stays planted while the body rotates above it. That is what happens when a person steps into a hole, slips on a step, or lands awkwardly from a height. The energy may be very low, and that is why the injury is underestimated, because a person can tear the midfoot ligament by stepping off a curb and the injury may be treated as a sprain for weeks before the foot fails to recover. A direct blow, a heavy object dropped on the foot, and a crush in a doorway produce a different pattern, often with several bones broken and the soft tissues badly bruised. In a vehicle crash the foot is driven against the floor or the pedals, and the same energy may also fracture the ankle or the bones of the lower leg, so the whole limb is examined rather than the part the patient mentions.

The findings of a midfoot injury are few, and each of them can be dismissed by an examiner in a hurry. The patient is tender over a precise line across the middle of the foot, where the midfoot meets the metatarsals. Swelling appears quickly and may obscure the tender line, so the examination is repeated once the swelling has settled, and bruising may appear on the sole rather than on the top of the foot. The ability to walk a few steps does not exclude a serious injury to the midfoot. Widening of the foot compared with the other side, or a step felt with the finger along the line of the joints, suggests that the arch has separated, and pain at that same line when the front of the foot is twisted gently against the back is the most useful sign the examiner can produce. A foot that has been walked on for several days may present with nothing more than persistent swelling and a patient who says the foot does not feel right.

Assessment begins away from the foot and works toward it, because a foot injury is rarely the injury that kills. The scene is made safe, the mechanism is established, and the primary survey is completed in the order remembered as ABCDE, which stands for airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure. Only when the patient is stable enough does the assessment turn to the limb, and a foot that is bleeding heavily is controlled while the survey continues, because life comes before limb. The history includes the height of the fall, the surface struck, the position of the foot at impact, and the time, together with what makes this patient different, such as diabetes, loss of sensation in the feet, and medicines that thin the bone. A patient with a distracting injury or a reduced level of consciousness may never mention the foot, so the foot is examined in every patient who has fallen from a height. The lay first aider reports what was seen and when, the trained responder adds what was found, and the diagnosis belongs to the clinician.

The examination of the injured foot follows the same rule as the examination of every other part, which is to look, to feel, to ask the patient to move, and never to force. The examiner looks at the position of the foot, the shape of the arch, the swellings, the wounds and the color of the skin, then palpates in a fixed order from the heel to the toes, comparing each spot with the other foot. The patient is asked to curl the toes, to bend the ankle up and down, and to turn the foot inward and outward, and the examiner stops at the first movement that is lost or that sharply increases the pain. The examiner does not test the stability of the midfoot by twisting it and does not pull on the foot, because both are clinical skills learned under supervision and both can convert an injury that was stable into one that is not. A foot that cannot be examined properly because of pain is splinted and reassessed rather than examined through the pain.

The neurovascular examination is performed before any splint is applied and repeated after every intervention, and its result is recorded in words, because a finding that was not written before a splint went on cannot be attributed afterwards. The examiner feels for the pulse on the top of the foot and behind the inner ankle, and notes whether each is strong, weak or absent, remembering that in some healthy people one of those pulses cannot be felt at all, so an absent pulse alone is not proof of injury. The skin is checked for color, warmth and the time a pressed nail takes to refill, and sensation is tested over the web of skin between the first and second toes and along the borders of the foot. Movement is tested by asking the patient to curl the toes and to bend the ankle, and a movement the patient could perform a moment earlier and cannot perform now is recorded at once. Numbness, tingling, a cold and pale foot, and pain far worse than the injury explains are all findings that stop the examination and send the patient for urgent assessment.

Compartment syndrome of the foot is the complication this chapter fears most, and it is caused by the injury and sometimes worsened by the treatment. The foot is divided into several closed compartments by sheets of fibrous tissue, and bleeding or swelling inside one of them raises the pressure on the muscles, the nerves and the small vessels within it. The earliest and most reliable finding is pain out of proportion to the injury that becomes worse rather than better, particularly when the toes are gently stretched. A tense, shiny and swollen foot whose sensation is changing, and a foot whose pain does not respond to the measures the local protocol allows, are reasons to act, and a pulse that is still present does not exclude the condition. The diagnosis is clinical and is made by a clinician, and time is muscle, so the responder does not wait for a confirmation. The constricting bandage, splint or cast is released by someone authorized to do so within the local protocol, the foot is not elevated above the level of the heart, the patient is kept warm, and the patient is referred urgently.

Stabilization of the injured foot in the field is a small set of acts, and the most important of them is restraint. The foot is supported and splinted in the position in which it was found and is never straightened in the field, however deformed it looks, because vessels and nerves lie against the bones of the foot and a movement that looks helpful can tear what remained intact, turn a closed injury into an open one, or release the pressure that was holding a torn vessel closed. The lay first aider supports the foot with the hands, rests it on a pillow or a folded blanket, keeps the patient warm and waits for help. The trained responder and the paramedic may apply a splint within the equipment their service authorizes, and both of those levels protect the foot from further movement and from cold. Cold is a complication of trauma and not a detail of comfort, so the foot is insulated from the ground and the patient is covered. Nothing is given by mouth except on the decision of the receiving team, and any substance, route or amount belongs to the prescriber and to the local protocol.

Immobilization means preventing movement of the injured part and of the joints above and below it, because a bone that is splinted alone can still be moved from either end. For an injury of the talus or the midfoot this normally means a padded splint that supports the lower leg, the ankle and the whole foot in the position found, with the foot held at a right angle when that can be reached without force. The splint is padded where it presses on bone, it is applied over the position found, and it is secured firmly enough to hold without becoming a band that stops the circulation. The trained responder and the paramedic apply splints within the procedures their service authorizes, while the physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides what definitive immobilization is required, which may be a cast, a walking boot, a rigid shoe or an operation. Casting is learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and no chapter of this course teaches a listener to cast a limb, to reduce a fracture or to apply traction from a book, while a cast applied too tightly can cause the very compartment syndrome the splint was meant to prevent.

An open injury of the foot is a special emergency because the soft tissue covering the talus and the midfoot is thin, so bone that lies barely under the skin can be exposed by a modest wound. An open fracture is contaminated even when it looks clean, because the skin carries organisms that reach the bone and the deep tissues in the moment of injury. The part played by the responder is limited and precise, which is to cover the wound with a sterile dressing, control the bleeding that can be controlled, record the neurovascular status, immobilize the foot in the position found, keep the patient warm, and arrange urgent transfer, while the lay first aider performs the acts that need no equipment and the trained responder works within the procedures the service authorizes. An embedded object is never removed and exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, because the object may be holding pressure on a torn vessel and because bone that has come out carries contamination back in. Exploring an open wound blindly adds harm, and definitive cleaning and closure belong to the operating room and to nobody else. Antibiotics and tetanus protection are decided by the clinician and follow the local protocol, and the substance and the amount belong to the prescriber.

Imaging confirms what the examination has already suggested, and it is ordered by a clinician trained to interpret it. A radiograph is usually the first study, taken in at least two directions so that a displacement in the third direction is not hidden, and for a suspected injury of the talus or the midfoot the clinician usually needs pictures of the ankle and of the whole foot as well. Views taken while the patient stands and puts weight through the foot may reveal a separation that a picture taken lying down cannot show, but they are taken only when the clinician judges them safe. Computed tomography shows the detail of the talar body, of the talar processes and of the small joints of the midfoot, and it is the study that finds the injury when the first radiograph looks nearly normal while the foot is clearly injured. Magnetic resonance imaging is used for stress injuries of bone, for ligament damage, for cartilage and for the early detection of avascular necrosis. The lay first aider never orders or interprets imaging, and the trained responder does not decide which study is needed, while the clinician remembers that an image is a picture of one moment and that the patient must still be reassessed after it.

Reassessment is performed after every intervention, and it is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the record, because the comparison is what carries value. The foot is checked for color, warmth, pulse, sensation, movement and pain, and the splint is checked for tightness and for pressure over the bony points. Pain that increases instead of settling, pain out of proportion to the injury, numbness, tingling, weakness, and a foot that becomes tense and swollen are the findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation. Reassessment is also the moment to notice that the patient has become drowsy, pale, short of breath or cold, because a foot injury may be only one part of a larger set of injuries. A responder who suspects a compartment syndrome does not wait for the diagnosis to be confirmed and acts within the local protocol, so the limb is not elevated above the level of the heart while the patient is kept warm and referred urgently.

Referral or definitive treatment is the final step of the sequence, and injuries of the talus and the midfoot cannot be managed where the patient lies. A fracture of the talar neck or body, a displaced fracture of a talar process, and any injury that has separated the joints of the midfoot are referred to an orthopedic surgeon, almost always at a facility with computed tomography, an operating room and an intensive care unit, because the talus lives on a slender blood supply and the midfoot lives on precise alignment, and both are decided in the hours after the injury. A patient whose foot is clearly injured but whose first radiographs are reported as normal is referred for reassessment rather than sent home, and the lay first aider does not decide where a patient goes. Definitive treatment includes reduction, which restores the position of the bone ends, open reduction and internal fixation, which holds them with metal devices, external fixation, which holds the bone from outside the skin, and fusion of a joint, which removes a painful joint surface that cannot be saved. Every one of those acts is learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and none of them is taught from a page of this course.

A reduction of a talar or midfoot injury must not be attempted when the source lists a contraindication, and there is a reason behind every item on that list. It is not attempted when the injury is a suspected complex fracture with a dislocation, because the fragments and the joint surfaces are arranged in a way that only an operating room can rebuild, or when a fragment of bone is caught in the joint. It is not attempted when the vessels or the nerves are failing and need a specialist, because a second attempt can finish what the first injury began. It is not attempted when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, when the responder lacks the training or the equipment, or when an earlier attempt has already failed. In every one of those situations the attempt can turn an injury that would have healed into one that cannot, and force is never a technique. The same discipline applies to traction, which is a gentle continuous pull that holds bone ends in line and is used only where a qualified clinician directs it.

Several complications are common enough to be expected rather than surprising, and the patient is warned about them before leaving care. Avascular necrosis of the talus is the slow death of the bone that follows an injury to its blood supply, and it may declare itself only after weeks or months, a period that is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. Post-traumatic arthritis is the wearing away of a joint surface that healed out of line or was damaged at the moment of injury, and it is why a midfoot injury that seems minor is treated seriously. Malunion, which is healing in an unacceptable position, and nonunion, which is failure to heal at all, both change the shape of the foot and the way it takes weight, while infection may follow any open injury and appear after the patient has left the hospital. Stiffness and persistent swelling are expected to some degree and are managed by rehabilitation, and the findings that bring a patient back urgently are fever, a wound that becomes red or discharges, pain that increases after it had begun to settle, and a foot that becomes numb or cold.

Two groups of patients deserve a lower threshold for imaging, immobilization and referral. A child's foot contains growth regions near the ends of the bones and several centers of bone that have not yet joined, and an injury there may be invisible on a radiograph while it can still disturb growth, so a child who refuses to walk after an injury is treated with the same caution as a child with an obvious break. An older adult whose bone has lost density may break the talus or the midfoot in a fall from standing height, and the injury may be far less painful than its severity suggests. A patient with diabetes and loss of sensation in the feet may not feel the injury at all, and a foot that is warm, swollen and painless in such a patient is an emergency that needs a clinician rather than a new pair of shoes. The unconscious patient, the patient with a spinal injury and the patient with several injuries cannot report pain in the foot, so the foot is examined early and the finding is recorded before anything is applied to it.

The record and the handover are part of the treatment rather than a formality that follows it. The time of the injury, the mechanism, the position in which the foot was found, the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention, and the splint that was applied are written or spoken in a fixed order, so that the receiving team continues a story instead of starting a new one. An injury of the spine is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has done so, and no lone manual handling technique is safe, so a patient with a foot injury and a possible spinal injury is moved by a trained team using the precautions the local protocol requires. Everything described in this chapter is learned with a teacher and certified by an authority that defines what may be done without supervision, and where this chapter names a period or a threshold it names an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

To summarize, the talus carries the weight of the body into the foot on a blood supply that enters through its narrow neck, so a fracture there can kill the bone slowly, while the midfoot is a row of small joints that must line up exactly, so a displacement of a few millimeters is a serious injury. The findings to keep are tenderness over a precise line across the middle of the foot, bruising on the sole, a widening of the foot compared with the other side, and tenderness over the outer side of the talus that is worse than the ligaments beside it. These injuries are missed because the foot looks better than it feels, so the clinician is asked early for the imaging that shows the detail of the bone and the joints. The sequence is always the same, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status and record it before and after every intervention, to immobilize in the position found, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and the scope of practice is named for every act. A deformed foot is splinted as it lies and is never straightened in the field, an embedded object is never removed, exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, and definitive closure of an open wound belongs to the operating room. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, never from a book, and who to call is the orthopedic surgeon at a facility with imaging and an operating room. Cold is treated as a complication, the spine is treated as unstable until it is cleared by someone qualified, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.
