# Chapter 13 — Scapular Fractures

The scapula is the flat bone that lies over the back of the chest wall and gives the shoulder its sliding base, and a fracture of it is a message about force rather than about a broken bone. It has a ridge across its back called the spine, a hook-shaped projection at the front called the coracoid process, a roof called the acromion, and a shallow cup at its outer corner called the glenoid, which receives the head of the upper arm bone. Because the glenoid is part of the shoulder joint, a break that crosses it is an injury to a joint surface and deserves more caution than the same break in the flat body of the bone. Wrapped in muscle and cushioned by the rib cage, the shoulder blade breaks only when the energy arriving there has already passed through the body around it, so a patient may sit up and talk while a torn lung or a bleeding vessel decides whether that patient lives. This chapter therefore treats the scapular fracture first as a marker of injury elsewhere and only then as an injury of the shoulder itself.

Fractures of the ribs on the same side are common, and a rib broken in two places can leave a segment of the chest wall moving against the rest, which is called a flail chest. Air can escape from a torn lung into the space around it to produce a pneumothorax, and blood can collect there to produce a hemothorax, and either can enlarge until it presses on the lung and the heart. The lung itself may be bruised, the collar bone and the breast bone may be broken, and the neck, the back, and the head may have taken the same blow. Because the shoulder blade is protected in this way, its fracture is called a sentinel injury, and it is a reason to look harder at everything else. The first step is to look for these injuries, and only after that to examine the shoulder.

One pattern deserves its own place in the mind even though it is rare. When enormous force pulls the shoulder blade away from the chest wall, the muscles that bind the two together are torn, the nerves of the arm may be stretched with them, and the vessels that supply the arm may be injured at the same time. The shoulder on that side may appear displaced outward while the arm is pale, cool, and pulseless, and such an injury cannot be managed where the patient lies. Any suspicion of it is an emergency, and the patient is moved to a facility with imaging, an operating room, and a vascular surgeon.

Taking the history is part of the assessment, because the answer describes the energy and the energy predicts the injuries. The responder records the time, the height of the fall, the speed of the vehicle, and any loss of consciousness as they were told. A constructed illustration may help: a person thrown from a bicycle at speed and found with a painful shoulder after landing on one side has taken a force that could have reached the lung, the spleen, the neck, and the head. Nothing in that example is a real patient, and it is offered only to show how a mechanism is used.

Every chapter of this course follows one order, and the order protects the patient because each step decides what the next may safely be. The sequence is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. In a scapular fracture the assessment is dominated by the search for the chest injury that often accompanies it, and the shoulder is examined only when the patient is stable enough. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work, and it names the provider level for every act it describes.

Assessment begins at the scene with protection of the rescuer and the patient, and it continues with the primary survey whose steps are remembered as ABCDE, meaning airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure. The rule that governs the whole survey is that life comes before limb, because a limb can wait for minutes that a brain and a heart cannot. A patient speaking in full sentences has a protected airway for the moment, while a patient who is hoarse or drowsy does not, and the difference is acted upon by the trained responder within the procedures the service authorizes. The lay first aider recognizes the problem and calls for help, while the airway itself is managed only by someone trained and equipped to manage it.

Breathing is assessed by looking, listening, and feeling, and in this injury it is the step that most often changes the plan. The responder looks at the rate and effort of breathing, at whether both sides of the chest rise equally, and at the color of the lips. Listening over the chest with a stethoscope belongs to the trained clinician, but any responder can hear a wheeze or a silence where breath should be. Feeling the chest wall may reveal tenderness over a broken rib, a crackling sensation under the skin where air has escaped, or a segment that moves against the rest. Any of those findings is a reason to stop examining the shoulder and to arrange urgent transfer.

Circulation is judged by the pulse, the skin, and the level of consciousness rather than by any single number. A rapid, weak pulse, skin that is pale and cool, and a patient who is anxious or drowsy together suggest that blood is being lost somewhere, and the chest is one of the few places where a large volume can collect unseen. A volume offered in teaching is an illustration only and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. The trained responder reports what has changed since the previous set of observations, because a trend is worth more than an isolated reading.

Disability is assessed by the level of consciousness and by movement and feeling in each limb, and it is recorded before anything is given or splinted. Exposure means removing enough clothing to see the whole injured region while covering everything else, because cold is a complication of trauma. A patient who has fallen or been thrown is treated as having an unstable spine until someone qualified to clear it has done so, and no single manual handling technique is safe, so the move is made by a trained team using the precautions the local protocol requires. Personal protective equipment is worn before contact with blood or body fluid, and providing it belongs to the responding service. The survey is repeated whenever the patient changes, and a patient who deteriorates goes back to the beginning.

Only when the patient is stable does the assessment turn to the shoulder, and even then the history continues. The responder asks whether the shoulder has been injured before and whether the arm could be used at all at the moment of the injury. Medicines that thin the blood, medicines taken for years that weaken bone, and conditions that make bone fragile are recorded, because they change the threshold for imaging and for referral. Inspection comes before touch, with both shoulders uncovered so that the two sides can be compared, and it looks for swelling, bruising, a wound, and the position in which the arm is held. Bruising over the shoulder blade may not appear at once, so a shoulder that looks normal in the first hour can be marked by a broad stain a day or two later.

Palpation begins away from the area of greatest pain and moves toward it, following the bones that can be felt through the skin. The examiner feels the collar bone along its length, both of its joints, the ridge across the back of the shoulder blade, the bony point of the shoulder called the acromion, and the ribs beneath. Tenderness over the body of the scapula, a step in the bone, or a crackling sensation suggests a fracture, and each finding is described in words rather than given a diagnosis. Pressing hard, pulling on the arm, and forcing a painful joint through its range are not part of this examination, because they can turn a stable injury into an unstable one. Feeling for the pulse in the arm is always part of it.

Movement is tested by asking, never by forcing, and a finding taught as reliable is not always present. The patient is asked to lift the arm forward, out to the side, and to rotate it gently, reporting where pain begins rather than pushing through it. A patient who cannot move the arm at all may have a fracture, a dislocation, or a torn muscle, so the finding tells the examiner that imaging is needed rather than which of those it is. Swelling may be modest because the bone lies deep beneath thick muscle, and the arm may still move a little despite a break across a joint surface. Moving the arm for the patient is a clinical test learned under supervision, because doing it without training can displace a fracture or complete a tear.

The examination of the shoulder that must accompany a scapular fracture is the examination of the whole shoulder girdle, because the same force rarely respects the boundary of one bone. A break that crosses the glenoid cup allows the joint to become uneven, which the patient feels as a catch or a block when the arm is rotated. The joints at both ends of the collar bone and the collar bone itself are examined in the same pass, together with the upper part of the arm bone. A dislocation of the shoulder and a fracture of the scapula can occur together, and a squared-off shoulder with the arm held away from the body is a reason to stop and to immobilize.

The neurovascular examination is performed before any intervention and repeated after it, and its result is recorded in the words of the patient as far as possible. The reason is not administrative: a finding that was not recorded before a sling was applied cannot be attributed afterwards, and an injury present from the beginning may be blamed on the person who applied the support. The test covers the nerve supply, the blood supply, and the skin of the whole arm, and it belongs to the trained responder, the nurse, or the clinician. It notes the time, the side, the pulse, the color, the warmth, the sensation in each area, and the strength of each movement. It is repeated after every intervention and read aloud at every handover, because the comparison between two sets is what shows whether the injury is changing.

The nerves of the arm pass through the region of the shoulder in a tight bundle called the brachial plexus, which the scapula lies directly against. Sensation is tested on both sides and compared, in the outer shoulder, the outer forearm, the little finger, and the index finger, each served by a different nerve. Movement is tested by asking the patient to make a fist, to spread the fingers, to bend the wrist, and to lift the arm, and weakness is recorded as a change from what the other side can do. Numbness, tingling, a loss of grip, and a movement the patient could perform a moment earlier are findings of the same importance as a break seen on a radiograph. The test is repeated after the arm is supported, because the sling itself can press on a nerve.

The vessels are examined in the same pass, and the findings that matter are the pulse at the wrist, the color and warmth of the hand, and the time for color to return after a nail is pressed. The artery that supplies the arm passes close to the shoulder joint and can be torn by the same force that breaks the scapula. A pulse that is present does not exclude a serious injury to a vessel, because a pulse can survive a tear that is contained by the tissues around it, and a pulse that is absent is an emergency. An expanding swelling around the shoulder, a bruise that grows while the patient is watched, and a limb that becomes cool and pale are reasons to summon help at once. The lay first aider notices color, warmth, and movement and reports them, while the formal examination belongs to the trained responder or the clinician.

Stabilization comes next, and for a scapular fracture it means the ordinary acts that keep a patient alive and comfortable while the plan is made. The patient is kept warm, because cold worsens bleeding and shock, wet clothing is replaced with covering, and the patient is insulated from the ground. Bleeding that can be controlled is controlled with direct pressure through a dressing, and bleeding that cannot be controlled is a reason for urgent transfer. The arm is supported in the position in which it was found, with the hand of the patient, a pillow, a rolled blanket, or the body itself, and the support is adjusted until the patient reports less pain. A deformed limb is never straightened in the field, because the vessels and the nerves lie against the bone and a movement that looks helpful can tear what remained intact.

A wound over the shoulder or the chest wall changes the priority but not the principle. An open injury is contaminated from the moment it happens, and the part played by the responder is limited and precise: cover the wound with a sterile dressing, control the bleeding that can be controlled, record the neurovascular status, immobilize in the position found, keep the patient warm, and arrange urgent transfer. An embedded object is never removed, and exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, because the object may be holding pressure on a torn vessel and because pushing bone inward drives contamination deeper. The wound is never explored blindly with a finger or an instrument, and definitive cleaning and closure belong to the operating room and to nobody else.

Immobilization of a scapular fracture is achieved by supporting the arm, not by binding the chest. The usual device is a sling, which cradles the forearm and takes the weight of the arm from the injured shoulder, and a shoulder immobilizer adds a strap that holds the arm against the side of the body. The sling is applied over the position found, it is padded where it presses on the neck or on the point of the shoulder, and it is secured firmly enough to hold without stopping the circulation. The lay first aider trained in basic first aid may apply a simple sling, the trained responder and the paramedic apply the devices their service issues and authorizes, and the nurse or the clinician adjusts the device when the patient reaches care. The material matters far less than the principle, and a triangular cloth or a pillow held in place can serve.

The temptation to wrap a bandage around the chest in order to hold the arm still must be resisted, and the reason is that the same force that broke the scapula may also have broken ribs or bruised a lung. A bandage that encircles the chest limits the movement of the ribs and the depth of every breath, and in a patient with a chest injury it can turn a stable situation into a failing one within minutes. If the chest has been injured, the arm is supported with a sling alone, the strap across the body is left loose, and the patient may sit up if that is comfortable. The chest takes priority over the shoulder in every decision, because a shoulder can be treated tomorrow and a lung cannot wait. Anyone who applies a sling checks the fingers for color and warmth immediately afterwards and at intervals until the handover.

Pain is treated, and the decision about how belongs to the prescriber rather than to this course. The finding that makes the decision urgent is pain that is severe, rising instead of settling, or out of proportion to the injury seen, because that pattern may signal a compartment syndrome or a vessel injury. The responder reports the pain in the words of the patient, records the time of the report and any change, and offers no substance that has not been authorized for that patient by someone qualified to prescribe it. The name of the medicine, the amount, and the way it is given belong to the local protocol and to the prescriber, and a listener must not be able to take a prescription from this book. Nothing in this chapter describes a dose, a route, or a regimen, and none should be inferred from it.

Imaging is the step that confirms what the examination has already suggested, and it is ordered by a clinician trained to interpret it. The first study is usually a radiograph of the shoulder taken in more than one direction, because a break invisible in one view may be obvious in another, and the joint above and the joint below are included so that a second injury is not missed. A radiograph of the chest is commonly taken as well, since the injuries that accompany a scapular fracture are found in the chest more often than anywhere else. Computed tomography shows the detail of a break that crosses the glenoid or the neck of the scapula, and magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for soft tissues, including the nerves and the tendons of the rotator cuff. Neither the lay first aider nor the trained responder decides which study is needed, and any interval named in this chapter is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

Reassessment is performed after every intervention, and it is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the record, and the comparison is what has value rather than any single measurement. The arm is checked for color, warmth, pulse, sensation, movement, and pain, the sling is checked for tightness and for pressure on the neck, and the chest is checked for the rate and the effort of breathing. Some findings arrive late, since air can leak slowly from a torn lung and swelling can increase until the skin is tight, so the patient is watched at intervals. Pain that increases instead of settling, a hand that becomes cool or pale, numbing that was not there before, and a chest that becomes harder to fill are the findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation.

Compartment syndrome is rare around the shoulder, since the bone lies in a broad bed of muscle, but it can occur in the arm and the forearm, and the rule that governs it is the same everywhere. The pressure inside a closed compartment rises after bleeding or swelling, the flow through the smallest vessels fails, and the first structures to suffer are the muscle and then the nerve. The diagnosis is clinical and is made by a clinician, and the finding that raises the suspicion is pain out of proportion to the injury, worse when the muscles are stretched. The constricting bandage or sling is released within the limits of the local protocol by someone authorized to do it, the limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, and the patient is referred urgently, because time is muscle. A pulse that remains present does not exclude the diagnosis, and waiting for it to disappear is waiting too long.

Referral or definitive treatment is the final step, and for most scapular fractures the definitive treatment is not an operation. The bone is enveloped in muscle, the blood supply is generous, and the flat body of the scapula tolerates a break that would be unacceptable in a bone that bears weight, so the fracture is usually held in a sling until the pain settles and then moved gently under supervision. An operation is considered when the break involves the glenoid surface and leaves the joint uneven, when the neck of the scapula is displaced, or when the shoulder has been pulled away from the chest wall. The decision, the timing, and the technique belong to an orthopedic surgeon working in a facility with an operating room, and an operation that holds the fragments with metal devices is called open reduction and internal fixation. What the responder contributes is the safe transfer of the patient to that decision, with the record intact and the arm supported, because the shoulder can wait until the patient is safe.

Reduction, traction, and casting are named in this course only so that the listener understands what they are and who may perform them, and the technique itself belongs to supervised clinical training. Reduction restores the position of the bone ends, traction holds them in line with a gentle continuous pull, and a cast or a surgical fixation holds the position while the bone heals. Each is learned under supervision, practiced on models under a teacher, and performed according to local protocol by a clinician or an orthopedic surgeon qualified to do it. A reduction is not attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when the vessels or the nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, when the responder lacks the training or the equipment, or when an earlier attempt has failed, because the attempt can convert an injury that would have healed into one that cannot, and force is not a technique.

Two groups of patients deserve a lower threshold for imaging and for referral. The scapula of a child still contains a great deal of cartilage, so a break there may be difficult to see and may disturb growth, and a child who will not use an arm after a fall deserves the same caution as a child with an obvious deformity. An older adult whose bone has lost density may break a scapula with far less force than a younger person would need, and the fracture may hurt less than its appearance suggests, so the mechanism is recorded rather than the pain alone. In both groups a sign treated as reliable may be missing, so the threshold for immobilization, imaging, and referral is lower, while the decisions stay with the clinician.

Complications after a scapular fracture are uncommon, but they are not imaginary. A break that crosses the glenoid can leave the shoulder joint uneven and lead to pain and stiffness months later, and the muscles that move the shoulder can lose their attachment, so a shoulder that has healed may still not lift the arm fully. A nerve of the brachial plexus can be stretched by the same force and recover slowly, or not at all, and a patient who notices numbness or weakness weeks afterwards is referred back for assessment. Stiffness of the shoulder, sometimes called a frozen shoulder, is the commonest late problem after any shoulder injury, which is one reason the clinician begins gentle movement early. Rehabilitation begins when the fracture is stable enough to tolerate it, its pace is set by the clinician, the physical therapist directs the program, and progress is measured by what the patient could not do last week and can do this week.

What each provider may do is decided by training, by authorization, and by local protocol. The lay first aider supports the arm with a sling or a pillow if trained to do so, keeps the patient warm, notices color and warmth in the hand, and calls for help. The trained responder and the paramedic perform the primary survey, apply the supports their service authorizes, record the neurovascular findings, and report what has changed. The nurse observes, reassesses, and reports within the procedures of the department, while the physician examines, orders imaging, and decides what treatment is needed, and the orthopedic surgeon decides whether an operation is required and performs it. Everything described here is learned with a teacher and certified by an authority that also defines what may be done without supervision, so a course heard in a car teaches the reasons and the limits but cannot confer a skill.

To summarize, a scapular fracture is a break in a bone protected by muscle and by the rib cage, so it usually means that a great deal of energy passed through the chest, the neck, and the arm, and it is treated first as a marker of injury elsewhere. The assessment begins with the scene and the primary survey, in which airway, breathing, circulation, disability, and exposure are addressed before the shoulder is touched, because a patient can talk while a lung is collapsing or blood is collecting in the chest. The shoulder is then examined in full, including the collar bone and both of its joints, and the neurovascular status of the whole arm is recorded before any intervention and repeated after it. The arm is immobilized in the position found, usually in a sling, and the chest is never wrapped, because a band around the ribs can stop a patient from breathing. Imaging is ordered by a clinician, and computed tomography is used when the glenoid or the neck of the scapula is involved. The patient is reassessed after every intervention, a constricting bandage or sling is released within the local protocol if a compartment syndrome is suspected, and the limb is not elevated above the level of the heart. Any suspicion of a chest injury, a torn vessel, a torn nerve, or a shoulder pulled away from the chest wall is a reason for urgent referral to a facility with imaging, an operating room, and an intensive care unit. Reduction, traction, and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.
