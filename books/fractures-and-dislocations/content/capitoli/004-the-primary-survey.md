# Chapter 4 — The Primary Survey

The primary survey is the part of the assessment that happens before anyone has decided what is broken, and it exists because the injuries that kill in the first minutes are rarely the ones that look worst. It searches for the conditions that will end a life within minutes if they are not found, and it searches in a fixed order, so that a frightened or tired responder still does the right thing first. The whole of this course follows one sequence, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and the primary survey is the beginning of that sequence rather than a subject beside it. Each step buys the time in which the next can be performed at all, and that is why the order is not a tradition. This chapter is educational material, written for health professionals, students and trained responders, and it does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

The rule to carry away is that the limb never comes before the airway, and the reason is that different tissues tolerate a lack of oxygen for very different lengths of time. A brain starved of oxygen begins to suffer within a few minutes, so a patient whose breathing is obstructed, or whose chest is filling with blood, may be dead before a splint has been padded. A patient bleeding from a torn vessel can lose the volume that keeps the heart working in a similar span of time, and skin that is cold, pale and clammy says that this is already happening. The limb is the injury that is most visible and least immediately fatal, which is exactly why it captures attention that belongs somewhere else, and a deformed leg is frightening to look at. The order is fixed for that reason, and not because the limb does not matter.

Every act described here is described together with the provider level that may perform it, because a course that teaches the act and forgets the authority gets someone into trouble. The lay first aider performs the acts that need no equipment and no authorization, which are calling for help, keeping the head and the neck still when there is reason to fear a spinal injury, pressing on a wound that is bleeding, keeping the patient warm, and staying with the patient. The trained first responder and the emergency medical technician work within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, which usually include positioning the airway, suction, oxygen, ventilation with a bag and a mask, splinting, and the control of external bleeding. The paramedic and the nurse may perform the interventions their legislation, their training and their employer's protocol permit, and those permissions differ between countries and between services, so the listener must learn the list that applies where the listener works. The physician and the orthopedic surgeon decide the questions that medicine and surgery must answer, including whether an airway must be taken over, whether a chest must be released, and how an injury will be treated definitively.

The survey does not begin with the patient, because a responder who becomes a second casualty helps nobody. The scene is checked for traffic, fire, electricity, unstable ground, an animal and violence, and personal protective equipment is put on before the patient is touched, because blood and other body fluids carry infection. The mechanism of injury is noted, since it tells the responder what to expect, and the number of patients is counted, because a single responder facing several casualties must call for more help before beginning care. The time of the injury and the position in which the patient was found belong to the history as well, and they are spoken aloud at the handover rather than carried in the memory, because a time that is remembered is a time that drifts.

The first step is the airway, and the question is whether air is moving in and out of the patient. A patient who answers a question in a normal voice has an airway that is open at that moment, and that observation is the most useful one available, though it must be repeated, because an airway can close after it has been open. The findings that say the airway is threatened are a voice that has become hoarse or gurgling, a snoring sound in an unconscious patient, blood, vomit or a lost tooth in the mouth, swelling of the tongue or the throat, and a burn of the face with soot around the nose. A patient who is not breathing, or who breathes only with a noise that can be heard without a stethoscope, is treated before the survey moves on, because every later step depends on this one. The lay first aider opens the mouth, removes only what can be seen and lifted out with a finger, and calls for help, while the trained responder may position the head, use suction and place an airway adjunct within the procedures the service authorizes. Only a clinician trained and authorized to do so takes over the airway definitively, and forcing a device into a mouth or sweeping a finger blindly down a throat adds injury rather than removing it.

The second step is breathing, and it is examined before the chest is examined for broken bones, because the question is whether the lungs are filling and whether the two sides are doing equal work. The responder looks at the rate, the depth and the effort of breathing, at whether the chest rises on both sides, and at the color of the lips and the skin, and listens for air escaping from a wound of the chest. A rate faster than 30 breaths each minute in an adult, or slower than 8, must be acted on, and those figures are illustrations to be confirmed against the current guideline and the protocol of the service, because thresholds differ between countries and editions. A part of the chest wall that moves inward while the rest moves outward is called a flail segment, and a wound that makes a sucking sound with each breath is a finding to be reported at once, because both point to injuries only a hospital can manage. Where the service authorizes it the trained responder assists the breathing with a bag and a mask and oxygen, the paramedic may perform the procedures for a chest that has filled with air or blood under the standing orders of the service, and releasing trapped air with a needle or a finger is reserved for a clinician or for a paramedic trained, equipped and authorized to do it, which this course does not teach.

The third step is circulation, and its first purpose is to find and stop the blood that is leaving the body where a hand can reach it. The responder looks at the skin for pallor, feels it for warmth and moisture, and presses briefly on a nail bed to watch how quickly the color returns. A weak, rapid pulse at the wrist or in the neck, a patient who has become confused or restless, and skin that is cold and sweaty are the signs that the circulation is failing, and they carry more meaning together than any one of them alone. Bleeding that can be controlled is controlled by direct pressure with a dressing or a clean cloth, and the pressure is held rather than lifted to look underneath, because looking restarts the bleeding. Where bleeding cannot be controlled by pressure and the service permits it, the trained responder or the paramedic may apply a tourniquet according to the protocol of the service, and the time it was applied is written down where it can be seen. Bleeding inside the chest, the abdomen, the pelvis or the thigh cannot be seen, and it is treated by keeping the patient warm, by moving the patient quickly to a place where surgery is possible, and by the interventions the local protocol reserves to a physician or to a paramedic.

A pelvis that is unstable can bleed into the space it encloses without any wound on the skin, so a patient with pain in the pelvis after a fall from a height or a collision at speed is handled gently and is not rolled or pressed upon to test the joints. A pelvic binder, where the service provides one, is applied by the trained responder or the paramedic according to the protocol of the service, and it buys time rather than treating the injury. A broken thigh bone bleeds into the thigh, so a thigh that is swollen and firm tells the responder that a large volume of blood may have left the circulation even though the skin is unbroken. Keeping the patient warm is part of the treatment of shock rather than a courtesy, because cold worsens bleeding and makes the heart work harder, so wet clothing is replaced with dry covering and the patient is insulated from the ground. The quantity of blood that such an injury can hold is taught as a figure, and any such figure is an illustration to be checked against the guideline and the protocol in use where you work, while fluids and blood are given only by those authorized to give them and according to local protocol.

The fourth step is disability, which in the first minutes means the state of the brain and the spinal cord rather than the state of the limb. The responder speaks to the patient and notes whether the answer is appropriate or the patient is drowsy, confused or unresponsive, and compares the two pupils, because a difference between them is a finding to be passed on immediately. The blood sugar is measured by a responder trained and equipped to measure it, since a low blood sugar can mimic an injury of the head and can be corrected by those authorized to correct it, while the substance and the amount belong to the prescriber and to the local protocol. The neck and the back are treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear them has done so, and no lone manual handling technique is safe, so a patient with a suspected injury of the spine is moved by a trained team using the precautions the local protocol requires. A patient whose level of consciousness has fallen since the last check is getting worse, and that change stops the survey and sends the responder back to the airway, while the lay first aider reports the change and keeps the head and the neck still.

The fifth step is exposure, which has two halves that pull in opposite directions. The patient is undressed enough that the whole body can be looked at, because the injury that matters may be hidden under clothing at the back, in the armpit, in the groin or between the buttocks, and a wound that is not seen is a wound that is not treated. At the same time the patient is protected from cold, because the loss of body heat is a complication of trauma rather than a detail of comfort, and it worsens bleeding and confuses the assessment. A patient who has been lying on a cold road or a wet floor loses heat quickly, so covering is applied as soon as each part has been examined and the removed clothing is put back or replaced.

Throughout the survey the rule is that a problem found at any step is treated before the next step begins, and the survey is then started again from the beginning, because an intervention can change a finding that was normal a moment earlier. The findings that stop the survey and demand treatment are an airway that is not open, breathing that cannot be supported, bleeding that will not stop with pressure, a pulse that cannot be felt in an unresponsive patient, a fall in the level of consciousness, and a new deformity or a change in the shape of the chest. A responder who is alone and cannot both treat and continue must treat what kills first and call for help early, while a service with more than one responder divides the tasks rather than queueing them one behind the other. The lay first aider stops at the limit of what can be done without equipment and without training and calls for the level that can go further, and the trained responder stops at the limit of the equipment, the procedures and the authorization the service provides. The principle behind all these limits is that the responder should do what is within reach and should not improvise what is beyond it.

Only when the survey has been completed, and any problem it found has been treated, does attention turn to the limb, and the patient with the obviously deformed leg is the patient for whom this rule was written. Such a leg is supported in the position in which it was found and is not straightened, not rotated, not pulled and not tested for stability, because a movement that looks like tidying can convert a closed fracture into an open one and can tear a vessel or a nerve that was still intact. The reason is anatomical, since vessels and nerves run along the surface of the bone rather than through the middle of the limb, so they lie exactly where the displaced end of the bone is pointing. The limb is examined by looking for swelling, bruising, wounds, deformity and shortening and by comparing it with the other side, while feeling for tenderness is done gently and only to the extent the patient tolerates. The lay first aider supports the limb with both hands, with a pillow or with whatever is at hand, keeps the patient warm, and does not move the limb again until it has been splinted or until help arrives.

Before the limb is splinted, the neurovascular status is examined and written down, and this step is never skipped and never assumed, because a finding that was not recorded before an intervention cannot be attributed afterwards. The examiner looks at the color and the temperature of the skin beyond the injury, compares the two limbs, feels for a pulse below the injury, presses briefly on a nail to watch the color return, and asks the patient to move the fingers or the toes. Sensation is tested by touching the skin lightly on both sides, and any numbness, tingling, weakness or loss of a movement the patient could perform a moment earlier is recorded in the words the patient uses. The findings that suggest damage to a vessel are pallor, coolness, a weaker pulse and pain out of proportion to the injury, while the findings that suggest damage to a nerve are numbness, tingling and weakness. A pulse that is present does not exclude serious injury, because the pressure inside a compartment can rise until the smallest vessels have stopped carrying blood while a large pulse at the wrist or the ankle is still felt, and an absent pulse is an emergency. The lay first aider observes the color, the warmth and the ability of the patient to move and to feel and reports what has changed, while the formal examination belongs to the trained responder or the clinician, and its result is spoken at the handover as well as written.

Immobilization means preventing movement of the injured part and of the joint above it and the joint below it, because a bone that is held alone can still be moved by the joints at either end. The limb is splinted in the position in which it was found, the splint is padded where it presses on bone, and it is secured firmly enough to hold without becoming a band that stops the circulation. The material matters less than the principle, since a folded magazine, a padded board, a rolled blanket, a sling or the body of the patient can each serve, and an improvised device is checked more often than a manufactured one because it shifts. The trained responder and the paramedic apply splints within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, and the nurse applies and checks those the ward or the emergency department authorizes. Casting, traction and reduction are described in this course only in terms of what they are for, what they must achieve and what can go wrong, because the techniques are learned under supervision on real limbs and performed according to local protocol, and none of them is taught from a book. If the limb becomes more painful, more swollen or more pale after the splint is applied, the responder stops and seeks advice, because the splint that was meant to protect the limb has become the thing that threatens it.

Reassessment is performed after every intervention, and it is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment rather than by the injury. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the record, and it is the comparison that carries the information rather than any single measurement. The limb is checked for color, warmth, pulse, sensation, movement and pain, the splint is checked for tightness, and a dressing over a wound is checked for blood that has come through it. Pain that increases instead of settling, pain that is out of proportion to the injury, numbness, tingling, weakness, and a limb that becomes tense, swollen and hard are the findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation. A responder who suspects that the pressure inside a compartment has risen does not wait for a confirmation that will arrive too late, because the diagnosis is clinical and time is muscle, and the constricting bandage, splint or cast is released only by someone the local protocol authorizes to release it, with the limb kept below the level of the heart while the patient is kept warm and referred urgently.

Imaging is the step that confirms what the examination has already suggested, and it is ordered by a clinician trained to interpret it and to act on the result. A radiograph is usually the first study, and it is taken in at least two directions so that a displacement in the third direction is not hidden, while the joint above and the joint below the injury are included so that a second injury is not missed. Computed tomography shows the detail of a complex break, particularly where it enters a joint, and magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for the soft tissues, including ligaments, cartilage, tendons and muscle. An image is requested when the answer it will give changes what is done next, and the lay first aider never orders or interprets imaging while the trained responder does not decide which study is needed.

Definitive treatment begins with the recognition that some injuries cannot be managed where the patient lies, so the last step of the sequence is to refer or to treat according to what the injury is and who is present to treat it. A patient with an open fracture, a suspected compartment syndrome, a dislocation, a damaged nerve or vessel, a fracture that enters a joint, a fracture of the pelvis, or a fracture in a child that crosses the growing part of the bone is referred to a facility with imaging, an operating room and an intensive care unit. Reduction, which restores the position of the bone ends, traction, which holds them in line with a gentle continuous pull, and casting or surgical fixation, which holds the position while the bone heals, are performed by those qualified to perform them and according to local protocol. A reduction is not attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when the vessels or the nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, when the training or the equipment is lacking, or when an earlier attempt has failed, because in each of those situations an attempt can convert an injury that would have healed into one that cannot. The physician decides what must be done in the emergency department, the orthopedic surgeon decides what must be done in the operating room, and the nurse keeps the record, the observations and the handover in order.

Some patients are harder to survey than others, and the same sequence is used with a lower threshold for calling for help. A child who is injured may be frightened and silent, and a child who is alert and then becomes quiet is getting worse rather than better, so the change matters more than any single number. An older adult may break a bone in a fall from standing height with little pain, and one who takes a medicine that thins the blood may continue to bleed from a place that looks trivial, so the threshold for imaging and for admission is lower. A patient who is pregnant carries two patients to consider, and the position in which she is placed and moved follows the local protocol for her stage of pregnancy. A patient who cannot answer questions because of an injury of the head, a language barrier, deafness, intoxication or sedation is examined as though an injury is present rather than absent, and a sign treated as always present will be believed and will eventually be wrong.

A short constructed illustration may help to fix the order of the survey in the mind, and it is an illustration rather than the record of any real patient. A responder arrives at a road and finds a person lying beside a fallen motorcycle with an obviously deformed thigh, the foot turned outward and the leg shorter than the other, and the patient is calling out in pain. The responder does not begin at the leg, because the patient answers a question, so the airway is open for the moment, and then the chest is watched while both sides are seen to move and the breathing is counted. The skin is cool and clammy and the pulse at the wrist is rapid and weak, so the circulation is failing, and the leg is only one of the places the blood may be going, along with the pelvis, the abdomen and the chest. Help is called for early, the bleeding that can be reached is pressed upon, the patient is covered to keep the warmth in, and only then is the leg supported exactly as it lies and splinted by the trained responder with the equipment the service provides. The neurovascular status of the foot is written down before the splint and again after it, and nothing was straightened, pulled or explored at any point.

The record and the handover are part of the treatment rather than a formality that follows it, and a chapter about a survey is mostly a chapter about what is noticed and what is passed on. The findings of the neurovascular examination are the ones most often lost, because they are written in a hurry by a responder who is about to move the patient, and they are precisely the findings that decide, hours later, whether a limb was already damaged before a splint was applied. Times are written as times rather than as intervals remembered afterwards, and the condition of the patient is described as it was found rather than as it was assumed to be. Where this course names a time, a distance or a threshold, that number is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between services, countries and editions.

To summarize, the primary survey looks for what kills in the first minutes, and it looks in a fixed order, which is the airway, then breathing, then circulation, then disability, then exposure, and the limb never comes before the airway, however deformed the limb may be. A problem found at any step is treated before the next step begins, and the survey is then started again from the beginning, because an intervention can change a finding that was normal a moment before. The neurovascular status of the limb is examined before it is splinted and again afterwards, and it is written down, because a finding that was not recorded before an intervention cannot be attributed afterwards. A deformed limb is supported and splinted in the position in which it was found and is never straightened in the field, an embedded object is never removed, exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, and an open wound is never explored blindly. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, a reduction is not attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when the vessels or the nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, when the training or the equipment is lacking, or when an earlier attempt has failed, and force is never a technique. The provider level is named for every act, no drug, dose, route or frequency is given anywhere in this course, cold is treated as a complication of trauma rather than a detail of comfort, and the spine is treated as unstable until it is cleared by someone qualified to clear it. Every number heard here is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and your local protocol, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.
