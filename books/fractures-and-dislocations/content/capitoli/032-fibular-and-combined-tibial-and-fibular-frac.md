# Chapter 32 — Fibular and Combined Tibial and Fibular Fractures

The fibula is the slender bone that runs down the outer side of the lower leg, and it carries so little of the weight of the body that it could be taken away without destroying the ability to walk. It is a strut rather than a column, and yet it holds two things that matter far more than weight bearing: the outer side of the ankle joint, which it forms at its lower end, and the nerve that winds around its upper end on its way to the muscles that lift the foot. This chapter follows the order used throughout this course, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and it keeps to the fibula and to the injury in which both bones of the lower leg are broken. What follows is educational material, and it does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

The fibula begins just below the knee at a small rounded head, narrows into a neck, and runs down as a shaft that is thin in its middle and widens again into the lateral malleolus, the bump on the outside of the ankle. It is joined to the tibia along almost its whole length by the interosseous membrane, a strong sheet of fibrous tissue, and again at both ends by ligaments that allow very little movement. The tibia, the fibula, the membrane between them and the two joints at their ends together form a ring, and a ring broken in one place is usually broken or bent in another. That idea explains these injuries better than any list of types, because a break anywhere in the ring directs the examiner to look at the rest of it. A tender ankle in a patient with a fracture higher up the fibula is not a coincidence but the second part of the same injury.

The common peroneal nerve, also called the common fibular nerve, gives the fibula its reputation, because it leaves the back of the knee, wraps around the neck of the fibula just beneath the skin, and then divides to supply the muscles that lift the toes and turn the foot outward. Around the neck of the fibula it lies against bone with very little muscle to shield it, so a direct blow, a tight bandage, a splint that presses there, or the displaced end of a broken bone can injure it. The findings are a foot that cannot be lifted, a slap of the foot on the ground when the patient tries to walk, numbness over the outer side of the lower leg and the top of the foot, and a loss of the ability to turn the foot outward against resistance. A nerve already injured before the splint was applied must not be blamed on the splint, so the examination is done before any intervention and again afterwards, and a change is treated as an emergency.

The mechanism decides how much energy passed through the leg, and the energy decides how much of the soft tissue has been damaged. A direct blow to the outer side of the leg, as from a bumper, a falling object or a boot, breaks the fibula at the point of impact and bruises the muscle beneath the skin. A twisting force on a planted foot, common in ball games and in a fall on stairs, produces a spiral break that may run a long way along the bone. A fall from a height or a road traffic crash drives the force along the leg and may break both bones into several pieces and tear the membrane between them. The same words describe the injury in a young athlete and in an older adult, but the energy required is very different, and so is the meaning of the same crack.

When only the fibula is broken, the leg usually still takes weight, because the tibia continues to carry the body, and the patient may walk in with a sore outer leg and nothing more. That is exactly why the injury is dangerous to dismiss, since the fracture itself is rarely the whole story. The examiner looks for tenderness along the whole fibula, at the head and neck beside the knee, and at the ankle, and compares the two sides before deciding that the injury is confined to the shaft. Tenderness at the front of the ankle, between the two bones, suggests that the fibrous joint binding them has been torn and that the ankle mortise, the socket that holds the ankle bone, has lost its grip. An isolated fracture with a quiet knee, a quiet ankle and a normal neurovascular examination may be a modest injury, but the decision about how to treat it belongs to a clinician and not to the responder who first touches it.

A fracture of the fibula above the ankle may be the least painful part of an injury whose true damage lies at the ankle, because the force that tears the membrane between the bones has to escape somewhere. The ankle swells, the patient cannot bear weight, and the tenderness sits over the ligaments of the outer ankle rather than over the broken bone. In the same way, a painful ankle after a twisting injury may conceal a fracture of the fibula higher up, so the whole length of the bone is examined in both directions. This is why the joint above and the joint below are assessed whatever the patient points to first, and a responder who examines only where the patient complains will miss the injury that decides the outcome.

When both bones are broken, the leg loses both of its struts, and the injury becomes a different problem in kind rather than in degree. The membrane between the bones may be torn over a long distance, so the two fragments are no longer tied to one another, and the broken ends can slide, overlap, tilt and twist. The leg can shorten, the foot can turn outward, and the whole limb can move as a unit above and below the break, which is what the examiner means by an unstable leg. An unstable leg is immobilized in the position found before it is moved anywhere, because every movement of the limb moves the bone ends with it, and a bone end that moves can tear a vessel, stretch a nerve, cut the skin from within and turn a closed fracture into an open one. The responder does not test how unstable the leg is, because the test itself does the harm.

Assessment begins with the scene and with the story, and the story is taken while the limb is being supported rather than examined. The responder asks about the mechanism, the time of the injury, whether the patient could stand afterwards, whether the skin was broken and by what, and whether the foot felt normal at the moment the leg gave way. The medical history matters as much as the accident, because a bone thinned by age, by disease or by long use of a medicine that weakens it may break in a fall that would leave a healthy person unhurt. A patient whose sensation is already reduced by diabetes may feel very little, and medicines that make the blood slower to clot make bleeding into the leg more likely. Tetanus protection is a decision for the clinician who knows the local schedule and the patient's history, and the part of the responder is to ask and to report, never to decide.

Looking is the first act of the examination and it is done with the limb still supported, comparing one side with the other. The responder notes the shape of the leg, the direction in which the foot points, whether the limb looks shorter, and where the swelling and the bruising have appeared. A foot that has turned outward while the patient lies still is a warning that the leg is rotating at the fracture, and it is never corrected by hand in the field. The skin is inspected from the knee to the toes, including the back of the calf and the heel, because a small wound at the level of the break may be the only sign that the fracture is open. Any wound is covered with a sterile dressing, an embedded object is left exactly where it is, and exposed bone is never pushed back into the limb.

Feeling the leg follows looking, and it is done gently, from the head of the fibula beside the knee down to the lateral malleolus at the ankle, and then around the ankle and along the front ridge of the tibia. The examiner is looking for the place that is tender, for a step in the bone, and for the joint that is swollen, while remembering that a patient in shock or distracted by pain may report very little. Crepitus, the crackling of broken ends rubbing together, is not deliberately sought, because seeking it moves the fracture and adds pain without information. Movement is asked for rather than imposed, so the patient is invited to move the toes and to lift the foot if it is comfortable, and a movement that is refused is information too. The limb is never straightened and never used as a lever to see what happens, and testing the stability of the knee, of the ankle or of the bones themselves is a clinical skill learned under supervision and is not a field test. Palpation and the request to move belong to a trained responder, a nurse or a clinician, within the limits of the local protocol.

The neurovascular examination is never skipped and never assumed, and it is performed before any intervention and repeated after every one. The examiner looks at the color and the temperature of the foot, presses briefly on a nail to watch the color return, and feels the pulse on the top of the foot and behind the inner ankle. Sensation is tested on both legs and compared, including the outer side of the lower leg, the top of the foot and the sole, and the patient is asked to lift the foot and to turn it outward. The result is written before the splint is applied and after it is applied, because a finding that was not recorded before an intervention cannot be attributed afterwards. A formal neurovascular examination belongs to the trained responder, the nurse or the physician, while the lay first aider reports what the patient says about feeling and movement. Any change after an intervention stops the responder and prompts escalation.

Some of these signs are less reliable than they look, and a listener who trusts one of them alone will be misled. A pulse that remains palpable does not exclude a compartment syndrome, because the pressure inside a compartment can stop the flow through the smallest vessels long before it stops the flow through the main artery. A patient whose nerves are already damaged by diabetes or by alcohol may have little pain and may not notice numbness that is new, so the injury is found late. Swelling may be modest in a leg that is bleeding into a deep compartment behind the bone, and a foot drop may be present from the moment of the injury rather than appearing afterwards. The response to that uncertainty is not a different examination but a lower threshold for immobilization, for repeated reassessment and for urgent referral.

Stabilization means keeping the patient still, keeping the limb in the position in which it was found, controlling the bleeding that can be controlled, and keeping the patient warm. A deformed leg is supported with the hands, with a pillow, with a rolled blanket or with the uninjured leg, and it is never straightened in the field, because the vessels and the nerves lie against the bone and the pressure a displaced fragment is applying to a torn vessel may be the only thing holding the bleeding back. An open wound is covered with a sterile dressing and is never explored blindly, an embedded object is left in place, and definitive closure belongs to the operating room and to nobody else. Cold is a complication of trauma rather than a detail of comfort, so the patient is insulated from the ground and wet clothing is replaced with covering. The lay first aider performs the acts that need no equipment, and the trained first responder and the paramedic work within the procedures their service authorizes.

Immobilization means preventing movement of the injured part and of the joints above and below it, which for the lower leg are the knee and the ankle. A splint is padded where it presses on bone, it is applied over the position found, and it is secured firmly enough to hold without becoming a band that stops the circulation, and its purpose is to stop the bone ends from moving rather than to put them back where they belong. The material matters less than the principle, since a padded board, a folded blanket or the uninjured leg can each serve, and an improvised device is checked more often because it shifts. For the unstable leg in which both bones are broken, the limb is immobilized before it is moved at all, and it is then moved only as part of the whole patient, with one person supporting the leg while others move the body. The trained responder and the paramedic apply splints within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, the nurse works within the same limits, and the physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides what definitive immobilization is required. Casting, surgical fixation and every device that holds a reduced fracture are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and none of them is taught from a book in this course.

Moving a patient with an unstable leg is a team task rather than a solo skill, and no lone manual handling technique is safe. If the patient must be turned, the whole body is turned as a unit while the leg is supported through the fracture and across both joints, and a spine injury is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has done so. The limb is lifted along its whole length and never by the foot or by the knee alone, because the weight of the lower leg hanging from a broken shaft bends the fracture and pulls on the tissues around it. A patient who must wait is kept warm, kept still and watched, and the waiting time is used to repeat the neurovascular examination and to record what it shows. The number of responders and the method of movement are set by the procedures of the service and by the equipment available.

Traction is a gentle continuous pull that holds the bone ends in line, and devices that apply it exist for the thigh and, in some services, for the lower leg, but whether such a device is used, and by whom, belongs to the local protocol and to the equipment the service carries. A device of that kind is applied only by a responder trained and authorized to use it, it is never improvised around a fractured lower leg, and it is checked before and after application because a splint that pulls too hard can injure skin, vessels and nerves. Reduction, which restores the position of the bone ends, and casting, which holds the position while the bone heals, are acts learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and this course describes what they are meant to achieve and what can go wrong rather than how they are done. A reduction is not attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when the vessels or the nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, when the responder lacks training or equipment, or when an earlier attempt has failed. In each of those situations the attempt can convert an injury that would have healed into one that cannot, and force is not a technique.

Imaging confirms what the examination has already suggested, and it is ordered and interpreted by a clinician trained to do it. A radiograph is usually the first study, and it is taken in at least two directions so that a displacement in the third direction is not hidden, with the joint above and the joint below included so that a second injury is not missed. When the fibula is broken, the whole length of the bone is examined, because a fracture at one end is often accompanied by an injury at the other. Computed tomography shows the detail of a complex break and of the ankle mortise when an operation is being planned, and magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for the soft tissues, including the membrane between the bones, the ligaments and a suspected stress injury that an early radiograph does not show. Either study is requested when the answer it gives will change what is done, and the decision belongs to the clinician responsible for the care of the patient. The lay first aider never orders or interprets imaging, and the trained responder does not decide which study is needed.

Reassessment is performed after every intervention, and it is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the record, and the comparison is what has value rather than any single measurement. The limb is checked for color, warmth, pulse, sensation, movement and pain, the splint is checked for tightness, and a dressing over a wound is checked for bleeding that has soaked through. Pain that increases instead of settling, pain out of proportion to the injury, pain when the toes are stretched gently upward, a calf that becomes tense and swollen, numbness between the first and second toes and a foot that cannot be lifted are the findings that stop the responder. A responder who suspects a compartment syndrome does not wait for the diagnosis to be confirmed, releases a constricting bandage or splint only within the limits of the local protocol, keeps the limb at the level of the heart rather than above it, keeps the patient warm and refers urgently, because the diagnosis is clinical and time is muscle.

The fibula itself usually heals well, because it is wrapped in muscle over much of its length and has a generous blood supply, so an isolated fracture of the shaft often unites without difficulty. The tibia is a different matter, since its lower third lies just under the skin and depends on a supply that a high energy break can damage, and a fracture there may take months to unite or may fail to unite at all, with a period of 8 to 12 weeks offered in teaching as an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. Infection follows an open injury, which is why the wound is covered, the limb is immobilized and the patient reaches an operating room as soon as the service can arrange it. A nerve that was stretched may recover slowly over weeks to months, while one that was cut does not, and rehabilitation belongs to a physiotherapist working within a plan the treating clinician sets.

Referral or definitive treatment is the final step of the sequence, and it begins with the recognition that some injuries cannot be managed where the patient lies. A patient with a fracture of both bones, an open fracture, a suspected compartment syndrome, a fracture near the knee that has caused a foot drop, a fracture with a torn membrane at the ankle, a fracture that enters a joint, a fracture in a child that crosses the growing part of the bone, or a fracture in a limb with poor circulation is referred to a facility with imaging, an operating room and an intensive care unit. An isolated fracture of the fibular shaft with an intact tibia, a quiet knee, a quiet ankle and a normal neurovascular examination may be treated without an operation, but that judgment belongs to a clinician who has examined the patient and seen the images. The decision about how urgently a patient must travel is made by the clinician or by the service protocol, and the responder who is unsure treats the injury as urgent and says so in the handover. Nothing in the field is helped by waiting to see whether the leg gets better on its own.

Definitive treatment of these injuries takes several forms, and which of them is used depends on the bone that is broken, the state of the soft tissues and the needs of the patient. A fracture of the fibula alone may be treated with support, with a walking boot or with a cast, while a break of both bones is usually held with a metal plate and screws, with a rod passed down the canal of the tibia, or with a frame of pins and bars outside the limb when the soft tissues will not tolerate an operation. An operation that puts the fragments back into place and holds them with metal devices is called open reduction and internal fixation, and it is performed by an orthopedic surgeon in an operating room. Sometimes the surgeon repairs the fibula first to restore the length of the leg and then fixes the tibia, and sometimes the fibula is left alone because the tibia carries the weight. Each of those decisions is made with images, with an examination under anesthesia and with the judgment of a surgeon, and none of it is a field decision.

This chapter describes no dose, no route and no drug regimen, because the substance and the amount belong to the prescriber and to the local protocol. Pain is treated by the clinician who can assess it, and the finding that makes the decision urgent is pain that is severe, increasing, out of proportion to the injury, or accompanied by a limb that is tense, cold or numb. A responder at any level may make the patient more comfortable by supporting the limb, by keeping the patient warm and by explaining what is happening, and a trained responder may give an analgesic only under the standing orders and the authorization of the service. Antibiotics for an open injury and protection against tetanus are decisions for the clinician, and the part of the responder is to report the wound, the time of the injury and what is known of the patient's immunization. Sedation for any procedure belongs to a clinician working in a monitored setting, and a listener must not be able to take a prescription from this book.

Two groups of patients deserve a lower threshold for immobilization and for referral. A child's fibula has a growth region at each end, and an injury near the ankle that looks like a sprain may damage the growing part of the bone, so a child who will not use the leg, or who limps for days after a fall, is examined rather than watched. An older adult whose bone has lost density may break the fibula and the tibia in a fall from standing height, and the pain may be far less than the severity of the injury. An athlete may have a stress fracture of the fibula that hurts only with running and that an early radiograph does not show, so a persistent pain in the outer leg after training deserves assessment rather than another session. The record and the handover are part of the treatment, and the time of the injury, the mechanism, the position in which the limb was found and the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention are handed over in a fixed order. A leg that was unstable when it was splinted is described as unstable, so that nobody blames an intervention for a finding that came before it.

To summarize, the fibula carries little weight and much importance, because it forms the outer side of the ankle and because the nerve that lifts the foot winds around its neck just beneath the skin. A fracture of the fibula alone may be a modest injury, but the knee and the ankle are examined with it, since a break at one end of the bone often means an injury at the other. When both bones are broken the leg is unstable, and it is immobilized in the position found before it is moved anywhere. The order is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status before and after every intervention and to record it, to immobilize the joints above and below, to image when a clinician orders it, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. A deformed limb is never straightened in the field, an embedded object is never removed, exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, an open wound is never explored blindly, and cold is treated as a complication. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and a reduction is not attempted when the diagnosis is unclear, the injury is open, the vessels or the nerves are failing, the joint is shattered, a fragment is trapped, or the responder lacks training and equipment. Keep the patient warm, record every finding, and call for a team that can reach an operating room.
