# Chapter 47 — Recognizing the Open Fracture

An open fracture is a break in which the bone, or the deep tissue around it, has communicated with the outside world, so that the inside of the limb has become continuous with the environment. The wound may be a long laceration with bone visible in its depth, or a puncture no larger than the head of a pen, and the size of the wound says very little about the severity of the injury beneath it. What makes it different is what the tear has let in, which is organisms from the skin and the ground, foreign material, and air, carried into a space that was sterile a moment before. Such a fracture is contaminated at the instant of the injury, even when the wound looks clean and even when it is washed at once. The open fracture is the injury in which the recognition made by the first person on the scene changes the patient's future more than any later decision does. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

The wound and the bone are connected by a story that the responder has to reconstruct from what the patient says and what the limb shows. A wound made from the outside inward, by a blade, by a fall onto a sharp edge, or by a machine, carries surface organisms and foreign material into the limb along its track. A wound made from the inside outward, by the sharp end of the broken bone pushing through the skin and then withdrawing, is a puncture in a limb that may look almost undisturbed, and it is the pattern most often underestimated. In that pattern the bone end has traveled through the skin and then back into the limb, and it may carry a cuff of skin and cloth into the depth of the wound. The observer cannot tell the two patterns apart from the appearance of the wound alone, so any wound on a limb with a suspected fracture is treated as though it communicated with the bone, and the lay first aider does not need to make that judgment, only to report the wound and keep everything still.

A puncture hides a serious injury for three reasons, and each of them can be defeated by a systematic look. The first is position, because a wound in the fold behind the knee, in the armpit, under the edge of a boot, or beneath matted hair is not seen unless the limb is fully exposed and turned. The second is that the wound stops bleeding early and is then hidden by a dressing, a sock, or dried blood, so that the clinician who arrives later sees only a stain. The third is that the patient may not connect a small hole with the pain of a broken bone and may not mention it unless asked where the skin was broken. Full exposure of the limb is the way such a wound is found, with the patient kept warm and the spine protected according to the local protocol, and clothing is cut away rather than pulled over the injury.

Contamination is not a single substance, and the likely organisms differ with the place where the injury happened. Skin carries organisms of its own, so a wound made by a fall on a paved road in a city is contaminated by those organisms and by grit. A wound made in a field, a stable, or a yard where animals are kept carries soil, manure, and plant material, and the organisms that arrive with them are more numerous and harder to treat. A wound in contact with standing water or with clothing soaked in animal waste is contaminated in a way that changes the urgency of referral but never changes the field acts, which remain the same. The responder records what the patient was doing, where the injury happened, and how long ago, because those three facts are asked for first, and describes the wound in the words of what is visible rather than giving it a name. Naming the severity of the contamination is a judgment made later, in the operating room, by the surgeon who can see the depth of the injury.

Every injury in this course is approached through the same order, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and an open fracture is the injury in which that order is most easily broken. The temptation is to look at the wound first and to lose sight of the patient, and the result can be a patient who bleeds, becomes cold, or stops breathing while the wound is being examined. Assessment therefore begins with the scene and the mechanism, passes through the primary survey whose steps are remembered as ABCDE, meaning airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure, and only then turns to the limb. When two correct actions compete for the same pair of hands, life comes before limb, because a limb can wait for minutes that a brain cannot. The open fracture is a wound that must be covered early, but covering it takes seconds and does not delay the search for a cause of shock. The rest of this chapter follows the order named above, and each step names who may perform it.

Assessment of the open fracture is a history and a look, and the history is unusually valuable because the wound cannot be re-created afterwards. The responder asks the time of the injury, the mechanism, the environment, whether the patient could move the limb afterwards, and whether anyone has touched, washed, packed, or dressed the wound. Medicines that affect bleeding and the ability of tissue to resist infection, illnesses such as diabetes, and the state of protection against tetanus are recorded as facts, because the decisions that follow from them belong to the receiving clinician. In the examination the whole limb is exposed and compared with the other side, the wound is described without probing it, and the position in which the limb was found is written before anyone moves it. Tenderness, swelling, deformity, crepitus, and the wound itself are recorded as findings rather than as a diagnosis, and a finding not written down when it was obtained will be argued about later. The lay first aider reports what was seen and done, the trained responder records the observations in a fixed order, and the physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides what the findings mean.

Stabilization of an open fracture begins with bleeding, because an open wound on a limb can bleed in a way that a closed one cannot. The lay first aider presses firmly on the wound with the cleanest material available and keeps pressing, without lifting the material to look underneath it. The trained responder works with personal protective equipment, which protects both the patient and the responder, and covers the wound with a sterile dressing while maintaining pressure. When bleeding cannot be controlled by pressure, a trained responder or paramedic may apply a manufactured tourniquet within the procedures the service authorizes, and the time is written down, because a tourniquet without a record is a hazard of its own; the technique belongs to supervised training. A dressing that has become soaked is reinforced with another dressing rather than lifted away, and the limb is left in the position in which it was found. The patient is kept warm from below as well as above, wet covering is cut away rather than pulled over the injury, and the patient is shielded from wind and from the ground, because cold is a complication of trauma and not a detail of comfort.

Three acts are forbidden in the field, and each is forbidden for a reason that can be said in a single sentence. An embedded object is never removed, because it may be pressing on a torn vessel and its removal may release bleeding that cannot then be stopped. Exposed bone is never pushed back into the wound, because the act pushes contamination deeper and can turn a dirty wound into a deep and dirty wound. An open wound is never explored blindly with a finger or an instrument, because the exploring finger cannot see the vessels and nerves in the depth of the wound and carries its own organisms into it. The wound is covered with a sterile dressing and stabilized in place, and the patient is moved with the dressing and with the object left undisturbed. Definitive cleaning and closure belong to the operating room and to nobody else, because a wound closed in the field over dead tissue and contamination is a wound that will open again with infection.

The neurovascular examination is performed before any intervention and repeated after every one, and in the open fracture it has a second purpose, because the wound itself may have divided a nerve or a vessel. The examiner looks at the color and temperature of the skin beyond the wound, feels the pulses on both sides and compares them, presses briefly on a nail to watch the color return, tests sensation, and asks the patient to move the fingers or the toes. A pulse that is present does not exclude serious injury to a vessel or to the deep tissues, and a pulse that is absent is an emergency, although a pulse may occasionally be absent for a time in a limb that is still viable. Numbness, tingling, weakness, a cold and pale limb, and pain out of proportion to the injury are recorded and reported rather than explained away. The lay first aider observes warmth, color, and the ability of the patient to move and to feel, while the formal examination belongs to the trained responder, the nurse, or the clinician.

Immobilization of the open fracture follows the same principles as immobilization of any other fracture, with one addition, which is that the dressing must stay where it was placed. A splint is applied over the position in which the limb was found and it includes the joint above and the joint below, because a bone splinted alone can still be moved by the joints at either end. It is padded where it presses on bone, it is secured firmly enough to hold without becoming a band that stops the circulation, and it keeps the wound covered without pressing on it. A deformed limb is not straightened to make the splint fit, because vessels and nerves lie against the bone and a movement that looks helpful can tear what remained intact, and because straightening may turn a closed injury into an open one. The trained responder and the paramedic apply splints within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, while the physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides what definitive immobilization is required. Casting, traction, and surgical fixation are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and none of the three is taught from a book in this course, and the neurovascular status is examined and recorded again once the splint is in place.

Imaging is ordered by a clinician and interpreted by a clinician, and it confirms what the examination has already suggested rather than replacing it. A radiograph is usually the first study, taken in at least two directions so that a displacement in the third direction is not hidden, and it includes the joint above and the joint below so that a second injury is not missed. Computed tomography shows the detail of a complex break, particularly one that enters a joint, and it is requested when the answer it gives will change what is done. Magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for soft tissues, and here it is requested only when the question it answers matters more than the delay it causes, with the safety screening for metal and implants belonging to the clinician who orders it. The lay first aider never orders or interprets imaging, and the trained responder does not decide which study is needed. An image is a picture of one moment, and the limb must still be examined afterwards, and in a patient who is unstable the imaging is deferred until the patient is stable enough to travel.

Reassessment is performed after every intervention and compared with the record made before it, and in the open fracture the observations include the wound and the dressing as well as the limb. The examiner repeats the same observations in the same order, which are color, warmth, pulse, sensation, movement, and pain, together with the tightness of the splint and whether blood has struck through the dressing. Pain that increases instead of settling, pain out of proportion to the injury, numbness, tingling, weakness, and a limb that becomes tense and swollen are the findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation. A responder who suspects a compartment syndrome does not wait for the diagnosis to be confirmed and acts within the local protocol, which may allow a constricting bandage, splint, or cast to be released by someone authorized to do it; the limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, the patient is kept warm, and the patient is referred urgently, because the diagnosis is clinical and time is muscle. Reassessment is repeated while the patient waits, and any change is spoken aloud to the team and written in the record, and a wound that begins to smell or to discharge is reported at once.

An open fracture is a different problem from a closed one because contamination has entered a space that the body cannot easily clean, and because the same energy that broke the bone has often destroyed the tissue that would have defended it. Muscle that has been crushed loses its blood supply, and muscle without a blood supply dies and becomes a culture medium rather than a barrier. Periosteum stripped from bone leaves a segment of bone without a blood supply, and dead bone is both unable to heal and unable to resist organisms. The result is that an open fracture has two problems to solve at once, the mechanical problem of a broken bone and the biological problem of contaminated, dead tissue, and the second decides the outcome of the first. Cleaning the wound, removing dead tissue, and deciding what may safely be left in place are surgical acts performed in an operating room with anesthesia, and the number of operations needed cannot be known in advance. Antibiotics and protection against tetanus are decisions of a prescriber, made under the local protocol and never announced as a substance or an amount in this course.

An open fracture that communicates with a joint is more urgent than the same wound in the middle of a bone, and the reason lies in how a joint is built. The surfaces of a joint are covered by cartilage that has no blood supply of its own and is nourished by the fluid inside the joint, and that fluid is an excellent medium for organisms, so infection inside a joint can destroy those surfaces within a short time. A wound over the knee, the elbow, the ankle, or the wrist therefore deserves a high index of suspicion, because the bone may lie close to the skin there and only a thin layer of tissue may stand between the wound and the joint space. The signs that suggest the joint has been entered are a wound that discharges clear or blood-tinged fluid, droplets of fat in the wound fluid, or a joint that fills and becomes tense, but each is unreliable and none can exclude the injury. Air within a joint seen on imaging is a strong finding, and it is a finding made by a clinician rather than by the responder. The responder treats a wound near a joint exactly as a wound over a bone, which is to cover it, to immobilize the limb, to record the neurovascular status, and to refer without delay, because the question of whether the joint was entered is answered in the operating room.

A fracture that happens on a farm, in a stable, or in any place where animals and soil are present is a different problem for reasons that can be stated but not dismissed. Soil and manure carry a wide range of organisms, some of which resist the treatments available in an ordinary hospital, and plant material carries fragments that are not visible on a plain radiograph. A wound that has been dragged through soil or bedding is contaminated along its whole track rather than at its surface, and washing the surface does not reach the depth. Farm machinery crushes rather than cuts, so the wound at the skin may be small while the muscle, the vessels, and the bone beneath are extensively damaged. The responder records the place, the contact with animals, the machinery involved, and the time, because those details change which specialist the patient needs. The wound is covered, nothing is poured into it, and nothing is probed, because the substances that would clean such a wound are used in an operating room under anesthesia and not in a field, so a wound that looks trivial on a farm is referred with the same urgency as one that looks severe.

Delay changes the problem in several ways, and a patient who arrives days after the injury is not a patient for whom the urgency has passed. Swelling that develops over time makes the limb tense and makes the neurovascular examination less reliable, and the wound may have been covered by a dressing that hides its depth. A delay allows contamination to establish itself in the deep tissues and allows dead muscle to become a focus that the body cannot clear, and the infection that follows may involve the bone itself. A wound treated at home with a leaf, a powder, or an oil is recorded as a fact, with the substance named if the patient can name it, and the responder never applies a substance to the wound in response. The patient may have eaten, which matters to the anesthesia team and to nobody in the field, and the time of the last meal is recorded. The patient who waited may also be dehydrated, frightened, and in pain, and that pain is reported to the clinician who decides what may be given, because analgesia is a medical decision and the substance and the amount belong to the local protocol and to the prescriber.

Every act in the care of an open fracture has a provider level attached to it, and a chapter that leaves the level unsaid invites someone to act beyond that training. The lay first aider covers the wound with the cleanest material at hand, presses on bleeding that can be controlled, keeps the patient still and warm, and calls for help, and does nothing else. The trained first responder and the emergency medical technician or paramedic expose the limb, cover the wound with a sterile dressing, apply pressure, splint within the procedures the service authorizes, record the neurovascular status before and after each intervention, and report the environment and the time. The nurse observes, records, reassesses, prepares the patient for transfer, and carries out the orders that the local protocol and the prescriber authorize, without deciding the substance or the amount of any medicine. The physician examines the wound, orders imaging, decides what analgesia and what protection against infection are needed, and refers the patient onward, while the orthopedic surgeon cleans the wound, removes dead tissue, stabilizes the bone, and closes the wound in the operating room, where the true extent of the injury is finally seen. Reduction, traction, and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and the technique of each belongs to supervised clinical training rather than to this book, and the record is part of the treatment and not a formality that follows it.

Referral is not a single telephone call but a transfer of responsibility, and the open fracture is referred to a facility that has an operating room, imaging, and an intensive care unit. The receiving team needs the age of the patient, the time and mechanism of the injury, the environment, the wound as it was first seen and how it changed, the neurovascular findings before and after each intervention, the treatment already given by anyone, the medicines that matter, and the state of protection against tetanus. The responder says plainly what was done and what was not done, because a transfer that hides a delay repeats that delay in a new place. The patient is kept warm and still during the journey, the wound stays covered, and the limb stays splinted, with the splint checked at intervals and the neurovascular status examined again if the patient's condition changes. If the patient becomes unstable on the way, the nearest facility able to stabilize the patient takes priority over the facility able to repair the fracture, because life comes before limb. The grading of an open fracture describes how much soft tissue has been destroyed and how heavy the contamination is, and it is assigned by the surgeon in the operating room, and whatever grade is finally recorded, the part played by the responder is to have recognized the injury, to have protected the patient, and to have sent the patient to the right place in time.

Two groups of patients deserve a lower threshold for referral, and the reasons are different in each. In a child the bone is more elastic, the periosteum is thicker, and the region near each end of a long bone contains the growth area, so a wound on a child's limb may accompany an injury that is not obvious on a radiograph and may threaten the future growth of that limb, and such a child is referred where a pediatric orthopedic surgeon can be reached. In an older adult the skin is thin and the blood supply to it is poor, so a wound over a fracture heals badly and an infection spreads easily, and a fracture that stretches the skin over a displaced bone end may kill that skin and behave as an open injury even before the skin breaks. A patient whose bone is weakened by disease or by long use of a medicine that thins it may sustain an open fracture in a fall from standing height, and the small energy of such a fall must not be read as a small injury. The same lower threshold applies to a patient who has had surgery on that limb, to a patient with a prosthesis, and to a patient whose sensation is already reduced, because there the usual signs are less reliable. The sequence does not change for any of these patients; only the threshold for imaging, for immobilization, and for referral becomes lower.

A few rules in this chapter exist to prevent the responder from causing a harm that the injury has not yet caused. Force is not a technique, so if an attempt to move or to position a limb meets resistance, the attempt stops and the limb is supported where it lies, because the tissues that resist are the ones that would tear. Nothing is poured into a wound, nothing is placed inside it, and no attempt is made to line up the bone ends, because reduction and traction are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and a reduction is not attempted when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, when fragments are caught in the joint, when the vessels or nerves are failing and need a specialist, or when the responder lacks the training or the equipment. A tourniquet is used only by a provider trained and authorized to use it, and its time is recorded, because a device that saves a life in one minute can cost a limb in the next. The patient is kept warm at every stage, since cold worsens bleeding, weakens the defense against infection, and makes the patient harder to assess. The wound is not closed in the field, and it is not left open to the air either, because a dressing protects the wound and shows the receiving team how much it has bled. Every number quoted here is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since such figures differ between services, countries, and editions.

To summarize, an open fracture is any break in which the bone or the deep tissue has communicated with the outside, so the whole limb is exposed and inspected before the injury is judged. What to look for is a wound anywhere on an injured limb, soil or plant material inside the wound, a wound near a joint, a delay before care arrived, and an injury that happened among animals, because each changes the urgency without changing the sequence. What to do is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, with the neurovascular findings recorded before every intervention and repeated after every one. What never to do is to straighten a deformed limb, to remove an embedded object, to push exposed bone back into a wound, to explore a wound blindly, to pour anything into a wound, or to close a wound in the field. What to do next is to cover the wound with a sterile dressing, to control the bleeding that can be controlled, to splint the limb in the position found, to keep the patient warm, and to refer urgently to a facility with an operating room. Antibiotics, analgesia, and protection against tetanus are decisions of a prescriber under the local protocol, and the substance and the amount never come from this course.
