# Chapter 48 — Initial Management of the Open Fracture

The first minutes after an open fracture are spent on a small set of decisions that are simple, few, and unforgiving, because what is done then decides whether the limb can be saved and whether the patient survives to have it repaired. An open fracture is a break in which the bone or the deep tissue around it has communicated with the outside, so the wound has carried organisms, foreign material, and air into a space that was sterile a moment earlier. Initial management is not a small version of definitive treatment; it is a holding action that buys time without adding harm. Every chapter of this course follows one order, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and this chapter follows that order and names the provider level for every act it describes. Three problems compete for attention at once, which are bleeding that will not stop, contamination that cannot be cleaned in the field, and a bone that will not hold still, and of the three the first is the one that kills soonest. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

When two correct actions compete for the same pair of hands, the rule that decides the order is that life comes before limb, because a limb can wait for minutes that a brain and a heart cannot. Assessment therefore begins with the scene rather than with the wound, and a responder who is not safe cannot help anyone, so traffic, machinery, electricity, fire, and unstable ground are dealt with first and the patient is moved only when moving is safer than staying. The primary survey follows, and its steps are remembered as ABCDE, meaning airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure, and the wound is covered only when the survey allows a pause of a few seconds. A patient with an open fracture may be bleeding from a torn vessel in the limb, may be bleeding from another injury that has not been looked for, or may be bleeding nowhere that can be seen, and the last of those is the most dangerous because a break of a large bone can hide a liter of blood inside a thigh. Exposure means that the whole patient is examined, and it is done with the patient kept warm, because cold is a complication of trauma and not a detail of comfort.

The history of an open fracture is unusually valuable, because the wound cannot be re-created and the story of how it was made explains what is inside it. The responder asks when the injury happened, what made it, where it happened, and whether the patient was in contact with soil, manure, standing water, or machinery, because those facts tell the receiving surgeon which organisms to expect. It matters whether the wound was made from the outside inward by a blade or a sharp edge, or from the inside outward by the sharp end of the bone, although the two cannot be told apart by looking and any wound on an injured limb is treated as though it reached the bone. The patient is asked whether the limb could be moved afterwards, whether any part of it felt numb or dead, and whether anyone has washed, packed, dressed, or poured anything into the wound, and every answer is written down as a fact rather than as an opinion. Medicines that affect bleeding, illnesses such as diabetes, previous surgery on the limb, and the state of protection against tetanus are recorded, because the decisions that follow from them belong to the receiving clinician. The lay first aider gives the story in plain words, the trained responder takes a structured history in a fixed order, and the physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides what the story means.

The examination of the limb begins with looking, because so much of what matters is visible if the limb is fully exposed. Clothing is cut away rather than pulled over the injury, the limb is compared with the other side, and the position in which it was found is written before anyone moves it. The wound is described in the words of what is seen, which are its length in centimeters, its edges, whether it is bleeding, whether it discharges, whether bone or tendon is visible in its depth, and whether foreign material such as grit, cloth, or plant matter lies within it. That description is a record of findings and not a diagnosis, and no name for the severity of the wound is invented by the person who describes it. Feeling is confined to the parts of the limb away from the wound, because pressure on the wound is painful and because a finger that enters the wound carries organisms into a space that sees no light and cannot be cleaned. The lay first aider reports only what can be seen from outside, and the examination of the wound itself belongs to the physician or to the orthopedic surgeon.

Three acts are forbidden in the field, and each one is forbidden for a reason that can be said plainly. An embedded object is never removed, because it may be pressing on a torn vessel and its removal can release bleeding that no one can then stop, so the object is left where it lies, the wound is covered around it, and the patient travels with it. Exposed bone is never pushed back into the wound, because the act drives contamination deeper into the limb and turns a contaminated wound into a deep and contaminated one, so the bone end is covered instead. An open wound is never explored blindly with a finger or an instrument, because the exploring finger cannot see the vessels and nerves that lie in the depth of the wound and carries its own organisms in with it. Nothing is poured into the wound either, since the substances that clean a wound are used in an operating room under anesthesia and in a controlled setting, and a wound that has already been treated at home with a leaf, a powder, or an oil is reported as it is found. No attempt is made to close a wound in the field, because closing a wound over dead tissue and contamination traps the infection inside and produces a wound that will open again.

Bleeding is the first thing stabilized, because an open wound on a limb can bleed in a way that a closed one cannot, and because the patient who bleeds is the patient who dies first. The lay first aider presses firmly on the wound with the cleanest material at hand, keeps pressing, and does not lift the material to look underneath it, since looking interrupts the pressure that is doing the work. The trained first responder and the emergency medical technician work with personal protective equipment, which protects the responder and the patient both, and they cover the wound with a sterile dressing while maintaining pressure. A dressing that becomes soaked is reinforced with another dressing placed on top rather than removed, and the amount of blood on the dressing is reported, because a dressing that has been lifted has told the receiving team nothing and has cost the patient blood. When pressure cannot control bleeding, a trained responder or paramedic may apply a manufactured tourniquet within the procedures the service authorizes, and the time of application is written down at once, because a tourniquet without a record is a hazard of its own. The technique of applying a tourniquet belongs to supervised training and to the local protocol, and this course does not teach it from a book.

The sterile covering is a simple act that is often done badly, and the reason it matters is that a covered wound is protected from further contamination and shows the receiving team how much it has bled. A sterile dressing from a sealed pack is the standard, and the responder opens it without touching the surface that will lie against the wound. When no sterile dressing is available, the cleanest material at hand is used, and the responder does not delay transport in a search for a better one, because minutes of bleeding and hours of contamination cost more than the difference between one clean covering and another. The dressing is placed over the wound and held there, and if an object is embedded in the wound the dressing is built around the object rather than pressed onto its tip. The covering does not replace the pressure and it does not replace the splint, because the aim is to protect the wound, to keep the bone ends from moving, and to reach a surgeon. The dressing stays in place until a clinician in an appropriate setting removes it, since each examination of the wound in the field raises the risk of contamination and requires the covering to be renewed.

The neurovascular examination is performed before any intervention and repeated after every one, and its result is recorded in writing, because a finding that was not recorded before a splint was applied cannot be attributed afterwards. The examiner looks at the color and the temperature of the skin beyond the wound, feels the pulses on both sides and compares them, presses briefly on a nail to watch the color return, and tests sensation with a light touch on each side. The patient is asked to move the fingers or the toes, and any movement that was present a moment earlier and is now gone is treated as an emergency rather than as a curiosity. A pulse that is present does not exclude serious injury to a vessel, and a pulse that is absent is an emergency, although a pulse may occasionally be absent for a time in a limb that is still viable. Numbness, tingling, weakness, a cold and pale limb, and pain out of proportion to the injury are recorded and reported rather than explained away, and a sign that is unreliable in one patient may be reliable in another, so the comparison with the other side is what carries the weight. The lay first aider observes warmth, color, and the ability of the patient to move and to feel, while the formal examination belongs to the trained responder, the nurse, or the clinician.

Immobilization follows the neurovascular examination and precedes any movement of the patient, and its purpose is to keep the bone ends from moving so that they cannot tear what is still intact. The limb is splinted in the position in which it was found, and a deformed limb is never straightened in the field, because vessels and nerves lie against the bone and a movement that looks helpful can tear them, and because straightening can turn a closed injury into an open one. The splint includes the joint above and the joint below the fracture, since a bone that is splinted alone can still be moved by the joints at either end, and it is padded where it presses on bone. It is secured firmly enough to hold the limb without becoming a band that stops the circulation, and it covers the wound without pressing on the dressing or on any object that has been left in place. The trained responder and the paramedic apply splints within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, while the nurse checks the skin, the dressing, and the circulation at intervals during the wait. The physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides what definitive immobilization is required, which may be a cast, a brace, or a surgical fixation, and casting, traction, and surgical fixation are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, never from a book.

Whatever is used as a splint, the limb is checked after it is in place rather than only before. A commercial splint, a padded board, a folded magazine, a sling, or the body of the patient can each serve, and an improvised device is checked more often because it shifts and because its padding is usually thinner. Cold is treated as a complication in its own right, so the patient is insulated from the ground, wet clothing is cut away, and the covering is applied over the splint rather than under it. The spine is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has done so, and no lone manual handling technique is safe, so a patient with a suspected spinal injury is moved by a trained team using the precautions the local protocol requires. During the wait and during the journey, the same observations are repeated in the same order, which are color, warmth, pulse, sensation, movement, and pain, together with the tightness of the splint and whether blood has struck through the dressing. Any change in those observations is spoken aloud to the team and written in the record, because a change that is not communicated is a change that does not exist for the person who takes over.

Imaging confirms what the examination has already suggested, and it never replaces the examination, the covering, or the splint. A radiograph is usually the first study, and it is taken in at least two directions so that a displacement in the third direction is not hidden, with the joint above and the joint below included so that a second injury is not missed. Computed tomography shows the detail of a complex break, particularly one that enters a joint, and magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for soft tissues including ligaments, cartilage, and muscle, and either study is requested when the answer it gives will change what is done. The decision to image and the interpretation of the image belong to a clinician trained to make them, while the lay first aider never orders or interprets imaging and the trained responder does not decide which study is needed. An image is a picture of one moment, so the limb is examined and the findings recorded again after the picture has been taken. In a patient who is bleeding, cold, or losing consciousness, imaging is deferred until the patient is stable enough to travel, because the picture can wait and the patient cannot.

Reassessment is performed after every intervention, and it is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment rather than by the injury. The complication this course fears most in a limb that has been splinted or cast is a rise in pressure inside a compartment, where muscle and nerve are enclosed by bone and fascia with very little room. The diagnosis is clinical and is made by a clinician, and time is muscle, so a responder who suspects it does not wait for a confirmation that will arrive too late. Pain that increases instead of settling, pain out of proportion to the injury, pain that is worse when the fingers or the toes are stretched, numbness, tingling, weakness, and a limb that becomes tense and swollen are the findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation. Where the local protocol permits and someone authorized is present, a constricting bandage, splint, or cast is released, the limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, the patient is kept warm, and the patient is referred urgently. A pulse that remains present does not exclude a compartment syndrome, and the responder who waits for the pulse to disappear has waited too long.

Antibiotics and protection against tetanus follow the local protocol, and this course gives no substance, no amount, and no schedule, because a listener must not be able to take a prescription from a book. The decision belongs to the physician or to another prescriber authorized by the local protocol, and the finding that makes it urgent is an open wound contaminated by soil, manure, or standing water, a wound made by machinery, a wound neglected for hours or days, or a wound that lies over a joint. The time from the injury to the first appropriate treatment matters, and the receiving team will ask for it, so the time of the injury and the time of arrival are written down and handed over. The state of protection against tetanus is recorded as a fact, which means whether the patient has been immunized, when the last protection was given if the patient knows, and whether any record of it exists, and the clinician decides what is required. The nurse prepares the patient, carries out the orders that the local protocol and the prescriber authorize, and never decides the substance or the amount, while the responder in the field gives nothing by any route. Antibiotics given before the wound is cleaned and the dead tissue removed do not replace the operation, and nobody in the field should believe that they do.

What the operating room does is the definitive treatment that the field cannot provide, and the task of the responder is to deliver the patient to it without having made the injury worse. Cleaning the wound, removing dead and contaminated tissue, and washing the surfaces under anesthesia are surgical acts, and the number of operations needed cannot be known in advance because dead tissue declares itself over the following days. Stabilizing the bone may require a cast, a brace, a metal device, or an external frame, and the choice belongs to the orthopedic surgeon who can see the wound from the inside. Reducing a fracture means restoring the position of the bone ends, and traction means holding them in line with a gentle continuous pull, and both are used in the operating room or under close supervision with imaging at hand. A reduction is not attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when the vessels or the nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, when the responder lacks the training or the equipment, or when an earlier attempt has already failed. In each of those situations an attempt can convert an injury that would have healed into one that cannot, and force is not a technique, so if a movement meets resistance the attempt stops and the limb is supported where it lies.

Referral is a transfer of responsibility rather than a telephone call, and an open fracture is referred to a facility that has an operating room, imaging, and an intensive care unit. The receiving team needs the age of the patient, the time and mechanism of the injury, the environment in which it happened, the wound as it was first seen and how it has changed, the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention, what anyone has already done to the wound, the medicines that matter, and the state of protection against tetanus. The responder says plainly what was done and what was not done, because a transfer that hides a delay repeats that delay in a new place. The patient is kept warm and still during the journey, the wound stays covered, the limb stays splinted, and the splint is checked at intervals and the neurovascular status examined again if the condition changes. If the patient becomes unstable on the way, the nearest facility able to stabilize the patient takes priority over the facility able to repair the fracture, because life comes before limb. A patient who arrives late is not a patient for whom the urgency has passed, and the transfer is arranged with the same speed as though the injury had just happened.

Every act in the initial management of an open fracture has a provider level attached to it, and a chapter that leaves the level unsaid invites someone to act beyond that training. The lay first aider presses on bleeding that can be controlled, covers the wound with the cleanest material available, keeps the patient warm and still, and calls for help, and does nothing else. The trained first responder and the emergency medical technician or paramedic expose the limb, cover the wound with a sterile dressing under personal protective equipment, maintain pressure, apply a manufactured tourniquet only where the service authorizes it and the training has been completed, splint within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, and record the neurovascular status before and after each intervention. The nurse observes, records, reassesses, prepares the patient for transfer, and carries out the orders that the local protocol and the prescriber authorize, without deciding the substance or the amount of any medicine. The physician examines the wound, orders imaging, decides what analgesia and what protection against infection are needed, and refers the patient onward, while the orthopedic surgeon cleans the wound, removes dead tissue, stabilizes the bone, and closes the wound in the operating room where the true extent of the injury is finally seen. Where this chapter names a provider, it names the level at which the act is normally learned and certified, and the law of the country in which the reader works decides what may be done without supervision.

The record is part of the treatment rather than a formality that follows it, and it is written while the patient is in front of the responder rather than reconstructed later. The time of the injury and the time of each intervention, the position in which the limb was found, the appearance of the wound as it was first seen, the neurovascular findings before and after every act, and any change in the condition of the patient are written or spoken in a fixed order, so that the receiving team continues a story instead of starting a new one. The record does not name a severity grade, because grading an open fracture is a judgment made by the surgeon who can see how much tissue has been destroyed and how heavy the contamination is. A wound that begins to smell, that discharges, or that bleeds more heavily is reported at once rather than noted for later. Where a number appears in this chapter it is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because times, distances, and thresholds differ between services, countries, and editions. Everything described here is learned with a teacher, practiced on models and on one another, and certified by an authority that also defines what may be done without supervision.

A few errors are so common and so damaging that they deserve to be named in the plainest words. Lifting a dressing to look underneath it interrupts the pressure that was controlling the bleeding, and it shows the receiving team a wound that has been disturbed twice. Straightening a deformed limb to make a splint fit can tear a vessel or a nerve that the injury had left intact, and it can convert a closed fracture into an open one. Pushing exposed bone back into a wound drives contamination inward, and removing an embedded object can release bleeding that cannot then be stopped. Closing a wound in the field traps dead tissue and organisms inside, and giving an antibiotic instead of arranging the operation treats the anxiety of the moment rather than the wound. Force is not a technique, so an attempt that meets resistance stops at once, and every intervention is followed by the same observations in the same order, because the treatment is the most likely cause of the next problem.

To summarize, the initial management of an open fracture is a short sequence of protective acts, and the sequence is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. What to look for is a wound anywhere on an injured limb, bleeding that will not stop, bone or tendon in the depth of the wound, soil or plant material inside it, a wound near a joint, a limb that is deformed, and any loss of pulse, sensation, or movement. What to do is to make the scene safe, to work through the primary survey whose steps are remembered as ABCDE, to cover the wound with a sterile dressing under personal protective equipment, to control the bleeding that can be controlled, to record the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention, to splint the limb in the position found with the joint above and the joint below included, to keep the patient warm, and to arrange urgent transfer to a facility with an operating room, imaging, and an intensive care unit. What never to do is to straighten a deformed limb, to remove an embedded object, to push exposed bone back into a wound, to explore a wound blindly, to pour anything into a wound, to close a wound in the field, or to believe that an antibiotic replaces the operation. Antibiotics, analgesia, and protection against tetanus follow the local protocol and belong to the prescriber, and this course gives no substance, no amount, and no route. Reduction, traction, and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and the technique of each belongs to supervised clinical training rather than to this book. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.
