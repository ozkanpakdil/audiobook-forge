# Chapter 44 — Growth Plate Injuries

The growth plate is the layer of cartilage near each end of a child's long bone where the bone lengthens, and an injury to it differs from a broken shaft because the tissue that was damaged is the tissue that was going to make the limb grow. A plate that is bruised, split or crushed may heal, and the bone may still stop lengthening or grow crooked years later. This chapter therefore asks the listener to look past the bone that shows on an image and to think about the plate that does not. The course follows one order in every chapter, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and it names the provider level for every act. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

The plate is built in layers, and hearing them in order explains why it fails where it does. Nearest the end of the bone lie small rounded cartilage cells that divide slowly and serve as the store from which the plate is renewed. Farther toward the middle of the bone the cells multiply and line up in columns, and then they enlarge, take in water and prepare their matrix for calcification. The last layer is invaded by vessels from the shaft, which bring in the cells that lay down bone on the cartilage scaffold, so that the plate moves away from the middle of the bone and the bone grows longer. The layer of enlarged cells is the weakest part of the structure, because the matrix between the cells is thin, and that is the layer a pulling or a shearing force tears. A ring of fibrous tissue at the edge of the plate holds it in position, and when that ring is torn the plate may bridge to the shaft as it heals.

Because the plate is the weakest link in the growing skeleton, the tissue that gives way first in a child often differs from that in an adult. A joint that would tear a ligament in an adult may instead separate the plate, and a force along the length of the bone is spread across the joint and into the plate, so an injury that seems to spare the joint may still disturb growth. Rotation is the displacement most easily missed, because a limb can look straight while it is twisted, and a twist recognized late heals as it lies and does not remodel. The responder records the position in which the limb was found and compares it with the other side but does not test the joint by pulling on it, because that examination belongs to a clinician and can add damage.

An injury of the plate announces itself less clearly than a broken shaft, so the responder takes the history seriously and the examination gently. The child may hold the limb still, refuse to use it or cry when it is handled, and yet the limb may look straight, may not swell for hours and may still move through most of its range. Tenderness that sits exactly over the plate rather than over the joint line is the finding that matters most, and it is easily lost once a splint has covered the limb, so it is written down at the first examination. Pain with a twist, pain when the bone is squeezed along its length and a reluctance to bear weight all point to the plate and not only to the joint.

Clinicians describe injuries of the plate in a small set of patterns, and the descriptions matter more than the numbers attached to them, because the number is local language while the pattern is anatomy. This group is often called the Salter-Harris classification, and the names and their order differ between editions and countries, so a listener who hears a number should ask what it describes. In the mildest description the fragment separates through the plate itself, leaving the bone on both sides intact, and this is the pattern that may not show on the first image at all. In the next, the break runs across part of the plate and exits through a corner of the shaft, carrying a small triangle of bone with it, and this is the commonest description in childhood. A third crosses the plate and leaves through the end of the bone, so that the joint surface is broken as well, and a fourth combines the third and the second. The last is a crush of the plate in which nothing looks displaced, and it is often recognized only afterwards, when the bone fails to grow.

What the listener should carry away is not the order of the numbers but the four questions those patterns answer. The first is whether the joint surface is involved, because a break that enters the joint can leave the surface uneven and the joint arthritic in later life. The second is whether the plate has moved, because a displaced plate must be brought back into position by someone qualified to do it, and that decision belongs to the orthopedic surgeon. The third is whether the blood supply to the end of the bone is at risk, because the head of the thigh bone and the head of the radius in a child have supplies that tear easily. The fourth is how much growth remains, because a plate that is nearly closed has little future to lose, while the same injury in a toddler may change the length of the whole limb.

Assessment begins with the scene and the mechanism, and in a child it also includes the age, the time since the injury and anything that has made the bones fragile. The primary survey comes next, and its steps are remembered as ABCDE, meaning airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure. A child compensates for blood loss by raising the heart rate and tightening the vessels and may look well until that compensation fails, so a child who becomes quiet, pale or drowsy is more worrying than the same behavior in an adult. Life comes before limb whenever two correct actions compete for the same pair of hands, and exposure is followed at once by covering the child again, because cold is a complication of trauma and not a detail of comfort. Only when the child is stable enough does the assessment turn to the limb, and the limb is assessed by looking, by feeling gently and by asking the child to move it, never by forcing it.

Some children cannot describe their pain at all, and the examination has to be built from what someone else can see. A baby who has not yet learned to speak, a child whose development is delayed, a child with a condition that alters sensation, a child who is sedated in an intensive care unit and a child too frightened to answer may each present with a fracture and no complaint. A baby may stop moving one arm and hold it still, and a child may refuse to feed, sleep badly or cry in a way the caregiver has never heard before. The caregiver is the expert on that child's normal behavior, so asking what is different today is more useful than asking where it hurts. A change in the use of a hand, a limp that was not there yesterday and a cry that changes when the limb is touched all describe an injury even when no swelling is present.

None of those signs can be relied upon to be present, and the reverse matters just as much, because a child who is using a limb may still have an injured plate. The absence of swelling, the ability to move the joint and the absence of visible deformity are all unreliable in a plate injury, and a responder who rests on any one of them will miss the child who is quiet rather than loud. Pain out of proportion to the injury is the finding this course teaches the listener to take most seriously, and in a child it may appear as distress that cannot be soothed rather than as an answer the child can give. A child who cannot report numbness cannot be asked to, so the examiner tests sensation by comparison and by watching the face.

Stabilization of an injured limb in a child is a small set of acts, and the first of them is restraint. The limb is supported and splinted in the position in which it was found and is never straightened in the field, because the vessels and nerves lie against the bone and a movement that looks helpful can tear what remained intact and can disturb the plate. The lay first aider performs the acts that need no equipment, which are supporting the limb with the hands or a pillow, keeping the child warm and staying with the child until help arrives. The trained responder and the paramedic work within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, and neither straightens a deformed limb, pushes exposed bone back into a wound or removes an embedded object. A child loses heat faster than an adult, so wet clothing is replaced with dry covering, the child is insulated from the ground and the head is covered.

The neurovascular examination is performed before any intervention and repeated after it, and its result is written down, because a finding that was not recorded before a splint was applied cannot be attributed afterwards. The examiner looks at the color and temperature of the skin, feels the pulse beyond the injury, presses briefly on a nail to watch the color return and asks the child to move the fingers or the toes. Sensation is tested on both sides and compared, and with a small child the testing is often turned into a game or done while the caregiver distracts the child. The lay first aider observes color, warmth and the child's ability to move and feel, while the formal examination of pulses and sensation belongs to the trained responder or to the clinician. A pulse that is present does not exclude serious injury to the vessels or to a compartment, and a pulse that is absent is an emergency.

A compartment syndrome is harder to recognize in a child than in an adult and is more easily missed, because a frightened child may cry for reasons that have nothing to do with the injury and a quiet child may be assumed to be comfortable. The limb that is tense, swollen and painful out of proportion to the injury is an emergency at any age, and pain that increases instead of settling and a child who cannot be comforted should stop the responder. Numbness, tingling, weakness, pallor and coolness come later, and waiting for them is waiting too long, because the diagnosis is clinical and time is muscle. A responder who suspects it does not wait for confirmation, does not elevate the limb above the level of the heart and releases a constricting bandage only within the local protocol and only when authorized. The substance, the amount and the route of any medicine for pain belong to the prescriber and to the local protocol, and no dose may be taken from this course. A child in a cast whose pain is rising must be seen rather than observed.

Immobilization means preventing movement of the injured part and of the joints above and below it, because a bone that is splinted alone can still be moved by the joint at either end. A splint is padded where it presses on bone, is applied over the position found and is secured firmly enough to hold without becoming a band that stops the circulation. In a child the margins are narrow, so the device is checked again after it is applied and the part beyond it is watched for color, warmth, swelling and movement. Padding that presses over the tender plate may add pain and hide the very finding the next examiner needs, so the padding goes over the bony points and the tender region is left undisturbed. A folded magazine, a padded board, a sling or the body of the caregiver can each serve, and a device that leaves the part beyond it pale, cool or painful is loosened only within the local protocol and by someone authorized to do it. Casting, traction and surgical fixation are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and none of them can be learned from a book.

Imaging confirms what the examination has already suggested, and it is ordered and interpreted by a clinician trained to do so, never by a responder in the field. A radiograph is usually the first study, and it is taken in at least two directions so that a displacement in the third direction is not hidden, while the joint above and the joint below are included so that a second injury is not missed. In a child the reading is more difficult, because the plate appears as a gap, the centers of bone at the ends of the long bones appear as separate pieces and small extra centers that are entirely normal can look like fragments that have been pulled away. An image of the other limb may be taken for comparison when the clinician is unsure, and that decision belongs to the clinician. A break through the plate may not be visible at all on the first image, so a clinic may review the child later, and any interval quoted for that review is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

Computed tomography shows the detail of a break that runs into a joint and is used when that detail will change what is done, and magnetic resonance imaging shows cartilage and soft tissue and is the study a clinician may choose when a plate injury is suspected but not seen. Magnetic resonance imaging can also show a bridge of bone forming across the plate before the deformity appears. Children are more sensitive to radiation than adults, so every study is weighed against the answer it will give. Ordering an image, choosing between the studies and reading the result are the work of the clinician responsible for the child's care, and the responder's part is to describe the mechanism, the tenderness over the plate and the neurovascular findings. An image is a picture of one moment, and the child must still be examined and reassessed after it has been taken.

Reassessment is performed after every intervention, and it is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the record, because the comparison is what has value rather than any single measurement, and the limb is checked for color, warmth, pulse, sensation, movement and pain while the splint is checked for tightness. Pain that increases instead of settling, numbness, tingling, weakness and a limb that becomes tense and swollen stop the responder and prompt escalation, and in a child the same findings may appear as distress that cannot be soothed. A responder who suspects a compartment syndrome acts within the local protocol rather than waiting for confirmation, and the child is kept warm while the limb is not elevated above the level of the heart. The receiving team is warned before the child arrives, and the findings travel with the child in a fixed order.

Referral or definitive treatment is the final step, and it begins with the recognition that some injuries cannot be managed where the child lies. A child with a suspected injury of the growth plate, a displaced fracture, a fracture that enters a joint, an open fracture, a damaged nerve or vessel, a suspected compartment syndrome or an injury that does not match the explanation given is referred to a facility with imaging, an operating room and an intensive care unit. A suspected plate injury deserves that referral even when the image looks reassuring, because the finding that decides the outcome may not be visible yet. When the plate has moved, the timing of definitive care matters more than in an adult, and the decision about when and how belongs to the orthopedic surgeon. Transport is arranged with the limb immobilized, the child warm and the neurovascular findings handed over. Nothing in this chapter permits a responder to reduce a plate injury, to apply traction or to reduce a joint, because those acts are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol.

Definitive treatment of a plate injury may be an operation, and what the surgeon does is decided by the pattern and by the growth that remains. A displaced fragment may be brought back into position and held, a fragment blocking the joint may be removed and a bridge of bone that has formed across the plate may be taken away in the hope that growth resumes, and every one of those acts belongs to the orthopedic surgeon in an operating room. A bridge that is not removed acts as a staple, tethering one side of the bone while the rest lengthens, so the limb becomes crooked or shorter than its partner. That outcome may appear only after a growth spurt and may be measured in years rather than weeks, which is why a child with a plate injury is followed until growth is finished. Some angulation in a young child is accepted and left to remodel, and that acceptance is a considered plan made by a surgeon who expects to review the child again. The family must understand that the pain will go long before the watching ends, because a family that understands the plan is far more likely to return for the reviews that make it safe.

An open injury in a child deserves its own considerations, because the wound and the plate change each other's meaning. An open fracture in a child is sometimes produced by less energy than the same injury in an adult, but the wounds of farm equipment, of a mower or of a road crash carry heavy contamination, and any such example is a constructed illustration. The wound may be small and may look almost clean while the plate beneath it is crushed or displaced, so an open injury is never judged by the size of the hole in the skin. The responder covers the wound with a sterile dressing, controls the bleeding that can be controlled, records the neurovascular status, immobilizes the limb in the position found, keeps the child warm and arranges urgent transfer. An embedded object is never removed and exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, because the object may be holding pressure on a torn vessel. An open wound is never explored blindly, because definitive closure belongs to the operating room and to nobody else.

An open injury near the plate is an emergency for the limb and sometimes for the life, because contamination that reaches a damaged plate can destroy the growth of the bone and infection in an open fracture can become a general illness. Whether the child needs an antibiotic, whether tetanus protection is required and how the wound is cleaned belong to the prescriber and to the local protocol, and this course names no substance, no amount, no route and no frequency. What the responder must decide is only how quickly the child reaches a facility with an operating room and an intensive care unit, and how to keep the wound covered, the limb still and the child warm. Because a plate injury and an open wound may occur together, a child with a wound over a joint or over the end of a bone is treated as having a plate injury until a clinician says otherwise. The neurovascular examination is recorded before the dressing is applied and after it is applied, so that a change following the dressing can be recognized.

Among the injuries a child may bring to a responder are those that were inflicted, and an injury of the growth plate is one of the patterns that must raise that question when the story and the injury do not match. Concern is raised when the explanation offered does not account for the force the injury required, when the account changes between the people who tell it, when there has been a delay in seeking help without a reason that fits and when a child who cannot yet walk or roll has a fracture. Concern is raised again when injuries of different ages are found together, when the family has made repeated visits for injury and when the injury lies where a fall cannot reach, such as the trunk, the ears, the neck or the buttocks. In a young infant, a break through the corner of the shaft near the plate and fractures of the ribs are descriptions that clinicians treat as inflicted until they are explained, and a responder does not have to make that judgment in order to act on the concern. The question is raised by the mismatch, not by the responder's certainty, and every example here is a constructed illustration.

The correct acts are to keep the child safe and warm, to treat the injury that is present and to pass the concern to the clinician in charge in the words in which it was raised. The responder does not interrogate the child, does not question or confront the family in front of the child and does not say aloud what is suspected, because a confrontation can place the child in greater danger and can spoil an investigation. What was seen, what was said, who was present and the exact words used are written in the record, together with the time the child arrived and the time the injury was said to have happened. The duty to report is set by local law and local protocol, and the reporting is done through the channel the protocol names and by the person the protocol names. A clinician, not the responder, decides whether the injury was inflicted, but a responder who suspects it does not send the child home to be brought back later, and the concern is recorded even when the explanation seems satisfactory to everyone else.

The record and the handover are part of the treatment rather than a formality that follows it, and in a child they carry information that no later examiner can recover. The age of the child, the time of the injury, the mechanism, the position in which the limb was found, the tenderness over the plate and the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention are written in a fixed order. Where an injury was inflicted, the record also carries what was observed about the account itself, because that observation may be the only thing that prevents the next injury. An injury of the spine is treated as unstable until it is cleared by someone qualified to clear it, and no lone manual handling technique is safe, so a child with a suspected spinal injury is moved by a trained team using the precautions the local protocol requires. Everything described here is learned with a teacher and certified by an authority that defines what may be done without supervision, and any time, age or distance named is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

To summarize, the growth plate is the cartilage layer where a child's bone lengthens, and it is the weakest link in the growing skeleton, so a force that would tear a ligament in an adult may separate the plate in a child. The descriptions of these injuries matter more than the numbers attached to them, because what decides the outcome is whether the joint surface is broken, whether the plate has moved, whether the blood supply to the end of the bone is at risk and how much growth remains. A child who will not use a limb, will not bear weight, cries when the limb is touched or simply stops feeding has described an injury even when nothing can be seen. The course follows one order in every chapter, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and it names the provider level for every act. A deformed limb is splinted in the position found and is never straightened in the field, an embedded object is never removed, exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, an open wound is never explored blindly, and a constricting splint or bandage is released only within the local protocol and by someone authorized to do it. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and a reduction is not attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when the vessels or the nerves are failing, when the injury is open, when the training is missing or when an earlier attempt has failed. An open injury in a child is covered, immobilized and transferred urgently, no substance and no amount is named in this course for pain, for infection or for tetanus, and an injury whose story does not match the child is reported through the channel that local law and local protocol name.
