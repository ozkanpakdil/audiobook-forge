# Chapter 25 — Pelvic Trauma Management

The pelvis is a ring of bone, and a ring that gives way in one place has almost always given way or torn somewhere else as well, so an injury of the pelvic ring is a bleeding problem before it is a bone problem. The bones enclose a rich network of veins and the arteries that supply the buttock, the thigh and the organs within the pelvis, and those vessels lie in a space that can accept a very large volume of blood without a drop of it appearing on the skin. A patient can therefore be bleeding to death from a pelvic injury while the abdomen looks flat and the skin looks unmarked, and the fracture may still look like nothing at all. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work. Its subject is what is done for the patient with a pelvic injury, and it follows the order this course uses everywhere, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively.

The bleeding from a broken pelvis comes chiefly from torn veins, from the raw spongy surfaces of the bone, and from branches of the arteries that run along the back of the ring. The veins that cross the pelvis are large and thin-walled, so they tear rather than seal, and the space behind the lining of the abdomen has room to accept a volume that teaching quotes in liters for an adult. That figure is an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the volumes named in different services and different editions do not agree. The consequence that matters is not the number but the direction of the thinking, since until someone qualified has shown otherwise, a pelvic fracture is treated as a source of major bleeding. A broken ring is also a mechanical problem, since every movement of the patient can open it further.

A pelvic fracture in a young adult is usually the mark of great force, such as a vehicle crash, a motorcycle crash, a pedestrian struck, a fall from a height or a crush. Whatever the mechanism, the patient is approached in one order, and that order is the spine of this chapter. The first step is assessment, the next is stabilization, then the neurovascular examination, then immobilization, then imaging, then reassessment, and only after that the decision to refer or to treat definitively. Two of those steps are non-negotiable in every patient, because the neurovascular examination is performed and recorded before any intervention and repeated after it, and because the reassessment after every intervention is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment.

Assessment begins before the patient is touched, with the scene, because a responder who is injured adds a second patient to a difficult rescue. The hazards are counted, the number of patients is noted, and the mechanism is read from the wreckage as well as from the story, because the direction of the force predicts where the energy has gone. The primary survey follows, and its steps are remembered as ABCDE, meaning airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure. Catastrophic bleeding that can be seen is controlled first by the trained first responder or the paramedic, using the equipment the service provides and within the procedures it authorizes. The lay first aider calls for help, does not move the patient unless there is immediate danger, keeps the patient warm and stays with the patient. Only when the patient is stable enough does the examination turn to the pelvis and the limbs.

The pelvis is examined by looking before it is examined by hand, and the eyes are asked specific questions. A limb that lies rotated outward, a leg that appears shorter than the other, bruising over the perineum, swelling of the scrotum or the labia, blood at the opening of the urethra, blood at the anus and any wound all point toward the ring. The trained responder may then press the ring gently and once, feeling for movement between the two halves of the front of the pelvis, and writes the finding down. What must not happen is the repeated springing, rocking and re-testing of the pelvis that turns the patient into a demonstration for each new pair of hands, because every movement can break the clot that has begun to seal a torn vein. A pelvis that feels firm under gentle pressure may still be broken, so the absence of movement is not reassurance. Three examinations belong to the clinician rather than to the responder, and they are the internal examination of the rectum and of the vagina, the passing of a tube into the bladder, and the probing of a wound near the perineum. Each of those acts can do harm in untrained hands, because a channel that is partly torn can be made completely torn by a tube pushed blindly through it. The responder looks, reports, protects those regions and lets the clinician decide.

Stabilization of the ring is the act that buys the most time, and the device that most services use is a wide band called a pelvic binder. It is placed around the pelvis at the level of the greater trochanters, which are the prominent bony points on the outer side of the upper thigh, and not at the level of the crests of the iliac bones. The reason is mechanical, since the front of the ring is closed by bringing the two halves of the pelvis together at the joint between them, while a band placed higher, over the crests, can press the wings of the pelvis apart instead. A commercial binder is designed for that level, while an improvised one is a folded sheet or a wide strip of cloth, at least 15 centimeters wide, a figure offered as an illustration to be confirmed against the guideline in force and the local protocol. The band is applied over the clothing where possible, it is centered, it is wide enough that the pressure is spread rather than focused, and it is tightened firmly but never so tight that it acts as a tourniquet. The trained first responder and the paramedic apply the device within the procedures and the equipment their service authorizes, and the lay first aider at most presses the knees together gently or holds a folded sheet in place under the direction of a dispatcher until trained help arrives.

A pelvic binder is a bridge and not a treatment, and a listener who understands that will not be surprised when the patient remains unstable after a neat band has been applied. The binder closes the volume of the ring and gives the clot a chance to form, but it does not stop arterial bleeding and it does not replace blood, warmth or an operating room. It is left in place during movement, during imaging and during transfer, and it is not removed by anyone who is not qualified to decide what happens next. The decision about when it comes off, and what is done in its place, belongs to the receiving team and to the physician. The skin beneath the band and the tightness of the band are checked at every move and at every reassessment, because a device left too long or made too tight causes an injury of its own.

The log roll is the maneuver by which the patient is turned as a single unit so that the back can be seen, and in a pelvic injury it is performed once, by a team, with the spine protected. It must not be repeated for the convenience of a new examiner and it must not be repeated to prove a finding, because every roll moves the ring, disturbs a clot and can displace an unstable spine. The lay first aider does not roll a patient alone, since no lone manual handling technique is safe where a pelvic or spinal injury is suspected. The pelvis and the spine are injured together often enough that a pelvic injury is treated as a spinal injury until the spine has been cleared by someone qualified to clear it, the neck is kept in line while the airway is managed, and a collar is applied according to the local protocol. Clearing the spine is a clinical decision made with examination and imaging together, and it is never made by a responder at the roadside.

Cold is a complication of trauma and not a detail of comfort, and the patient with a pelvic injury is at risk from the moment of the crash. The patient lies on the road or on a hard floor, clothing is cut away so that the pelvis can be seen, blood is lost from the circulation, wet clothing holds the cold against the skin, and the back of an ambulance loses its warmth every time the doors open. Cooling makes the clotting system fail, so a patient who is allowed to become cold bleeds more and then bleeds still more, and that spiral is difficult to interrupt once it has begun. Prevention starts with insulation from the ground, continues with the removal of wet clothing and its replacement with dry covering, includes covering the head and the hands, and includes warming the vehicle before the patient is loaded. Warmed fluids are a physician's decision within the local protocol, and the temperature is measured wherever the equipment allows, because a falling temperature is a finding to be reported rather than a detail to be tolerated.

The neurovascular examination is performed and recorded before the binder is applied and again after it is applied, and it is repeated at every reassessment because the trend of the findings matters more than any single set. The examiner feels for the pulse at the back of the ankle and on the top of the foot, notes the color and the warmth of the skin, presses a nail briefly to watch the color return, tests sensation on the front and the back of the leg and in the region that would touch a saddle, and asks the patient to move the ankle and the toes. The result is written with the time, before the intervention and after it, because a finding that was not recorded before a binder or a splint was applied cannot be attributed afterwards. A pulse that is present does not exclude serious bleeding inside the pelvis, and a pulse that is absent is an emergency that forces the fastest possible transfer. The lay first aider notices color, warmth, and whether the patient can move and feel, and reports a change at once, while the formal examination belongs to the trained responder or to the clinician.

Nerves that run through the pelvis can be injured by the broken bone, by the bleeding around it, or by pressure from a device, and the findings are easy to miss in a patient who is distracted by pain. The large nerve bundle that leaves the spine and passes through the pelvis supplies the muscles of the leg and the foot, and its injury may show as a foot that will not lift, a leg that feels numb, or a limb weaker than the other one. Numbness in the region that would touch a saddle, the inability to pass urine, and the loss of the tone of the anal sphincter are findings that suggest pressure on the lowest nerves of the spine, and they are urgent because that injury can become permanent within hours. Such a finding is reported immediately by whoever notices it, and it is acted upon by the clinician, since decompression and definitive care belong to a surgical service. A binder that is too high, too tight or badly centered can press on nerves as well, which is a further reason to check it whenever the patient is moved.

Circulation is judged from the whole patient rather than from the pelvis alone, and the signs are the ones taught in the first weeks of every clinical training. A pulse that rises, a blood pressure that falls, skin that is pale, cool and damp, a nail that stays white after it is pressed, thirst, restlessness and then confusion all point to a circulation that is losing ground. A single set of observations can look reassuring in a young patient who is compensating, and the patient who improves for a few minutes and then worsens again is often the patient who is bleeding once more. The observations are therefore repeated and compared, and the comparison is what is reported. The responder's part is to recognize the pattern early, to keep the patient warm and to move the patient toward blood and an operating room.

The decisions about fluid and blood belong to the physician and to the local protocol of the service, and this course describes the reasoning without ever supplying the prescription. Where the service authorizes it and the training certifies it, the paramedic or the nurse obtains access to a vein so that the physician's decision can be carried out without delay, and nothing is given on personal authority. Which fluid is used, how much is given and how quickly it runs are matters for the physician, who weighs the risk of continued bleeding against the risk of dilution and knows the stock of the hospital. Strategies differ between countries and between editions of the same guideline, and one service may deliberately keep the pressure lower than usual until bleeding is controlled while another follows a different path. The same boundary applies to every medicine that might be considered around a pelvic injury, whether for pain, for infection or to support the clotting of blood, because the substance, the amount and the way it is given belong to the prescriber and to the protocol. What the responder can do is the same in every service, which is to recognize shock, to keep the patient warm, to protect the access the protocol permits, and to say aloud that the patient is deteriorating.

Immobilization of a pelvic injury begins with the binder and continues with everything else attached to the pelvis. The legs are kept together and still, and the trained responder may bind the ankles and the knees to one another with padding between them, provided the local protocol permits it, since a leg that swings freely moves the ring. The patient is lifted and carried on a device that supports the pelvis and the spine together, used by people who have practiced with it. A deformed limb is splinted in the position in which it was found and is never straightened in the field, because vessels and nerves lie against the bone and a movement that looks helpful can tear what remained intact. Where a thigh bone is broken as well, a traction splint may be used by a responder trained and authorized to use one, and the technique belongs to supervised practice. Nothing is achieved by pulling on a leg to make a pelvis look straighter, and definitive alignment belongs to the operating room.

Imaging for a pelvic injury begins with a radiograph of the pelvis, which is usually made early in a patient with major trauma because it answers a question that changes what is done. The single picture shows the ring from the front and reveals gross displacement, and computed tomography then shows the ring in three dimensions, the joints of the pelvis, the sacrum and the volume of blood collected behind the lining of the abdomen. The decision to image, and the choice of study, belong to the clinician responsible for the patient, and that decision is never made by the lay first aider or by the responder. The binder is left in place while the pictures are made unless a clinician directs otherwise, and the team that makes the images is told that it is there. No image replaces the examination, and a first radiograph that looks normal does not exclude a fracture of the ring, because some breaks are seen only on another view or on a later study.

Reassessment happens after every intervention, and it is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment rather than the complication of the injury. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the record, so that the direction of travel is visible to whoever reads it next. Several findings should stop the responder and force an escalation, and they include pain that increases rather than settles, new numbness or tingling, a foot that becomes pale or cool, a pulse that disappears, an abdomen that becomes distended, a patient who becomes confused, and a binder that has slipped, loosened or become too tight. Pain and swelling in a thigh that is tightly bandaged may mean a compartment syndrome, which is a clinical diagnosis and an emergency, and the response within the local protocol is to release whatever is constricting the limb, to keep the limb at the level of the heart rather than above it, to keep the patient warm, and to refer urgently, because time is muscle. A responder who suspects that condition does not wait for confirmation and does not watch quietly.

An open pelvic fracture is one in which the bone or the deep tissues have communicated with the outside, and the wound may lie in the perineum, in the buttock, in the groin, or within the rectum or the vagina where it is not seen at a glance. The part played by the responder is limited and precise, which is to cover the wound with a sterile dressing, to control the bleeding that can be controlled, to record the neurovascular status, to immobilize the pelvis, to keep the patient warm and to arrange urgent transfer. A wound is never explored blindly, an embedded object is never removed, and exposed bone is never pushed back into the wound, because the object may be holding pressure on a torn vessel and because the deep tissues are contaminated from the moment of the injury. Cleansing, removal of dead tissue and definitive closure belong to the operating room, and they are done early by the surgical team rather than improvised at the roadside. The responder says clearly what was seen, what was covered and what was left alone.

Referral or definitive treatment is the last step of the sequence, and for a patient with a pelvic injury the question is rarely whether to refer but where, how and how soon. The destination that can treat this injury has imaging, an operating room, an intensive care unit and, where such a service exists, the ability to block a bleeding vessel from inside the artery without opening the abdomen or the pelvis. Not every hospital has all of those, so the nearest hospital is not always the right one, and the physician and the receiving center decide together and early. The responding team's part is to ask for that decision as soon as the injury is suspected, to prepare the patient while the decision is made, and to follow the destination the system names rather than the destination that seems convenient. Figures quoted for transfer times are illustrations to be checked against the guideline in force and the local protocol, since the geography of one region is not the geography of another.

The preparation for transfer is a short list of acts performed in a fixed order, and the order is part of the treatment. The first is to control what can be controlled where the patient lies, which means bleeding, airway, breathing, warmth, the binder and any splint. The next is to make the record complete, with the time of the injury, the mechanism, the position in which each limb was found, the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention and the trend of the pulse, the pressure and the level of consciousness. Only then is the patient moved onto the carrying device, with the spine protected and the pelvis supported, and only then is the journey begun with the monitor, the covering and the access that the protocol provides. During the journey the observations are repeated, the binder and the splints are checked whenever the patient is handled, and any change is reported before arrival rather than at the door. A patient who becomes unstable on the way is a patient for whom the receiving team must be warned in advance.

The handover carries the story rather than a set of numbers, and it is spoken in an order that the receiving team can follow without having to ask. It states how old the patient is, what happened and when, what was found on the primary survey, what the pelvis looked like and whether the ring moved, what the neurovascular status was before the binder and after it, what was done and by whom, what has changed since, and where the patient was going before arriving here. Everything given to the patient is named with the person who authorized it, which is why a spoken chapter gives no amount and no schedule. A handover that ends with the question of what the receiving team would like next respects the fact that the definitive decisions belong to them.

Children, older adults and pregnant patients change the weight of the findings without changing the sequence. A child's pelvis is more elastic than an adult's, so it takes greater force to break, and a break in a child is therefore a marker of severe energy and a reason to look harder for injuries elsewhere. An older adult may break the pelvis in a fall from standing height, may bleed slowly and for a long time, and may be taking a medicine that prevents clotting, so the history of those medicines is part of the assessment and is reported to the physician. A pregnant patient carries two patients, and the care of the mother and of the child must be planned together, with the position of the mother on the carrying device chosen according to the local protocol and the stage of the pregnancy. In each of those patients the threshold for immobilization, for warmth and for urgent transfer is lower rather than higher.

A constructed illustration may help to hold the sequence together, and it is invented for teaching rather than drawn from any real patient. A motorcyclist is thrown from the machine, is awake and pale, and has a fast pulse and a leg that lies turned outward, while the responder keeps the head in line and the team applies a binder at the level of the greater trochanters over the clothing. The patient is rolled once onto a vacuum mattress after the back has been examined, wet clothing is cut away and replaced with dry covering, and the ambulance is warmed before the doors close. The neurovascular findings of both feet are written down before the binder and after it, warmth is maintained through the journey, the receiving hospital is warned, and the handover ends with the trend of the observations rather than with the fracture. Nothing in that illustration is a technique, and every act in it was performed by someone trained and authorized to perform it.

What a listener must never do is easier to state than what to do, and it is worth saying plainly before the summary. A deformed limb is never straightened in the field, the pelvis is never sprung repeatedly to confirm a finding, a pelvic binder is never removed by someone who is not qualified to decide what happens next, a wound is never explored blindly, an embedded object is never removed, exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, and no medicine is given on personal authority. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and no part of this course teaches them as techniques to be acquired by listening. Nothing said here replaces training, certification, supervision, the protocol of the service or the law of the country in which the listener works.

To summarize, a pelvic injury is a hemorrhage problem before it is a bone problem, because the ring surrounds large veins and an artery-rich space that can hold a great volume of blood while the skin stays unmarked. The order is always the same, and it is to assess, to stabilize, to examine and to record the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, with the provider level named for every act. The pelvic binder is placed at the level of the greater trochanters and not on the crests of the pelvis, it is a bridge to definitive care and not a treatment, and it is not removed by anyone who is not qualified to decide what comes next. The log roll is performed once, by a team, with the spine protected, and the pelvis is never sprung or rocked repeatedly. Cold is treated as a complication and not as a detail, the spine is treated as unstable until it is cleared by someone qualified to clear it, and the decisions about fluid, blood and medicine belong to the physician and to the local protocol. The patient is reassessed after every intervention, and the destination is a center that can image the injury, operate on it and care for it in an intensive care unit.
