# Chapter 1 — Foundations of Musculoskeletal Trauma

Musculoskeletal trauma is the injury that most patients survive and then live with, so it deserves the same care as the injuries that kill within minutes. A broken wrist, a dislocated shoulder and a bruised thigh rarely kill in the first hour, yet each of them can end a career or the independence of a person who was healthy the day before. What decides the outcome is not the fracture alone but every tissue the energy passed through, because the soft tissues around the bone are the hardest to see and to repair. Every chapter of this course follows one order, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

Bone is a living organ with a blood supply, a covering and a capacity to repair itself, and it is described in two kinds according to how tightly its material is packed. Cortical bone, also called compact bone, forms the hard outer shell and is thickest in the shaft of a long bone. Cancellous bone, also called spongy or trabecular bone, forms a lattice of thin plates inside the ends of the bone, where it spreads the load of the joint across a wider area. The junction between the shaft and the end is the metaphysis, and in a child it is the region where the bone lengthens, so an injury there needs different attention than the same injury in an adult. These names are used in every later chapter, so they are worth learning slowly.

The periosteum is the tough fibrous sleeve that wraps the bone except where a joint surface is exposed, and it is far more than packaging. It carries blood vessels into the cortex, it holds the layer of cells that can make new bone, and it is the reason a fracture in a child may bend rather than break cleanly. In a child the sleeve is thick and lifts from the bone without tearing, so a partly broken bone may stay stable because one side remains intact. In an adult the same sleeve is thinner and tears more readily, which takes away part of the blood supply and part of the healing capacity of the fracture. A fracture that strips the periosteum over a long distance is therefore more serious than its appearance on a radiograph suggests.

Every fracture bleeds, and the blood that collects between the broken ends forms a hematoma that is the first stage of healing rather than a complication to be drained. The size of that collection depends on the bone, and a break of the thigh bone can release enough blood to threaten the circulation, with a figure offered in teaching as an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. A closed fracture can therefore cause shock without a drop of blood on the skin, which is one reason a patient with a swollen limb and a rising pulse is treated as an emergency. The vessels inside the bone are damaged as well, and a segment of bone may lose its supply altogether, which is one route to a fracture that never heals. Nothing done in the field restores that supply.

A joint is the place where two bones meet, and in a synovial joint the surfaces are covered by articular cartilage, which is smooth, white, springy and has no blood supply of its own. It is nourished by the fluid that fills the joint, and because it cannot be repaired by the ordinary healing process that mends bone, damage to it is the injury most likely to cause years of pain and stiffness. A capsule lined with a membrane holds that fluid in place, and the capsule is strengthened by the ligaments that cross it. Blood inside a joint after an injury, which is called a hemarthrosis, makes the joint swollen, painful and hard to move, and that finding tells the clinician that something within the joint has torn. A joint that fills quickly with blood is not to be manipulated by anyone not qualified to examine it, because the examination itself can add damage.

Ligaments are bands of strong fibrous tissue that connect one bone to another and guide the joint through its range of motion. They resist a pull along their length, and they are injured when a joint is forced beyond the range they allow. The injury is described in words rather than numbers, from a stretch in which the fibers stay intact, through a partial tear in which the joint remains stable, to a complete tear in which the joint no longer holds. The responder notices the swelling and protects the joint, while the judgment about how severe the tear is belongs to a clinician, because a joint that feels loose to one examiner may feel firm to another. Testing the stability of a joint by pulling on it is a clinical skill learned under supervision, and it is not a field test.

Tendons connect muscle to bone and carry the pull of a muscle across a joint into a small area of bone. Because that area is small, the force arriving there during a sudden contraction can be enormous, and a tendon can pull a piece of bone away with it in an injury called an avulsion. Muscle is injured by direct blows, by stretching and by being torn, and the bleeding within a muscle can be considerable, so a thigh that is swollen and tense after a blow needs early assessment rather than exercise. Fascia is the strong sheet of fibrous tissue that wraps muscles, groups of muscles and whole compartments of a limb, and it is the structure that makes some injuries dangerous in a way that is not visible. Evaluating these injuries is the work of a clinician, while the responder recognizes the swelling, protects the limb and reports what has changed.

A compartment is a closed space bounded by bone and fascia, and it holds muscle, nerves and blood vessels with very little room to spare. When bleeding or swelling raises the pressure inside a compartment, that pressure is transmitted to everything within it, and the first structures to suffer are usually the muscle and then the nerve. Fascia does not stretch quickly, so the pressure keeps rising instead of dispersing, and the flow through the smallest vessels fails even while a large pulse at the wrist or the ankle can still be felt. This is why an intact pulse does not exclude a compartment syndrome, and why a limb that is tense, swollen and painful out of proportion to the injury is an emergency. The diagnosis is clinical and is made by a clinician, and time is muscle, so a responder who suspects it does not wait for a confirmation that will arrive too late.

Nerves and blood vessels travel along the bone rather than through the middle of a limb, and they often lie directly against its surface where muscle and fascia protect them. That arrangement is efficient in health and dangerous in injury, because a bone that breaks and displaces moves exactly where the vessel and the nerve are lying. A break above the elbow can injure the vessel that supplies the forearm, and a break behind the knee can injure the vessel that supplies the leg, and the nerves of the arm and the leg are exposed in the same way. The findings that suggest damage to a nerve are numbness, tingling, weakness and the loss of a movement the patient could perform a moment earlier. The findings that suggest damage to a vessel are pallor, coolness, a diminished pulse and pain out of proportion to the injury, so the field acts are to record them and to send the patient urgently for assessment.

The skin is the envelope of this system, and its state decides how the injury will be managed. When the skin is intact the fracture is called closed, and when the bone or the deep tissues have communicated with the outside the fracture is called open, whether the wound is large or small. An open fracture is contaminated even when it looks clean, because the skin carries organisms that reach the bone and the deep tissues in the moment of the injury. The part played by the responder is limited and precise: cover the wound with a sterile dressing, control the bleeding that can be controlled, record the neurovascular status, immobilize the limb in the position found, keep the patient warm, and arrange urgent transfer. The lay first aider performs the acts that need no equipment, and the trained responder works within the procedures the service authorizes. An embedded object is never removed and exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, because the object may be holding pressure on a torn vessel. Exploring an open wound blindly also adds harm, and definitive closure belongs to the operating room.

Every word that describes position in this course assumes one starting point, called the anatomical position, and every clinician uses the same one. It is a person standing upright, facing forward, with the arms at the sides, the palms turned forward and the feet together and pointing ahead. An injury is described as though the patient were standing in that position even when the patient is lying on a stretcher, so that a description written in one country means the same thing in another. A forearm with the palm turned forward is described as supinated, and the same forearm with the palm turned backward is described as pronated. Without a shared starting point, words such as near, far, up, down, inside and outside would change meaning with every patient, and a record would be useless to the person who reads it next.

Directional terms describe where one structure lies in relation to another, and they are always used in relation to the patient rather than to the observer. Proximal means closer to the trunk and distal means farther from it, so the elbow is proximal to the wrist and the ankle is distal to the knee, however the limb happens to be lying. Medial means toward the midline of the body and lateral means away from it, so the little finger is medial to the thumb when the palm faces forward. Anterior means toward the front of the body and posterior means toward the back, while superior means toward the head and inferior means toward the feet. Superficial means nearer the surface and deep means farther from it, and that difference matters when a wound lies close to fascia. Palmar refers to the palm, plantar to the sole and dorsal to the back of the hand or the top of the foot, and the same side of the body is described as ipsilateral while the opposite side is described as contralateral.

The body is also divided by three imaginary planes, and every description of displacement is placed within them. The sagittal plane runs from front to back and divides the body into a left part and a right part, and the midline sagittal plane divides it into two equal halves. The coronal plane, sometimes called the frontal plane, runs from side to side and divides the body into a front part and a back part. The transverse plane, also called the axial plane, runs horizontally and divides the body into an upper part and a lower part. A fracture may be described as angulated in the sagittal plane, in the coronal plane, or in both, and that tells the clinician which way the far end of the bone has tilted. Rotation is described about the long axis of the bone, which is the axis that the transverse plane cuts across.

The words used to describe how a broken bone has moved are few, and each one answers a different question. Angulation describes a bend at the fracture and is named for the direction in which the far end of the bone points, so a fracture may be angulated toward the front or toward the back. Translation, also called displacement, describes the broken ends sitting side by side rather than end to end, and it is expressed in relation to the width of the bone rather than in units of length. Shortening describes a limb that has become shorter because the ends have slid over one another, and distraction describes the opposite, in which the ends have been pulled apart. Rotation describes a twist about the long axis, and it is the displacement most often missed unless the examiner compares the two sides. Comminution describes a bone broken into more than two pieces, and it warns the listener that the soft tissues are more likely to have suffered.

A joint can also be injured without a bone breaking, and the words for that injury describe how far the joint surfaces have moved apart. A dislocation means the surfaces are no longer in contact at all, and a subluxation means they are only partly out of contact, and both are injuries of the joint as a whole. A joint that has dislocated has almost always torn its capsule and at least one ligament, and it may have injured the cartilage or the bone at the edge of the joint. The joint may slip back into place by itself, which is called a spontaneous reduction, and the examiner then sees only swelling and tenderness and must still consider the injury that has just happened. A dislocation left unreduced may damage the bone or the vessels that supply the head of the bone, and the decision about how and when to reduce it belongs to a clinician working within the local protocol.

The sequence this course follows in every chapter begins with assessment, and the order of the steps is deliberate because each one protects the next. Assessment begins with the scene, continues with the mechanism of injury, and then moves to the primary survey, whose steps are remembered as ABCDE, meaning airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure. Only when the patient is stable enough does the assessment turn to the limb, and the limb is assessed by looking, by feeling and by asking the patient to move it, never by forcing it. Stabilization comes next, and it means keeping the patient warm, controlling the bleeding that can be controlled, and supporting the limb in the position in which it was found. The neurovascular examination is performed before any intervention and repeated after it, and its result is written down, because a finding that was not recorded before a splint was applied cannot be attributed afterwards. Immobilization, imaging, reassessment and definitive care follow in that order, because the complication this course fears most is the one caused by the treatment.

Assessment is more than a search for a broken bone, because the guiding question is what the energy did rather than what the skin shows. The mechanism of injury, the age of the patient, the time of the injury and the illnesses and medicines that affect bone are all part of the history. A patient whose bone is weakened by disease or by long use of a medicine that thins it may break a bone in a fall that would leave a healthy person unhurt. When two correct actions compete for the same pair of hands, the principle that decides the order is that life comes before limb, because a limb can wait for minutes that a brain and a heart cannot. A falling level of consciousness, a changed pattern of breathing, a weak or rapid pulse and skin that has become cold and pale all send the responder back to the beginning.

Stabilization of a limb injury in the field is a small set of acts, and the most important of them is restraint. A deformed limb is supported and splinted in the position in which it was found and is never straightened in the field, because vessels and nerves lie against the bone and a movement that looks helpful can tear what remained intact. Pulling a limb straight may also convert a closed fracture into an open one and release the pressure that was holding a torn vessel closed. The lay first aider supports the limb with the hands, with a pillow or with whatever is at hand, keeps the patient warm and waits for help. The trained responder and the paramedic may apply a splint within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, and both levels protect the limb from further movement and from cold. Cold is a complication of trauma, so the patient is insulated from the ground and wet clothing is replaced with covering.

The neurovascular examination is never skipped and never assumed, and it is performed before the limb is splinted and again afterwards. The examiner looks at the color and the temperature of the skin, feels the pulse beyond the injury, presses briefly on a nail to watch the color return, and asks the patient to move the fingers and the toes. Sensation is tested on both sides and compared, and numbness, tingling or the loss of a movement is recorded in the words of the patient as far as possible. The result is written before the splint is applied and after it is applied, because a finding that was not recorded before an intervention cannot be attributed afterwards. A pulse that is present does not exclude serious injury to the vessels or to a compartment, and a pulse that is absent is an emergency. The lay first aider observes color, warmth and the ability of the patient to move and feel, while the formal examination belongs to the trained responder or to the clinician.

Immobilization means preventing movement of the injured part and of the joints above and below it, because a bone that is splinted alone can still be moved by the joint at either end. A splint is padded where it presses on bone, it is applied over the position found, and it is secured firmly enough to hold without becoming a band that stops the circulation. The material matters less than the principle, since a folded magazine, a padded board, a sling or the body of the patient can each serve, and an improvised device must be checked more often because it shifts. The trained responder and the paramedic apply splints within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, while the physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides what definitive immobilization is required, which may be a cast, a brace or a surgical fixation. Casting and surgical fixation are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and neither of them is taught from a book in this course.

Reassessment is performed after every intervention, and it is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the record, and the comparison is what has value rather than any single measurement. The limb is checked for color, warmth, pulse, sensation, movement and pain, and the splint is checked for tightness. Pain that increases instead of settling, pain out of proportion to the injury, numbness, tingling, weakness and a limb that becomes tense and swollen are the findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation. A responder who suspects a compartment syndrome does not wait for the diagnosis to be confirmed and acts within the local protocol, which may allow a constricting bandage or splint to be released by someone authorized to do it. The limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, the patient is kept warm, and the patient is referred urgently, because the diagnosis is clinical and time is muscle.

Imaging is the step that confirms what the examination has already suggested, and it is ordered by a clinician trained to interpret it. A radiograph is usually the first study, and it is taken in at least two directions so that a displacement in the third direction is not hidden, while the joint above and the joint below are included so that a second injury is not missed. Computed tomography shows the detail of a complex break, particularly around a joint, and magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for soft tissues, including ligaments, cartilage, tendons and muscle. Either study is requested when the answer it gives will change what is done, and the decision belongs to the clinician responsible for the care of the patient. The lay first aider never orders or interprets imaging, and the trained responder does not decide which study is needed. An image is a picture of one moment, and the patient must still be examined and reassessed after the picture has been taken.

Referral or definitive treatment is the final step of the sequence, and it begins with the recognition that some injuries cannot be managed where the patient lies. A patient with an open fracture, a suspected compartment syndrome, a dislocation, a damaged nerve or vessel, a fracture that enters a joint, or a fracture in a child that crosses the growing part of the bone is referred to a facility with imaging, an operating room and an intensive care unit. Definitive treatment includes reduction, which restores the position of the bone ends, traction, which holds them in line with a gentle continuous pull, and casting or surgical fixation, which holds the position while the bone heals, and every one of those acts is learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol. A surgeon may hold the fragments with metal devices, an operation called open reduction and internal fixation. A reduction must not be attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when the vessels or nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the injury is open or the diagnosis unclear, when the responder lacks training or equipment, or when an earlier attempt has failed. In each of those situations the attempt can convert an injury that would have healed into one that cannot, and force is not a technique.

Some patients break bones more easily and heal them less predictably. The bone of a child is more elastic than the bone of an adult, and it has a growth region near each end that can be injured without the bone breaking, so an injury that looks minor and leaves a child unwilling to use a limb deserves the same caution as an obvious break. An older adult whose bone has lost density may break a hip or a wrist in a fall from standing height, and the fracture may be far less painful than its severity suggests. A sign that is treated as reliable may be absent in these patients, and an examiner who relies on one sign alone will miss the injury. The response is not a different sequence but a lower threshold for immobilization and for referral, while the decisions about imaging and about treatment belong to the clinician.

The record and the handover are part of the treatment rather than a formality that follows it. The time of the injury, the mechanism, the position in which the limb was found, the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention, and any change in the condition of the patient are written or spoken in a fixed order, so that the receiving team continues a story instead of starting a new one. An injury of the spine is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has done so, and no lone manual handling technique is safe, so such a patient is moved by a trained team using the precautions the local protocol requires. Everything described in this chapter is learned with a teacher, practiced on models and on each other, and certified by an authority that also defines what may be done without supervision. Where this chapter names a time, a volume or a distance, it names an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between services and editions.

To summarize, a fracture is an injury of the whole limb rather than of the bone alone, and the tissues it travels through decide what the patient loses. Bone bleeds when it breaks, so a closed fracture can cause shock without a wound on the skin, and a nerve or a vessel may be torn while the skin stays intact, so the neurovascular status is recorded before any intervention and repeated after every one. The course follows one order in every chapter, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and it names the provider level for every act. A deformed limb is splinted in the position found and is never straightened in the field, an embedded object is never removed, exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, an open wound is never explored blindly, and definitive closure belongs to the operating room. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and a reduction is not attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, or when the vessels and the nerves are failing and need a specialist. Cold is treated as a complication, the spine is treated as unstable until it is cleared by someone qualified to clear it, and the patient is reassessed after every intervention and watched until the handover.
