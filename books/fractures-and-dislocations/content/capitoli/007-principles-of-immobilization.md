# Chapter 7 — Principles of Immobilization

Immobilization is the act of holding an injured part still, and in the sequence this course follows it comes after the neurovascular examination and before imaging. The whole order runs from assessment to stabilization, to the neurovascular examination, to immobilization, to imaging, to reassessment, and then to referral or to definitive treatment, and each step exists to protect the ones that follow. A limb that is held still cannot be moved by the patient, by the stretcher, by the vehicle or by the hands of the next person who touches it, and that single fact prevents a great deal of harm. Immobilization is also the step that most easily becomes a second injury, because a splint is a device applied by hand to a limb that is already damaged. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

Immobilization has four purposes, and a responder who knows them can improvise when the correct device is not there. The first is to prevent movement at the fracture itself, because the broken ends are the source of most of the damage that follows. The second is to protect the vessels and the nerves that run close to the bone, because those structures are injured by movement rather than by the original force alone. The third is to reduce pain, which is part of the treatment of the injury and not a comfort to be postponed until the end. The fourth is to limit bleeding, because every fracture bleeds and movement keeps the bleeding alive. Each of the four is a reason, and a rule that arrives with a reason attached is a rule that survives a difficult night.

A fracture is not a clean cut. The broken ends are irregular, and the sharp points of bone that appear on a radiograph are hard enough to cut whatever they are pressed against, which includes muscle, fascia, vessels and nerves. Every time the limb is moved, those ends travel through the tissues around them, and the damage is done a second time by hands that meant only to help. Movement can also push a bone end through the skin and turn a closed fracture into an open one, which changes the operation, the antibiotics and the risk of infection for the patient. A clot that has formed between the ends can be disturbed by movement, and bleeding that had stopped begins again. This is why the responder supports rather than examines, and why the first splint is applied to the position in which the limb was found.

Nerves and blood vessels travel along the surface of the bone rather than through the center of the limb, and they pass through places where there is very little room to spare. A break above the elbow can injure the vessel that supplies the forearm, a break behind the knee can injure the vessel that supplies the leg, and the nerves of the arm and the leg lie against the bone in the same exposed way, so the limb may look acceptable while a vessel is kinked or a nerve is stretched. A splint that holds the limb in the position found keeps those structures where they are, and a splint that pulls the limb into a different shape may be the force that tears them. Protecting the vessels and the nerves is therefore the second purpose of immobilization, and it is the one most easily defeated by trying too hard.

Pain is not only a symptom to be noted and set aside. It produces a reflex tightening of the muscles around the injury, that spasm pulls on the broken ends, and the movement increases the pain, so the limb enters a cycle that feeds itself. A limb held still breaks that cycle, and the relief of pain lowers the pulse and the demand on a heart that may already be struggling. Reducing pain is therefore part of the treatment of the injury. Nothing in this chapter allows a responder to give a drug, and where pain relief is needed, the substance, the amount and the route belong to the prescriber and to the local protocol.

Every fracture bleeds into the tissues around it, and the blood that collects forms the hematoma that is the first stage of healing rather than a complication to be drained. That collection also raises the pressure inside the limb, and the rising pressure is part of what slows the bleeding, so a clot knocked loose does more than make the limb swell again. Bleeding continues while the ends move against each other, and in a break of the thigh bone the amount lost can threaten the circulation, with a figure offered in teaching as an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. Immobilization limits that loss, which is why a swollen limb is splinted early rather than watched. A closed fracture can therefore cause shock without a drop of blood on the skin.

The rule that governs every splint in this course is that the limb is immobilized in the position in which it was found and is never straightened in the field. The reason is that vessels and nerves lie against the bone, and a piece of bone sitting near a vessel may be the only thing holding a tear closed. Straightening the limb also turns the soft tissue injury into a fresh one, and it can convert a closed fracture into an open wound on the way to the vehicle. The lay first aider supports the limb with the hands, with a pillow, with a rolled blanket or with whatever is at hand, and keeps it in the shape it already has. The trained first responder, the emergency medical technician and the paramedic apply a splint within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, while the physician and the orthopedic surgeon may later correct the position in a facility with imaging and with an operating room available.

A splint that covers only the fracture does not immobilize the fracture, because a long bone is moved by the joint at each end. The joint above the injury and the joint below it are held as well, because it is easy to forget them when the fracture is the only thing in view. The elbow moves the forearm and the wrist moves the hand, so an injury of the forearm is splinted with the elbow and the wrist included, while a splint for the lower limb reaches from well above the knee to below the ankle where the equipment and the local protocol allow it, because the knee and the hip control the thigh bone. Including both joints also protects the joint surfaces, which can be injured by the leverage of a bone that is still free to swing. There are limits, since in some injuries the two joints lie far apart or the position is so painful that a shorter splint is the safer choice, and the paramedic and the emergency medical technician decide within the procedures their service authorizes.

Padding is the part of immobilization that is most often skipped and most often regretted, because a splint presses on the limb where the bone lies just beneath the skin and the skin of an injured limb tolerates that pressure poorly. The pressure of an unpadded device can injure a nerve where it crosses a bony prominence, and the injury is silent at first because the patient may not be able to feel it. Padding spreads the load over a wider area, softens the edge of the device, takes up the small changes in swelling that follow an injury, and absorbs the sweat and the blood that would otherwise stiffen a bandage into a hard ring. It is placed over the bony prominences, over the joints within the splint and along the line of the device, and soft material is folded to a thickness that the local protocol describes, with any figure offered as an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline. The reason for padding is therefore not comfort but the prevention of a second injury caused by the treatment.

The splint is then secured firmly enough to hold and not so firmly that it becomes a band around the limb. A bandage can stop the flow through the smallest vessels long before it stops a pulse at the wrist or the ankle, and a device tightened until the limb below it changes color has been tightened beyond its purpose. After the splint is applied the limb is checked again, and the check is repeated at intervals because swelling changes the fit. Every service teaches a rhythm for that check, with a figure such as every 15 minutes offered as an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. The observations are the same ones made before the splint was applied, and a limb that becomes cooler, paler, more numb or more painful after a splint has been applied has been made worse by the splint.

The material of a splint matters far less than the principle, and that is the most useful thing a listener can carry into a place where nothing is stocked. A rigid device holds best, so a padded board, a rolled newspaper or a walking stick can serve a limb, while a pillow or a folded blanket serves a limb that must be kept in a bent position. A commercial splint is easier to secure, but it still needs padding and checking, and an unfamiliar device should not be used for the first time on a patient. An improvised splint is checked more often because it shifts and because its edges were not designed for skin, and the limb is never straightened to fit the device. An air splint and a vacuum splint mold themselves to the limb and are applied by trained responders within the procedures their service authorizes. A traction splint applies a gentle continuous pull along the line of the limb and is used mainly for an injury of the thigh bone, and it is applied only by a provider trained and authorized in its use, since a device that pulls the wrong way adds harm.

For an injury of the arm or the hand, the familiar arrangement is a sling that supports the forearm with the elbow bent, together with a swathe that binds the arm to the chest so that the shoulder cannot swing. The reason for both is that a sling alone leaves the shoulder free, and the shoulder is the joint above the injury. The hand is positioned so that the fingers point slightly upward where the injury and the local protocol allow it, because a hand that hangs downward swells more, and swelling is the enemy of a limb in a splint. The lay first aider can apply a sling and a swathe, and the trained responder checks the pulse, the color and the sensation in the fingers after doing so. A bandage applied in a crossing pattern is used for some injuries near the collarbone, and applying it correctly is a skill learned under supervision rather than from a description.

The lower limb presents a different problem because it has to carry weight and because the thigh bone is surrounded by powerful muscles that pull on the broken ends. When no splint is available, the injured leg can be bound to the uninjured leg, with padding between the knees and between the ankles, so that the healthy limb becomes the splint and the two legs move as one unit. That binding must be done carefully, because the uninjured leg must not be pulled into an awkward position, and the trained responder or the paramedic may instead choose a rigid splint or a traction device within the procedures the service authorizes. An injury of the hip or of the thigh bone is not treated by binding the legs together where a traction device and a trained provider are available, and the local protocol decides. Nothing here authorizes any provider to pull on a limb to make it straight.

An injury of the spine is treated as unstable until it has been cleared by someone qualified to clear it, and immobilization of the spine is a team task rather than an individual one. A cervical collar, head blocks and a rigid board are applied by trained responders and paramedics who have practiced the procedure, and the way the patient is turned and lifted is decided by the team leader according to the local protocol. The lay first aider does not move such a patient unless the scene is unsafe, and even then the movement is kept to the minimum that the danger requires. Neither walking after a collision nor the absence of pain clears the spine. The reason for the caution is that the spinal cord lies within a canal that a displaced vertebra can narrow, and a cord that is injured is injured permanently.

Cold is a complication of trauma and not a detail of comfort, and immobilization puts a patient on the ground and keeps him or her there for as long as the splinting takes. A patient who is losing blood tolerates cold badly, because cold worsens the clotting problem and drives the circulation away from the skin, and a cold patient is harder to assess at every step. Wet clothing is removed where the situation allows it and replaced with covering, and the patient is insulated from the ground as well as from the air, since the ground takes heat away faster than the air does. The lay first aider keeps the patient warm with whatever is at hand, and the trained responder carries the means to do it properly. Every figure about temperature or about time is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

No intervention in this chapter may be performed before the neurovascular status of the limb has been examined and written down. The examination is a short set of observations that a trained responder repeats in the same order every time, so that two records can be compared: the skin is inspected for color and temperature, the pulse is felt beyond the injury, a nail is pressed briefly and the color watched as it returns, and the patient is asked to move the fingers or the toes and to report what is felt. Sensation is compared from one side to the other, and any numbness, any tingling and any movement lost since the previous check are recorded in the words the patient uses where that is possible. The findings are entered before a splint is applied and again after it is applied, because an observation that was not written down cannot be attributed afterwards. The lay first aider can report color, warmth and whether the patient can move and feel, while the formal examination belongs to the trained responder, the nurse, the physician or the orthopedic surgeon according to the setting.

Reassessment follows every intervention, and it is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the previous record, and it is the comparison that carries the value rather than any single measurement. The limb is checked for color, warmth, pulse, sensation, movement and pain, and the splint is checked for tightness, for slippage, for wet padding and for pressure at its edges. A patient who says the limb feels worse after a splint has given the most useful answer in the whole examination, and that answer is not to be argued with. The responder who finds a change stops the plan that was being followed and reports it before continuing. A reassessment without a written comparison is a memory, and memory fails at the end of a long shift.

Compartment syndrome is the complication this course fears most, and an immobilized limb is one of the settings in which it develops. A compartment is a closed space bounded by bone and fascia, and it holds muscle, nerves and vessels with very little room, so bleeding or swelling inside it raises the pressure on everything within. Pain that increases instead of settling, pain out of proportion to the injury, numbness, tingling, weakness and a limb that has become tense and swollen are the findings that stop the responder. A pulse that is still present does not exclude the diagnosis, and pulselessness is a late sign that must never be awaited. When the condition is suspected, any constricting bandage or splint is loosened or released within the limits of local protocol by someone authorized to do it, the limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, the patient is kept warm, and the patient is referred urgently, because the diagnosis is clinical and time is muscle.

Imaging comes after immobilization, and the order matters because a limb that is still free to move will move while it is being positioned for a picture. A radiograph is usually the first study and is taken in at least two directions so that a displacement in the third direction is not hidden, while the joint above and the joint below are included so that a second injury is not missed. Computed tomography shows the detail of a complex break, particularly around a joint, and magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for soft tissues, including ligaments, cartilage, tendons, muscle and the spinal cord. Either study is requested when the answer it gives will change what is done, and the decision belongs to the clinician responsible for the care of the patient, while the lay first aider never orders or interprets imaging. An image is a picture of one moment, and the patient must still be examined again after the picture has been taken.

A splint applied in the field is not taken off until someone qualified to replace it decides that it should be, because the limb has just been carried through a journey and a removal repeats the injury. Where an image of the limb is required, the device is removed by the team that will put it back, or the study is arranged so that it stays in place. A cast, a brace and surgical fixation are the definitive forms of immobilization, and each is chosen by the orthopedic surgeon or by the treating physician according to the injury, the swelling and the plan for the bone. Casting is learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and the technique of it belongs to supervised clinical training rather than to a chapter of a book. A cast applied while the limb is still swelling can become a band that stops the circulation, so it is checked by the clinician who applied it, and the listener should not attempt any of these treatments.

Reduction means restoring the position of the bone ends, and traction means holding them in line with a gentle continuous pull while the bone heals or until an operation is possible. Both change the shape of the limb, and both are performed by a physician or an orthopedic surgeon with imaging, with analgesia decided by the treating team and with the option of an operating room. A reduction is not attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught within the joint, when the vessels or the nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, or when the responder lacks the training and the equipment or an earlier attempt has already failed, because in each of those situations an attempt can turn an injury that would have healed into one that cannot be repaired. Force is not a technique, and resistance is a signal to stop rather than a problem to overcome. The lay first aider never performs a reduction, and the trained responder supports and transports.

Referral is the last step of the sequence, and it begins with the recognition that some injuries cannot be managed where the patient lies. A patient with an open fracture, a suspected compartment syndrome, a diminished or absent pulse, a loss of sensation or movement, a dislocation, a fracture that enters a joint, heavy contamination of a wound, an unstable injury of the pelvis, or a break in a child that crosses the growing part of the bone is referred to a facility with imaging, an operating room and an intensive care unit. The urgency is decided by the findings rather than by the name of the diagnosis, and the neurovascular findings are what make a referral immediate. The record travels with the patient and is spoken in a fixed order, from the time of the injury and the mechanism, through the position in which the limb was found, to the findings before and after every intervention. The trained responder and the paramedic pass those findings to the receiving facility while the transport is being arranged, and a nurse or a physician who receives such a patient repeats the examination rather than trusting a report.

Some patients are immobilized with more care than others, and a sign that is reliable in a healthy adult may be absent in them. The bone of a child is more elastic than the bone of an adult, and it has a growth region near each end that can be injured without the bone breaking, so an injury that looks minor and leaves a child unwilling to use a limb deserves the same caution as an obvious break. An older adult whose bone has lost density may break a wrist or a hip in a fall from standing height, and the fracture may hurt far less than its severity suggests, while the skin of that patient tolerates the pressure of a splint poorly. A patient with diabetes or with an established neuropathy may not feel a splint pressing on the foot, so the absence of pain proves nothing, and a patient who is confused, sedated or unable to speak cannot report numbness at all. Such patients are checked by looking rather than by asking, and a swollen limb inside a cast applied before the swelling peaked is watched with particular care by the clinician responsible.

An illustration may help, and it is constructed for teaching rather than drawn from any patient. A middle-aged walker slips on wet ground and lands with the weight of the body on one hand; the wrist is bent, the skin is unbroken, and the fingers can be moved but the patient says the hand feels strange. The first aider supports the forearm with both hands in the position found, keeps the wrist as it lies, does not attempt to straighten it, and covers the limb to keep the patient warm. The trained responder records the color, the warmth, the pulse at the wrist and the sensation in the fingers, applies a padded splint that includes the wrist and the elbow, secures it firmly without making it a band, and then repeats the same observations. The hand is supported so that it does not hang down, and the receiving clinician is told what the findings were before the splint and after it.

Everything described in this chapter is learned with a teacher, practiced on models and on each other, and certified by an authority that also defines what may be done without supervision. The scope of practice differs between countries and between services within one country, and an act that a paramedic performs freely may be reserved to a physician elsewhere, so a listener confirms the local rule rather than borrowing one from another system. Courses that teach immobilization assess the learner on a real device, with feedback on padding, on the joints included and on the observations made before and after. A responder who has not practiced the skill supports the limb in the position found and calls for someone who has. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

To summarize, immobilization holds an injured part still in order to prevent movement at the fracture, to protect the vessels and the nerves, to reduce pain and to limit bleeding, and it is applied in the position in which the limb was found. The sequence never changes, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and the neurovascular findings are written down before any device is applied and again after every intervention, because a finding that was not recorded cannot be attributed afterwards. A deformed limb is never straightened in the field, a splint includes the joint above and the joint below the injury, and the device is padded where it presses and secured firmly enough to hold without becoming a band. Pain that increases, numbness, tingling, weakness, pallor, coolness or a limb that becomes tense and swollen means the splint is the problem, so the constricting part is released within local protocol by someone authorized to do it, the limb is not raised above the level of the heart and the patient is referred urgently, since time is muscle. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and they are not attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when the vessels or the nerves are failing, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear or when an earlier attempt has failed. Cold is treated as a complication, the spine is treated as unstable until it is cleared by someone qualified to clear it, every act is named with the provider level permitted to perform it, and where this chapter mentions a time or a distance it offers an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.
