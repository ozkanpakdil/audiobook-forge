# Chapter 49 — Principles of Dislocation Management

A dislocation is the injury in which the surfaces of a joint lose contact with each other, and it is best understood as an injury of the whole joint, because the capsule tears, at least one ligament tears, and the displaced bone end comes to rest where vessels and nerves are lying. The direction in which the bone end has moved decides which structures are at risk, and the state of the skin decides how urgent the injury has become. This chapter follows the order this course follows everywhere, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. It is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

A joint whose surfaces are only partly out of contact is described as subluxated, so the difference between a subluxation and a complete dislocation is one of degree rather than of kind. Many dislocated joints also tear the rim of the socket or pull a small piece of bone away with the capsule, which makes the injury a fracture and a dislocation at once, and that combination changes both the urgency and the plan. A joint can also slip back into place before anyone arrives, which is called a spontaneous reduction, so the examiner is left with a swollen, tender joint and no clear account of what happened. The absence of a visible deformity therefore proves very little, because the displaced bone end may already have returned to a position in which it still causes harm. A joint that has reduced by itself is examined, recorded, and referred in the same way as one that is still out of place.

Time matters in a dislocation for three separate reasons, and each of them runs on its own clock. The first is the blood supply of the bone end, because a bone that is levered out of its socket can stretch or tear the vessel that feeds it, and a bone end that loses its supply may die, which is called avascular necrosis. The second is the nerve and the vessel that travel against the bone, because they are compressed or torn by the displaced bone end and may recover once the pressure is relieved but not if the relief comes days later. The third is the cartilage, because joint surfaces that stay out of contact suffer changes that lead to pain and stiffness long after the injury has healed. Teaching often attaches a number of hours to the window before these risks rise, and any such figure is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between services and editions.

Before anything is done about the joint, the patient is assessed, and the assessment begins with the scene and with the mechanism of injury. The question that comes first is whether the patient is stable enough to be examined at all, and the steps of that primary survey are remembered as ABCDE, meaning airway, breathing, circulation, disability, and exposure. A patient who has fallen from a height may have injuries to the head, the chest, the abdomen, or the spine that matter more than any joint, and the principle that decides the order of work is that life comes before limb. The lay first aider looks for danger, calls for help, and does not move the patient unless there is an immediate threat. The trained first responder, the emergency medical technician, and the paramedic carry out the survey within the procedures their service authorizes, and only when the patient is stable does attention turn to the injured joint.

The history of a dislocated joint is short but worth taking carefully, and several of its details change what happens next. The time of the injury matters, because the length of time a joint has been out of place bears on the urgency of reduction, and the mechanism matters, because a fall onto an outstretched hand produces a different pattern of injury from a direct blow. The number of previous dislocations matters, because a joint that has dislocated before comes out again more easily, and a joint that has already been operated on behaves differently again. The illnesses and medicines of the patient matter, because joint disease, a connective tissue condition, and medicines that thin the bone all change the picture.

Examining the joint itself means looking, feeling, and asking the patient to move, and it never means forcing the limb to prove a point. The examiner looks at the shape of the limb compared with the other side, at the skin, and at whether the patient is holding the limb in a guarded position. Feeling is done gently, over the joint and along the bone above and below it, because tenderness far from the joint suggests a second injury. Asking the patient to move is different from moving the patient, and a joint is never put through a range of motion by the examiner in the field, because the movement can convert an injury that would have healed into one that cannot. Comparing the two sides is the single most useful habit, because the patient's own healthy limb is the best available reference. The lay first aider observes and reports, while the formal examination of the joint belongs to the trained responder and to the clinician.

Stabilization of a dislocated joint in the field is a small set of acts, and the most important of them is restraint. The limb is supported in the position in which it was found and is never straightened, because the vessels and the nerves lie against the bone and a movement that looks helpful can tear what remained intact. Bleeding that can be controlled is controlled with direct pressure, and an embedded object is never removed, because it may be plugging the hole in the vessel beneath it. An open wound is covered with a sterile dressing and is never explored blindly, and cold is treated as a complication of trauma, so the patient is insulated from the ground and the limb is kept warm while it waits. The lay first aider supports the limb with the hands or with a pillow, while the trained responder and the paramedic may apply a splint within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes.

The neurovascular examination is the step this course will not allow anyone to skip, and in a dislocation it is performed before any intervention and repeated after every one. The examiner looks at the color and the temperature of the skin beyond the injury, feels the pulse beyond the injury, and asks the patient to move the fingers or the toes that the injured joint controls. Sensation is tested on both sides and compared, and numbness, tingling, or the loss of a movement is recorded in the patient's own words as far as possible. The result is written before the support is applied and again after it is applied, because a finding that was not recorded before an intervention cannot be attributed afterwards. An intact pulse does not exclude serious injury to a vessel or a compartment syndrome, so a pulse that is present is reassuring rather than conclusive. The lay first aider observes color, warmth, and the ability to move and to feel, while the formal examination belongs to the trained responder or to the clinician.

The findings that suggest damage to a vessel are pallor, coolness, a pulse that is weaker than on the other side, and pain that is out of proportion to the injury. The findings that suggest damage to a nerve are numbness, tingling, weakness, and the loss of a movement that was present before. Which structures are at risk depends on the joint, because the nerve and the vessel lie against the bone in a different place at the shoulder than at the elbow, the hip, the knee, or the ankle. Knowing which one is most often injured at a particular joint tells the examiner what to test and what to watch, and that interpretation belongs to the clinician, while the responder records what is found and reports it without delay.

An associated fracture is looked for in every dislocation, and the search begins with the assumption that one is present rather than with the hope that one is not. The reason is simple physics, because the energy that dislocates a joint also acts on the rim of the socket and on the tendons that insert near it, so an avulsion of a small piece of bone is a common companion of a dislocation. A fracture may also be produced by the dislocation itself, as the displaced bone end strikes the edge of the socket on its way out, and the fracture may then be the reason the joint will not go back in. None of this can be told apart by looking and feeling alone, and a joint that seems to have simply come out of place may conceal a fragment lying between the surfaces. The responder therefore treats every dislocation as though a fracture were present, while the clinician confirms it with imaging.

When a dislocation and a fracture are present together, the injury is described as a fracture dislocation, and it is managed differently from a simple dislocation in almost every respect, because a fragment of bone may sit in the way of the returning bone end. An open dislocation, in which the joint has communicated with the outside, is contaminated even when the wound looks small and clean, because the skin carries organisms that reach the joint in the moment of the injury. The responder's part is to cover the wound with a sterile dressing, control the bleeding that can be controlled, examine and record the neurovascular status, immobilize the limb in the position found, keep the patient warm, and arrange urgent transfer. Blind exploration of the wound adds harm, exposed bone is never pushed back into the wound, and definitive cleansing and closure belong to the operating room.

Analgesia has a place in the care of a dislocation, and that place is decided by a prescriber rather than by the responder or by the patient's family. Pain relief is not a comfort measure alone, because severe pain produces muscle spasm that holds the joint out of place, and because pain masks the very findings that the neurovascular examination depends on. The finding that makes the decision urgent is pain that is severe, escalating, or out of proportion to the injury, and such pain is treated as a sign of a developing problem. The lay first aider does not give any medicine, and the trained responder gives only what the local protocol authorizes that level of provider to give, while sedation and strong analgesia belong to a physician. No dose, no route, and no regimen is given in this course, because the substance and the amount belong to the local protocol and to the prescriber.

Imaging is ordered when the answer it gives will change what is done, and its place in the sequence of a dislocation is dictated by that purpose. The first study is usually a plain radiograph, taken in at least two directions so that a displacement in the third direction is not hidden, and it includes the joint above and the joint below the injured one so that a second injury is not missed. Computed tomography shows the detail of a complex break around a joint, and it is often the study that reveals a fragment lying within the joint surfaces. Magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for the soft tissues, including ligaments, cartilage, tendons, and muscle. Each of these studies is requested by a clinician trained to interpret it, while the lay first aider never orders and never interprets imaging.

Whether reduction is needed urgently is a clinical decision, and it is made on findings. The findings that place a dislocation in the urgent group are a limb with a failing pulse, a limb with a new and persistent nerve deficit, a joint whose displacement threatens the skin from within, an open dislocation, and a joint whose blood supply is known to be fragile. A hip dislocation and a knee dislocation are treated urgently in most services for this reason, and any figure quoted for how quickly such a joint must be reduced is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. A dislocation that has been out of place for a long time, even without a failing pulse, is also treated with urgency, because the risk to the cartilage grows with every hour. The responder's task is not to make this decision but to recognize the findings that make it urgent and to move the patient toward the person who will make it.

Reduction is not attempted when the source lists a contraindication, and each contraindication carries its own reason. A suspected complex fracture dislocation is one, because a fragment may be caught between the surfaces and the attempt may drive it deeper or break more bone, and entrapped fracture fragments are another, for the same reason. Significant neurovascular compromise that needs a specialist is a third, because the specialist may need to repair a vessel, and a reduction performed on the way to the operating room may destroy the tissue that was to be repaired. An open injury is a fourth, because the wound must be managed in an operating room. An unclear diagnosis, inadequate training or equipment, and a previous failed attempt complete the list, and in every one of those situations the correct act is to stop rather than to try harder. Force is not a technique, and resistance during an attempt is a signal to stop and to escalate.

Reduction within the limits of scope of practice means that the person who reduces a joint is the person the local protocol and the law authorize to do it. Reduction is the act of restoring the joint surfaces to their normal relationship, and it is done to relieve the pressure on the vessels and on the nerves, to restore the blood supply to the bone end, to protect the cartilage, and to make the joint stable again. It is performed by a physician or an orthopedic surgeon, or by an emergency physician or an advanced practitioner whose service has granted that privilege, and it is not performed by the lay first aider, the trained first responder, or the emergency medical technician. The technique itself, with its holds, its direction of pull, and its timing, is learned under supervision on real patients and is not taught from a book in this course or in any other. A joint that cannot be reduced safely in one place is moved to another.

What can go wrong during a reduction is worth stating plainly, because a responder who understands the risks will not be tempted to improvise. The bone can break during the attempt, particularly when it is fragile or when a fragment is already present, and a closed injury can be converted into an open one. A nerve or a vessel that was stretched can be torn, the cartilage can be scuffed, and a fragment can be pushed into the joint. A reduction performed without adequate relaxation may fail and leave the joint worse than before, and repeated attempts are more dangerous than the first one. The reason this course describes the risks rather than the method is that the method belongs to supervised clinical training, and the risks are what a listener needs in order to recognize when to call for help instead of acting.

The setting in which a reduction is performed is part of the decision, not a detail that follows it. Adequate lighting, monitoring of the patient's breathing and circulation, suction, oxygen, and a plan for what will be done if the attempt fails are the ordinary requirements of a procedure that may suddenly become an emergency. Muscle relaxation is often what makes a reduction possible at all, and the medicines that produce it belong to a physician working under the local protocol. In some services a large joint is reduced in an operating room with an orthopedic surgeon present, and in others it is reduced in an emergency department by a physician with the equipment the protocol requires.

Reassessment is performed after every intervention, and in the management of a dislocation it is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the record, and it is the comparison rather than any single measurement that has value. The examiner checks the color, the warmth, the pulse, the sensation, the movement, and the pain, and checks the support or the splint for tightness. The findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation are pain that increases instead of settling, pain that is out of proportion to the injury, numbness, tingling, weakness, and a limb that becomes tense and swollen. A pulse that disappears after a reduction is an emergency, and so is a limb that becomes pale and cool, because both suggest that the reduction has disturbed the circulation rather than restored it.

Compartment syndrome is the complication this course fears most after a dislocation, and the reason is that it can begin quietly and progress while everyone is reassured by a pulse. The pressure inside a closed space of the limb rises after the injury or after the reduction, and the flow through the smallest vessels fails even while a large pulse at the wrist or the ankle can still be felt. The diagnosis is clinical and is made by a clinician, and time is muscle, so a responder who suspects it does not wait for a confirmation that will arrive too late. The constricting bandage, splint, or cast is released within the limits of the local protocol and by someone authorized to do it, the limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, and the patient is kept warm and referred urgently. The finding that most often gives the diagnosis away is pain when the muscles of the compartment are stretched, and that test is one a clinician performs.

Immobilization of a dislocated joint means preventing movement of the joint and of the bones above and below it, because a joint that is splinted alone can still be moved by the limb around it. The support is padded where it presses on bone, it is applied over the position in which the joint was found, and it is secured firmly enough to hold without becoming a band that stops the circulation. The material matters less than the principle, and a folded magazine, a padded board, or the body of the patient can each serve the purpose. After a reduction the joint is often unstable in one direction, and the support is chosen to block that direction, which is a clinical judgment rather than a household one. A support that is too tight is as dangerous as no support at all, because it can produce the very compartment syndrome the immobilization was meant to prevent. The responder checks the fingers or the toes after the support is applied and records what was found.

The scope of practice for immobilization differs by provider level, and it is worth naming at each step. The lay first aider supports the limb with the hands and with whatever padding is at hand, keeps the patient warm, and waits for help. The trained first responder, the emergency medical technician, and the paramedic apply splints and supports within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes. The nurse prepares the materials, monitors the limb, and reports changes, while the physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides what definitive immobilization is required, which may be a cast, a brace, a sling, or a surgical fixation. Casting is a technique learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and it is not taught from a book. The position in which a joint is held after a reduction, and the length of time it is held there, are clinical decisions as well, and every figure quoted for that time is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline.

Imaging again after a reduction is not optional, and the reason is that the clinical appearance of a reduced joint can be reassuring when the radiograph is not. The joint surfaces may be back in contact while a fragment lies between them, or the reduction may be incomplete in a way that is not visible from outside, or a fracture that was hidden before the reduction may now be obvious. The post-reduction study is taken in the same directions as the pre-reduction study so that the two can be compared, and it is interpreted by the clinician responsible for the care of the patient. The responder's part is to keep the limb supported and the patient still while the image is taken, and to report any change in the neurovascular findings. Reassessment continues after the imaging, because the sequence followed in this course is not a line that ends but a loop that returns to the patient.

Referral is the final step of the sequence for most dislocations, and it begins with the recognition that some injuries cannot be managed where the patient lies. The patients who are referred without delay include the one with an open dislocation, the one with a fracture dislocation, the one with a nerve or vessel deficit that has not recovered, the one whose joint will not stay reduced, and the one who arrives long after the injury. The receiving facility needs imaging, an operating room, and access to an intensive care unit, because a dislocation can become a vascular emergency within minutes of arrival. The receiving team is given the same story that the record tells, with the time of the injury, the position found, the findings before and after each intervention, and the treatment already given, because a referral that omits what was done invites the next team to repeat it. A dislocation of the spine is treated differently from a dislocation of a limb, since it is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear the spine has done so, and no lone manual handling technique is safe.

Children are a separate case, because the bone of a child is still growing, and the end of a long bone contains a growth region that can be injured without the bone breaking. The threshold for immobilization and for referral is lower in a child, and the threshold for reduction is higher, because a careless attempt can change the growth of the limb for years. An older adult presents a different difficulty, since a bone that has lost density can break with little force, and a joint that has been replaced with a prosthesis can dislocate in a way that belongs to the surgeon who knows the implant. A patient who has dislocated the same joint several times is describing a third kind of problem. The plan in all three of these groups is made by a clinician, while the responder protects the limb, keeps the patient warm, and moves the patient toward that person.

Several errors recur in this work, and each of them has a reason attached. The first is straightening a deformed limb in the field, which risks the vessels and the nerves that lie against the bone, and the second is attempting a reduction more than once after a failed attempt, which adds damage with every repetition. The third is delaying referral in order to obtain an image that will not change the immediate plan, and the fourth is failing to record the neurovascular findings, which makes it impossible to know afterwards whether a deficit is new. The fifth is attributing a new numbness or a new weakness to the original injury without having looked for it before the intervention. The patient is told what to watch for, which includes increasing pain, numbness, tingling, weakness, and a limb that becomes pale or cool.

To summarize, a dislocation is an injury of the whole joint, so the capsule, the ligaments, and often a piece of bone are injured with it, and the displaced bone end rests where vessels and nerves are lying. The sequence is always the same, which is to assess the patient, to examine and record the neurovascular status before and after every intervention, to look for an associated fracture, to relieve pain within the limits of the prescriber and the protocol, to image when the answer will change what is done, to decide whether reduction is urgent, to reduce only within the scope of practice that authorizes it, to reassess, to immobilize, and to image again afterwards. A deformed limb is splinted in the position found and is never straightened in the field, an embedded object is never removed, exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, an open wound is never explored blindly, and definitive closure belongs to the operating room. Reduction, traction, and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and a reduction is not attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught between them, when the vessels or the nerves are failing, when the injury is open, when the training or the equipment is lacking, or when an earlier attempt has failed. Compartment syndrome is treated as an emergency, the limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, cold is treated as a complication, and the spine is treated as unstable until it is cleared by someone qualified to clear it. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.
