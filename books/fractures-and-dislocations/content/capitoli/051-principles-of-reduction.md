# Chapter 51 — Principles of Reduction

Reduction is the act of restoring a displaced fracture or a dislocated joint to the position in which the surfaces meet again, and it is a principle rather than a procedure memorized from a page. The bone ends and the joint surfaces belong in a relationship that lets the limb work, and everything done before a reduction aims at reaching a place where a qualified clinician can restore that relationship safely. This chapter follows the order used in every part of this course, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. The technique of reduction is not taught here and cannot be learned here, because it is acquired under supervision, on living patients, with a teacher who can correct a hand before it does harm. What a page can teach is the reasoning around the act, which is what a reduction is meant to achieve, what must be examined before and after it, and which situations forbid it altogether. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

A reduction is performed for reasons that have little to do with how the limb looks on an image. Restoring the alignment of a bone, or the contact between two joint surfaces, reduces the tension that a displaced fragment places on the soft tissues around it, and that tension is what threatens the skin, the vessels and the nerves. A joint that has been out of place for hours may recover its blood supply only when the surfaces are put back into contact, because the vessels feeding the head of the bone are stretched until the position is restored. An aligned fracture is also easier to immobilize, to image and to assess, and a limb left in extreme deformity may lose the skin over the bone and become an open injury. None of that authorizes anyone to attempt the movement without the training, the equipment and the authorization the act requires, and the purpose of the act is a limb that can heal rather than a limb that looks tidy.

Time is one of the tissues in this injury, and the window in which a reduction is easiest is the window in which the limb is least swollen. Swelling begins within minutes, continues for many hours, and stiffens the soft tissues that a clinician must move through in order to bring the bone ends together. A joint reduced early is usually easier to hold than the same joint reduced after the tissues have filled with blood and fluid, and a fracture reduced after a long delay may need an operation that would not have been necessary at the beginning. Teaching often names a number of hours as a threshold, and any such figure is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because services and editions differ and the state of the limb matters more than the clock. What a responder can rely on is the direction of the pressure, since earlier is generally easier and a long delay with a tight, swollen limb is a reason to involve a surgeon rather than to try harder. The exception is the patient too unstable to be examined or moved, because the limb can wait for minutes that the airway and the circulation cannot.

Reduction begins with anatomy rather than with force, and the anatomy that matters most is the relationship between the bone and the soft tissues against it. Nerves and vessels do not run through the middle of a limb, where a moving bone could not reach them, but along the surface of the bone, in the plane between the bone and the fascia. The bone is therefore the safest guide in health and the greatest danger in injury, because a displaced fragment has already traveled through the space where a vessel or a nerve was lying. The capsule of a joint and the ligaments that strengthen it also decide how a dislocation behaves, since a torn capsule may allow the bone end to pass into a position from which it cannot return by the same path. Knowing which structures lie at risk in a particular region is the first act of preparation, and it is why reduction is learned region by region under supervision rather than as one universal movement.

Assessment comes before any decision about reduction, and it begins with the scene, the mechanism of injury and the patient rather than with the limb. The primary survey follows the order of airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure, because a patient who cannot breathe will not benefit from a straightened arm, and life comes before limb whenever two correct actions compete for the same pair of hands. The history adds what the injury cannot say, which is the age of the patient, the time of the injury, the force involved, and the illnesses or medicines that weaken bone or make sedation unsafe. The limb is examined by looking at it, feeling it gently and asking the patient to move whatever can be moved, never by forcing a movement in order to see what happens. A limb examined once and then left alone until someone qualified arrives has been examined correctly, while a limb tested repeatedly by several pairs of hands is being injured further. The lay first aider observes and supports, the trained responder and the emergency medical technician record what they see and immobilize within the procedures their service authorizes, and the physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides what the findings mean.

A displaced bone is rarely the only injury present, and the search for associated injuries is part of preparing for any reduction. The joint above and the joint below the fracture are examined and later imaged, because the energy that broke one bone often injured the next structure in its path. A fragment of bone may be pulled away by a tendon or a ligament, and a piece may come to lie inside a joint, where it blocks the surfaces from meeting and turns a straightforward reduction into an operation. The skin is inspected over the whole limb, because a small wound near a fracture changes the injury from closed to open and changes the urgency of everything that follows. A patient who has fallen from a height may have a fracture of the pelvis, the spine or the heel in addition to the limb that hurts most, and a patient struck by a vehicle may have injuries of the chest and the abdomen that matter more in the next hour. A dislocation may also be accompanied by a fracture of the same bone, which is one reason a reduction attempted without an image is a gamble rather than a treatment.

The neurovascular examination is performed before any attempt at reduction and repeated after it, and its result is written down before the next act begins. The examiner looks at the color and the temperature of the skin, feels the pulse beyond the injury, presses briefly on a nail to watch the color return, and asks the patient to move the fingers or the toes. Sensation is tested on both sides and compared, and any numbness, tingling or loss of movement is recorded in the words the patient used, because a summary such as reduced sensation loses what those words carry. A pulse that is present does not exclude serious injury to a vessel, since the flow through a limb can fail while a pulse at the wrist or the ankle remains, and a pulse that is absent is an emergency that changes every plan that follows. The record exists because a finding not written before an intervention cannot be attributed afterwards, and because the clinician who reduces the injury must know what the limb could feel and do before the attempt. The lay first aider reports what the patient says about feeling and movement and touches nothing that hurts, while the formal examination belongs to the trained responder, the nurse or the clinician.

Analgesia and sedation are part of humane fracture care, and they are also the part of it most tightly bound to authorization, monitoring and law. The decision to give a medicine belongs to a prescriber, which in most services means a physician or a senior nurse acting under a written protocol, and the choice of substance, the amount and the way it is given belong to that protocol rather than to any page of this course. What a listener may take from this chapter is the reasoning around the decision, which is that an injury severe enough to need a reduction is severe enough to need relief, and that a calm patient is easier to examine, easier to position and safer to move. Sedation for a procedure requires the equipment and the trained staff to watch breathing, blood pressure and consciousness, which is why it is performed where those exist and not in the back of a moving vehicle. The trained responder and the paramedic give only the medicines their service authorizes, while the lay first aider offers comfort, covering, calm and a steady hand and gives nothing at all. Nothing in this course names a substance, an amount or a route, and no listener should be able to take a prescription from it.

Reduction is a planned act performed by a prepared team in a place that has what the act requires, and that setting is part of the principle rather than a detail of comfort. The team needs someone qualified to perform the movement, someone to support and steady the limb, monitoring of breathing and circulation where sedation is used, and a plan for what happens if the attempt fails. The environment needs light, space, warmth and enough quiet for the patient to be heard, because a frightened patient holds the muscles tight and a limb held tight is harder and more dangerous to move. Authorization decides who may do each part, so the nurse may prepare, monitor and document, the trained responder and the paramedic may support, splint and transport, and only the physician, the emergency physician or the orthopedic surgeon performs the reduction itself within the local protocol. Where no such clinician is available, the correct action is not improvisation but stabilization, documentation and the fastest safe transfer to a facility that has one.

There are situations in which the correct decision is to leave the deformity exactly as it was found, and a listener must recognize them before any movement of the limb is considered. The first is the suspected complex fracture with a dislocation, in which a fragment and a joint have both lost their normal relationship, because the path a reduction would take cannot be known without imaging and an operating room. The second is an entrapped fragment or a capsule caught in the gap, where soft tissue has closed around the bone and holds it, since pulling harder will not free it and will tear what is still intact. The third is a limb whose vessels or nerves are failing, because that situation calls for a specialist with instruments and a surgical theater rather than an attempt in a corridor. The fourth is an open injury, where the attempt may push contamination deeper and where the wound and its closure belong to a surgeon. The fifth is an unclear diagnosis or an image not yet obtained, the sixth is a responder without the training or the equipment the act requires, and the seventh is an earlier failed attempt, because repetition adds swelling and makes the next trial harder for everyone. In any of those situations the limb is supported in the position found, the neurovascular status is recorded, the patient is kept warm, and the patient is referred urgently.

Stabilization is what happens while the decision about reduction is being made, and it is deliberately a small set of acts. A deformed limb is supported and splinted in the position in which it was found and is never straightened in the field, because a movement that looks helpful can tear the vessel or the nerve that was still intact and can convert a closed fracture into an open one. The lay first aider supports the limb with the hands, with a pillow or with whatever is at hand, keeps the patient still and warm, and waits for help. The trained responder and the paramedic may apply a splint, a sling or a support their service authorizes, and a traction device is used only for the injuries and by the providers named in the local protocol, never as a general remedy for a painful leg. Bleeding that can be controlled is controlled with direct pressure over a dressing, an embedded object is left in place because it may be holding pressure on a torn vessel, and exposed bone is covered rather than pushed back into the wound. Cold is treated as a complication of trauma rather than a detail of comfort, so the patient is insulated from the ground and wet clothing is replaced with covering.

Immobilization after a reduction protects what the reduction achieved, and its principle is that the joints above and below the injury must be held as well as the broken bone itself. A support that holds only the fracture leaves the bone free to move at either end, and that movement can displace the fragments all over again. Padding is placed where the device presses on a bony prominence, and the device is secured firmly enough to hold without becoming a band that stops the circulation. Casting is one form of immobilization, and it is learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, because a cast applied too loosely fails and a cast applied too tightly damages the limb it was meant to protect. The physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides which form of immobilization is appropriate and for how long it is worn, while the trained responder and the paramedic apply the temporary devices their service authorizes. The patient is told what to watch for and when to return, and anyone who cannot be given that instruction reliably needs a place where they can be observed.

Imaging is the step that turns a clinical impression into a plan, and it is ordered by a clinician trained to interpret it. A radiograph is usually the first study, and it is taken in at least two directions so that a displacement lying in the third direction is not hidden, with the joint above and the joint below included so that a second injury is not missed. Computed tomography shows the detail of a complex break, particularly where a joint surface is involved or where fragments must be counted, and magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for soft tissues such as ligaments, cartilage, tendons and muscle. The image that matters most before a reduction is the one that shows where the fragments and the joint surfaces actually lie, because a decision taken without it is a decision taken blind. Whether a reduction is attempted before or after imaging depends on the urgency of the injury and on what the facility can provide, and that judgment belongs to the most senior clinician present acting within the local protocol. An image is a picture of one moment, so the limb is examined and reassessed after the picture has been taken, not instead of it.

A controlled reduction is one in which the clinician knows what the movement is meant to achieve before it begins, and that knowledge is the difference between a technique and a guess. In general terms, the movement follows the path that the bone or the joint took, in reverse, applied slowly and steadily rather than suddenly, with the limb supported so that the patient's own muscles are not left pulling against the hand. An assistant may provide countertraction at the trunk or at the proximal part of the limb so that the force is balanced rather than spent on sliding the whole patient across the stretcher. The clinician watches the limb and the patient rather than the clock, and recognizes the moment of reduction by the change in the contour of the limb and by the easing of the resistance under the hands. None of that description is a technique, and it is deliberately not one, because the angle, the handhold, the direction and the amount of force differ for every region and every patient. The technique belongs to supervised clinical training and to the local protocol, and a listener who has never performed a reduction under a teacher has not learned it from this paragraph.

Force is not a technique, and resistance is information rather than an obstacle to be overcome. When the limb will not move along the expected path, the usual causes are a fragment caught in the joint, a capsule or a tendon trapped in the gap, a fracture whose ends have interlocked, or a diagnosis that is wrong, and in each of those cases more force makes the injury worse. A clinician who meets resistance stops, reassesses the neurovascular status, looks at the image again, and either waits for better conditions or refers the patient onward. Repeated attempts deserve particular caution, because each one adds swelling, and swelling makes a joint harder to reduce and a compartment more likely to fail. A joint that reduces and then dislocates again is telling the listener that the injury is unstable and that holding it will need more than hands. Crepitus felt during an attempt is not a sign of success, and the absence of a satisfying click does not justify another pull.

Reassessment is performed after every intervention, and after a reduction it is the step that decides whether the patient improves or travels. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the record made before the attempt, which means color, warmth, pulse, sensation, movement, and the pain the patient reports. Pain that increases instead of settling, pain out of proportion to the injury, numbness, tingling, weakness and a limb that becomes tense and swollen are the findings that stop the clinician and prompt escalation. A limb reduced into a splint or a cast is checked with particular care, because a device that is comfortable at first can become constricting as the limb swells. A finding that is worse after the intervention is a reason to undo the intervention or to escalate, never a reason to wait and see.

A compartment is a closed space bounded by bone and fascia, and it holds muscle, nerves and vessels with very little room to spare. When bleeding or swelling raises the pressure inside such a space, that pressure is transmitted to everything within it, and the first structures to suffer are usually the muscle and then the nerve. A responder who suspects a compartment syndrome does not wait for a measurement or for the diagnosis to be confirmed, and acts within the local protocol so that a constricting bandage, splint or cast is released by someone authorized to release it. The limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, because raising it can lower the pressure that is still pushing blood through the smallest vessels, and the patient is kept warm and still. The patient is then referred urgently to a facility that can measure the pressure and, if it is needed, open the compartment in an operating room. The diagnosis is clinical and time is muscle, so the interval between suspicion and surgical care is the interval that decides what the limb can do afterwards.

The record and the handover carry more weight after a reduction than at any other point in the sequence, because the next clinician must be able to tell what the limb was like before the attempt and what changed after it. The time of the injury, the mechanism, the position in which the limb was found, the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention, the imaging obtained, and the patient's own description of pain and sensation are written or spoken in a fixed order. An injury of the spine is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has done so, and no lone manual handling technique is safe, so a patient with a possible spinal injury is moved by a trained team using the precautions the local protocol requires. Some patients need a lower threshold for referral rather than a different sequence, because the bone of a child bends and its growing regions can be injured without an obvious break, so a child who will not use an arm after a fall deserves the same caution as a child with a visible deformity. An older adult whose bone has lost density may break a hip in a fall from standing height with surprisingly little pain, and the injury may be far less dramatic than its consequences. In those patients a sign that is usually reliable may be absent, and an examiner who trusts a single sign will miss the injury.

Referral or definitive treatment is the last step of the sequence, and it begins with the recognition that some injuries cannot be managed where the patient lies. A patient with a complex fracture and dislocation, an entrapped fragment, an open injury, a failing vessel or nerve, a fracture that enters a joint, or a fracture that crosses the growing part of a child's bone is referred to a facility with imaging, an operating room and an intensive care unit. Definitive treatment may be a reduction held by a cast, a brace or an external support, or an operation in which a surgeon holds the fragments with metal devices, a procedure called open reduction and internal fixation. Every one of those acts is learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and none of them is taught from a book, including in this course. Traction, which holds a limb in line with a gentle continuous pull, is likewise a supervised technique with its own indications and its own dangers, and it is not a field remedy to be improvised.

Consider a constructed illustration rather than a real patient, since no example in this course describes a real person or a real institution. A middle-aged person falls from a ladder and arrives with a deformed forearm, an intact pulse at the wrist and numbness along the thumb side of the hand, so the limb is supported in the position found, the findings are written down, and the image shows a fragment lying inside the joint. The correct decision in that illustration is not to attempt a reduction at the bedside, because the fragment blocks the surfaces and the numbness warns that a nerve is already involved, so the limb is immobilized as found and the patient is referred for an operation. A second illustration might show a similar deformity with no fragment in the joint, in a facility with imaging and a clinician trained in that region, where the reduction is performed under appropriate pain relief and the limb is reassessed immediately afterwards. The two situations look alike from across the room and are managed differently, and that difference is exactly why the decision belongs to a clinician who has the image and the examination in front of them.

A few limits are worth stating plainly before this chapter closes. This course describes principles and the order in which they are applied, and it deliberately does not describe the movements, the handholds or the forces that a reduction requires. Every number it names, whether it is an interval of hours, a distance or a threshold, is an illustration whose value differs between services, countries and editions and must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol. The rules that decide who may perform a reduction are not the same everywhere either, so a listener works within the authorization, the equipment and the law of the place where they practice. Where the local answer is unclear, the safest course is to stabilize, to record, to protect the limb and the patient from cold, and to hand the patient to someone who is qualified to decide.

To summarize, reduction is the restoration of the normal relationship between bone ends or joint surfaces, and it is a principle of care rather than a movement that a listener learns from a book. The sequence never changes, which is to assess the patient before the limb, to stabilize the limb in the position found, to record the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention, to immobilize the joints above and below the injury, to image when the image will change what is done, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, naming who is authorized to perform each act. A reduction is never attempted when there is a suspected complex fracture with a dislocation, an entrapped fragment, a failing vessel or nerve, an open injury, an unclear diagnosis, a lack of training or equipment, or an earlier failed attempt. Force is not a technique, resistance means stop, and a limb that is worse after an intervention is a reason to escalate rather than to try again. Pain relief and sedation belong to the prescriber and to the local protocol, the suspected compartment is acted on at once, the constricting device is released by someone authorized to release it, and the limb is kept at the level of the heart rather than above it, because time is muscle. This chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work, and reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol.
