# Chapter 39 — Cervical Spine Injuries

The neck is the narrowest and the most mobile part of the spine, and it carries the heaviest load per unit of bone in the body, which is the head. It lets the head turn, nod and look up, and for that freedom it gives up the protection the ribs and the trunk muscles give the rest of the spine. Inside it lies the spinal cord, with almost no room to spare around it. An injury here can kill within minutes by stopping the breathing, or leave a patient paralyzed from the neck down while the skin stays unbroken. Every chapter of this course follows one order, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and this chapter follows that order for the bones of the neck. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

The cervical spine is built from seven bones stacked in a curve that bows gently forward. The first is a ring rather than a block, and it carries the skull the way a hand carries a bowl. The second has a peg that points upward into that ring, and that peg is the pivot on which the head turns, which is why a break of the second bone is so often an injury of turning. The remaining bones have a body in front, a disk between one body and the next, and small joints at the back that guide each segment on the one below. Strong ligaments run along the front of the bodies and along the back of the canal, and one runs inside the canal behind the cord, where it limits how far the neck can bend forward. Because the neck moves more than any other part of the spine, it depends on those ligaments more than any other part does, and a torn ligament can be as dangerous as a broken bone.

Within the canal lies the spinal cord, and at every level a nerve root leaves the cord and passes out between two bones toward the arm. The cord carries the fibers that move and feel the arms and the hands, the fibers that serve the trunk and the legs, and the fibers that drive the diaphragm, the muscle of breathing. Those fibers for the diaphragm leave the cord high in the neck, so an injury at the top of the neck can stop the breathing while the heart is still beating, and an injury lower down can leave the breathing weak and the cough useless. Through channels in the bones pass the arteries that carry blood to the back of the brain, so a break that tears one of those arteries can cause a stroke hours or days after an injury that looked minor at the time. The cord is therefore not the only structure at risk in the neck, and a responder who thinks only of the bones will miss the vessels.

The mechanism of injury is the first thing that should raise the suspicion of an injury to the neck, and the useful question is what happened to the head rather than what happened to the neck. Any sudden change in the speed of the body while the head keeps moving bends the neck past the range its ligaments allow, and the cord is squeezed between the bones in front of it and the ligament behind it. A collision between vehicles, a fall from a height, a dive into shallow water, a direct blow to the head, being struck by a vehicle as a pedestrian, a heavy object falling onto the head, a tackle in a contact sport and a hanging are the mechanisms taught as significant. A fall from a height greater than 1 meter belongs on that list in many services, though that figure is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because services and countries differ. The violent mechanisms come to mind first, and they are not the only ones.

A fall from standing height can break the neck of an older patient whose bone has lost density, and a fall from a bed or a chair can do the same in a patient whose spine has been weakened by disease or by a medicine that thins the bone. The neck of a patient whose canal is already narrow may be injured by a movement that would leave a healthy young person unhurt, and such a patient may feel a dull ache rather than a sharp pain. Any patient who has been found unconscious, and any patient who cannot give a clear account of the injury because of intoxication, a head injury, a young age or a language the responder does not share, is treated as having an injured neck until someone qualified to do so has cleared it. The rule that follows is short, and it is that the absence of neck pain is not the absence of an injury to the neck.

What the responder looks for in the neck is tenderness in the midline at the back, felt with the patient lying still while the examiner presses gently on each bony point. Pain on any movement, muscle spasm that holds the head to one side, and a step or a gap felt between two spines all suggest a broken or a displaced segment. The patient may hold the head with both hands to keep it still, a striking sign when it is seen. Bruising, a scrape or a wound of the face and the scalp points to a blow that reached the head, and blood behind the eardrum may be the only mark of a break at the base of the skull. None of these findings is reliable on its own, and several may be absent in a patient with a serious injury. Tenderness and the story of the mechanism together decide whether the neck is treated as injured, and that decision belongs to a clinician rather than to a single sign.

Some patients describe a brief numbness, a tingling or a burning in the hands or the feet immediately after the injury which has gone by the time the responder arrives. Such a symptom warns that the canal was narrow or that the cord was touched, even if it lasted only a moment, and it changes the way the patient must be handled. The same is true of a weakness that lasted for a few minutes and then passed, and of an odd feeling in the hands that the patient dismisses as the effect of landing on an arm. The responder records those words as exactly as possible, because the patient may not repeat them to the next clinician, and a symptom that was never written down cannot be used later. A patient who reports such an episode is treated as injured and referred for assessment, and the meaning of the episode belongs to the clinician who examines the cord.

Assessment begins with the danger to the rescuer and then with the primary survey, whose steps are remembered as ABCDE, meaning airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure. The airway is the first place where an injury of the neck changes what a responder does, because the usual way of opening an airway tips the head back and moves the neck. The trained first responder and the paramedic open the airway with the jaw thrust, which moves the jaw instead of the neck, while a second person holds the head in the position found, and the lay first aider supports the head and follows the dispatcher. Breathing may be shallow and the cough weak when the cord has been injured, so the chest is watched rather than assumed. Circulation may show the strange pattern of a slow pulse with warm, dry skin after a cord injury, which is not the same as the shock of blood loss and is not treated as though it were.

Stabilization of the neck means that the head, the neck and the trunk are kept in one line and moved as a single block, and the position preserved is the position in which the patient was found. The trained responder holds the head with both hands, without pulling, without turning and without straightening, and keeps holding until the patient is secured or the spine has been cleared. That hold is called manual in-line stabilization, and it is a team skill learned under supervision and practiced, because one responder who tries to hold a head while moving a patient will fail. Traction is never applied by hand in the field, since a pull on the neck may distract a broken segment and stretch the cord. The lay first aider is taught to tell the patient not to move, to support the head gently and to wait. A child is not held in the same way as an adult, because a child's head is large in proportion to the trunk and the padding that keeps the neck in line differs accordingly.

A rigid collar is a device that surrounds the neck and limits some of its movement, and it is applied by a trained responder, a paramedic, a nurse or a physician working within the local protocol. The collar does not remove movement, it does not replace the hands of a trained responder while a patient is being moved, and it does not clear the spine. A collar of the wrong size is worse than none, because one that is too small fails to support the head and one that is too large pulls the neck into extension, and both errors can move a broken segment. A collar that is too tight presses on the veins of the neck, which raises the pressure inside the head, and it presses on the skin, which can cause a sore within hours in a patient who cannot feel it. The collar is checked after it is applied, the skin beneath it is inspected whenever the patient is turned, and it is adjusted or removed only by someone the local protocol authorizes. The responder who applies a collar records the neurovascular examination before and after, because a change that was not recorded before cannot be attributed afterwards.

The long board is a tool for moving a patient a short distance out of a vehicle or away from a hazard, and it is not a bed. A patient left on a hard board for a long journey suffers pain, pressure sores and difficulty breathing, and many services remove the board as soon as the patient is on a mattress, according to their own rules. Turning a patient with a suspected injury of the neck requires a team working on a count, with one person holding the head and enough hands to keep the trunk in line, and that maneuver is learned under supervision. A helmet is left in place unless it blocks the airway or prevents care, and removing one takes 2 trained people, a figure to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. Every one of these acts is a team act, and no single manual handling technique is safe when it is used alone.

The neurovascular examination of a patient with a suspected injury of the neck examines the cord, the nerve roots and the arteries, and it is performed before any intervention and repeated after it. The examiner asks the patient to shrug the shoulders, to lift the arms, to bend the elbows, to extend the wrists, to grip the fingers and to spread them, and compares the two sides, because a difference is more informative than any single grade. The legs are tested in the same way, and the level of feeling is mapped by asking where a light touch is felt, beginning at the shoulders and moving down the arms, the chest, the abdomen and the legs. The lowest level at which feeling is normal is recorded in a sentence, with the time, and the same sentence is repeated after every intervention. Reflexes and the internal examination of the bowel belong to a clinician and are not field tests, and a responder says plainly that an examination was not performed rather than guessing at it.

Immobilization here means preventing movement of the injured part and of the segments above and below it, and in the neck that means the head, the neck, the trunk and the pelvis move together or do not move at all. The head is supported in the position in which it was found, with the collar, with blocks or rolled towels on each side, and with the trunk secured to whatever surface the patient is lying on, as the protocol directs. Padding is placed behind the head of a child and, in some adults, under the shoulders, because the shape of the body decides where the gap lies. A deformed neck is supported as it lies and is never straightened in the field, because the cord and the vessels lie against the bone and a movement that looks helpful can tear what remained intact. Splints, collars and boards are applied by trained responders, paramedics, nurses and physicians within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, and the physician or the spine surgeon decides what definitive immobilization is required.

Imaging is ordered by a clinician trained to interpret it, and it confirms what the examination has already suggested rather than replacing the examination. A radiograph of the neck is usually taken in 3 views, one of them through the open mouth so that the second bone can be seen, and the junction between the neck and the chest often needs a special position, so the views are local practice to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. Computed tomography shows the bones in fine detail, and in many services it has replaced the plain radiograph as the first study for an adult with a significant mechanism, because it finds breaks that a radiograph hides. Magnetic resonance imaging shows the cord, the disks, the ligaments and the blood within the canal, and it answers the question when a patient has a deficit and the bones look normal. A responder never orders imaging, never interprets it and never tells a patient that a scan was normal, because the spine is cleared only by someone qualified to clear it.

The second bone of the neck deserves special attention, because the peg that points upward from it is the pivot of all turning of the head and it has a fragile blood supply. The peg may break near its tip, at its base where it joins the body of the bone, or through the body itself, and the break at the base is the one that most often fails to heal because the supply to that part is poor. In an older patient the mechanism is usually an extension of the neck in a fall, and the injury may cause little more than a dull ache at the top of the neck and a headache at the back of the head. The examination is unreliable here, because tenderness may be vague and the nerve findings may be normal, so the diagnosis is made by good imaging rather than by the hands. The care of that break may be a rigid collar, a special device that holds the head with a ring and pins, or an operation, and the choice depends on the pattern, the age of the patient and the state of the bone. All of those treatments are decided and performed by a spine surgeon in a hospital, and the techniques belong to supervised clinical training and never to a book.

The neck of an older patient is often already narrowed before any injury happens, because the disks lose height, the joints at the back grow spurs and the ligaments inside the canal thicken with age. That patient's cord has less room than the cord of a young person, and a movement that would be harmless in youth can bruise it. The cord is pinched between the spur in front of it and the folded ligament behind it, and the deficit that results is often strange, in that the arms are weaker than the legs, the hands burn, the fingers are clumsy and the bladder may fail. The radiograph may show no fracture at all, and a responder who relies on it will conclude that the neck is fine while the cord is injured. The deficit can worsen over the first hours and days, so such a patient is referred urgently to a hospital with imaging of the cord and a team able to watch the breathing and the blood pressure. The care of the airway, of the blood pressure and of the cord belongs to the physician and to the team in the intensive care unit, and the responder's part is to recognize the pattern, protect the neck and move the patient quickly.

A cord can also be injured when no bone is broken and no joint is displaced, and this possibility is why a normal radiograph is never allowed to end the assessment. In young children the spine is elastic and the cord is not, so the bones may bend and spring back while the cord is stretched, crushed or torn, and the injury may be present with normal images. In adults a disk may burst into the canal, a small vessel inside the canal may bleed and press on the cord, or an artery that supplies the cord may be injured. The findings to look for are weakness, numbness, tingling, a loss of the ability to move the arms or the legs, difficulty breathing, and a loss of control of the bladder or the bowel. A child who will not move the neck, who cries when picked up or who is unusually quiet after a fall deserves the same caution as an adult with an obvious fracture. The responder does not decide whether the cord is injured but instead recognizes that it might be, protects the neck and sends the patient to a place where the cord can be examined.

Reassessment is performed after every intervention, and in the neck that means after the collar is applied, after the patient is moved, after any attempt to open the airway and after every turn. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the record, because the comparison and not the single measurement carries the meaning. The findings that stop a responder are a falling level of consciousness, breathing that becomes weaker or noisier, new weakness or numbness in the arms or the legs, a patient who can no longer lift an arm that moved a moment before, and pain that increases instead of settling. A collar that has become too tight, a strap that presses on the chest and a board that has become unbearable all cause harm of their own, so the device is checked at each stage. A patient who vomits while lying flat is at risk of drawing the vomit into the lungs, and turning that patient is a team act performed within the local protocol with the head, the neck and the trunk kept in line. A patient who is losing cord function is referred urgently, and the responder does not wait for a second examination to confirm what the first one found.

Referral begins with the recognition that an injury of the neck cannot be managed where the patient lies, and the destination is chosen at the same time. A patient with a nerve deficit, a displaced break, a break of the second bone, an unstable neck or a neck that cannot be cleared is taken to a hospital where a spine surgeon, an intensive care unit and imaging of the cord are available. The receiving team is told before arrival what happened, what was found, what was done and what is still uncertain, in that order, so that the next team continues a story instead of starting a new one. The spine is not cleared over the telephone and is not cleared by the responder, however confident the examination appears. A patient with a normal examination but a dangerous mechanism is transferred for assessment rather than sent home, and that decision belongs to a clinician who has the imaging and a validated rule. A collar or a supporting hold is a precaution and not a diagnosis, and the symptoms that should bring the patient back are explained in words the family can repeat.

Definitive treatment begins with the question of whether the spine is stable, and everything that follows depends on the answer. A stable injury may be treated with a rigid collar or a molded brace, worn for a period that the treating clinician sets and that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, and the skin beneath it is inspected regularly, because a patient with altered sensation may not feel a sore forming. Some injuries are treated with traction, which is a gentle and continuous pull on the head meant to restore the alignment of the bones or to hold it while healing proceeds, and it is applied only in a hospital by a qualified team with imaging and monitoring at the bedside. Some injuries need an operation to take pressure off the cord and to join one bone to the next with grafted bone and metal devices, so that the neck becomes stable without external support. Those techniques, and the reduction of a displaced segment, are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and no chapter of this course teaches the technique itself. Pain relief and every medicine are chosen by the prescriber under the local protocol, and neither the substance nor the amount can be taken from this book.

Throughout the prehospital phase the aim is not to make the injury better but to keep it from becoming worse. The patient is kept still, kept warm, kept informed and kept covered, because cold worsens clotting, raises the work of breathing and makes an agitated patient fight the people who are helping. A patient who pulls at the collar and tries to sit up needs calm words and a plan rather than force, and the plan is made within the local protocol by the team leader, because a struggle moves the neck more than a controlled turn. The responder does not examine the neck by asking the patient to move it, does not test the strength of the neck, and does not remove the collar unless the protocol allows it and the spine has been assessed. Where a patient must be moved urgently because of fire, traffic or another hazard, the move is made as a team with the head controlled. Every number named in this chapter is an illustration to be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, because services differ in their equipment and in their rules.

To summarize, the neck protects the cord in a canal with almost no reserve, and an injury there can stop the breathing or paralyze a patient while the skin stays intact. Suspect it after any violent mechanism, after a fall in an older or a weakened patient, after a blow to the head, and in any patient who cannot give a clear account of what happened, because the absence of neck pain proves nothing. Follow the sequence in order, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and name the provider level for every act. The trained team holds the head in the position found without traction, applies the collar and the board, and keeps the patient warm, while the lay first aider supports the head and never straightens a deformed neck. Record the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention, reassess after each one, and escalate at once when the breathing weakens, when new weakness or numbness appears or when the level of consciousness falls, and the emergency service of the area is called for any patient who cannot be moved safely by those present. A normal radiograph does not exclude an injury of the cord, especially in a child, in an older patient whose canal is narrow, and in a patient with a broken peg of the second bone. Reduction, traction and the fitting of specialist devices are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and the technique is never taken from a book.
