# Chapter 58 — Conservative Treatment

Most fractures in the world are treated without an operation, and so are many dislocations once they have been put back into place. Conservative treatment is not the treatment left over when nothing better is available, because a cast, a brace, a splint, a boot and, in some settings, traction are each a deliberate choice made by a clinician. That clinician has judged that the bone can be held well enough from the outside while it heals, and each device carries its own risks and its own way of failing. This chapter follows the order used throughout this course, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. What follows is educational material, and it does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

The decision to treat an injury conservatively belongs to a clinician trained in fracture care, and it rests on a judgment about the injury and about the patient. That clinician asks whether the bone ends lie in a position the body can accept, whether the pattern of the break tends to stay where it is put, whether the joint surface is involved, and whether the nerves and vessels are intact. The clinician also asks whether the skin and the soft tissues can tolerate a device pressing on them for weeks. A second set of questions has nothing to do with the radiograph, and it asks whether this patient can return for follow-up, whether someone can bring them, and whether they can keep a device dry and in place. Where a service has no operating room and no orthopedic surgeon, conservative treatment may be the only treatment available, and then the urgent question is how the patient reaches a place where an operation exists. The lay first aider, the trained first responder and the paramedic do not make this decision, because it depends on images and on an examination that only a clinician performs.

Assessment begins before the limb is touched, and in conservative treatment the history carries as much weight as the examination. The responder notes the scene, the mechanism of injury, the time that has passed and the age of the patient. The primary survey follows, in the order remembered as ABCDE, meaning airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure, because life comes before limb. Certain details decide whether a bone will be easy to hold in a device, and among them are a medicine that thins the bone, a previous fracture in the same place, tobacco use and poorly controlled diabetes. The patient's circumstances belong in the same history, because a treatment that needs many visits cannot be given to a patient who has no way to make them. Exposure means uncovering the patient enough to see the whole limb, and it is followed at once by covering the patient again, because cold is a complication of trauma and not a matter of comfort.

The limb itself is examined by looking, by feeling and by asking the patient to move it, never by forcing it. Both sides are uncovered and compared, because a limb that looks almost normal may still hold a fracture, and a fracture that has not moved is the very injury most often treated conservatively. The responder looks for swelling, for a wound, for a change in color and for skin that is stretched and shiny. Feeling is done gently and in a fixed order, moving from the joint above to the joint below. Pain far worse than the appearance of the limb suggests is itself a finding rather than a complaint, and a limb that cannot be moved may mean that a tendon is torn. Everything is recorded in the words that describe it, and the record is begun before the first device is applied, because a finding that was not written down cannot be settled afterwards.

Stabilization is a small set of acts performed in a deliberate order, and the most important of them is restraint. A deformed limb is supported and immobilized in the position in which it was found, and it is never straightened in the field. The reason is that the nerves and the vessels lie against the bone, and the pressure holding a torn vessel closed may be the only thing preventing further bleeding. Pulling a limb straight may also drive a sharp bone end through skin that was intact, which turns an injury that could have been treated in a cast into one that needs an operating room. Bleeding that can be controlled is controlled with a dressing, exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, and an embedded object is never removed, because it may be plugging a torn vessel. The lay first aider supports the limb with the hands, with a pillow or with a rolled blanket, keeps the patient warm and still, and calls for help. The trained first responder and the paramedic may apply a splint within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, while an open wound is never explored blindly by anyone.

The neurovascular examination is performed before any intervention and repeated after every one, and its result is written down. A finding that was not recorded before a device was applied cannot be attributed afterwards. The examiner looks at the color of the hand or the foot and compares it with the other side, feels its temperature, and presses briefly on a nail to watch the color return. Sensation is tested on both sides in the same places, and the patient is asked to move the fingers or the toes and to say whether the movement feels normal and strong. In conservative treatment it has a second purpose, because a cast, a brace or a boot hides the skin beneath it, so it is the only way to know that the device is not pressing on a nerve or a vessel. It is repeated immediately after the device is applied, after any adjustment of a strap, a hinge or a weight, and at every follow-up visit. A pulse that can be felt does not exclude serious injury, while a pulse that cannot be felt, a hand or foot that is pale and cool, or a new numbness is an emergency that stops the process at once.

Immobilization means preventing movement of the injured part and of the joints above and below it. The devices that do this work form one family, and the splint is the device of the field and of the first hours. The cast is a rigid shell that encircles the limb, the brace is a removable support that often allows some movement, and the boot or the stiff-soled shoe holds the foot and the ankle while allowing protected weight bearing. All of them are padded where they press over a bone, all are applied over the position in which the limb was found, and all are secured firmly enough to hold without becoming a band that stops the circulation. The splint is also used in hospital while swelling is at its worst, because a limb that is still swelling inside a rigid cast will be squeezed by it. The trained first responder and the paramedic apply splints within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, while the choice among the devices belongs to the clinician who has examined the patient and seen the image. What every level must avoid is wrapping a device so tightly that it presses, because what was meant to protect then becomes the cause of the complication that follows.

The cast is a rigid shell, made of plaster or of a synthetic material, that is applied soft and molded around the limb and then hardens where it sits. Its purpose is to hold the bone ends in the position the clinician has accepted while the bone heals, and because it grips the limb it controls movement in every direction rather than in one. A cast is applied by a physician or an orthopedic surgeon, or by a trained technician working under a written protocol. The technique of applying it, molding it and judging its fit belongs to supervised clinical training rather than to a book, and a cast is never applied in the field. What can go wrong is worth saying plainly, because the device itself is silent, and a limb that swells inside a rigid shell is squeezed. The pressure can close the small vessels and press on a nerve, the material can heat as it sets and burn the skin, and skin beneath a cast can become infected without anyone seeing it. A stain, a bad smell or new pain is reported at once, nobody splits, cuts, tightens or removes a cast outside the local protocol, and nobody makes a cast comfortable by pushing padding under its edge.

The brace is the removable member of the family, and it trades a little security for the ability to see the skin and to move the joint. A brace may be a molded shell held by straps, a hinged frame that limits how far a knee or an elbow may bend, or a soft support used for a stable injury. It may be chosen for a fracture expected to stay in place, for an injury in which the ligaments rather than the bone carry the problem, or after an operation when the surgeon wants the joint to move within a defined range. Because it is removable, its value depends on the patient wearing it and on the straps and hinges being set as the clinician ordered. Straps that are too tight press on skin and on the nerves beneath them, straps that are too loose let the brace slide, and a brace worn over a swollen limb may be far tighter in the evening. The boot and the stiff-soled shoe let a patient walk while a bone in the foot or the ankle heals and are used for stable fractures and for stress injuries. A boot can be taken off, and a device that can be taken off will be taken off, so the fit is checked and the instruction about when to wear it is given. A brace or a boot is fitted by a clinician, a nurse or a physical therapist, and a patient who limps away with a boot but no instruction has been supplied rather than treated.

Traction is the oldest of these treatments and the one that must be handled most carefully, because it is still used in some settings when an operation is not available or not safe. Traction means a gentle and continuous pull along the long axis of the limb, held steady to overcome the pull of the muscles that have shortened it. Its purpose is to hold the bone ends in better alignment, to reduce movement at the fracture, to reduce bleeding and pain and to make the journey to hospital safer. It is never a way of setting a fracture or of putting a dislocated joint back into place, because a pull strong enough to force bone ends into position can tear vessels, nerves and skin. Skin traction transmits the pull through straps or a boot, while skeletal traction transmits it through a metal pin placed in the bone, and the second requires sterile technique and an anesthetic decision that belongs to the physician. Both forms are applied only by a clinician trained and authorized to apply them, according to a written protocol, and the placement of the pin and the adjustment of the weight belong to supervised practical training. Traction is not applied when there is a suspected pelvic fracture, when an open wound lies under the device, when a fragment is caught in the joint, or when the pulse is failing and the foot is numb. In each of those situations the limb is immobilized in the position found and the patient is referred, and a weight is never increased to overcome resistance, because force is not a technique.

Imaging is the step that tells the clinician what the hand cannot, and in conservative treatment it has two jobs. The first is the study that confirms the injury and shows its pattern, usually a radiograph taken in at least 2 directions with the joint above and the joint below included. Computed tomography shows the detail of a complex break around a joint, while magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for the soft tissues, including ligaments, cartilage, tendons and muscle. The second job is the follow-up study, taken after the device has been applied and then at intervals the clinician sets. The bone ends can move in the first weeks as the swelling goes down, and a device that fitted a swollen limb becomes loose on a thinner one. That loss of position is the commonest reason a conservative plan is abandoned, and it is often silent, since the limb is inside a device and the patient feels less pain rather than more. Every figure quoted for the interval between those studies is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because services differ in how often they look. The lay first aider never orders or interprets an image, and only the clinician responsible for the care decides what the picture means and what is done next.

Reassessment is performed after every intervention and at every visit, and it is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment rather than the one caused by the injury. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the record, and it is the comparison that carries the information. The limb is checked for color, warmth, pulse, sensation, movement and pain, and the device is checked for tightness, for slipping and for moisture. The skin is inspected at the edges of the device, under every strap and at the heel, where pressure is easiest to miss. The findings that stop the process are pain that increases instead of settling, numbness, tingling, weakness, a hand or foot that becomes pale or cool, and a limb that grows tense and swollen. A responder who suspects a compartment syndrome does not wait for confirmation, because the diagnosis is clinical and time is muscle, and the constricting bandage, splint or cast is released within the local protocol by someone authorized to do it. The limb is kept at the level of the heart rather than raised above it, the patient is kept warm, and the patient is referred urgently.

Weight bearing is the instruction that patients misunderstand most often, and the misunderstanding is not a failure of attention but a failure of our words. Not weight bearing means that none of the patient's weight goes through the injured limb at all, and the limb is held clear of the ground with crutches or a frame. Touch-down weight bearing means that the foot may rest on the floor only to help balance, with no more pressure than it takes to feel the ground beneath it. Partial weight bearing means that some share of the body's weight is allowed through the limb, and that share is defined by the clinician and often practiced on a scale in a training session. Weight bearing as tolerated means that the patient may put weight on the limb as comfort allows while the device is worn, and full weight bearing means that the limb may carry the whole body as it normally would. Each of those instructions belongs to the clinician who examined the patient and reviewed the images, and none of them is changed or guessed by the responder, the nurse, the physical therapist or the patient.

What goes wrong with those instructions is easy to predict and is worth saying in advance. A patient told to bear weight as tolerated hears that the leg is healed and begins to walk without the boot. A patient told to bear weight partially hears that the leg must be protected and stays in bed until the joint stiffens. Pain is a poor guide to how much weight is safe, because a device that holds a bone still also dulls the signal that would otherwise warn the patient. A patient whose sensation is impaired by diabetes or by a nerve injury may have no warning at all. Hopping on one leg is not a way of keeping weight off the injured side, because it is how patients fall, and a fall can undo a position that took weeks to hold. The instruction applies to every step, at home, in the bathroom and on the stairs, with the device on and fastened, and it applies until the clinician says otherwise rather than until the pain stops. When a patient reports that the limb feels better, the reply is not permission to do more but an explanation that a bone which has stopped hurting has not necessarily joined.

The way the instruction is given decides whether it is followed. The clinician authorizes the amount of weight, the device and the walking aid, while the nurse and the physical therapist teach the patient how to use them. The instruction is spoken and written in the same words, it is stated in terms the patient uses, and it names the activity rather than the abstract category. The patient is asked to say the instruction back in their own words and then to demonstrate it while someone watches, because a patient who can repeat the category may still be doing the opposite. The commonest way conservative treatment fails is not the device but the patient who does not come back, and the reason is usually distance, cost, lost wages or the care of children. At every later encounter the responder asks when the next appointment is and what the patient was told to do, and a missed appointment is treated as an urgent finding rather than an administrative one. A patient whose device has been ruined by water or who describes new numbness should be seen before the scheduled date, because those findings change what the next image will show.

A device that stays on the limb for weeks carries its own list of instructions, and the patient should hear all of them before leaving. The device is kept dry unless the clinician says that water is allowed, because wet padding holds moisture against the skin and a wet cast loses strength. Nothing is inserted into the device to scratch, no powder or cream is put beneath it unless it has been prescribed, and the edges are not cut or bent by anyone outside the treating team. A device that becomes loose, soft, cracked or foul smelling is a reason to seek care at once rather than at the next appointment. The patient is shown where to look and to feel for redness, blisters and pressure marks, and is told to use a mirror where the device hides the skin from view. Driving, work, sleep, keeping the device clean and managing dependents are part of the plan too, because a patient who is given a treatment that cannot be lived with will quietly abandon it.

Some patients need a different plan, because a device that cannot be checked safely is more dangerous than no device at all. A young child cannot describe numbness or tingling and grows out of a device within weeks, so the follow-up must be closer and the instruction belongs as much to the parent. An older adult may have thin skin that breaks down under a strap and a poor sense of balance that a rigid boot makes worse, so the walking aid and the home are assessed with the same care as the fracture. A patient whose sensation is impaired by diabetes, by a nerve injury or by a spinal cord injury may feel no warning at all and must be taught to inspect the skin at fixed times. A patient who is confused or intoxicated needs a plan that does not depend on cooperation, and the decision about what that plan should be belongs to the clinician. In every one of those situations the responder's part is to recognize the finding, to say it aloud in the handover and to bring the patient back rather than to adjust the device.

Referral or definitive treatment is the final step, and for conservative treatment it means knowing when the plan must be changed. A fracture that has lost its position on the follow-up image, a fracture that shows no sign of joining, an infection beneath a device and a limb that is painful out of proportion are all reasons to involve the orthopedic surgeon. So are the injuries in which conservative treatment was never the first choice, which include an open fracture, a dislocation that has not been reduced and a fracture in a child that crosses the growth region. A patient who cannot return for follow-up or who has no one to help at home may need to be admitted rather than sent home, and that is a decision about safety rather than about bone. When the plan is changed, the patient is told that this is a change in the treatment and not a failure of the patient, because a person who feels blamed will not report the next problem. The orthopedic surgeon decides what form the definitive treatment takes, whether that is a longer period in a cast or an operation with plates, screws, nails or an external frame.

The levels of practice in this chapter are easy to keep in order, because every act described here has an owner. The lay first aider supports the limb in the position found, controls the bleeding that can be controlled with a dressing, keeps the patient warm and still, calls for help, and observes color, warmth, movement and feeling, but never straightens the limb and never applies anything rigid to it. The trained first responder performs the neurovascular examination and records it, applies splints within the equipment and the procedures the service authorizes, and reports any change before arrival. The emergency medical technician and the paramedic work within a broader written protocol, may apply a traction device where the service trains and permits it, and may give a medicine for pain only as that protocol directs. The nurse examines and records, checks the skin under straps and cast edges, maintains the position the clinician has ordered, teaches the patient within the plan that has been set, and reports any change at once. The physician and the orthopedic surgeon examine the patient, order and interpret the images, decide whether treatment is conservative or operative, and set the amount of weight bearing. The physical therapist teaches the patient to walk with the aid and the device the clinician has chosen, and no dose, no route and no regimen appears in this chapter. The substance given for pain, for infection and for tetanus belongs to the prescriber and the local protocol, and the finding that makes any of those decisions urgent is reported rather than treated by a listener.

The record and the handover carry the plan as well as the injury. The time of the injury, the mechanism, the position in which the limb was found, the device that was applied and by whom, the weight bearing that was authorized, and the date of the next appointment are written or spoken in a fixed order. It is worth saying aloud that the limb was not straightened and that no object was removed, because the absence of an act is as important to record as its performance. A patient with a fracture of a long bone has often taken energy through the trunk or the spine, so the spine is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has done so. Everything described in this chapter is learned with a teacher and certified by an authority that also defines what may be done without supervision, and where a number appears here it is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

To summarize, a cast, a brace, a splint, a boot and traction are treatments in their own right, each chosen by a clinician who has examined the patient and reviewed the images, rather than the treatment that remains when an operation is unavailable. What to look for is a device that has become tight, loose, wet or cracked, a limb that is pale, cool, numb, weak or painful out of proportion to the injury, a stain or a smell from beneath a cast, and a patient who cannot return for follow-up. What to do is to follow the order this course uses in every chapter, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. The neurovascular findings are recorded before and after every intervention, the patient is kept warm, and the instruction about weight bearing is said plainly, in the patient's own words, and checked. What never to do is to straighten a deformed limb in the field, to remove an embedded object, to push exposed bone back into a wound, to explore an open wound blindly, to apply a cast in the field, or to increase a weight to overcome resistance. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and a responder who suspects a compartment syndrome releases the constricting device within the local protocol, keeps the limb at the level of the heart and refers urgently, because time is muscle. Who to call is decided by the service and the local protocol, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.
