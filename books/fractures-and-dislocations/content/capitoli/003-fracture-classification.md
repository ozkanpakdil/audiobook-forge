# Chapter 3 — Fracture Classification

A description of a fracture is worth only what it predicts, so the words in this chapter are taught as forecasts rather than as labels to be memorized. The same broken bone can be called several things at once, and each name answers a different question about what the injury will do next. A transverse break in a strong shaft behaves unlike a spiral break of the same bone, and the difference matters long before the patient reaches a hospital. Every injury in this course follows one sequence, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

Assessment comes first, and it begins with the mechanism of injury rather than with the bone, because the way the energy entered the limb usually predicts the shape of the break. A fall onto an outstretched hand drives force along the arm, while a twisting injury to a planted foot produces a different pattern. The age of the patient, the quality of the bone, the time since the injury and any illness or medicine that weakens bone are part of the same history. The responder records what was seen, what was heard and the position in which the limb was found, and does not name the pattern beyond what the mechanism plainly suggests. Naming the fracture definitively is the work of a clinician who can study the image, so the field description is expected to be revised.

The first and most consequential division is between a closed fracture and an open one, and it is decided by the skin alone. A closed fracture has no communication between the broken bone and the outside world, so the deep tissues stay sealed and the risk of infection is low. An open fracture has a wound that reaches the bone or the deep tissues, whether that wound is a long laceration or a small puncture, and it is contaminated at the moment of injury. The size of the wound does not settle the question, because a tiny wound over a broken bone can open a path into the deep tissues. The lay first aider covers the wound with the cleanest dressing available and keeps hands off it, while the trained responder and the paramedic do the same within the procedures their service authorizes.

The field acts for an open fracture are few. The wound is covered with a sterile dressing, the bleeding that can be controlled is controlled, the neurovascular status is examined and written down, the limb is supported in the position found, and the patient is kept warm while transfer is arranged urgently. An embedded object is never removed, because it may be pressing on a torn vessel, and exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound. The wound is never explored blindly, because a finger or an instrument that enters it can carry contamination deeper and can disturb what was holding the bleeding back. Definitive cleaning and closure belong to the operating theater, and the surgeon decides when the wound is taken there, following the local protocol rather than a rule from a book.

A description of the bone is only half of a description of the injury, because the same break can leave a limb quiet or can threaten it. The skin, the muscle, the fascia, the vessels and the nerves have all taken the same energy, and the tissues that were crushed rather than cut heal least predictably. A clean wound and a heavily soiled wound are different injuries even when the bone beneath them looks identical on an image. Swelling that keeps rising after the limb has been splinted is a finding about the soft tissues rather than about the bone. The responder records what can be seen, felt and reported, and leaves the grading of soft tissue injury to the clinician who will treat it.

Stability is a second division, and it is a statement about behavior rather than about appearance. A fracture is described as stable when the broken ends stay in acceptable position under ordinary load, and as unstable when they displace again as soon as the limb is moved. A break with a single clean line across the bone is often more stable than a long oblique or spiral break, because flat surfaces can interlock where slanted ones slide. An examiner may test stability by gentle handling within the limits of the local protocol, and such testing is a clinical skill learned under supervision rather than a maneuver for the field. A responder who hears the word unstable should treat it as a warning that the limb will not hold its position without proper support and early specialist care.

The transverse fracture runs across the bone at close to a right angle to its long axis, and it usually follows a direct blow or a bending force. Because the broken surfaces are broad and flat, they resist sliding, so a transverse break is often described as relatively stable once it is supported. It takes a substantial force to produce such a break, so the soft tissues at that level have absorbed a substantial force as well, and a wound over the bone should be suspected even when none is seen. The periosteum is torn on the side that was stretched and may stay intact on the side that was compressed, and an intact sleeve is one reason these breaks can be held by a well fitted support. The word therefore predicts that the bone will hold position better than a spiral break, but it does not predict that the skin and the muscle beneath it are unharmed.

An oblique fracture crosses the bone at a slant rather than straight across, and its shape carries the answer to how the limb will behave. The slanted surfaces can slide on one another, so an oblique break tends to shorten and to angulate under the pull of the muscles. A force that bends and twists the bone at the same time produces this pattern, and it often injures the tissues lying against the bone. A short oblique break behaves more like a transverse one, while a long oblique break behaves more like a spiral, so these words describe positions on a range. The responder does not straighten the angulation, because movement at the fracture is what the splint prevents, and the final position belongs to the treating clinician.

A spiral fracture winds around the shaft, and it is the signature of a twisting force applied along the length of a limb. A skier whose foot is held while the body turns, or a child whose arm is pulled and twisted, is the kind of constructed illustration that teaching uses, and any example in this course is an illustration rather than a report of a real patient. The line of the break is long, the surfaces are smooth and the ends rotate about the long axis, so the limb may point in a direction the patient did not choose. This is one of the patterns most likely to be unstable, and the twist that caused it may have torn the vessels and the nerves that travel along the shaft. A limb that looks rotated is splinted as it lies, and the examiner compares its resting posture with the other side, because that difference is often more informative than any measurement.

A longitudinal fracture splits along the length of the bone rather than across it, and it is uncommon enough that the word is worth knowing so that not every line is assumed to run the way one expects. It often appears together with another pattern, as a split that extends from a break near a joint into the shaft. Because the split follows the bone, the pieces can separate sideways when the joint above or below is moved. The finding that suggests it is pain along the line of the bone rather than at a single point, together with a joint that cannot be moved comfortably. Only a clinician who can study the image should decide what the line means, because a split reaching a joint surface changes the plan of treatment.

A comminuted fracture is one in which the bone is broken into more than two pieces, and the word tells the listener how much energy passed through the limb. The fragments may be few and large or many and small, and the more numerous they are, the more likely it is that the soft tissues were crushed as well. Comminution predicts difficulty in holding the bone in position, because there is no single pair of surfaces for the ends to interlock, and it predicts slower healing, because the blood supply to some fragments has been cut off. It also raises the chance that a fragment has entered a joint or is pressing on a vessel or a nerve. The responder who hears the word does not change the field acts, but that word should raise the level of concern and shorten the tolerance for delay, and the definitive decision about how such a break is held belongs to an orthopedic surgeon.

A segmental fracture is a serious form of comminution in which the bone is broken at two levels, leaving a free piece of shaft with no connection to either end. The middle piece keeps only the attachments that were not torn, and its blood supply may be poor, so it is at risk of never uniting with the rest of the bone. Because the free segment can move independently, the limb may change shape with the smallest movement, which can injure what is left of the blood supply. The responder supports the whole limb rather than the fracture alone and asks for transfer to a facility where the bone can be stabilized definitively. Deciding how that stabilization is done, whether with a frame outside the limb or a device inside the bone, is the work of an orthopedic surgeon.

An avulsion fracture is one in which a tendon or a ligament pulls a piece of bone away at the place where it attaches. The mechanism is a sudden contraction or a sudden stretch, so the injury often happens without any blow and the patient may describe a snap rather than an impact. It matters because the piece of bone that has moved is the anchor of a structure that moves a joint, and a joint whose anchor has moved may not work even though the fragment looks small on an image. Whether the fragment needs to be returned to its place, and how, is a decision made by a clinician or a surgeon. The responder identifies the loss of movement, records it and supports the limb, and does not test the strength of the structure repeatedly, because that adds pain and can pull the fragment farther from its bed.

An impacted fracture is one in which the broken ends have been driven into one another, so the bone is shorter and the surfaces are jammed together. A fall onto the end of a limb drives the shaft into the softer bone at the end, and this is a common pattern in the wrist of an older adult. Because the surfaces are compressed into each other, an impacted break is often stable, and it may be far less painful than its consequences suggest. Shortening of the limb, a change in the way the hand or the foot is held, and pain on moving the joint above or below are the findings that point to it, and a reliable sign may be absent here. Only the clinician decides whether the position is acceptable and whether it will hold while the bone heals, and the older adult whose bone has lost density deserves a lower threshold for splinting and referral.

A compression fracture is one in which the bone is crushed rather than split, and it is seen most often in cancellous bone, the lattice inside the ends of the long bones and inside the bodies of the vertebrae. The bone loses height and the joint surfaces are driven together, so the injury is a failure of the material rather than a break across a shaft. In the spine this may happen with a fall from standing height or a movement that seems trivial, and it may produce pain that the patient localizes poorly. The responder treats such a patient gently and keeps the spine still until someone qualified has cleared it, because the force that crushed one vertebra may have injured another. Assessment is carried out by a clinician, and the decisions about imaging and about bearing weight belong to that clinician within the local protocol.

The greenstick fracture takes its name from a young branch that bends and splinters on one side instead of breaking in two, and it happens because the bone of a child is softer and more elastic than the bone of an adult. The cortex breaks on the side that was stretched while the opposite side bends and stays intact, and the thick periosteum of a child usually stays in one piece, which holds the fragments together. Because the bone is bent rather than only cracked, the limb may keep a curve after the injury, and straightening that curve is not a field act. An injury that leaves a child unwilling to use an arm or a leg, even without swelling or deformity, deserves the same caution as an obvious break. Correction of the bend and the decision about how to hold it are carried out by someone trained in the care of children's fractures, under supervision and according to local protocol. The lay first aider supports the limb as it lies, keeps the child warm, and does not pull on the limb to see whether the curve will come out.

A buckle fracture, also called a torus fracture, is caused by compression of the cortex, so the bone bulges at one place rather than breaking through. It occurs almost only in children, whose bone can crumple under a load that would break the bone of an adult. The swelling may be modest, the deformity may be absent and the child may move the limb a little, so tenderness over a small area of bone is the finding a responder can record. Because the break does not pass through the whole bone, it is usually described as stable, but stable is not the same as trivial, and the parent must be told what to watch for. The diagnosis is made by a clinician with imaging, and the treatment, often a simple support, is chosen by that clinician in the light of the child's age and the position of the break.

A pathological fracture is one that happens through bone that was already weakened by disease, and it is therefore a symptom as much as an injury. The disease may be a tumor, an infection, a cyst or a condition that softens or thins the bone, and the patient may or may not have known about it. The mechanism is often trivial, such as a fall from standing height or a turn in bed, and pain may have been present for weeks before the bone gave way. Because the bone is abnormal, it heals differently and it may not heal at all unless the underlying condition is treated, so these patients are referred rather than simply supported and sent home. The responder supports the limb, keeps the patient warm and does not test how much weight the bone will take, while the search for the cause belongs to the physician or the orthopedic surgeon.

A stress fracture is a break that follows repeated load rather than a single event, and it is the pattern in which the history matters more than the examination. The bone fails after many repetitions of a force that it could bear once, and the pain begins as a dull ache with activity and settles with rest before it becomes constant. Runners, soldiers and dancers are the groups in which this pattern is most familiar, and any such example in this course is a constructed illustration rather than a description of a real person. The examination may find only a small area of tenderness, and an image taken early may show nothing at all, because the change in the bone becomes visible only after the injury has been present for a time. That is why a normal image does not exclude the injury, and why the clinician may ask for a further study or for a period of rest and review.

These descriptions are not exclusive and they are not arranged in a hierarchy, so one fracture is usually named in several ways at once. A break may be open, spiral, comminuted and unstable, and each of those words answers a different question about the injury. The shape of the line describes the pattern, the state of the skin describes the risk of infection, and the stability describes how the ends behave. A description that omits one of these dimensions can mislead the person who reads it next. For that reason the record is written in a fixed order, so the receiving team sees at once what was known and what was not.

Stabilization and immobilization come next in the sequence, and the classification of the fracture changes nothing about how a responder performs either of them. A deformed limb is supported and splinted in the position in which it was found and is never straightened in the field, because the vessels and the nerves lie against the bone and a movement that looks helpful can tear what remained intact. The lay first aider supports the limb with the hands, with a pillow or with whatever is at hand, keeps the patient warm and waits for help, while the trained responder and the paramedic may apply a splint within the procedures their service authorizes. Immobilization includes the joints above and below the break, because a bone that is splinted alone can still be moved by the joint at either end, and the splint is padded where it presses on bone. Cold is a complication of trauma rather than a detail of comfort, so the patient is insulated from the ground, and casting and surgical fixation are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol.

The neurovascular examination is performed before the limb is splinted and repeated afterwards, and its result is recorded both times, because a finding that was not written before an intervention cannot be attributed afterwards. The examiner looks at the color and the temperature of the skin, feels the pulse beyond the injury, and asks the patient to move the fingers or the toes. Sensation is tested on both sides and compared, and numbness, tingling or the loss of a movement is written in the words the patient used. The pattern of the fracture tells the examiner where to look hardest, because a break above the elbow and a break behind the knee endanger the vessels that supply the forearm and the leg. A pulse that is present does not exclude serious injury to the vessels or to a compartment, and a pulse that is absent is an emergency. The lay first aider observes color, warmth and movement, while the formal examination belongs to the trained responder or to the clinician.

Imaging is the step that confirms what the examination has already suggested, and it is ordered by a clinician trained to interpret it. A radiograph is usually the first study, and it is taken in at least two directions, with the joint above and the joint below included so that a second injury is not missed. Computed tomography shows the detail of a complex break, particularly around a joint, and it is often used to plan an operation. Magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for soft tissues, including ligaments, cartilage, tendons and muscle, and it may reveal a stress injury before a radiograph does. Either study is requested when the answer it gives will change what is done, and that decision belongs to the clinician, while the lay first aider never orders or interprets imaging.

Reassessment follows every intervention, and it is the step that catches the complication caused by treatment rather than by the injury. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the record, because the comparison is what has value rather than any single measurement. The nurse and the clinician check the limb for color, warmth, pulse, sensation, movement and pain, and the splint is checked for tightness and for pressure over a bony point. Pain that increases instead of settling, pain out of proportion to the injury, numbness, tingling, weakness and a limb that becomes tense and swollen are the findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation. A responder who suspects a compartment syndrome does not wait for the diagnosis to be confirmed and acts within the local protocol, and the limb is not elevated above the level of the heart while the patient is kept warm and referred urgently.

Referral or definitive treatment is the final step, and the vocabulary of classification guides who is called and how quickly. A patient with an open fracture, a suspected compartment syndrome, a damaged nerve or vessel, a fracture that enters a joint, a badly comminuted or segmental break, or a fracture in a child across the growing part of the bone is referred to a facility with imaging, an operating room and an intensive care unit. Definitive treatment includes reduction, which restores the position of the bone ends, traction, which holds them in line with a gentle continuous pull, and casting or surgical fixation, which holds the position while the bone heals. Every one of those acts is learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and none of them is taught from a book in this course. A reduction must not be attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when the vessels or the nerves need a specialist, when the injury is open or the diagnosis unclear, when the responder lacks training or equipment, or when an earlier attempt has failed, because in each case the attempt can convert an injury that would have healed into one that cannot. Force is not a technique, and where a substance is needed for pain or for protection against infection, the choice of the substance, the amount and the way it is given belong to the prescriber and to the local protocol.

Every number in this course is an illustration, and each one must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between services, countries and editions. The time of the injury, the mechanism, the position in which the limb was found, the description of the pattern, and the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention are written or spoken in a fixed order, so that the receiving team continues a story instead of starting a new one. An injury of the spine is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has done so, and no lone manual handling technique is safe, so such a patient is moved by a trained team using the precautions the local protocol requires. Everything described in this chapter is learned with a teacher, practiced on models and on each other, and certified by an authority that also defines what may be done without supervision. A patient classified in the field and reclassified in the hospital has not been misled, because a description is expected to change as more is learned.

To summarize, the words used to describe a fracture are predictions about behavior and not labels to be memorized. The first division is between a closed fracture and an open one, decided by the skin alone, and an open fracture is contaminated from the moment of injury, so the wound is covered with a sterile dressing, an embedded object is never removed, exposed bone is never pushed back, and definitive closure belongs to the operating theater. The pattern of the break, whether transverse, oblique, spiral, longitudinal, comminuted or segmental, predicts how the bone will hold position and how the soft tissues suffered, while stable and unstable describe how the ends behave under load. An avulsion is pulled off by a tendon or a ligament, an impacted break is driven together, a compression break is crushed, a greenstick or buckle break belongs to a child, and a pathological or a stress break is the sign of abnormal bone or of repeated load. Every injury is handled in one order, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and the provider level is named for every act. A deformed limb is splinted in the position found, the neurovascular status is recorded before and after each intervention, the patient is kept warm, and a limb that becomes tense and painful out of proportion is treated as an emergency. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and they are never taught from a book.
