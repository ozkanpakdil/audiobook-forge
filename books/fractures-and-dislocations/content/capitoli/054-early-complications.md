# Chapter 54 — Early Complications

A fracture that is survived in the first hour can still be lost in the first days, and the complications that take it are quieter than the injury that caused it. The limb has been splinted, the bleeding has stopped, the radiograph has been taken and the patient is comfortable, and it is exactly then that the pressure inside a closed compartment keeps rising, that a wound begins to smell, that a lung which is not taking deep breaths begins to close, and that a clot forms in a leg which has not moved. Every chapter of this course follows one order, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and this chapter follows that order while naming the provider level for every act. This is educational material, and it does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

The reason the first days are dangerous lies in two simple facts about the tissues around a broken bone. Swelling does not stop when the splint is applied, because bleeding and the leak of fluid from injured vessels continue for hours, so a limb can be far larger on the second day than it was in the first. Pain is the messenger that reports that increase, and the patient who says the pain is worse than it was, or worse than the injury appears to explain, is handing the examiner the most valuable finding in this chapter. A comfortable patient is not automatically a safe one, because a limb that has lost its blood supply may hurt less once the muscle inside it has died, and the same false comfort follows a strong painkiller given before anybody examined the limb. Life comes before limb when two correct actions compete, and in the first days the threat to the limb is the one that is missed most easily.

The compartment syndrome declares itself as a limb that is tense, swollen and painful out of all proportion to the injury, and the pain matters most because it arrives before function is lost. A compartment is a closed space bounded by bone and by the tough sheet of fascia, holding muscle, nerves and blood vessels with almost no room to spare. When bleeding or swelling raises the pressure inside that space, the smallest vessels fail first, so muscle starves and then nerve, and the large pulse at the wrist or the ankle can still be felt while both are dying. Pain when the muscles of the compartment are gently stretched, a limb that feels hard, numbness or tingling in the territory of the nerve that crosses it, and weakness in a movement the patient could perform earlier are the findings that declare it. Pallor, coolness and an absent pulse are late signs, and a responder who waits for them waits for a limb that can no longer be saved. The diagnosis is clinical and belongs to a clinician, and where a service measures the pressure inside a compartment, that reading supports the judgment rather than replacing it, and the threshold differs between services and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

Once a compartment syndrome is suspected, the acts that follow are few and each has an owner. The responder who applied an encircling bandage or splint does not tighten it, and a constricting bandage, splint, cast or dressing is released only by someone the local protocol authorizes to do so, because the device applied to protect the limb can be the very thing strangling it. The limb is not raised above the level of the heart, since lifting it lowers the pressure that drives blood through capillaries already failing, and here the instinct to elevate an injured limb is plainly wrong. The patient is kept warm, because cold adds constriction and pain to a limb short of blood, and the time of every observation and every intervention is written down. The nurse escalates a change at once rather than at the next round, while the physician makes the diagnosis and calls the surgeon, because the operation that opens the compartments, called a fasciotomy, belongs to the operating room and to nobody else.

When a compartment syndrome is missed, the damage is permanent and it is measured in what the patient can no longer do. Muscle deprived of blood for hours dies and is replaced by scar, and the resulting contracture pulls the fingers or the toes into a fixed position that no splint can correct, which is why this course repeats that time is muscle. Dead muscle releases pigment into the bloodstream, and a patient whose urine turns dark, like tea or cola, may be suffering the kidney injury that follows a large crush or a large area of dead tissue. That patient belongs in an intensive care unit, where the physician supports the circulation and the kidneys while the surgeon completes the removal of dead tissue. The lay first aider and the trained responder make none of those decisions, and their task is to notice the tense limb, the dark urine or the deteriorating patient and to say it aloud, early, to someone who can act.

Infection is the second complication that declares itself in the first days, and the open fracture is the injury that invites it. The skin carries organisms that reach the bone and the deep tissues in the moment of the injury, so a wound that looked clean at the roadside is contaminated, and the size of the wound says nothing about the amount of contamination. The signs that should raise alarm are pain that increases instead of settling, swelling, redness and warmth around the wound, discharge that may be thin or thick, a wound that begins to smell, and a patient who feels unwell, shivers, or has a temperature above 38 degrees Celsius, a figure to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. A wound that was dry and becomes wet is worth reporting even when the patient feels well. The lay first aider covers the wound with a clean or sterile dressing, the trained responder records the appearance and the neurovascular findings, and neither explores the wound blindly, removes a dressing that is stuck, or squeezes anything out of it, because blind exploration pushes organisms deeper and can displace a clot that was doing useful work.

A deep infection announces itself less clearly than a wound infection, because the wound may have healed before the pain begins. A patient whose pain increases days after an operation, whose wound leaks a little, or who feels generally unwell and cannot say why may be developing an infection in the bone or in the tissues around the metal placed to hold it, and that diagnosis belongs to the physician and the surgeon, who examine, image and take samples. Fever, a rapid pulse, rapid breathing and confusion in an injured patient point to infection that has spread through the body and constitute a medical emergency. A limb whose pain is severe, whose skin is changing color and whose tissues feel stiff or crackle under the fingers may be carrying an infection that requires the operating room within hours and admits no delay for pictures. The nurse sees the wound, the temperature and the behavior of the patient at every round, so a change reported then is worth more than one discovered the next day.

Antibiotics are selected and prescribed under the local protocol by those authorized to prescribe and to give them, and this course names no substance, no amount, no route and no frequency, so that no listener can carry a prescription away from these chapters. Protection against tetanus is likewise a decision for the treating clinician and for the local protocol, and the question belongs in the first conversation rather than in the memory of the patient. An embedded object is never removed and exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, because the object may be holding pressure on a torn vessel. Definitive closure of a wound, the washing out of an infected joint and the removal of infected bone belong to the operating room, and a wound is never closed in the field by anyone at any level of practice.

The lung is the organ that suffers most from a patient who cannot move, and the first days after a fracture are when that suffering begins. A patient who lies still, especially one whose ribs hurt or who is afraid to take a deep breath, stops opening the small airways at the base of the lungs, and the secretions that collect there become a chest infection. A cough that brings up sputum, a fever, a rising breathing rate, breathlessness and a falling oxygen level measured on a finger probe are the findings, and they appear gradually rather than suddenly, which is why they are compared with the record rather than judged alone. A patient who becomes breathless and confused in the first day or two after a fracture of a long bone, and who develops a fine rash of tiny red spots across the chest, the neck and the armpits, may be suffering the fat embolism syndrome, which is a clinical suspicion rather than a diagnosis a responder can make. That suspicion is reported urgently to the physician and is managed in an intensive care unit.

Prevention of the chest complications of immobility is shared work, and most of it costs nothing. The nurse sits the patient up as soon as the injury and the protocol allow, encourages deep breaths and an effective cough, and uses whatever device the service provides to help the lungs open. The physical therapist teaches the patient how to clear secretions and how soon to move, within the limits set by the surgeon and by the local protocol, and the physician decides whether a chest infection is present and whether anything is to be prescribed. The lay first aider and the trained responder contribute by keeping the patient warm, supporting the injured part so that coughing hurts as little as possible, and reporting a cough, a fever or a change in breathing to the receiving team. Nothing in that list contains a drug or a dose, because any program of movement is set by the treating team for that particular patient.

Blood that does not move clots, and a patient immobilized by a fracture, a splint or pain is a patient whose leg veins are asking for a clot. A clot in the deep veins of the leg is called a deep vein thrombosis, and it may announce itself with a calf that is tender, swollen, warm and painful when the foot is bent. It may equally announce itself with nothing at all, and the classic sign of calf pain on bending the foot is taught as unreliable. The clot is dangerous not because it blocks the leg but because a piece of it can travel to the lungs, and that risk lasts through the first days and the first weeks of immobility. A patient who is dehydrated, older, or has had a clot before is at greater risk, and that assessment belongs to the physician.

A pulmonary embolism is a clot that has traveled to the lungs, and it can be the first sign that a clot existed anywhere. The patient becomes suddenly short of breath, may clutch the chest, may cough blood, may have a racing pulse and may faint or collapse. In the worst case the collapse is the first event, and the patient then needs CPR at once while emergency help is called, performed by anyone trained to perform it. A patient with a swollen and tender calf is not massaged, not walked about to test the leg and not treated as a curiosity, because handling a clot can help it travel, so the leg is left alone, the patient is kept still and the physician is called. The measures that reduce this risk are chosen by the treating team, and the physician decides what prevention is appropriate, whether mechanical or prescribed, under the local protocol.

A splint that is too tight or too long does its damage slowly and silently, and the first sign is usually the only chance there is to prevent a deep wound. The device presses hardest over the bony points, which include the ankle, the heel, the knee, the elbow and the back of the wrist, and the skin under a splint cannot be seen, so the report of burning pain under an edge carries more weight than any inspection. The first visible change is a red area that does not pale when it is pressed, and if the pressure continues the skin blisters, breaks and then dies, leaving a wound that takes weeks or months to heal and can become infected. Nerves suffer in the same way, and pressure from a splint or a sling can weaken the hand or produce a foot that drops when the patient tries to raise it. The nerve that lifts the foot passes around the head of the smaller bone of the lower leg, and the nerve at the inner side of the elbow is exposed in the same way.

Prevention of a splint injury is largely a matter of padding and of checking, and both have a named owner. A splint is padded over every bony point and along its edges, it is not applied over a wet, dirty or already reddened limb, and it is secured firmly enough to hold without becoming a band. Whoever applied the device, whether a trained responder, a paramedic or a physician, remains responsible for checking its edges and for teaching the patient to report pain beneath it, while the nurse inspects the skin at every opportunity, especially at the heel and the ankle. Only someone the local protocol authorizes removes or replaces a cast, splits a cast to relieve pressure, or cuts a window in it, and the orthopedic practitioner decides what device the injury needs once the swelling has changed. A collar or a spinal board left in place too long produces the same skin injury, so the spine is treated as unstable only until someone qualified to clear it has done so, and the device is then removed by that person rather than by a responder acting alone.

A splint that is not checked can also lose the position of the bone, and the loss may stay invisible until somebody looks for it. The fracture is held not only by the device but by the swelling around it, so when the swelling subsides over the first days the device that was tight becomes loose and the bone ends can move again. A splint that does not include the joint above and the joint below leaves the fracture free to be moved by one of those joints, and a splint that does not control rotation allows the far end of the bone to turn while the patient shifts in bed. A patient transferred with a loose splint and no record of how the limb was positioned may arrive with a deformity nobody can explain. The check that prevents this is simple, and it consists of looking at the limb, comparing it with the other side and comparing it with what was written down.

The findings of a lost position are the return of a deformity that had settled, a new angulation or rotation, pain that increases where it had been easing, and a hand or foot the patient can no longer move as well as before. Those findings are reported at once to the clinician responsible, who decides whether a new radiograph is needed, taken in at least two directions so that a change in the third is not hidden. Restoring the position of the bone ends is called a reduction, and holding them with a gentle continuous pull is called traction, and both are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, with the sedation and the setting decided by the clinician who performs them. No responder attempts a reduction in the field to spare the patient a journey, because force applied to a broken bone can convert a closed injury into an open one and can tear a vessel that was intact, and force is not a technique. A reduction is also not attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when the vessels or the nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, or when an earlier attempt has failed.

Assessment in a chapter about the first days differs from assessment in the first hour, because the patient is already splinted, already imaged and often already comfortable. The history begins with the injury, its time and its mechanism, and then continues with everything done since, including every device applied, every transfer and every change reported by anyone who has touched the patient. The examination of the limb is repeated in the same order as before and compared with the earlier record rather than judged alone, because a limb that is tense today was described yesterday and a hand that is weaker today was tested yesterday. The general condition of the patient belongs to the same examination, and a rising pulse, a rising temperature, a falling oxygen level, a new cough, confusion or a calf that has become swollen all change what is done next. The interval at which these observations are repeated differs between services and injuries, and it must be confirmed against the local protocol rather than assumed.

Stabilization for a patient in the first days still means keeping the patient warm, controlling any bleeding that can be controlled and supporting the injured part, and it now includes protecting the patient from the complications that are already developing. Warmth matters for the same reason it mattered in the first hour, because a cold patient clots badly, feels pain more keenly and resists infection less well, so insulation is part of the treatment rather than a comfort measure. The splint is supported rather than adjusted by the responder, and any device that is cutting into the skin or constricting the limb is brought to the attention of someone authorized to modify it rather than loosened in a corridor. Whether the patient may eat or drink before a possible operation, and what relief of pain is appropriate, are decisions of the treating clinician and the local protocol, and the substance, the amount and the route belong to the prescriber. Any relief given before an examination is recorded, and the examination is performed anyway, because a patient who is comfortable can still be a patient whose limb is in danger.

The neurovascular examination is performed before any intervention and repeated after it, and in the first days it is repeated again because the limb itself keeps changing. Color is compared side to side, temperature is felt with the back of the examiner's hand, the pulse beyond the injury is described rather than merely noted as present, and the nail bed is pressed briefly to watch the color return. Sensation is tested on both sides and compared, and the patient is asked to move each finger or each toe and to say whether the limb feels numb, tingling or weaker than it did. The findings are written with the time, because a deficit that was not recorded before a splint was applied cannot be attributed afterwards, and in the first days the same rule applies to a deficit discovered after a transfer or after an operation. The lay first aider reports color, warmth and whether the fingers or the toes still move, while the trained responder, the nurse and the physician perform the formal examination and the clinician interprets it.

Immobilization in the first days is a matter of keeping the decision that was made and of noticing when it has stopped working. The device is checked for tightness, for edges that press, for padding that has slipped and for a fit that has changed as the swelling has fallen, and the check is repeated rather than performed once. A splint is never tightened by a responder to make it feel secure, and an improvised device is checked more often than a commercial one because it shifts. The trained responder and the paramedic apply and adjust splints within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, while the physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides what definitive immobilization the injury needs, which may be a cast, a brace or surgical fixation. Casting, reduction and traction are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and nothing in this course teaches a listener to perform them, because a book cannot see the limb and cannot feel the resistance that means stop.

Imaging in the first days answers specific questions, and it is ordered by a clinician trained to interpret it. A radiograph is used to check that the position of the fracture is being held and that the device has not allowed movement, and it is taken in at least two directions so that a change in the third direction is not hidden. Computed tomography shows the detail of a complex break around a joint and is used when the answer will change what is done, while magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for soft tissue, including ligaments, cartilage, tendons and muscle. An image is a picture of one moment, and a patient who is developing a compartment syndrome must not wait in a radiology department while pictures are taken, because that diagnosis is clinical and time is muscle. The lay first aider never orders or interprets imaging, the trained responder does not choose the study, and the decision belongs to the clinician responsible for the patient.

Reassessment is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment, and in the first days it is performed after every intervention and at the intervals the local protocol sets. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the record, because the value lies in the comparison rather than in any single measurement. Pain that increases instead of settling, pain far worse than the injury appears to explain, numbness, tingling, weakness, a limb that becomes tense and hard, a wound that begins to discharge, a cough or a fever, a calf that swells and a patient who becomes confused are the findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation. A patient who was comfortable an hour ago and is distressed now is telling the team that something has changed, and the answer is to examine again, to check every device and to call for help rather than wait. The responder also says aloud what would make the situation unbearable, which here means a tense limb with rising pain, a wound that is spreading infection, a patient who cannot breathe, or a patient who collapses.

Referral or definitive treatment is the final step of the sequence, and most of the complications in this chapter are the reason a patient is referred rather than watched. A patient with a suspected compartment syndrome, a spreading infection, a clot in the leg or in the lung, a chest infection that is not settling, or a fracture whose position has been lost is referred to a facility with imaging, an operating room and an intensive care unit. The referral is made by the clinician responsible for the patient, while the lay first aider and the trained responder call for help early rather than deciding what treatment is needed, and the nurse escalates a change at once. The surgeon opens a compartment, washes out an infection and removes dead tissue, and everything given to the patient is decided and provided under the local protocol by those authorized to prescribe and to give it. The record and the handover travel with the patient, so the time of the injury, the position of the limb and the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention are written in a fixed order.

To summarize, the complications that decide the first days are quieter than the injury that caused them, and they are found by looking for them before the patient complains. Compartment syndrome declares itself with pain out of proportion to the injury, a tense and swollen limb and numbness or weakness, it is diagnosed by a clinician and treated by a surgeon in the operating room, a pulse that is still present proves nothing, and the limb is not raised above the level of the heart. Infection in an open fracture or around an implant shows itself as increasing pain, redness, warmth, discharge, smell and fever, and it is treated by the surgeon while every substance and every amount belongs to the prescriber and the local protocol. Immobility threatens the lungs with infection and the veins with clots that can travel to the lungs and kill, so the patient is sat up, encouraged to breathe and to cough, moved as soon as the treating team allows, and watched for breathlessness, chest pain or a swollen calf, and a patient who collapses needs CPR at once. A splint that is too tight, too long or no longer checked injures the skin over the bony points, presses on nerves and lets the bone lose the position it had, so it is padded and watched, and only someone the local protocol authorizes modifies it. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, a deformed limb is never straightened in the field, an embedded object is never removed, an open wound is never explored blindly, and the patient is reassessed after every intervention and referred early.
